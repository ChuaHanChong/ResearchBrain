# Failing Gracefully: Mitigating Impact of Inevitable Robot Failures

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces a framework for quantitatively assessing the impact of inevitable robot failures, distinguishing between minor and severe consequences, and develops FailBench, a MuJoCo-based simulation environment for studying robot-environment interactions under various failure modes. This approach enables robots to make planning decisions that balance task efficiency with mitigating potential harm from unpreventable malfunctions.

## Problem

- Service robots in human environments face inevitable failures (hardware degradation, software bugs, unexpected interactions) with potentially severe consequences like physical injury or property damage.
- Existing research primarily focuses on failure prevention or recovery, often treating failures as mere task incompletion, lacking systematic assessment of actual consequences.
- Studying failure consequences in real-world household environments is difficult due to rare events and a lack of standardized benchmarks, leading to inefficient naive mitigation strategies.

## Method

- A novel ==safety formulation== quantifies failure impact by combining the probability of a robot component interacting negatively with an environmental entity and the severity of that interaction.
- **FailBench**, a MuJoCo-based simulation framework, is introduced to systematically study robot-environment interactions under diverse, unexpected failure modes, featuring a detailed ==failure injector==.
- The proposed formulation is integrated into a ==motion planning objective== to minimize a combined cost of task efficiency and expected failure impact, allowing for principled decision-making.

## Results

- Evaluation in FailBench for a pick-and-place task showed that trajectories designed for lower theoretical safety costs (as low as **0.236**) incurred higher motion costs (up to **1.958**), illustrating the trade-off between safety and efficiency.
- Trajectory analysis identified an optimal balance (Trajectory 2) with the lowest total cost (**1.71**), while the trajectory prioritizing safety (Trajectory 4) achieved the lowest observed safety cost (**0.27**).
- Observed safety costs from simulations generally aligned with theoretical predictions, confirming the framework's capability to assess potential failure impact, though some discrepancies suggested refining interaction probability models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly quantifying the expected impact of inevitable failures allows robots to make informed planning decisions that balance task efficiency with consequence mitigation.
> - Directly altering underlying MuJoCo simulation parameters for failure injection provides a more realistic representation of hardware failures and their physical propagation.
> - Evaluating trajectories based on a combined cost (task efficiency + failure impact) reveals important trade-offs, indicating that optimizing solely for efficiency may increase potential harm.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of service robots into dynamic and human-shared environments, such as households, presents challenges related to safety and reliability. Robots operating in these settings are susceptible to various failures, including software malfunctions, hardware degradation, and unpredictable interactions with their surroundings. The broader research landscape primarily focuses on two aspects of robotic failures: prevention and recovery.

Previous research in robotics often emphasizes strategies to minimize the likelihood of failures or to enable robots to recover once a failure has occurred. This includes work on developing robust risk formulations,–, categorizing failure types, and designing systems for fault detection and recovery. For example, studies have classified failures as physical (e.g., power loss, sensor errors) or man-made (e.g., design flaws), and datasets like RoboFail have focused on evaluating policy robustness by defining failure as the inability to complete a task. However, these approaches often treat failure primarily as task incompletion and may not fully address the consequences of failures that arise from sudden, unpredictable events like hardware faults or unexpected dynamics, which are difficult to prevent or detect in advance.

Another significant area is safety filtering, which employs control-theoretic methods such as control barrier functions and Hamilton-Jacobi reachability, to ensure system safety by preventing robots from entering unsafe states. While effective for collision avoidance and constraint satisfaction, these methods are often insufficient when confronted with sudden, unpreventable hardware failures, such as joint degradation or actuator malfunctions. In household settings, the implications of such failures can extend beyond direct collisions to more complex hazards like spilled liquids or dropped objects, which can cause property damage or physical harm.

Fault injection techniques, have also been developed to understand system behavior under various failure conditions, focusing on identifying when failures occur and system vulnerabilities. These methods, however, typically do not quantify the potential consequences of failures that are truly unavoidable or undetectable in real-time.

This work contributes to the landscape by shifting the focus from solely preventing failures or identifying them, to systematically assessing and mitigating the *impact* of failures that are considered inevitable. It addresses a gap by providing a framework that enables robots to reason about the potential consequences of failures at a given moment, thereby informing planning decisions to minimize harm. This approach acknowledges that while failures can be reduced, they cannot be entirely eliminated in complex, dynamic environments, making consequence mitigation a critical aspect of safe robot deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of service robots in human-inhabited household environments. These robots are expected to perform diverse tasks such as cleaning, fetching, and cooking, often requiring operation near humans, pets, and valuable objects. In such dynamic and unstructured settings, robots are highly susceptible to various failures—ranging from hardware degradation and software bugs to unforeseen interactions. The potential consequences of these failures are significant, including physical injury to humans or pets, damage to fragile property, or the creation of secondary hazards (e.g., a spilled hot liquid causing burns or a slippery floor). Ensuring safety in the presence of these inevitable failures is therefore paramount for the widespread adoption and public acceptance of service robots.

The authors identify several key limitations in the existing research landscape that motivate their work:
- **Lack of systematic failure consequence assessment:** Most prior work focuses on failure prevention or recovery, often treating failures merely as task incompletion. There is a lack of systematic categorization and quantitative evaluation of the actual consequences (e.g., property damage, human injury) of failures when they occur.
- **Inefficiency of naive mitigation strategies:** Simple mitigation strategies, such as inflating object boundaries or defining "no-go zones," can drastically reduce a robot's operational workspace, thereby compromising task efficiency and utility. A more nuanced approach is required that balances safety with efficiency.
- **Scarcity of real-world failure data and standardized benchmarks:** Studying the consequences of robot failures in real-world household environments is challenging due to the rarity of real-world failure events and the lack of standardized benchmarks. Without sufficient data or a common evaluation platform, it is difficult for robots to analyze diverse failure consequences, plan motions, or learn policies that effectively distinguish between minor and severe failures while maintaining task efficiency.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out the following key objectives:
1. **Develop a novel safety formulation:** Introduce a quantitative framework that explicitly accounts for the consequences of failures during the planning process. This formulation evaluates the potential interactions between robot components and environmental entities when failures occur and quantifies the severity of each interaction to derive an overall impact measure.
2. **Introduce FailBench, a simulation framework:** Create a MuJoCo-based simulation environment to systematically study robot-environment interactions under diverse, unexpected failure modes. FailBench is designed to generate failure data and serve as a benchmark for evaluating failure mitigation strategies.
3. **Demonstrate the utility of the framework:** Validate that the proposed safety formulation can effectively reflect the failure consequences simulated within FailBench, thereby laying the groundwork for future research into failure mitigation for service robots.

These objectives collectively aim to enable robots to make informed planning decisions that proactively mitigate the impact of inevitable failures, balancing safety considerations with task efficiency for safer and more robust deployment in real-world household environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a novel problem formulation for assessing failure impact and a simulation framework, FailBench, for systematic evaluation.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The core of the methodology is a safety formulation that quantifies the impact of failures.
- **Robot Components and Environment Entities:** The robot (R) is conceptually divided into potential hazard-posing components: the robot body ($r_1$), the object being carried ($r_2$), and, if applicable, the object's contents ($r_3$). The environment consists of entities (E), static or dynamic, that can be affected by the robot.
- **Interaction Probability:** An interaction is defined as an accident following a failure that causes harm. The formulation introduces $P_t(x_t, e_i, r_j | Failure = True)$, which represents the likelihood that robot component $r_j$ interacts negatively with environmental entity $e_i$ at robot state $x_t$, given that a failure has occurred. This probability quantifies the likelihood of a negative *effect* from a failure, rather than the probability of failure itself.
- **Severity of Interaction:** To account for different levels of harm, a severity factor, $S(e_i, r_j)$, is defined. This factor quantifies the potential impact of an interaction between $e_i$ and $r_j$, allowing the robot to differentiate between minor and severe consequences.
- **Optimization Objective:** The motion planner's objective is to minimize a combined cost function:
 $argmin_{x_1...,x_T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} [V(x_t, x_{t-1}) + w \cdot \sum_{r_j \in R} \sum_{e_i \in E} P_t(x_t, e_i, r_j |F ) \cdot S(e_i, r_j)]$
 Here, $V(x_t, x_{t-1})$ represents the cost of transitioning between states (task efficiency), and the second term quantifies the failure impact, weighted by $w$. This objective allows robots to balance task efficiency with the mitigation of inevitable failure consequences.

**B. FailBench Simulation Framework**
FailBench is a MuJoCo-based simulation framework designed for studying robot-environment interactions under unexpected failures.
- **Simulation Core:** It utilizes MuJoCo for high-fidelity physics simulations, providing access to ground-truth states, contact forces, and other environmental metrics. The framework includes curated scenes and object meshes from existing datasets, and supports various robot embodiments (e.g., Clearpath Jackal, Fetch mobile manipulator, Franka Panda Arm). Its emphasis is on failure-aware planning and policy evaluation, rather than solely learning-based approaches.
- **Motion Planning:**
 - **Navigation:** Provides interfaces for generating floor maps, supporting search-based (A*, Dijkstra’s) and sampling-based algorithms for high-level path planning. Low-level control for wheel-based robots is implemented using Dynamic Window Approach and differential-drive controllers.
 - **Manipulation:** Leverages Mink for differential inverse kinematics in MuJoCo. It supports arm planning in both joint-space and task-space, integrating a variety of motion planners, including sampling-based (PRM, RRT, RRT*, RRTConnect, TRRT) and optimization-based (STOMP, CHOMP).
- **Failure Injector:** A core feature that introduces controlled perturbations to the robot and its environment.
 - **Taxonomy:** Categorizes failures into actuator, sensor, end-effector, and power system failures (e.g., complete shutdown, noisy sensor, gripper failure, battery degradation).
 - **Onset Patterns:** Failures can be instant (immediate occurrence) or gradual (develops over time).
 - **Mechanism:** Unlike approaches that modify only control signals, the injector directly alters underlying MuJoCo simulation parameters, such as actuator gains, joint constraints, sensor feedback, and mechanical properties, to physically represent hardware failures. This ensures realistic failure propagation and system responses.
 - **Control:** Allows for precise timing control, affecting single or multiple joints, and supporting both deterministic and stochastic failure scenarios. Configurable failure degrees (minor to critical) and durations (temporary or permanent) are supported.
- **Data Generation:** FailBench quantifies failure aftereffects through contact events. It captures contact point positions and corresponding forces using the MuJoCo physics engine, providing data for impact assessment that aligns with the problem formulation's interaction framework.

**C. Failure Impact Analysis**
To evaluate the proposed formulation, the authors developed computational techniques.
- **Interaction Probability Estimation (Algorithm 1):** This algorithm estimates interaction probabilities under joint failures, specifically focusing on object drop failures in the reported experiments. It computes the geometric overlap area in the X-Y plane between the axis-aligned bounding boxes of robot component geoms and environmental entity geoms. The probability is derived from the ratio of this overlap area to the areas of the components. A Z-axis check ensures that entities below the robot are considered for interactions.
- **Trajectory Analysis:** The framework was applied to evaluate four trajectories for a tabletop pick-and-place task using a Franka Emika Panda robot arm in FailBench.
 - **Setup:** RRT-Connect generated trajectories. Object severity values were predefined (e.g., red objects = high severity (10), white objects = standard severity (2)).
 - **Metrics:** Motion cost (cumulative Euclidean distance), safety cost (cumulative expected impact from Eq. 1 with $w=1$), and total cost (sum of motion and safety costs).
 - **Validation:** Each trajectory was simulated 60 times, with a 25% probability of an object drop failure occurring at a randomly sampled timestep. "Observed Safety" (OBS) was the average measured safety cost across these runs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed failure impact framework and FailBench involved analyzing four distinct trajectories for a pick-and-place task, comparing theoretical safety predictions with empirical validation through simulation.

The trajectory analysis revealed specific trade-offs between motion efficiency and safety:
- **Trajectory 4 (green):** This trajectory demonstrated the lowest theoretical safety cost (0.236), indicating its designed robustness against failure impact. However, it incurred the highest motion cost (1.958), resulting in the highest overall total cost (2.194). This suggests a strategy that prioritizes safety at the expense of operational efficiency.
- **Trajectory 2 (orange):** This trajectory achieved the lowest motion cost (1.280) and the lowest total cost (1.71). While its theoretical safety cost (0.430) was higher than Trajectories 3 and 4, it presented an optimal balance between efficiency and safety among the evaluated options.
- **Trajectory 1 (light blue):** This trajectory exhibited suboptimal performance with the second-highest motion cost (1.567) and total cost (1.914), placing it below Trajectory 2 in terms of combined efficiency and safety. Its theoretical safety cost was 0.347.
- **Trajectory 3 (pink):** This trajectory provided a moderate solution, with a motion cost of 1.359, a theoretical safety cost of 0.363, and a total cost of 1.722. It represented a middle-ground in the trade-off spectrum.

The validation with observed safety (OBS) from simulations generally aligned with the theoretical safety cost predictions, supporting the framework's predictive capability:
- Trajectory 4, with the lowest theoretical safety cost (0.236), also exhibited the lowest observed safety (0.27), empirically confirming its superior safety performance in practice.
- Trajectory 3 showed low observed safety (0.4), consistent with its moderate theoretical safety cost (0.363).
- However, some discrepancies were noted:
 - Trajectory 2 demonstrated a higher observed safety (5.6) than its theoretical cost (0.430) would suggest.
 - Trajectory 1, despite having a lower theoretical safety cost (0.347) than Trajectory 2, showed an intermediate observed safety (3.33).
 These discrepancies indicate the complexity of real-world failure scenarios and suggest potential areas for refining the interaction probability models to enhance accuracy.

In summary, the results indicate that the failure impact assessment framework can provide quantitative insights into the safety implications of different trajectories. It highlights that traditional planners, which primarily optimize for geometric or efficiency objectives, may not inherently account for these failure impact costs. The framework's ability to assess various trajectory candidates can inform the design of future failure-aware planning algorithms that explicitly integrate impact mitigation, allowing for principled decisions that balance operational efficiency with the minimization of harm when failures are unavoidable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for enhancing the safety and reliability of service robots operating in human environments by explicitly addressing the consequences of inevitable failures. The contributions are multi-faceted:

First, the introduction of a **novel safety formulation** that quantifies both the probability of impactful interactions and the severity of their outcomes is a significant step. By combining interaction modeling with severity metrics, the framework moves beyond traditional failure prevention or recovery to systematically assess safety risks associated with robot actions under failure conditions. This quantitative approach provides a structured method for evaluating how robots handle malfunctions, thereby establishing a more rigorous basis for safety assessment in service robotics.

Second, the development of **FailBench**, a MuJoCo-based simulation framework, provides a crucial tool for the research community. FailBench enables controlled, systematic studies of robot-environment interactions across diverse failure modes, including sensing issues, hardware malfunctions, and actuator degradation. Its features—such as curated household environments, standardized motion planning implementations, a comprehensive failure injector, and quantitative metrics for impact assessment—make it a valuable benchmarking platform. This resource is expected to accelerate research in robot safety by facilitating the generation of failure data and enabling systematic comparison of different approaches to failure handling and safety-aware robotics.

Third, the **demonstration that the new formulation reflects simulated failure consequences** within FailBench validates its utility. This empirical evidence indicates that the framework can provide actionable insights for evaluating trajectory safety. It highlights that planning solely for efficiency may inadvertently increase failure impact, suggesting that incorporating this new safety metric can lead to more robust and responsible robot behaviors.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for the widespread adoption of service robots. By shifting the focus from solely preventing failures to systematically evaluating and mitigating their consequences, the research provides tools and metrics necessary for developing robots that can "fail gracefully." This capability is critical for building user trust and ensuring the safe deployment of autonomous systems in dynamic household environments.

Looking forward, the authors identify several key areas for future work that underscore the potential impact:
- **Environmental Knowledge Requirements:** Integrating Visual-Language Models (VLMs) to automatically estimate interaction severity from visual input could overcome the current reliance on complete prior environmental knowledge, enabling practical deployment in unstructured environments.
- **Failure Interaction Modeling:** Leveraging FailBench to collect extensive contact data could facilitate learning more accurate probability distributions using neural networks or physical world models, leading to more realistic predictions of failure outcomes.
- **Integration with Motion Planning:** Exploring latent-space planning could help overcome computational challenges posed by the complex, discontinuous cost landscapes inherent in failure-aware planning, making it feasible to incorporate these safety considerations into real-time trajectory generation.
- **Task-Level Failure Awareness:** Extending the framework to Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) would allow robots to consider failure impact across entire task sequences, enabling higher-level strategic decisions (e.g., acquiring protective equipment before handling fragile items).

These future directions suggest that this foundational work could lead to more intelligent and resilient robotic systems capable of making principled decisions that prioritize safety and minimize harm, even when faced with unavoidable malfunctions.
