# Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining

> [!summary] Summary
> Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining (CTTP) establishes a self-supervised framework to learn unified, sensor-agnostic representations from paired tactile data across distinct sensor modalities. This method enabled "zero-shot" transfer between sensors, improving cross-sensor classification accuracy significantly over baselines and leading to a 3.6x higher success rate in a real-world peg insertion task.

## Problem

- Diverse tactile sensor designs lead to a lack of interoperability, making algorithms and models sensor-specific.
- Existing methods struggle with substantial data distribution shifts between different tactile sensors, hindering generalization.
- Adapting to new tactile sensors requires extensive effort, data collection, and labeling, limiting scalability and widespread adoption.

## Method

- Introduced **CTTP** (==Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining==), a ==self-supervised== framework.
- Utilizes ==InfoNCE loss== to learn a unified embedding space by maximizing similarity between tactile signals from different sensors observing the same physical interaction.
- Employs a ==ResNet-50 encoder== and an ==MLP projection head==, trained on paired tactile data from GelSlim and Soft Bubble sensors.

## Results

- CTTP significantly outperformed baselines in cross-sensor generalization for object classification, maintaining high accuracy while others approximated random chance.
- Achieved substantially lower errors in cross-sensor in-hand pose estimation (e.g., orientation errors within **±5°** vs. **±18-38°** for baselines).
- Demonstrated practical utility in a real-world robotic peg insertion task, yielding **18** successful insertions compared to **5** for the best baseline model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Contrastive learning can effectively align latent representations from distinct tactile sensor modalities, forming a unified, sensor-agnostic space.
> - An optimal batch size during training (e.g., 128) is crucial for balancing the differentiation of objects with the alignment of sensor representations.
> - The learned representations enable direct "zero-shot" transfer of models between different tactile sensors, overcoming the limitations of previous methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing is recognized as a crucial component for enabling dexterous robotic manipulation, providing data on contact forces, object properties, and interaction dynamics. However, the field of tactile sensing is characterized by a high degree of sensor diversity, in contrast to the standardization observed in vision and audio modalities. Various tactile sensors, such as GelSlim, Soft Bubble, and DIGIT, employ distinct designs and mechanisms, yielding different forms of tactile feedback. This diversity, while allowing for specialized applications, creates a challenge for developing general-purpose algorithms and models, as solutions designed for one sensor often do not generalize to others without significant adaptation or retraining. This sensor-specificity limits the widespread applicability of tactile manipulation techniques and increases development costs in real-world scenarios.

Existing approaches to address cross-sensor disparities include sensor-specific calibration, which is often difficult to generalize, and transfer learning techniques. However, these methods frequently struggle with the substantial distribution shifts between different tactile sensors, leading to performance degradation. Previous research in representation learning for tactile data has explored aligning tactile signals with other modalities, primarily vision, or training with task-specific supervised learning or masked autoencoding. While some works have investigated generative models for translating between tactile signals, the problem of learning unified, sensor-agnostic touch representations through direct cross-sensor alignment using contrastive learning has not been extensively addressed in the context of visuo-tactile sensors.

This work introduces Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining (CTTP) as a self-supervised learning framework that specifically aims to bridge the gap between different tactile sensor modalities. It draws inspiration from Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) but extends the principle of contrastive learning to paired tactile data from two distinct sensors. By focusing on learning a shared embedding space that captures common physical properties from different tactile signatures, CTTP differentiates itself from approaches that rely on vision-tactile alignment or extensive task-specific labeling. The objective is to enable "zero-shot" transfer of models trained on one sensor to another, a capability that existing methods have not consistently achieved. This positions CTTP as an advancement towards more generalized and scalable tactile sensing systems, reducing the reliance on sensor-specific model development and data collection.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the significant challenge posed by the diversity of tactile sensor designs in robotics. Current tactile sensors, despite their individual utility, lack interoperability; algorithms and models developed for one sensor type rarely generalize effectively to others. This sensor-specificity leads to several practical limitations:

- **Labor-intensive Adaptation:** Each time a new tactile sensor is introduced or a robot system is updated with a different sensor, substantial effort is required to adapt existing algorithms or develop new ones from scratch. This hinders rapid deployment and integration of tactile capabilities in various robotic applications.
- **Limited Generalization:** The unique features encoded by each sensor, based on its specific design and sensing mechanism, create significant distribution shifts that conventional transfer learning methods struggle to overcome. This results in inadequate performance when attempting to apply models across different sensor types.
- **Data Scarcity and Labeling Burden:** Many advanced tactile applications require large, labeled datasets for supervised learning. The need to collect and label sensor-specific data for every new sensor or task is resource-intensive and impractical for widespread adoption. Existing unified models often still necessitate aligned vision data or intensive labeling for object class and pose, adding to this burden.
- **Lack of Shared Representation:** Prior approaches have not effectively captured the underlying information shared between tactile sensors that employ different sensing modalities. This gap prevents the development of robust, sensor-agnostic representations that can generalize across heterogeneous tactile systems.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Unified Tactile Representation:** To learn an embedding that captures the shared physical information between different tactile sensors, making the representation sensor-agnostic. This unified representation should encapsulate meaningful similarities between disparate tactile signatures.
2. **Enable Cross-Sensor Generalization:** To create a framework that allows models trained using data from one tactile sensor to be directly deployed and perform effectively with data from a different tactile sensor, without requiring additional training or adaptation. This capability is referred to as "zero-shot" transfer.
3. **Provide Effective Pretraining:** To demonstrate that the learned shared representation serves as robust pretraining for various downstream tactile understanding tasks, such as object classification and in-hand pose estimation, improving their performance and reducing their reliance on task-specific labeled data.
4. **Leverage Self-Supervised Learning:** To achieve these objectives using a self-supervised approach, specifically contrastive learning on paired tactile data, thereby minimizing the need for extensive human-labeled data and facilitating scalability. The method focuses on preserving shared information in tactile signatures rather than reconstruction or task-specific objectives.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to overcome the current limitations of sensor-specificity, improve the generalizability of tactile algorithms, and promote the wider adoption of tactile sensing in robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining (CTTP), a self-supervised learning framework designed to learn a unified representation across different tactile sensors.

**A. Contrastive Learning on Paired Tactile Data**
The methodology adapts contrastive learning, a technique commonly used in domains like vision and language, to tactile data. The central idea is to learn an embedding space where similar inputs (positive pairs) are brought closer together, while dissimilar inputs (negative pairs) are pushed apart.

- **Positive Pairs:** A positive pair consists of tactile signals ($t_1, t_2$) collected from two different sensors (e.g., GelSlim and Soft Bubble) when grasping the *same object in the same configuration*. This ensures that the physical interaction generating the tactile signals is fundamentally identical, regardless of the sensor type.
- **Negative Pairs:** Negative pairs are formed by tactile signals from either sensor that originate from *different objects* or from *distinct grasp configurations* of the same object. These represent physically different interactions.
- **Embedding Function:** A neural network encoder, denoted $f_\theta$, is trained to map raw tactile signals into a latent embedding space, where $z^{(1)}_i$ and $z^{(2)}_i$ represent the latent embeddings of the tactile signals from the two sensors.
- **Loss Function:** The InfoNCE loss function is employed to optimize the embedding function. This loss maximizes the cosine similarity between the latent representations of positive pairs ($z^{(1)}_i, z^{(2)}_i$) while minimizing the similarity between an anchor embedding ($z^{(1)}_i$) and all other negative examples ($z^{(1/2)}_j$) within the batch. A temperature parameter ($\tau$) controls the sharpness of the distribution.

**B. Experimental Setup**
- **Robot and Sensors:** Experiments were conducted using a robotic setup equipped with two types of vision-based tactile sensors: GelSlim and Soft Bubble. GelSlim uses an RGB camera to detect elastomer deformations under LED illumination, while Soft Bubble uses a camera-based depth sensor to capture deformations of an air-filled membrane. These sensors were chosen due to their common use in robotics and their distinct sensing mechanisms, offering a challenging test for cross-sensor generalization.
- **Data Collection:** Data was collected by having the robot grasp a diverse set of objects, varying in shape and size, at multiple configurations. For each grasp, tactile signatures were recorded independently from both GelSlim and Soft Bubble sensors. Positive pairs were created by matching tactile signatures corresponding to the same object grasped in the same configuration but with different sensors. Negative pairs were generated from different objects or distinct grasp configurations. Robot pose information was also recorded.

**C. Default Setting**
- **Encoder Architecture:** A ResNet-50 architecture was used as the backbone for the encoder network.
- **Projection Head:** A two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) served as the projection head, mapping the ResNet-50 features to a 64-dimensional latent space.
- **Training Parameters:** The model was optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴. Training was performed with a batch size of 128 for 100 epochs.

**D. Baselines and Evaluation Protocols**
CTTP's performance was benchmarked against several baselines:
- **ImageNet PT:** ResNet-50 pretrained on the ImageNet dataset.
- **ResNet RI:** ResNet-50 with random initialization.
- **T3 PT:** The T3 architecture pretrained on a reconstruction objective.
- **T3 Class PT:** The T3 architecture pretrained on object classification (requires labeled data).
- **T3 Pose PT:** The T3 architecture pretrained on in-hand pose estimation (requires labeled data).
The latter two T3 baselines require supervised labels to learn representations, unlike CTTP.

Evaluation was performed on two main downstream tasks:
1. **Tool Classification:** A linear layer was trained on the generated embeddings to classify 9 different tools (for unseen grasps) or 3 different tools (for unseen tools), minimizing cross-entropy loss. Top-1 accuracy was the metric.
2. **In-Hand Pose Estimation:** A fully-connected network (two 256-dimensional hidden layers) was trained to predict the SE(2) in-hand pose (y, z, θ) from the 2048-dimensional latent vectors. Mean Squared Error (MSE) was minimized.

Two training and evaluation dataset configurations were used:
- **Unseen Grasps:** Downstream models trained on latent vectors from training set grasps, evaluated on unseen grasps of the *same tools*.
- **Unseen Tools:** Downstream models trained on latent vectors from grasps on tools *not used for representation learning*, evaluated on unseen grasps of *those new tools*.

Two generalization cases were examined:
- **Generalization Within Sensor:** Training and testing on images from the *same* tactile sensor.
- **Generalization Across Sensors:** Training on images from *one* tactile sensor and testing on images from the *other* tactile sensor.

**E. Auxiliary Analyses**
- **Latent Space Visualization:** t-SNE was used to visualize the 2048-dimensional latent vectors in 2D, assessing tool differentiation and sensor alignment (overlap).
- **Batch Size Impact:** The effect of different batch sizes (16, 32, 64, 128, 256) on CTTP's classification accuracy and latent space structure was investigated.

**F. Real-world Insertion Task**
A peg insertion task was performed using a robot to evaluate the utility of CTTP-generated latent spaces in a practical manipulation scenario. This task involved tool classification, reorientation, and insertion, performed in a cross-sensor generalization setting (trained on Soft Bubble, tested on GelSlim). CTTP was compared against the T3 model (specifically T3 Pose PT for classification and T3 Class PT for pose estimation).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining (CTTP) in learning unified tactile representations and enabling cross-sensor generalization for various downstream tasks.

**A. Classification Performance**
- **Generalization Within Sensor:** CTTP achieved the highest classification accuracy among all methods when generalizing within the same sensor, for both "Unseen Grasps" and "Unseen Tools" scenarios. Most methods, excluding ResNet with random initialization, showed accuracies above 80% in this case.
- **Generalization Across Sensors:** This is where CTTP showed its most significant advantage. While all baseline methods experienced a substantial drop in performance, approximating random chance, CTTP maintained significantly higher accuracy. This indicates that CTTP effectively aligns the latent spaces of different sensors.
- **Unseen Tools:** For generalization to unseen tools, CTTP and ImageNet PT outperformed both T3 baselines, suggesting superior generalization capability to novel objects.

**B. Impact of Batch Size on CTTP Performance**
- Increasing the batch size up to 128 generally improved classification accuracy, particularly for generalization within the same sensor.
- However, a further increase to batch size 256 led to a significant decrease in performance for generalization across different sensors. This suggests an optimal batch size (128) for balancing tool differentiation and sensor alignment in the contrastive learning process.

**C. In-Hand Pose Estimation Errors**
- **Generalization Within Sensor (Unseen Grasps & Unseen Tools):** CTTP generally outperformed the baselines, particularly in the θ (orientation) error, with most errors falling within ±1°. T3 and ImageNet PT baselines exhibited similar error magnitudes but were less accurate than CTTP. ResNet with random initialization performed the worst.
- **Generalization Across Sensors (Unseen Grasps & Unseen Tools):** CTTP significantly outperformed all other methods in both translation (y, z) and orientation (θ) error. For unseen grasps, CTTP kept orientation errors within ±5°, while baselines showed much larger errors. For unseen tools, CTTP maintained orientation errors within ±5° and translation errors within ±3 mm, clearly surpassing all other methods by substantial margins (e.g., orientation errors for baselines were often in the range of ±18-38°). The self-supervised T3 PT baseline consistently performed the worst in cross-sensor pose estimation.

**D. Latent Space Visualization (t-SNE)**
- **Tool Differentiation:** CTTP effectively differentiated between tool classes, forming large and unified clusters for each tool. Most other models, except ResNet RI, also showed some ability to differentiate tools.
- **Sensor Alignment:** Crucially, the t-SNE projection of CTTP embeddings exhibited high overlap between the Soft Bubble and GelSlim regions within tool clusters. This indicates that CTTP successfully aligned the latent representations from the two distinct sensors, creating a sensor-agnostic space. In contrast, baseline methods generally maintained a distinct separation between sensor regions, demonstrating their failure to achieve cross-sensor alignment.
- **Batch Size Effects:** Visualization confirmed that while increasing batch size improved tool differentiation, it concurrently led to a decrease in sensor alignment, particularly at batch size 256. This supports the observation from classification results regarding optimal batch size.

**E. Real-world Insertion Task**
- In the robotic peg insertion task, conducted in a cross-sensor generalization setting (trained on Soft Bubble, tested on GelSlim), CTTP-derived latent vectors led to a significantly higher insertion success rate (18 successful insertions) compared to the T3 model's best representation (5 successful insertions).
- For online classification within this task, CTTP-derived latent space correctly identified the tool 28 out of 30 times, whereas the T3 Pose PT latent space achieved only 18 out of 30 correct identifications. These results provide empirical evidence of CTTP's practical utility in real-world robotic manipulation scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining (CTTP) addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic tactile sensing: the lack of generalizability across diverse sensor designs. The findings of this research carry several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Sensor-Agnostic Tactile Intelligence:** CTTP provides a method for learning unified, sensor-agnostic representations of touch. This capability allows algorithms to process tactile information irrespective of the specific sensor hardware used, moving towards more robust and universally applicable tactile intelligence in robots.
2. **"Zero-Shot" Transfer and Reduced Development Cost:** The demonstrated ability to transfer models trained on one tactile sensor to another without additional training (zero-shot transfer) is a substantial advancement. This can significantly reduce the labor and computational resources traditionally required for adapting tactile systems when switching sensor types or deploying in new environments, thereby accelerating the development and deployment of tactile-enabled robots.
3. **Enhanced Performance in Downstream Tasks:** The learned representations serve as effective pretraining for critical downstream tasks such as object classification and in-hand pose estimation. CTTP consistently outperformed other methods, particularly in cross-sensor generalization scenarios, leading to more accurate and reliable task execution, as evidenced by the robotic peg insertion task.
4. **Scalability and Data Efficiency:** By leveraging self-supervised contrastive learning on paired tactile data, CTTP mitigates the need for extensive, task-specific human labeling. This approach is more scalable for collecting diverse tactile datasets across different sensors and reduces the data bottleneck often associated with supervised learning paradigms in tactile robotics.
5. **Bridging Research and Application Gaps:** The methodology can help bridge the gap between different research laboratories and robotic platforms that utilize varied tactile sensors. Progress made with one sensor type can be more readily translated and utilized by systems employing other sensors, fostering collaboration and accelerating overall advancements in the tactile sensing community.
6. **Opening New Research Directions:** This work opens avenues for further research into cross-modal touch supervision and its relation to other sensory modalities like vision. It also encourages the exploration of new contrastive learning models and augmentation strategies specifically tailored for tactile data, which could further enhance the robustness and generalization capabilities of tactile representations.

In summary, CTTP offers a foundational step towards overcoming the fragmentation caused by tactile sensor diversity. By enabling models to operate across heterogeneous tactile hardware, it has the potential to unlock more adaptable, scalable, and capable robotic systems for a wider range of real-world manipulation tasks.
