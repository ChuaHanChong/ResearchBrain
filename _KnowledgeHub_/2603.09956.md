# Kinodynamic Motion Retargeting for Humanoid Locomotion via Multi-Contact Whole-Body Trajectory Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> The Kinodynamic Motion Retargeting (KDMR) framework creates dynamically feasible and physically consistent reference trajectories for humanoid robots by integrating multi-contact whole-body trajectory optimization with human ground reaction force data. This approach generates smoother motions that adhere to physical constraints, leading to accelerated policy convergence and improved sample efficiency in downstream imitation learning tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional kinematic motion retargeting methods for humanoids often produce physically inconsistent artifacts like foot sliding, ground penetration, and dynamically infeasible transitions due to morphological differences.
- These physical inconsistencies in reference motions degrade the performance, sample efficiency, and stability of downstream imitation learning policies.
- Existing approaches struggle to accurately replicate complex human multi-contact gaits, such as the heel-to-toe roll, with proper dynamic feasibility for robots.

## Method

- A comprehensive pipeline that jointly optimizes kinematics and whole-body dynamics, formulated as a multi-contact trajectory optimization problem, to ensure dynamically feasible and smooth robot motions.
- An approach to estimate distinct heel-toe contact patterns and dynamically rescale ground reaction forces (GRF) from human biomechanics data, enabling the replication of natural human-like contact sequences.
- A two-stage optimization process: initial kinematic retargeting followed by dynamics-based refinement, explicitly enforcing robot dynamics, multi-contact holonomic constraints, and friction cone limits.

## Results

- KDMR produced significantly smoother robot trajectories and effectively eliminated physical artifacts like foot floating and ground penetration, strictly adhering to desired contact windows.
- The method successfully recovered both the timing of foot contact events and the magnitude of required ground reaction forces, demonstrating improved dynamic fidelity compared to mass-rescaled human data.
- Policies trained on KDMR-generated references exhibited faster convergence and superior sample efficiency in downstream imitation learning tasks, achieving higher mean episode rewards earlier than baseline methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly incorporating rigid-body dynamics and contact complementarity constraints during motion retargeting is essential for generating physically plausible and stable humanoid locomotion trajectories.
> - Leveraging biomechanics-inspired analysis of human ground reaction forces (GRF) allows for robust estimation and decomposition of complex heel-toe contact events, crucial for realistic robot gaits.
> - Providing dynamically consistent reference motions significantly reduces the learning burden on reinforcement learning agents, leading to more efficient and robust policy acquisition in imitation learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid locomotion has seen advancements driven by imitation learning (IL), which leverages human motion capture (MoCap) data to enable robots to perform agile and natural movements. High-quality motion references are essential for training robust and generalizable control policies. However, a significant challenge arises in transferring human motions directly to robots due to inherent morphological differences, such as variations in joint limits, mass distribution, and actuation capabilities. This discrepancy often introduces physically inconsistent artifacts into the retargeted motions, including foot sliding, ground penetration, and dynamically infeasible transitions. These artifacts can significantly degrade the performance, sample efficiency, and learning stability of downstream imitation learning policies, as the learning agents are implicitly burdened with compensating for physically impossible references.

Traditional motion retargeting methods, particularly those used in computer graphics and subsequently adapted for robotics (e.g., PHC, GMR), primarily rely on kinematic optimization. While these methods attempt to address morphological differences by formulating the problem as an inverse kinematics (IK) task, they frequently overlook rigid-body dynamics and contact complementarity constraints. This oversight means the resulting trajectories often lack dynamic feasibility. For instance, GMR, a recent state-of-the-art kinematic retargeting method, improves geometric consistency but still struggles to ensure dynamic feasibility across entire motion trajectories, particularly in complex multi-contact locomotion scenarios involving the natural human heel-to-toe roll.

More recent efforts have explored dynamics-informed motion retargeting to improve the physical plausibility of generated motions. Approaches like those for quadruped robots and loco-manipulation tasks (e.g., DynaRetarget) incorporate dynamics into the optimization process. However, these methods often either focus on different robot morphologies, lack explicit multi-contact whole-body dynamics, or rely on black-box sampling to approximate feasibility. The integration of ground reaction force (GRF) measurements into motion capture systems has been recognized as beneficial, primarily in manipulation tasks, but its comprehensive application to multi-contact humanoid locomotion, coupled with explicit dynamic and contact constraints, has been less explored.

This work, Kinodynamic Motion Retargeting (KDMR), positions itself to bridge this gap by offering a framework that explicitly enforces whole-body dynamics and multi-contact holonomic constraints during the retargeting process. By integrating GRF data directly, KDMR aims to generate dynamically feasible and physically consistent reference trajectories for humanoid robots, particularly those featuring complex human-like heel-to-toe contact patterns. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of purely kinematic methods and enhance the utility of motion references for advanced imitation learning applications in humanoid robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations observed in current humanoid locomotion strategies that rely on human motion capture data. While imitation learning has demonstrated success in enabling robots to perform human-like behaviors, the direct application of human motion data to robots is complicated by fundamental differences in morphology and dynamics. This leads to several critical issues:

1. **Physical Inconsistencies in Retargeted Motions:** When human MoCap data is mapped onto a robot, discrepancies in joint limits, mass distribution, and actuation capabilities inevitably introduce non-physical artifacts. These can include foot sliding (feet moving relative to the ground while supposedly in contact), ground penetration (feet passing through the ground), and dynamically infeasible transitions (movements that violate the laws of physics for the robot's mass and joint properties).
2. **Degradation of Downstream Learning Performance:** These physical artifacts, if not addressed, persist throughout the training process for imitation learning policies. This forces the learning agent to attempt to reproduce physically impossible or highly challenging references, which severely limits the sample efficiency (the amount of data or experience required for learning), stability of the learning process, and ultimately the performance and robustness of the resulting control policies. The robot may learn suboptimal strategies to compensate for these flawed references rather than learning naturally agile movements.
3. **Challenges in Multi-Contact Locomotion:** Replicating complex human gaits, such as the natural heel-to-toe roll during walking, presents a significant challenge. This continuous rolling motion involves a hybrid, time-varying contact sequence—beginning with a heel strike, progressing to a full flat-foot stance, and ending in toe-only contact for push-off. Existing purely kinematics-based methods are not equipped to accurately manage this intricate sequence of contact events and the associated weight transfer dynamics, often resulting in unrealistic and unstable robot movements.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of the Kinodynamic Motion Retargeting (KDMR) framework are detailed as follows:

- **To develop a comprehensive pipeline that jointly optimizes kinematics and whole-body dynamics:** This aims to generate mathematically guaranteed, smooth, and dynamically feasible reference trajectories that effectively bridge the morphological gap between humans and humanoid robots, ensuring physical consistency from the outset.
- **To integrate human biomechanics-inspired analysis of Ground Reaction Force (GRF) data for robust contact pattern and force distribution estimation:** By leveraging time-series GRF measurements alongside MoCap data, the objective is to automatically detect and accurately decompose heel-toe contact events and forces. This allows KDMR to generate reference trajectories that feature a natural, human-like heel-to-toe roll, enhancing the realism and stability of the robot's gait.
- **To systematically compare KDMR against state-of-the-art kinematic retargeting methods and demonstrate its efficacy in downstream imitation learning tasks:** The goal is to provide empirical evidence that the physically consistent references generated by KDMR significantly improve the sample efficiency, convergence speed, and overall performance of control policies, thereby validating the approach's practical utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Kinodynamic Motion Retargeting (KDMR) framework addresses the challenge of creating dynamically feasible humanoid locomotion trajectories by formulating the retargeting process as a multi-contact, whole-body trajectory optimization problem. Recognizing the inherent non-convexity and data volume associated with such a problem, KDMR divides the task into two subproblems: kinematic optimization and dynamic optimization. The overall pipeline integrates human motion capture (MoCap) marker data and ground reaction force (GRF) measurements.

**A. Human Reference Dataset**
The method utilizes an open-source biomechanics dataset that includes synchronized motion and force measurements from a walking patient. Motion data consists of 3D spatial trajectories of optical markers (from MoCap), while force data comprises ground reaction wrenches (from force plates). The authors note that when direct GRF measurements are unavailable, estimation methods can be employed.

**B. Compute Human Reference Poses**
The 3D marker positions are first converted into configurations for a human skeletal model. This is achieved using OpenSim, a biomechanical analysis software, specifically its inverse kinematics tool. This process solves for the human skeleton configuration (joint angles) that best fits the marker positions. Subsequently, poses of relevant target frames on the human skeleton (e.g., hip, knee, ankle joint centers) are computed using forward kinematics. This procedure generates a time-series of target frame poses, which serve as kinematic references.

**C. Estimate Heel-Toe Contact Patterns**
To replicate the multi-contact nature of human walking, KDMR embeds distinct heel and toe contact domains into the retargeting problem. Since raw force plate data provides only aggregated resultant forces, a biomechanics-inspired approach is introduced to extract individual heel and toe contact phases and decompose forces.
The method deduces discrete contact states (heel-only, toe-only, flat-foot) directly from the vertical GRF profile of each foot. A characteristic double-peak pattern in the vertical GRF during walking allows for the identification of a temporal window around the mid-stance dip as the flat-foot phase (both heel and toe in contact). Periods of non-zero vertical force preceding and succeeding this window are designated as heel-only and toe-only contacts, respectively.
Continuous GRF trajectories are then extracted. To account for morphological differences between human and robot, these forces are dynamically rescaled based on the mass ratio of the target robot to the human subject. The total mass-rescaled GRF is then mapped onto the humanoid model by formulating it as the sum of heel and toe forces (λ = λ_heel + λ_toe), resulting in four independent GRF vectors (left/right heel, left/right toe) for subsequent optimization.

**D. Kinematics-Based Retargeting (Initialization)**
Given the non-convexity of the full kinodynamic problem, an initial, kinematically feasible robot reference trajectory ($q_{ref}$) is generated. The robot is modeled as a floating-base system. This initial step formulates an Inverse Kinematics (IK) problem, aiming to map the human target poses to the robot’s configuration space. Rather than directly solving a nonlinear position-level optimization, the forward kinematics are linearized, and a differential IK problem is solved at the velocity level (Equation 2). This minimizes the squared difference between the robot's link velocities and desired Cartesian velocities derived from the corresponding human links, subject to robot joint position and velocity limits. A diagonal weighting matrix prioritizes specific task-space tracking objectives.

**E. Dynamics-Based Retargeting (Refinement)**
The kinematic reference trajectory obtained from the previous step is refined through a dynamic retargeting phase to ensure adherence to the robot's dynamics and eliminate physical artifacts.
The robot's dynamics are explicitly modeled to account for contact forces at each active contact site (Equation 3: $D \ddot{q} + H(q, \dot{q}) = Bu + \sum J_{st}^T \lambda_{st}$). Contacts at the heel and toe are modeled as three-dimensional point contacts, considering only the linear components of the contact wrench to reduce decision variables and avoid over-constraining the problem.
Contact constraints are integrated based on the previously estimated contact sequence:
- **Swing phase:** No reaction force (λ = 0).
- **Stance phase:** The contact point is constrained to be stationary. This implies zero spatial velocity ($J_{st}(q)\dot{q} = 0$, Equation 4) and acceleration ($J_{st}(q)\ddot{q} + \dot{J}_{st}(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} = 0$, Equation 5). Additionally, the vertical position of the contact point is constrained to zero ($p_{z,st} = 0$, Equation 6), though this is softly enforced in the cost function for numerical stability.
- **Friction Cone Constraints:** To prevent foot slipping, contact forces must remain within a friction cone. A linearized friction pyramid approximation (Equations 7-9) is used to bound tangential forces relative to the normal (vertical) force.
The objective function (Equation L(.)) is designed to minimize tracking error to the kinematic reference (for base and local joint positions), promote smoothness in velocities and contact forces, regularize joint torques, and softly enforce the ground contact height constraint.
The problem uses trapezoidal integration (Equations 10-11) to relate positions, velocities, and accelerations over time.
Ultimately, the dynamics-based retargeting problem is formulated as a nonlinear program (NLP) that minimizes the sum of the cost function over the trajectory horizon, subject to the constrained dynamics, kinematic constraints, force constraints, trapezoidal integration laws, and robot-specific limits on joint positions and actuation torques.

**Implementation Details**
The optimization framework is implemented using CasADi for NLP formulation and solving, and Pinocchio for efficient computation of rigid-body kinematics and dynamics. The cost function weights were empirically tuned. For downstream policy evaluation, the BeyondMimic pipeline is employed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of KDMR was conducted through a comparative analysis against GMR, a state-of-the-art kinematic retargeting baseline, using an open-source biomechanics dataset and targeting the Unitree G1 humanoid robot. The evaluation focused on motion characteristics, dynamic fidelity, and the performance of downstream imitation learning policies.

**A. Comparison of Retargeted Motion Characteristics**

- **Kinematic Trajectory Similarity:** KDMR produced reference trajectories that largely matched those generated by GMR, as illustrated in Figure 5, for robot base positions and orientations, as well as joint angles for a leg. Minor deviations were observed in base roll (ϕx), base pitch (ϕy), foot pitch (ϕz) angles, and a larger deviation in the foot ankle pitch (θ4). This divergence was later attributed to KDMR's enhanced contact handling.
- **Motion Smoothness:** KDMR generated significantly smoother trajectories compared to GMR, particularly evident in the base linear and angular velocity profiles (Figure 5b) and joint angular velocities (Figure 5d). GMR's output exhibited noticeable discontinuities, which KDMR avoided by explicitly incorporating dynamic smoothing objectives.
- **Physical Artifact Elimination and Contact Preservation:** A critical finding, shown in Figure 6a, was KDMR's superior ability to maintain consistent foot-ground interactions. While GMR frequently violated source contact patterns, leading to foot floating and other height artifacts, KDMR strictly adhered to the desired contact window derived from the human source GRF data. This contact preservation capability was highlighted as a key differentiator, explaining the observed kinematic deviations (e.g., in ankle pitch) as a reflection of KDMR's dynamic correctness rather than inaccuracy. GMR's compensatory strategy of subtracting minimum foot height to reduce penetration often compromised base smoothness, a drawback KDMR bypassed.
- **Ground Reaction Force (GRF) Tracking:** Unlike GMR, which does not optimize for contact forces, KDMR demonstrated successful recovery of both the timing of foot contact events and the magnitude of required ground reaction forces when compared to the mass-rescaled human source data (Figure 6b). While minor transient inconsistencies were noted during rapid weight transfer phases (heel-strike to toe-off), the overall dynamic fidelity in terms of GRF tracking was substantially improved.

**B. Comparison of Learned Policies in Downstream Tasks**

To assess the practical utility of the retargeted motions, motion tracking policies were trained using the BeyondMimic pipeline on datasets generated by both KDMR and GMR. Five independent training runs were conducted for each method.

- **Accelerated Policy Convergence:** As depicted in Figure 7a, policies trained on KDMR-generated references exhibited faster convergence, achieving higher mean episode rewards earlier in the training process compared to the GMR baseline.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The quicker convergence rate of KDMR-trained policies indicates superior sample efficiency. This improvement is attributed to the inherent physical viability of KDMR's reference motions. By resolving dynamic violations (such as foot penetration or base floating) during the retargeting phase, KDMR alleviated the burden on the reinforcement learning agent, allowing it to focus on learning robust control rather than implicitly compensating for severe physical artifacts in the reference data.
- **Reduced Value Function Loss:** Consistent with the improved mean reward, policies trained with KDMR data also demonstrated lower value function loss (Figure 7b), suggesting more stable and effective learning outcomes.

Overall, the results consistently indicate that KDMR produces dynamically feasible, smoother, and more physically consistent motion references. These improved reference trajectories, in turn, lead to more efficient and effective learning of control policies for humanoid locomotion tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Kinodynamic Motion Retargeting (KDMR) framework represents a notable advancement in the field of humanoid locomotion, particularly in its approach to generating high-fidelity motion references. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Resolution of a Critical Problem:** KDMR directly addresses the long-standing challenge of physical inconsistencies (e.g., foot sliding, ground penetration, dynamic infeasibility) that plague traditional kinematic motion retargeting methods. These artifacts have historically hindered the performance and robustness of humanoid robots attempting to imitate human movements. By explicitly enforcing rigid-body dynamics and contact complementarity constraints, KDMR provides a solution that yields physically plausible and stable motions.
- **Enhanced Dynamic Fidelity:** The framework's ability to incorporate ground reaction force (GRF) measurements and biomechanically model multi-contact events, such as the natural human heel-to-toe roll, is a key contribution. This integration ensures that the retargeted motions not only appear kinematically similar but also respect the underlying dynamics and contact forces, which is crucial for real-world robotic deployment and stable locomotion.
- **Improved Imitation Learning Performance:** A significant impact of KDMR is its demonstrated ability to accelerate policy convergence and enhance the sample efficiency of downstream imitation learning tasks. By providing dynamically viable reference trajectories, KDMR reduces the burden on reinforcement learning agents to implicitly compensate for physical artifacts. This facilitates faster learning, leads to more robust control policies, and potentially enables the development of more agile and natural humanoid behaviors.
- **Methodological Advancement:** KDMR's formulation as a multi-contact, whole-body trajectory optimization problem, solved through a two-stage (kinematic and dynamic) process using established tools like CasADi and Pinocchio, offers a principled and adaptable methodology. This approach can be generalized to different humanoid robot platforms and potentially extended to various complex locomotion or manipulation tasks.
- **Broader Accessibility and Future Research:** The commitment to open-sourcing the KDMR pipeline upon publication is poised to facilitate wider adoption by the robotics research community. This open access could accelerate further research into dynamically-aware motion generation, contact inference, and the development of more advanced humanoid control systems. Furthermore, the identified limitations, such as the current reliance on steady-state walking assumptions for GRF-based contact estimation and the need for synchronized motion-force data, highlight clear avenues for future research. This includes developing methods to infer GRF from kinematics or images alone, thereby broadening the applicability of kinodynamic retargeting to more diverse and less instrumented motion capture scenarios.

In summary, KDMR offers a robust solution for generating high-quality, dynamically feasible reference motions for humanoid robots. Its ability to improve the efficiency and stability of imitation learning pipelines holds substantial potential for advancing the capabilities of humanoid robots to perform complex, human-like tasks in unstructured environments.
