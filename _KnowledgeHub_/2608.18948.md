# RoboEdit: Turning Human Manipulation Videos into Scalable Robot Experience

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboEdit edits RGB human manipulation videos into physically plausible robot interaction videos plus aligned 3D hand states, using cross-embodiment adaptation modules and a 3D Robot-State Decoder, backed by an automatic curation pipeline (RoboEdit-ADC) producing the 174K-pair RoboEdit-14M dataset. It achieves state-of-the-art human-to-robot editing quality and enables real-robot control from decoded trajectories.

## Problem

- Robot interaction video collection is expensive and strictly embodiment-specific, so morphology and kinematic differences block direct data transfer across robot hands.
- Abundant human-object interaction videos remain unusable for robot training because embodiment mismatches prevent them from serving as direct robot supervision.
- Prior work extracts representations or action labels from human video, but directly translating them into physically plausible target-robot interaction videos with preserved scene context is largely unexplored.

## Method

- **RoboEdit-Trans**: a robot-conditioned video editor with ==cross-embodiment adaptation modules== (==LoRA== plus ==residual adapter==) that edits RGB human video directly, preserving scene, camera motion, and object dynamics while retargeting embodiment.
- **3D Robot-State Decoder**: a heatmap-based framewise spatial estimator plus temporal Transformer that recovers per-frame 2D palm/fingertip landmarks, uses ==PnP== for 3D wrist pose, then ==forward kinematics== for structured hand-trajectory supervision.
- **RoboEdit-ADC**: an automatic pipeline reconstructing 3D hand-object interaction with ==depth regularization== and ==physics-based refinement==, then inpainting and compositing to build the paired RoboEdit-14M dataset across 7 embodiments.

## Results

- On the 300-case benchmark under multi-keyframe conditioning, RoboEdit-Trans reaches SSIM **0.9282** and edit-region LPIPS **0.0171**, beating VACE, UniVideo, VINO, Kiwi-Edit, and five other baselines.
- Ablations show both LoRA and residual adapter improve every metric over the backbone-only variant, with the residual adapter giving the larger individual gain and combined use performing best.
- RoboEdit-14M spans **174,547** aligned pairs (**14.1M** frames) over 7 embodiments; a residual PPO controller trained on decoded trajectories reaches **71%** success with the Panda gripper and **62%** with XHand across 512 simulated environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Editing the source human video directly, rather than generating robot interactions from scratch, preserves scene context and physical plausibility better than de novo generation.
> - Cross-embodiment adaptation can be decomposed into complementary lightweight modules (task-adaptation LoRA plus embodiment-specific residual adapters) that combine additively rather than competing.
> - Recovering structured 3D state from edited video (not just pixels) is what makes human video usable as control supervision, bridging visual editing and downstream robot policy training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation learning is currently challenged by the high cost and embodiment-specific nature of collecting suitable training data. Existing robot interaction datasets typically cover a limited range of objects, scenes, viewpoints, and behaviors due to variations in robot morphology, kinematics, and control mechanisms, which restrict direct data transfer across different robot hands. In contrast, human interaction videos are abundant, offering diverse scenes, viewpoints, object motions, and contact-rich behaviors. However, direct application of these human videos to robot training is hindered by the fundamental mismatch in embodiments.

Prior research has explored methods to leverage human videos for robot learning. Some approaches focus on extracting transferable visual representations (e.g., Nair et al. 2022, Ma et al. 2023a) or learning value functions for imitation and reinforcement learning (e.g., Ma et al. 2023b, Zakka et al. 2021). Other methods aim to extract structured interaction priors, such as affordances and contact regions (e.g., Bahl et al. 2023, Shan et al. 2020), or reconstruct and retarget human hand-object motion into robot trajectories (e.g., Shaw, Bahl, and Pathak 2022; Mandikal and Grauman 2022; Sivakumar, Shaw, and Pathak 2022). These methods generally convert human videos into intermediate representations or sparse motion supervision, rather than directly generating a comprehensive visual record of robot interaction.

Human-to-robot retargeting specifically addresses the adaptation of human demonstrations to robot embodiments. Classical approaches utilize geometric, task-space, or joint-space objectives to align human hand or object-relative motion (e.g., Handa et al. 2019, Lakshmipathy et al. 2024). Learning-based methods train cross-embodiment policies for direct imitation (e.g., Arunachalam et al. 2022, Qin et al. 2022). For contact-rich tasks, retargeting often incorporates object dynamics and physical feasibility through optimization or reinforcement learning (e.g., Xin et al. 2025, Pan et al. 2025). Recent monocular pipelines combine 3D hand-object reconstruction with retargeting to derive robot trajectories (e.g., Paliwal et al. 2026). However, these efforts typically yield sparse trajectories, lacking the rich visual detail of full interaction videos.

A distinct, yet related, area is human-to-robot video editing, which seeks to alter video content while preserving temporal and visual consistency. Diffusion-based video editing models have progressed significantly (e.g., Mokady et al. 2023, Qi et al. 2023, Geyer et al. 2024), with extensions to multimodal control (e.g., Liang et al. 2025). While these advancements enable video generation for robotics (e.g., Yang et al. 2025, Song et al. 2025), transforming human videos into robot videos presents a specific challenge: accurately translating appearance, kinematic structure, and motion dynamics across different embodiments while maintaining interaction semantics and physical plausibility. Existing methods often face limitations due to significant morphological and kinematic differences between human and robot hands (e.g., Ci et al. 2025, Xie et al. 2025).

RoboEdit addresses these gaps by proposing a comprehensive suite that directly transforms human manipulation videos into physically plausible, high-fidelity robot interaction videos, complete with aligned 3D robot hand states. This approach provides both rich visual and kinematic supervision within a unified framework, moving beyond sparse representations to full video generation, and directly tackles the embodiment mismatch challenge in video editing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the inherent difficulties in acquiring robot manipulation data. Current data collection methods are expensive and result in datasets that are strictly specific to the particular robot embodiment used. This specificity limits the generalizability of robot learning policies, as existing datasets often lack the diversity in objects, scenes, viewpoints, and behaviors necessary for robust robotic tasks.

In contrast, human interaction videos are readily available and inherently rich in diverse manipulation scenarios. They contain extensive information about contacts, object motion, environmental context, and interaction dynamics that are critical for understanding and replicating complex manipulation skills. However, these human videos cannot be directly utilized for robot training due to the anatomical and kinematic disparities between human hands and robot manipulators. While previous research has attempted to extract certain forms of supervision or representations from human videos, the challenge of directly translating these into physically plausible robot interaction videos, suitable for large-scale training, has largely remained.

Against this background, the key objectives of the RoboEdit project are:

1. **To develop an end-to-end human-to-robot video editing suite, named RoboEdit.** This suite is designed to transform existing RGB human manipulation videos into high-fidelity robot interaction videos that are action-consistent and physically plausible. An additional objective is to simultaneously recover the corresponding aligned 3D robot hand states.
2. **To introduce RoboEdit-ADC, an automatic pipeline for scalable paired-data curation.** The objective is to reconstruct 3D human-object interactions from RGB videos and subsequently retarget these interactions to various robot embodiments. This pipeline is crucial for generating the necessary paired human-robot data for training the video editing model.
3. **To create RoboEdit-14M, a large-scale dataset of paired human and robot video supervision.** The objective is to build a comprehensive dataset comprising a substantial number of aligned video pairs and frames across diverse robot embodiments, scenes, and interaction types, specifically to support the training of cross-embodiment video generation models.
4. **To design RoboEdit-Trans, a core video editing engine with advanced adaptation capabilities.** This component aims to directly edit RGB human interaction videos to preserve original scene context, camera motion, and object dynamics, while effectively retargeting the human embodiment to a target robot. A specific objective is to incorporate cross-embodiment adaptation modules to manage appearance, kinematics, and contact dynamics across different robots and ensure temporal coherence in the generated videos.
5. **To integrate a 3D Robot-State Decoder within RoboEdit-Trans.** The objective is to recover per-frame 3D robot hand states from the generated robot videos, providing structured motion supervision that is essential for downstream robot learning and control tasks.
6. **To demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in human-to-robot video editing.** This objective includes rigorous quantitative and qualitative evaluations against existing baselines, confirming fidelity, quality, and consistency.
7. **To validate the utility of the recovered 3D hand states for real-world robot control policies.** This involves deploying the decoded trajectories on physical robots to show their effectiveness in guiding actual manipulation tasks.

Ultimately, RoboEdit aims to unlock the potential of abundant, unlabeled human videos, converting them into scalable, high-fidelity visual and 3D motion supervision, thereby facilitating more generalizable and robust robot learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RoboEdit suite is formulated to synthesize a target robot interaction video $\hat{v}_{r,e_{1:T}}$ and predict its corresponding robot hand state trajectory $\hat{q}_{e_{1:T}}$ given an RGB human hand-object interaction video $v_{h_{1:T}}$ and a target robot embodiment $e$. Due to the scarcity of aligned human/robot video pairs, the methodology involves three core components: RoboEdit-ADC for data curation, RoboEdit-14M as the resulting dataset, and RoboEdit-Trans for video editing and state recovery.

#### 3.1. RoboEdit-ADC: Automatic Data Curation

RoboEdit-ADC is an automatic pipeline designed to construct paired human/robot videos from RGB footage.

##### 3.1.1. 3D Interaction Reconstruction

The pipeline begins by reconstructing the full 3D human-object interaction and camera motion from a single RGB video:
- **Hand Trajectory**: The articulated human hand trajectory $H_{1:T}$ is estimated using HaMeR (Pavlakos et al. 2024).
- **Object Segmentation and Masks**: The manipulated object is segmented using SAM 2 (Ravi et al. 2024). Edit masks $m_{1:T}$ are generated by combining the object segmentation with the hand mask.
- **Object Geometry and Pose**: The object mesh $M_o$ is reconstructed using TRELLIS (Xiang et al. 2024), and its 6D pose trajectory $O_{1:T}$ is tracked using FoundationPose (Wen et al. 2024).
- **Camera State**: The source camera state $C_{1:T}$, encompassing intrinsics, extrinsics, and depth, is estimated by VGGT (Wang et al. 2025).
- **Rectification**: The raw reconstructions undergo coordinate-frame and scale calibration to ensure physical consistency.
- **Depth Regularization**: To address depth and scale ambiguities common in monocular hand reconstruction (e.g., from HaMeR), the pipeline leverages the aligned depth map. It projects recovered palm joints into the depth map, back-projects depth observations to 3D metric palm anchors, and estimates a scale factor. This factor is then used to rescale the camera-space hand reconstruction, aligning its palm depth with observations while preserving relative hand articulation. This process improves hand-object contact geometry for subsequent retargeting.
The output of this stage is a unified hand-object-camera representation $Z_{1:T} = (H_{1:T}, O_{1:T}, M_o, m_{1:T}, C_{1:T})$.

##### 3.1.2. Human-Robot Retargeting

Given $Z_{1:T}$ and a target robot embodiment $e$, RoboEdit-ADC retargets the human hand motion.
- **Kinematic Retargeting**: For anthropomorphic hands, SPIDER's (Pan et al. 2025) wrist-and-fingertip inverse kinematics (IK) is applied within the MuJoCo simulator. For morphologically distinct two- and three-finger grippers, specialized strategies are employed, such as deriving jaw pose from wrist and thumb-index geometry, or optimizing joint angles to match key fingertips. This yields an initial kinematic trajectory $q^{e,IK}_{1:T}$.
- **Physics-guided Refinement**: To address artifacts like object penetration, floating contacts, and motion jitter caused by reconstruction noise and morphological mismatches, the initial trajectory is refined via a physics-guided optimization: $q^e_{1:T} = \arg \min_{\{q^e_t \in \mathcal{Q}_e\}_{t=1}^T} \mathcal{L}_{ret}$. The objective function $\mathcal{L}_{ret}$ comprises four terms:
 - **Tracking Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{track}$)**: Ensures fidelity to the initial kinematic reference.
 - **Temporal Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{temp}$)**: Smooths motion and reduces jitter.
 - **Geometry Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{geo}$)**: A penalty on robot-object penetration detected via MuJoCo collision checks and visual-mesh geometry, penalizing both negative signed distances (penetration) and positive distances below a clearance threshold.
 - **Contact Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{contact}$)**: Preserves valid contacts identified from reconstructed human fingertips, penalizing deviations from the nearest object surface point for active contacts. Active contacts are determined by fingertip-to-surface distance thresholds and temporal consistency.
The optimized trajectory $q^e_{1:T}$ is used for rendering the target robot interaction.

##### 3.1.3. Robot Interaction Compositing

The final step constructs the paired robot video:
1. MiniMax-Remover (Zi et al. 2025) inpaints the human hand and object regions defined by $m_{1:T}$, producing a clean background $b_{1:T}$.
2. The robot hand and object are rendered under $C_t$ as foreground $r_t$.
3. $r_t$ is composited onto $b_t$ to obtain the target frame $v^r_e_t$ and the full video $v^r_e_{1:T}$. This preserves the source background and camera motion while substituting the human interaction with the target robot.

#### 3.2. RoboEdit-Trans: Human-Robot Video Editing

RoboEdit-Trans is the core editing engine designed to learn a mapping $F_\theta (v_{h_{1:T}}, e) \rightarrow (\hat{v}_{r,e_{1:T}}, \hat{q}_{e_{1:T}})$.

##### 3.2.1. Model Overview and Robot Conditioning

It extends the NovaEdit (Pan et al. 2026b) architecture, incorporating cross-embodiment robot editing and 3D robot-state prediction. The editor takes a masked human video (providing scene, camera motion, object dynamics) and sparse target-robot condition frames (specifying embodiment and representative hand-object configurations). A robot-conditioned editor $G_\theta$ predicts the target-robot latent representation $\hat{z}_{r,e}$, optimized via flow matching.

##### 3.2.2. Cross-Embodiment Adaptation

To handle the diverse appearance, morphology, kinematics, and contact patterns across different robots, RoboEdit-Trans introduces two complementary adaptation modules:
- **LoRA adapters (Hu et al. 2021)**: These adapt spatiotemporal representations to specific robot appearance and motion characteristics.
- **Residual bottleneck adapters**: These refine embodiment-specific hand geometry and interaction patterns.
These modules work in conjunction to adapt a shared editor across various target embodiments while maintaining its general human-to-robot editing capabilities.

##### 3.2.3. 3D Robot-State Decoder

The edited videos do not explicitly encode metric 3D motion. A 3D Robot-State Decoder is integrated to recover camera-space robot states:
- **Framewise Spatial Estimation**: It uses a shared mask-aware image encoder (He et al. 2016) and robot-specific prediction heads to estimate per-frame 2D palm anchors and fingertips, palm-frame fingertip coordinates, and joint configurations for diverse embodiments. A shared camera head predicts camera intrinsics. The 3D wrist pose is recovered via Perspective-n-Point (PnP) using predicted 2D palm anchors and predefined 3D palm geometry.
- **Temporal Refinement**: A temporal Transformer refines these framewise states over the full sequence to enforce temporal consistency and reduce jitter. Forward kinematics then produces the complete camera-space robot-hand trajectory.
The decoder is supervised using per-frame 2D localization, 3D geometry, articulation, camera estimation, and temporal state consistency, providing structured motion guidance for downstream robot learning.

#### 3.3. RoboEdit-14M: Paired Human-Robot Dataset

RoboEdit-14M is a large-scale paired human/robot dataset comprising 174,547 aligned video pairs, totaling over 14.1 million paired frames. It is constructed from 24,197 human-interaction clips using the RoboEdit-ADC pipeline.

##### 3.3.1. Source Diversity

Human videos are sourced from multiple datasets, including DexYCB (Chao et al. 2021), HOT3D (Banerjee et al. 2024), H2O (Kwon et al. 2021), GigaHands (Fu et al. 2024), and TACO (Liu et al. 2024), covering static and moving egocentric views, single- and bimanual manipulation, and diverse objects. These are converted into 145,459 real-scene pairs for seven robot embodiments: Inspire, XHand, Ability, SCHUNK SVH, Allegro, Unitree Dex3, and the Franka Panda gripper.

##### 3.3.2. Synthetic Scene Augmentation

To enhance visual diversity, 29,088 synthetic pairs are generated by rendering aligned human and retargeted robot interactions under varied camera poses and lighting conditions. These foregrounds are composited onto backgrounds generated by RoboEngine (Yuan et al. 2025), maintaining source-target alignment while diversifying scene context.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RoboEdit encompasses qualitative comparisons, quantitative benchmarks, ablation studies, and real-robot deployment.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Results

Visual comparisons demonstrate that RoboEdit-Trans consistently synthesizes the target robot embodiment while effectively preserving the original scene context and object motion. In contrast, several baseline methods frequently exhibited issues such as retaining elements of human hands or generating robot hands with inconsistent geometry within the edited videos. The 3D Robot-State Decoder was observed to successfully track the generated robot hands and recover their corresponding 3D motion trajectories.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Comparison

Performance was benchmarked on a 300-case dataset comprising diverse sources, cameras, interactions, and robot embodiments. RoboEdit-Trans was evaluated against various video editing baselines, including VACE, UniVideo, VINO, Kiwi-Edit, OmniWeaving, EditCtrl, AnyV2V, and ReCo.
- **Reconstruction Fidelity**: RoboEdit-Trans achieved an SSIM of 0.9282 and an LPIPS of 0.0470, outperforming other methods. For example, VACE with multi-keyframe conditioning, the next best, achieved 0.8996 SSIM and 0.0778 LPIPS.
- **Local Editing Accuracy**: RoboEdit-Trans showed an Edit LPIPS score of 0.0171, indicating high accuracy in modifying specific regions while integrating the robot embodiment. VACE (multi-keyframe) had an Edit LPIPS of 0.0258.
- **Background Preservation**: The background SSIM for RoboEdit-Trans was 0.9188. The paper attributes its slight inferiority compared to some baselines (e.g., VACE at 0.9419) to valid changes occurring near the edit-mask boundary, which are interpreted as background errors due to the differing spatial extent of human and robot hands.
- **Perceptual Video Quality and Temporal Consistency**: RoboEdit-Trans demonstrated competitive scores across VBench metrics, including Aesthetic Quality (0.4832), Dynamic Degree (0.6300), and Motion Smoothness (0.9956). Its overall OpenVE-Bench score was 3.2511, indicating strong performance in prompt compliance, visual naturalness, temporal stability, and physical/motion integrity.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

##### 4.3.1. Video Editing Components

An ablation study on the cross-embodiment adaptation modules within RoboEdit-Trans indicated that both the LoRA adapters and residual adapters contributed to improvements across all evaluated metrics. The residual adapter provided a larger individual performance gain, and the combination of both modules yielded the best overall results in terms of reconstruction fidelity and local editing accuracy.

##### 4.3.2. Retargeting Quality in RoboEdit-ADC

The contributions of depth regularization and physics-guided refinement in RoboEdit-ADC were assessed. Without depth regularization, monocular depth and scale ambiguities led to misplacement of the robot hand in 3D space. Without physics refinement, kinematic matching alone produced undesirable artifacts such as floating contacts or object penetrations. The combination of these components improved hand-object alignment and physical consistency in the retargeted interactions.

#### 4.4. Real-Robot Deployment

To evaluate the practical utility of the 3D Robot-State Decoder, a residual Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) controller was trained in the Genesis simulator to track robot-hand trajectories decoded from RoboEdit-edited videos.
- In 512 randomized simulation environments across 18 YCB objects, the controller achieved trajectory-reproduction success rates of 71% with the Panda gripper and 62% with XHand.
- Subsequently, the controller was deployed on a 7-DoF Franka Panda robot for YCB-object manipulation tasks. This deployment demonstrated that the decoded 3D trajectories effectively supported successful real-robot execution, validating their utility as motion guidance for downstream control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoboEdit suite represents a notable advancement in the fields of robot learning and video synthesis, with several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Addressing Robot Data Scarcity**: RoboEdit offers a scalable solution to the long-standing challenge of collecting extensive and diverse robot interaction data. By enabling the transformation of readily available human manipulation videos into robot-specific training experiences, it significantly mitigates the high cost and embodiment-specificity associated with traditional data collection. This could unlock a vast, previously unusable, data source for robotics.

2. **Enabling Generalizable Robot Learning**: The generation of high-fidelity visual and 3D motion supervision from diverse human videos provides a rich training ground for robot learning algorithms. This dual supervision—visual context and precise 3D kinematic trajectories—is crucial for developing robot policies that are more robust and capable of generalizing across various objects, environments, and manipulation tasks, reducing the dependency on tightly controlled, specialized datasets.

3. **Advancement in Cross-Embodiment Video Editing**: The development of RoboEdit-Trans, with its novel cross-embodiment adaptation modules, pushes the boundaries of video editing technology. It addresses the complex problem of translating not only appearance but also kinematic structure and interaction dynamics between significantly different entities (humans and robots), while maintaining temporal coherence and physical plausibility. This work contributes to the broader field of generative AI for video synthesis.

4. **Structured Motion Supervision for Control**: The integration of a 3D Robot-State Decoder is a critical contribution, providing explicit and actionable 3D kinematic trajectories from the synthesized videos. This structured motion guidance can directly inform robot control policies, bridging the gap between high-level visual observations and low-level robot joint commands. This explicit supervision is more direct and informative than sparse reward signals or implicit representations.

5. **Creation of a Large-Scale Dataset (RoboEdit-14M)**: The automatic data curation pipeline (RoboEdit-ADC) and the resulting RoboEdit-14M dataset constitute a substantial resource for the research community. This dataset, comprising millions of aligned human/robot video frames with corresponding 3D robot states across multiple embodiments, provides a robust foundation for training and evaluating future generations of robot learning and video generation models. Its automated generation process highlights a pathway for continuously expanding robot training data.

6. **Demonstrated Real-World Applicability**: The successful deployment of decoded 3D trajectories on a physical Franka Panda robot for YCB-object manipulation tasks validates the practical utility and physical plausibility of the generated data. This demonstrates that RoboEdit's outputs are not merely visually convincing but also functionally relevant for real-world robot control, suggesting potential for accelerating robot development and deployment in practical applications.

Collectively, RoboEdit provides a comprehensive framework that addresses a fundamental bottleneck in robot learning, paving the way for more efficient, scalable, and generalizable robotic systems capable of performing complex manipulation tasks in dynamic and unstructured environments.
