# Cooptimizing Safety and Performance Using Safety Value-Constrained Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework integrates Hamilton-Jacobi reachability-based safety value functions into Model Predictive Control to co-optimize performance and rigorously guarantee safety for high-dimensional autonomous systems. It achieved higher safety rates (81% in simulation, 80% on hardware) and maintained larger safety margins compared to baseline methods on a 14-dimensional manipulator.

## Problem

- Finite planning horizons in Model Predictive Control (MPC) often compromise recursive feasibility and can lead to safety violations beyond the immediate planning window.
- Traditional safety filtering methods typically treat safety as a reactive, corrective layer, which can result in myopic or overly conservative control actions, sacrificing performance.
- Existing methods for formal safety guarantees, such as those based on dynamic programming, suffer from the "curse of dimensionality," rendering them intractable for high-dimensional robotic systems.

## Method

- Augmented a finite-horizon ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework with a terminal constraint derived from Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis.
- Employed a learning-based method (DeepReach adaptation) to approximate the high-dimensional safety value function using neural networks for computational tractability.
- Formulated a Safety Value MPC (SV-MPC) problem that includes the learned safety value function as a terminal constraint, ensuring the predicted terminal state remains within a certified control-invariant safe set.

## Results

- In simulations, the proposed method achieved an **81%** safety rate compared to **70%** for Vanilla MPC, maintaining a larger average distance to obstacles and requiring fewer sequential convex programming iterations.
- On a physical Flexiv Rizon 10s manipulator, the method demonstrated an **80%** safety rate over 10 trials, significantly outperforming Vanilla MPC (**30%**) and a Smooth Blending safety filter (**40%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the method's high safety rates remained consistently high across varying planning horizons, unlike Vanilla MPC whose safety performance was highly dependent on longer look-ahead windows.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hamilton-Jacobi reachability can characterize the maximal control-invariant safe set, which, when used as an MPC terminal constraint, enables less conservative safety guarantees and a larger feasible region for performance optimization.
> - Integrating this maximal safe set as a terminal constraint within MPC provides provably persistent safety and recursive feasibility, effectively addressing the limitations of finite planning horizons.
> - Co-optimizing safety and performance within a single MPC formulation leads to inherently safer and more efficient robot behaviors, moving beyond separate, post-hoc safety correction layers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous systems are increasingly being deployed in real-world environments, necessitating controllers that can achieve high performance while rigorously adhering to safety constraints. The broader research landscape for ensuring both performance and safety in control systems involves several distinct paradigms, each with its strengths and limitations.

One approach is **safety filtering**, exemplified by methods using Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) or reachability analysis. These methods modify a nominal controller's output in real-time to prevent constraint violations, offering formal safety guarantees and computational efficiency. However, safety filtering typically treats safety as a corrective layer, separate from performance optimization. This disconnected treatment can lead to myopic or overly conservative control actions, where performance is sacrificed or the system operates suboptimally.

**State-constrained optimal control** represents a more unified approach, integrating safety requirements directly into the control formulation as state constraints while optimizing for a task objective. While this framework provides a principled way to co-optimize safety and performance, its scalability is limited. Traditional methods often rely on dynamic programming or Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equations, which suffer from the "curse of dimensionality," rendering them intractable for high-dimensional systems like complex robotic manipulators.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC)** has emerged as a framework for synthesizing performant controllers that explicitly account for constraints and offer robustness through closed-loop replanning. However, practical MPC implementations are subject to finite planning horizons due to real-time computational demands. This finite horizon can compromise recursive feasibility and lead to safety violations because constraint satisfaction is not explicitly guaranteed beyond the planning window. Efforts to address this in MPC include integrating CBFs for recursive feasibility or using terminal constraints and sets. The main challenge here is constructing terminal sets that are both control-invariant and computationally tractable. Existing CBF-based methods can be conservative, leading to small terminal sets that restrict the MPC's feasible region and degrade performance.

This work aims to bridge the gap between these paradigms by developing a scalable framework that co-optimizes safety and performance for high-dimensional systems, specifically addressing the finite-horizon safety limitations of MPC without incurring the scalability issues of traditional state-constrained optimal control or the conservativeness of some safety filtering approaches. It seeks to combine the global safety certification capabilities of reachability analysis with the computational tractability and performance optimization of MPC.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable framework for autonomous systems that simultaneously optimizes for task performance and rigorously guarantees safety under various state and input constraints. This overarching goal is motivated by the increasing deployment of robots in real-world, safety-critical environments, such as manipulators operating near obstacles or carrying heavy payloads under actuator limits.

Specific motivations and objectives include:

1. **Addressing Finite-Horizon MPC Limitations:** Standard Model Predictive Control (MPC) operates with a finite planning horizon dictated by real-time computational constraints. While effective for short-term optimal control, this finite horizon makes it difficult to guarantee safety and recursive feasibility over the entire task duration. Trajectories that appear safe within the planning window might lead to constraint violations or enter unsafe states beyond it. The work aims to overcome this by extending safety guarantees beyond the immediate horizon.

2. **Co-optimization of Safety and Performance:** Traditional approaches often treat safety as a separate, corrective mechanism (e.g., safety filtering) applied after performance optimization. This can lead to suboptimal or overly conservative behavior. The research is motivated by the need for a unified approach where safety is intrinsically considered during the optimization process, rather than as an auxiliary mechanism.

3. **Scalability for High-Dimensional Systems:** Many existing methods for formal safety guarantees (e.g., those based on dynamic programming or HJB equations) suffer from the "curse of dimensionality," making them impractical for high-dimensional robotic systems like manipulators with many degrees of freedom. A key objective is to achieve a scalable solution applicable to such complex systems.

4. **Leveraging Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) Reachability:** The paper proposes using HJ reachability analysis to define terminal constraints for MPC. This choice is motivated by two key advantages:
 - **Maximal Control-Invariant Safe Sets:** HJ reachability can characterize the *maximal* control-invariant safe set under given dynamics and constraints. Incorporating this as a terminal constraint in MPC ensures that the predicted terminal state lies within a set from which safety can be maintained indefinitely. This leads to less conservative and more expressive safety guarantees compared to traditional terminal set constructions that rely on local linearizations or conservative approximations, thereby allowing for a larger feasible region and better performance optimization.
 - **Accommodation of Complex Constraints:** Reachability analysis is naturally suited to handle complex state and input constraints, including nonlinear dynamics, actuator limits, obstacle avoidance, and coupled constraints frequently encountered in robotic applications. This expressiveness avoids the need to simplify constraint structures for safety enforcement.

Based on these motivations, the core contributions of the work are articulated as:
1. Developing an MPC formulation that integrates a safety value function-based terminal constraint to ensure persistent constraint satisfaction and recursive feasibility while optimizing performance. This includes a theoretical proof of recursive feasibility under specific conditions.
2. Synthesizing a safety value function for a 14-dimensional manipulator with complex state and control constraints using learning-based Hamilton-Jacobi reachability methods.
3. Demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed framework through comprehensive simulation studies and experimental validation on a physical Flexiv Rizon 10s manipulator, showing improved constraint satisfaction and robustness compared to baseline methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on augmenting a finite-horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework with a terminal constraint derived from Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis, specifically utilizing a safety value function. This approach aims to guarantee safety beyond the MPC's planning horizon while simultaneously optimizing performance.

**Problem Formulation (State-Constrained Optimal Control):**
The work frames the problem as a continuous-time, deterministic, control-affine optimal control problem over a horizon $[0, T]$. The system dynamics are given by $\dot{x} = f(x, u)$, where $x$ is the state and $u$ is the control input. The objective is to minimize a cost function $J(x, t, u)$ comprising a running cost $r(x, u)$ and a terminal cost $\phi(x)$. Safety requirements are encoded as state constraints $l(x) \geq 0$, and control inputs are subject to $g(u) \geq 0$.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC):**
The continuous-time problem is solved online in a discrete-time MPC fashion. At each time step $j$, an optimal control problem (Prob. 2) is solved over a shorter planning horizon $h$. The first $c$ controls from the optimal solution are applied to the system, and the process is repeated from the new state. This closed-loop nature provides robustness to disturbances and modeling errors. However, the finite horizon $h$ often leads to myopic behavior and potential safety violations beyond the planning window.

**Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) Reachability Analysis:**
HJ reachability analysis is employed to address the long-term safety challenge. It solves a "safety optimal control problem" (Prob. 3) to determine the maximum time a system can stay safe, or whether it can avoid an unsafe set indefinitely. The solution to this problem is the safety value function $V_s(x, t)$, which represents the minimum guaranteed time to violation (or maximum time to safety) starting from state $x$ at time $t$. This function is the viscosity solution to an HJB Variational Inequality (Eq. 4). The research primarily uses the *converged* safety value function, $V_s(x)$, which represents infinite-time safety information. The set $S = \{x \in X | V_s(x) \geq 0\}$ is defined as the safe set, from which the system can indefinitely maintain state constraint satisfaction.

**Computing the Safety Value Function:**
For high-dimensional systems (e.g., a 14D manipulator), grid-based methods for solving the HJB-VI are intractable. Therefore, the work adopts a learning-based method, specifically an adaptation of **DeepReach**, to approximate the safety value function using neural networks. Instead of solving the original safety optimal control problem (Prob. 3) directly with gradient-free methods for supervision points, this work computes the approximate solution to the HJB-VI at supervision points by solving an *approximate safety optimal control problem* with a modified objective function:
$J(x, t, u) = \sum_{\tau \in [t, T]} e^{-\alpha l(\xi_x^u(\tau))}$, where $\alpha$ is a large positive constant. This objective heavily penalizes state constraint violations, encouraging trajectories to remain in the safe set. This approximate problem is solved using a gradient-based solver, which was found to yield more accurate value function estimates due to a better-conditioned summation objective structure compared to the minimum-over-time objective of the original reachability problem.

**Safety Value MPC (Proposed Formulation):**
The core contribution is the integration of the HJ reachability-based safety value function as a terminal constraint in the MPC formulation. This results in **Problem 4 (Safety Value MPC)**:
The standard MPC objective and constraints (running cost, dynamics, input, and state constraints over the horizon) are augmented with a terminal constraint: $V_s(x_j(h)) \geq \epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is a small positive buffer for numerical robustness.
This terminal constraint ensures that the predicted state at the end of the MPC horizon lies within a certified control-invariant safe set. The paper proves that the set $S = \{x \in X | V_s(x) \geq 0\}$ is the *maximal* control-invariant set under the system dynamics and state constraints (Proposition 1). This maximality is crucial for avoiding unnecessary conservativeness and maximizing the feasible region for performance optimization.
Furthermore, the paper proves the **recursive feasibility** of the Safety Value MPC scheme (Proposition 2), provided the initial state is within the safe set $S$ and an exact safety value function is available. This guarantees that if a solution exists at one time step, a solution will exist for all subsequent steps, ensuring persistent safety.
The resulting MPC problem (Prob. 4) is generally non-convex. To solve it efficiently in real-time, the authors employ **sequential convex programming (SQP)**, a gradient-based method.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed Safety Value MPC framework was evaluated through extensive simulation studies and hardware experiments on a Flexiv Rizon 10s manipulator.

**A. Simulation Experiment**

1. **Setup:**
 - **System:** A 14-dimensional (7 joint positions, 7 joint velocities) Flexiv Rizon 10s manipulator with 7-dimensional joint torque control.
 - **Scenario:** Moving a heavy object under reduced torque limits while avoiding a cylindrical obstacle.
 - **Task:** Reaching a desired end-effector position.
 - **Baselines:**
 - **Vanilla MPC:** Standard state-constrained MPC without the terminal safety value function constraint.
 - **Smooth Blending (SB) Filter:** A reactive safety filter that modifies a nominal controller's output (here, vanilla MPC) using the same safety value function.
 - **Solver:** An SQP solver based on the Crocoddyl optimal control library.
 - **Metrics:** Safety Rate (collision-free trials), Average Distance to Goal, Average Distance to Obstacle, Average Active Control Constraints, Average SCP Iterations, Average Planning Time. Experiments were run from 100 randomly sampled initial states.

2. **Results (Planning Horizon h=8):**
 - **Safety Rate:** The proposed method (OURS) achieved an 81% safety rate, outperforming Vanilla MPC (70%) and performing comparably to SB-Filter (83%). This indicates that incorporating the safety value function, either as a terminal constraint or a filter, improves collision avoidance.
 - **Performance (Avg. Dist. to Goal):** OURS showed a larger average distance to the goal (0.4023m) compared to Vanilla MPC (0.3498m). This suggests a trade-off where OURS prioritizes safety, maintaining a wider margin from obstacles, at the expense of path optimality. SB-Filter also had a larger distance to goal (0.4088m).
 - **Safety Margin (Avg. Dist. to Obstacle):** OURS maintained a larger average distance to the obstacle (0.1903m) compared to Vanilla MPC (0.1833m) and SB-Filter (0.1713m), indicating a more cautious and safer trajectory.
 - **Control Authority (Avg. Active Ctrl. Constr.):** OURS resulted in fewer active control constraints (0.681) than Vanilla MPC (0.838) and SB-Filter (0.977), implying it better preserved control authority for low-level tracking.
 - **Computational Efficiency (Avg. SCP Iters):** OURS required fewer sequential convexification iterations (6.917) for the solver to converge, suggesting faster convergence of the optimization problem. Vanilla MPC and SB-Filter required 13.377 and 13.959 iterations, respectively.
 - **Planning Time:** OURS had a longer average planning time (28.926ms) compared to Vanilla MPC (12.939ms) and SB-Filter (21.142ms). This is attributed to the need for forward and backward passes on the neural network representing the safety value function at each convexification step.

3. **Ablation Study (Impact of Planning Horizon):**
 - The study varied the planning horizon (h) from 6 to 15.
 - **Safety Rate:** Vanilla MPC's safety rate improved with increasing horizon (from 53% at h=6 to 84% at h=15), as expected due to a longer look-ahead. In contrast, OURS and SB-Filter maintained relatively consistent high safety rates across horizons (e.g., OURS 80% at h=6 to 88% at h=15, SB-Filter 80% at h=6 to 82% at h=15), supporting the theory that their safety guarantees are less dependent on the finite horizon due to the infinite-time safety information from the value function.
 - **Safety Margin:** OURS consistently maintained a larger average distance to obstacles across all planning horizons compared to both baselines.

**B. Hardware Experiment**

1. **Setup:**
 - **System:** A physical Flexiv Rizon 10s 7-DOF manipulator with a 6.8kg payload.
 - **Scenario:** Navigating a virtual obstacle, similar to simulation.
 - **Control:** MPC operates at 20Hz, sending desired joint positions, velocities, and torques to a low-level PD controller running at 1000Hz. Stricter joint velocity constraints and penalties for rapid movements were applied to ensure hardware safety.
 - **Parameters:** MPC planning horizon of 12 (0.6s).

2. **Results:**
 - **Safety Rate:** OURS achieved an 80% safety rate over 10 trials, significantly outperforming Vanilla MPC (30%) and SB-Filter (40%). This demonstrates the method's effectiveness in a real-world scenario with model-reality mismatch (e.g., payload weight difference).
 - **Other Metrics:** Average distance to obstacle and goal were similar across methods, attributed to the robot operating at slower speeds due to imposed hardware safety constraints.

**C. Discussion of Limitations and Challenges (Identified by Authors):**
The authors acknowledge several practical limitations:
- **Formal Guarantees:** The learned safety value function is an approximation and does not provide formal mathematical guarantees of control invariance in practice, though theoretical proofs assume an exact function.
- **Non-convex Optimization:** Prob. 4 is non-convex. While SQP approximates a solution, it does not guarantee constraint satisfaction, and the learned safety value function's rapid variation (due to non-differentiability of viscosity solutions) can pose challenges for gradient-based solvers, leading to solver instability and constraint violations (72 out of 83 simulation failures were attributed to the safety value function constraint not being satisfied).
- **Conservatism:** Using the converged (infinite-time) safety value function results in a subset of the feasible region compared to a time-varying one, potentially leading to more conservative behavior.
- **Buffer Parameter ($\epsilon$):** While a buffer $\epsilon$ is used for numerical stability, increasing it to ensure safety can inadvertently tighten the constraint and destabilize the optimizer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a framework for co-optimizing safety and performance in autonomous systems, addressing a fundamental challenge in their real-world deployment. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Safety for High-Dimensional Systems:** The core contribution is enabling provably persistent safety guarantees for complex, high-dimensional systems like robotic manipulators, even when using finite-horizon MPC. By integrating a reachability-based safety value function as a terminal constraint, the method ensures that the system can always be maintained within a safe set, effectively mitigating the recursive feasibility and long-term safety issues inherent in traditional finite-horizon MPC. This is particularly impactful for systems operating in close proximity to obstacles, with complex dynamics, or under varying operational limits (e.g., payloads, degraded actuators).

2. **Less Conservative and More Expressive Safety:** In contrast to traditional terminal set constructions that often rely on local linearizations or conservative approximations, the use of a Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability-based safety value function allows for the characterization of the *maximal* control-invariant safe set. This leads to less conservative safety guarantees, meaning the robot can operate within a larger safe envelope, which often translates to improved task performance without sacrificing safety. The ability of HJ reachability to handle nonlinear dynamics and complex, coupled state and input constraints further enhances the framework's expressiveness, making it applicable to a wider range of realistic robotic scenarios.

3. **Co-optimization Paradigm:** The framework unifies safety and performance optimization within a single MPC formulation. This contrasts with common safety filtering approaches that treat safety as a corrective layer, which can lead to myopic or suboptimal behavior. By co-optimizing, the system plans trajectories that are inherently safe *and* performant, rather than just being made safe post-hoc. This integrated design philosophy can lead to more efficient and natural robot behaviors.

4. **Practical Validation:** The validation of the method through both extensive simulation studies and hardware experiments on a Flexiv Rizon 10s manipulator demonstrates its practical applicability. The significant improvements in safety rate and maintenance of larger safety margins compared to baseline methods, even under hardware-specific challenges like model-reality mismatch and stricter operational constraints, suggest that the proposed framework is robust and viable for real-world robotic control.

**Potential Impact:**
The research has the potential to accelerate the safe deployment of advanced autonomous systems in various domains, including:
- **Manufacturing and Logistics:** Robots operating in shared workspaces with human workers, requiring rigorous safety guarantees for collision avoidance.
- **Exploration and Inspection:** Autonomous systems navigating complex, dynamic, and potentially hazardous environments.
- **Healthcare Robotics:** Manipulators assisting in delicate procedures where safety is paramount.

The identified limitations, particularly concerning formal guarantees for learned safety value functions and optimization stability challenges, highlight areas for future research. Addressing these could further solidify the theoretical foundations and enhance the practical robustness of such co-optimization frameworks, paving the way for even broader adoption of provably safe and performant autonomous systems.
