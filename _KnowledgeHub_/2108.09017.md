# DeFRCN: Decoupled Faster R-CNN for Few-Shot Object Detection

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Megvii Technology developed DeFRCN, a framework enhancing few-shot object detection by decoupling Faster R-CNN's internal conflicts via a Gradient Decoupled Layer and a Prototypical Calibration Block. This approach achieves new state-of-the-art performance on PASCAL VOC and COCO benchmarks, improving mAP by up to 7.9% on COCO 30-shot Few-Shot Object Detection.

## Problem

- Deep object detection models require extensive annotated data, posing a significant bottleneck for adapting to novel categories with few examples (few-shot learning).
- The Faster R-CNN architecture, commonly used in few-shot object detection, suffers from inherent conflicts between its multi-stage (RPN vs. RCNN) and multi-task (classification vs. localization) components.
- These internal contradictions are exacerbated in data-scarce few-shot scenarios, leading to suboptimal feature learning, foreground-background confusion, and poor generalization to novel classes.

## Method

- Introduces **Decoupled Faster R-CNN** (**DeFRCN**), extending the standard Faster R-CNN with two new modules designed for end-to-end fine-tuning.
- Implements a ==Gradient Decoupled Layer== (GDL) to explicitly control the gradient flow between the shared backbone, Region Proposal Network (RPN), and RCNN head, mitigating multi-stage optimization conflicts.
- Incorporates a ==Prototypical Calibration Block== (PCB) as an offline, plug-and-play module that refines classification scores using a strong pre-trained classifier and support set prototypes, decoupling classification from localization.

## Results

- **DeFRCN** consistently achieved new state-of-the-art performance on PASCAL VOC and COCO datasets for both Few-Shot Object Detection (FSOD) and Generalized FSOD (G-FSOD) protocols.
- It demonstrated significant improvements, including approximately **7.9%** higher mAP on COCO for **30-shot** FSOD and over **7 percentage points** higher novel AP on PASCAL VOC compared to previous leading methods.
- The framework showed enhanced generalization ability in cross-domain scenarios, achieving a **13.6%** mAP improvement in fine-tuning from COCO base classes to VOC novel classes (**10-shot**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Customizing gradient flow via GDL can effectively resolve multi-stage optimization conflicts within Faster R-CNN, preventing specific modules (e.g., RPN) from negatively impacting the shared backbone's adaptation to novel classes.
> - Decoupling classification and localization at inference time through metric-based score calibration (PCB) significantly enhances classification robustness by leveraging high-quality features from a pre-trained model without affecting bounding box regression.
> - By systematically addressing architectural contradictions in Faster R-CNN, a simple fine-tuning paradigm can yield substantial performance gains in few-shot object detection, even surpassing complex meta-learning methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep neural networks have achieved notable advancements across various visual tasks, including image classification and object detection. However, the performance of these models typically relies on the availability of extensive annotated datasets. The laborious and time-consuming nature of data annotation creates a bottleneck, limiting the application of deep learning in scenarios with scarce data. This challenge contrasts with human capabilities to quickly generalize novel concepts from minimal examples.

Few-shot learning (FSL) emerged as a research area to address this limitation, focusing on enabling models to learn new concepts and generalize effectively from very few examples. Few-shot object detection (FSOD) is a particularly challenging sub-area within FSL, combining the complexity of object localization with the data scarcity of few-shot classification.

Current FSOD approaches primarily fall into two categories: meta-learning-based methods and fine-tuning-based methods. Meta-learning techniques aim to acquire task-level knowledge for better generalization to novel classes, often involving complex training processes and data organization. Fine-tuning-based methods, in contrast, are simpler and more efficient, typically involving a two-stage fine-tuning scheme. Despite their simplicity, fine-tuning methods can suffer from issues related to data distribution shifts and underutilization of novel data, especially when most parameters are frozen after pre-training on base classes.

Faster R-CNN, a prominent two-stage object detection framework, serves as a foundational architecture for many existing FSOD methods in both meta-learning and fine-tuning paradigms. However, Faster R-CNN was originally designed for conventional object detection with ample data and lacks specific architectural considerations for data-scarce few-shot scenarios. This architectural mismatch can limit the performance ceiling of FSOD models built upon it, introducing conflicts in multi-stage optimization and multi-task learning, which become exacerbated under conditions of limited data. This paper specifically addresses these inherent contradictions within Faster R-CNN to improve its performance in few-shot settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance of few-shot object detection by identifying and resolving specific architectural limitations within the widely adopted Faster R-CNN framework, particularly when operating in data-scarce environments. The authors aim to develop a simple yet effective architecture that can be learned via straightforward fine-tuning.

The motivation for this work stems from an analysis of the conventional Faster R-CNN and its inherent contradictions when applied to few-shot object detection. These contradictions are examined from two orthogonal perspectives:

1. **Multi-stage Conflict (RPN vs. RCNN):** The Faster R-CNN architecture comprises a shared convolutional backbone, a Region Proposal Network (RPN), and an RCNN head. The RPN generates class-agnostic region proposals, determining *where* objects might be, while the RCNN head performs class-specific classification and localization, identifying *what* the objects are. These two modules interact through the shared backbone, exchanging information and gradients during end-to-end joint optimization. A contradiction arises because the RPN is class-agnostic (focused on general objectness), while the RCNN is class-relevant (focused on specific categories). In a few-shot setting, where the novel detector is fine-tuned from a base domain detector, the RPN can experience "foreground-background confusion"—a region considered background in base training might be foreground for a novel class. This conflict in optimization goals can lead to the shared backbone being dominated by one task, propagating over-fitting tendencies from base classes to the backbone and RCNN, thereby hindering transferability to novel classes. The authors hypothesize that tailoring the degree of decoupling between these modules through gradient control could alleviate this issue.

2. **Multi-task Conflict (Classification vs. Localization within RCNN):** The RCNN head itself operates as a multi-task learning paradigm, performing both classification and localization. The classification task generally benefits from translation-invariant features, as the category of an object should not change with small shifts in its position. Conversely, the localization task (bounding box regression) requires translation-covariant features, as precise box adjustments depend on the exact spatial location. These mismatched feature requirements for the classification and regression branches can lead to a suboptimal shared feature representation, potentially generating low-quality classification scores and reducing the overall classification power. In data-scarce scenarios, this contradiction is further exacerbated due to the limited samples available to learn robust and specialized features for each task. The authors propose to address this by introducing an efficient score calibration module specifically for the classification branch to decouple these tasks.

By addressing these identified contradictions, the authors aim to tap into the full potential of Faster R-CNN-style detectors for few-shot object detection, leading to improved robustness and generalization capabilities in challenging data-limited environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, named Decoupled Faster R-CNN (DeFRCN), extends the standard Faster R-CNN architecture by introducing two novel components: the Gradient Decoupled Layer (GDL) for multi-stage decoupling and the Prototypical Calibration Block (PCB) for multi-task decoupling. The overall framework is designed for end-to-end fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. Gradient Decoupled Layer (GDL)

The GDL is an architectural unit designed to customize the relationship between upstream and downstream modules within the network, specifically by adjusting the influence of gradient flow.

- **Mechanism:**
 - **Forward Propagation:** During the forward pass, GDL applies a learnable affine transformation (parameterized by channel-wise weights ω and bias b) to the input feature maps. This operation aims to enhance feature representations and perform a 'forward-decoupling' by projecting features into different spaces for subsequent modules.
 - **Backward Propagation:** During the backward pass, GDL takes the gradient from the subsequent layer, multiplies it by a constant decoupling coefficient λ ∈, and then passes this scaled gradient to the preceding layer. This effectively scales the contribution of the downstream module's gradient to the upstream module's parameter updates.
- **Placement and Application:** Two GDLs are integrated into the Faster R-CNN:
 - `G_rpn`: Inserted between the shared backbone and the RPN.
 - `G_rcnn`: Inserted between the shared backbone and the RCNN head.
- **Optimization Impact:**
 - Setting λ to 0 (e.g., `λ_rpn = 0`) is equivalent to "stopping the gradient" from that module, meaning its updates do not influence the shared backbone.
 - Setting λ in (0, 1] (e.g., `λ_rcnn` in (0, 1]) is equivalent to "scaling the gradient," allowing the module to contribute to the backbone's update but with a controlled magnitude.
 - This gradient control allows for fine-tuning the balance of optimization influence on the shared backbone, addressing the multi-stage conflict by preventing one module (e.g., RPN) from disproportionately influencing the backbone's adaptation to novel classes. For DeFRCN, `λ_rpn` is set to 0 during base training, stopping the RPN's gradient influence on the backbone, while `λ_rcnn` is set to 0.75 during base training and 0.01 during novel fine-tuning, allowing the RCNN to scale its gradient contribution.

#### 3.2. Prototypical Calibration Block (PCB)

The PCB is a metric-based score refinement module introduced to decouple classification and localization tasks, particularly during inference, and enhance classification power in few-shot settings.

- **Mechanism:** PCB operates offline, meaning it requires no additional training and is plug-and-play. It consists of:
 - **A strong classifier:** A well pre-trained classification model (e.g., ImageNet pre-trained ResNet-101) serves as a feature extractor.
 - **RoIAlign layer:** Extracts region-of-interest (RoI) features from predicted boxes.
 - **Prototype bank:** Generated from the support set. For each novel class, a prototype `p_c` is computed as the mean of the instance features (`x_i`) from all K-shot samples belonging to that class `c` in the support set `S_c`.
- **Score Refinement:**
 - For an object proposal (`ˆy_i = (c_i, s_i, b_i)`) generated by the few-shot detector (with predicted category `c_i`, score `s_i`, and bounding box `b_i`), PCB first extracts a feature `x_i` from `b_i` using RoIAlign and the pre-trained feature extractor.
 - It then calculates a cosine similarity score (`s_cos_i`) between `x_i` and the corresponding prototype `p_c_i` from the prototype bank.
 - Finally, the original softmax score `s_i` from the detector's classification branch is fused with this prototype-based pairwise score `s_cos_i` using a weighted aggregation: `s_‡_i = α · s_i + (1 − α) · s_cos_i`, where α is a trade-off hyper-parameter (set to 0.5 in experiments).
- **Benefits:**
 - By leveraging a pre-trained strong classifier, PCB provides high-quality, classification-aimed translation-invariant features without affecting the detector's localization branch.
 - It addresses the issue of low-quality scores from the detector's classification branch by calibrating them with robust prototype-based similarity, reducing high-scored false positives and remedying low-scored missing samples.
 - Its offline, plug-and-play nature makes it data-efficient and easily integrable into various architectures.

#### 3.3. Training Paradigm

The overall learning procedure follows a two-stage fine-tuning paradigm:
1. **Base Training:** The detector is initialized and trained on a large base dataset (`D_base`) with abundant annotated instances belonging to `C_base` classes.
2. **Novel Fine-tuning:** The pre-trained detector is then adapted to a novel support set (`D_novel`) with only a few samples per category (`K`-shot) from `C_novel` classes.
The goal is to optimize a robust detector `F` that can classify and localize objects in a novel query set `D_query`.

The backbone used is ResNet-101 pre-trained on ImageNet. SGD is used for optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DeFRCN was evaluated on PASCAL VOC and COCO datasets, under both Few-Shot Object Detection (FSOD) (evaluating novel classes only) and Generalized Few-Shot Object Detection (G-FSOD) (evaluating base, novel, and overall performance) protocols.

#### 4.1. Performance on PASCAL VOC

- **FSOD and G-FSOD:** DeFRCN consistently outperformed existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) approaches across three different data splits and various shot numbers (K=1, 2, 3, 5, 10).
- **Novel AP:** Achieved significant improvements, with gains of up to 21.4% (e.g., 1-shot, Novel Set 1: TFA 39.8% vs. DeFRCN 53.6%, a 13.8% absolute improvement; 10-shot, Novel Set 2: MPSR 47.8% vs. DeFRCN 47.9%, modest improvement but also 10-shot, Novel Set 1: TFA 56.0% vs. DeFRCN 60.8%). In G-FSOD, DeFRCN's novel AP was typically over 7 percentage points higher than TFA.
- **Overall AP (G-FSOD):** DeFRCN achieved the best overall performance across all settings, demonstrating improvements of +1.4% to +4.0% AP compared to TFA.
- **Observations:** The results indicated that for FSOD, increasing novel shots did not always guarantee improved performance (e.g., 5-shot vs. 10-shot in Novel Set 1). G-FSOD performance grew faster with more shots than FSOD, likely due to the inclusion of more negative samples.

#### 4.2. Performance on COCO

- **FSOD and G-FSOD:** DeFRCN consistently surpassed recent SOTA methods in all configurations (K=1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 30 shots).
- **FSOD (Novel mAP):** Demonstrated substantial improvements, achieving approximately 6.0% higher mAP in 10-shot and 7.9% higher mAP in 30-shot scenarios compared to the best previous method (FSDetView). For example, at 10-shot, FSDetView achieved 12.5% mAP while DeFRCN achieved 18.5%. At 30-shot, FSDetView achieved 14.7% mAP while DeFRCN achieved 22.6%.
- **G-FSOD:** DeFRCN's performance on base classes was comparable to TFA, but it showed superior results for both novel and overall mAP. As the number of support shots increased, DeFRCN exhibited greater performance gains.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** The number of learnable parameters in DeFRCN was similar to FRCN-ft and greater than TFA, yet it avoided severe overfitting, indicating efficient parameter utilization.

#### 4.3. Cross-Domain FSOD (COCO to VOC)

- In cross-domain experiments (training on COCO base classes, fine-tuning on VOC novel classes, 10-shot), DeFRCN achieved an mAP of 55.9%, which was a 13.6% improvement over MPSR (42.3%). This indicated enhanced generalization ability in cross-domain scenarios.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Effectiveness of Different Modules (on COCO):**
 - **Plain FRCN:** Achieved 7.9% / 12.2% mAP for 10-shot / 30-shot, highlighting severe overfitting without few-shot techniques.
 - **GDL in Base Training (GDL-B):** Improved base class performance (0.6%) and novel class performance (0.3% to 2.1%), suggesting a better base model benefits few-shot detection.
 - **GDL in Novel Fine-tuning (GDL-N):** Provided significant boosts of 7.3% / 6.8% for 10-shot / 30-shot, primarily by allowing more learnable parameters for novel domain transfer while mitigating overfitting.
 - **Prototypical Calibration Block (PCB):** As a plug-and-play module, PCB consistently added 1.4% to 2.6% mAP, regardless of GDL settings.
 - **Combined DeFRCN:** The integration of all three modules (GDL-B, GDL-N, PCB) resulted in a total promotion of 10.6% / 10.4% mAP for 10-shot / 30-shot compared to plain FRCN (18.5% vs 7.9% for 10-shot, 22.6% vs 12.2% for 30-shot).
- **Effectiveness of Decoupling Degree (λ values):**
 - Experiments with varying `λ_rpn` and `λ_rcnn` showed that setting `λ_rpn` to a smaller value (near 0, effectively stopping gradients from RPN) generally yielded higher performance.
 - `λ_rcnn` required an appropriate value (e.g., 0.01 during novel fine-tuning) for optimal backbone optimization.
 - Analysis of four corners in Fig. 4(a) suggested that RPN had a negative role in FRCN backbone optimization, while RCNN had a positive effect.
- **GDL's Impact on Conventional Detection:** GDL was shown to be effective even in data-sufficient scenarios. When applied to standard COCO 2017 detection, GDL improved the baseline Faster R-CNN by 1.5% AP50 for ResNet-50 and 0.9% AP50 for ResNet-101. It also showed a +2.8% mAP improvement in conventional cross-domain object detection (Cityscapes to FoggyCityscapes).
- **PCB with Other Approaches:** When integrated into other FSOD methods (FRCN-ft, TFA, MPSR), PCB consistently yielded performance gains of +1.0% to +3.0% mAP on novel classes, demonstrating its flexibility and effectiveness as a plug-and-play module.
- **PCB with Different Pre-trained Models:** Using stronger pre-trained models (e.g., IN-SwAV, IG-WSL) for PCB consistently improved FSOD performance on VOC, with IG-WSL leading to the largest gains.
- **Why PCB Works:** Visualization showed that prototypes generated from novel classes could activate distinct and meaningful areas of feature maps, even for classes not present in the ImageNet pre-training. This supports the effectiveness of metric-based pairwise scores for calibration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DeFRCN framework offers a notable advancement in few-shot object detection by systematically addressing fundamental architectural contradictions within the widely used Faster R-CNN. Its significance lies in its ability to achieve new state-of-the-art performance across multiple standard benchmarks (PASCAL VOC and COCO), including challenging cross-domain scenarios, using a straightforward fine-tuning approach.

The introduction of the Gradient Decoupled Layer (GDL) provides a mechanism to explicitly control the influence of different network stages on the shared backbone during training. This addresses the multi-stage conflict between the RPN and RCNN, which is particularly detrimental in data-scarce few-shot settings. The demonstrated effectiveness of GDL in conventional object detection suggests its potential as a general architectural component for multi-task learning frameworks, offering a way to mitigate gradient conflicts beyond the few-shot domain.

The Prototypical Calibration Block (PCB) is a significant contribution for its ability to effectively decouple classification and localization tasks. By leveraging an offline, prototype-based classification model to calibrate scores, PCB enhances the robustness of classification without interfering with the regression task. Its plug-and-play nature makes it easily integrable into various existing or future few-shot detection architectures, potentially boosting their performance with no additional training effort. This modularity could accelerate research and development in FSOD by providing a readily available tool for improving classification accuracy.

The work demonstrates that a deeper understanding and targeted modification of existing, proven architectures like Faster R-CNN can yield substantial improvements in challenging low-data regimes. The findings have implications for developing more robust and generalizable object detectors capable of rapid adaptation to novel categories with limited examples, which is critical for real-world applications where obtaining vast annotated datasets is impractical. The simplicity and effectiveness of DeFRCN could serve as a strong baseline and inspire further research into architectural design principles for multi-task and few-shot learning.
