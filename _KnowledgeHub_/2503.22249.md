# FLAM: Foundation Model-Based Body Stabilization for Humanoid Locomotion and Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Harbin Institute of Technology developed FLAM, a control approach for humanoid robots that leverages a pre-trained human motion reconstruction foundation model to provide an explicit stability reward. This method improves learning efficiency and enhances performance across various locomotion and manipulation tasks, demonstrating superior results compared to baseline reinforcement learning methods on the Humanoid-Bench.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots are challenging to control due to their high degrees of freedom, inherent bipedal instability, and the need to generalize across diverse locomotion and manipulation tasks.
- Most reinforcement learning (RL) methods for humanoid control focus solely on task-specific rewards, often leading to inefficient learning and unstable movements without explicitly accounting for overall body stability.
- Controllers trained for specific tasks struggle to generalize to new environments or conditions, and do not inherently encode fundamental stability skills.

## Method

- **FLAM** introduces a stabilizing reward function that maps robot poses to human poses and uses a pre-trained human motion reconstruction foundation model (RoHM) to generate stable motion trajectories.
- A stability reward is calculated based on the similarity between the robot's current pose and the reconstructed stable human pose, encouraging the robot to adopt more stable configurations.
- This stability reward is integrated with conventional task-specific rewards, and the combined reward signal is used to train a model-based reinforcement learning policy (==TD-MPC2==) for effective control.

## Results

- FLAM significantly outperformed state-of-the-art baseline methods on most locomotion tasks, achieving nearly **2x** the performance in some cases like Walk, Stand, and Run.
- For manipulation tasks, FLAM consistently showed improved performance over baselines on tasks such as Bookshelf-Simple/Hard, Spoon, and Insert, though overall task completion remained challenging.
- An ablation study on the stability reward scaling factor (λ) showed that a balanced integration (e.g., λ=**0.5** or **1.0**) was optimal, with higher values sometimes hindering task performance by overemphasizing stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly incorporating body stability as a learning objective, using a human motion foundation model, addresses a critical gap in conventional reinforcement learning for humanoid robots.
> - Pre-trained foundation models can be effectively leveraged to provide implicit stability guidance to robots by assessing robot poses against stable human motion reconstructions.
> - Combining a stability-focused reward with task-specific rewards leads to more efficient learning and improved performance across diverse locomotion and manipulation tasks for humanoid robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Humanoid robots, with their complex structure and high degrees of freedom, present significant challenges in developing effective control mechanisms. The research paper "FLAM: Foundation Model-Based Body Stabilization for Humanoid Locomotion and Manipulation" by researchers from the Harbin Institute of Technology presents a novel approach that leverages foundation models to enhance body stability in humanoid robots.

Most reinforcement learning (RL) methods for humanoid control focus on task-specific rewards without explicitly addressing body stability. FLAM fills this gap by introducing a stability-focused reward mechanism that works alongside conventional task rewards. This approach mirrors human learning progression, where fundamental stability skills are developed before attempting complex movements or tasks.

### 2. The Challenge of Humanoid Robot Control

Controlling humanoid robots is inherently difficult due to several factors:

1. **High-Dimensional Control Space**: Humanoid robots typically have 30+ degrees of freedom, creating a vast action space that's challenging to navigate efficiently.

2. **Inherent Instability**: Bipedal structures are naturally unstable compared to wheeled or quadrupedal robots.

3. **Task Complexity**: Robots must maintain stability while performing diverse locomotion and manipulation tasks.

4. **Generalization Challenges**: Controllers trained for specific tasks often struggle to generalize to new environments or conditions.

Conventional reinforcement learning approaches for humanoid control rely primarily on task-specific reward signals (e.g., "move forward at a certain speed" or "grasp an object"). While these methods can achieve specific goals, they don't explicitly account for overall body stability, leading to inefficient learning and potentially unstable movements.

### 3. Foundation Model-Based Approach

FLAM introduces a novel approach that utilizes a pre-trained human motion reconstruction foundation model to assess and encourage stable postures. The key insight is that not all physically possible poses are equally stable or efficient for task completion.

The approach consists of two main components:

1. **Stabilizing Reward Function**: This component maps robot poses to human poses, uses a human motion reconstruction model to generate stable motions, and calculates stability rewards based on the similarity between original and reconstructed poses.

2. **Basic Policy**: A model-based reinforcement learning policy (TD-MPC2) that integrates both stability rewards and task rewards to learn effective control policies.

### 4. Methodology

#### 4.1. Robot-Human Pose Mapping

For the foundation model to assess robot postures, FLAM first maps robot poses to human poses through a two-stage process:

1. **Initial Pose Mapping**: Establishes a baseline mapping between the robot's initial state and a standard human pose.

2. **Joint Pose Mapping**: Maps subsequent robot joint orientations to corresponding human joint orientations.

#### 4.2. Human Motion Reconstruction

Once robot poses are mapped to human poses, a pre-trained diffusion-based foundation model (RoHM) reconstructs a stable human motion trajectory. This model effectively "denoises" potentially unstable poses, producing more stable alternative poses.

The stabilizing reward is calculated using the formula:

$$r_{stabilize} = \exp(-\beta \cdot (d_{pos} + \alpha \cdot d_{ori}))$$

Where:
- $d_{pos}$ is the position difference between original and reconstructed poses
- $d_{ori}$ is the orientation difference
- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are scaling parameters

#### 4.3. Policy Learning

The total reward combines the stabilizing reward with task-specific rewards:

$$r_{total} = r_{task} + \lambda \cdot r_{stabilize}$$

Where $\lambda$ is a scaling factor that balances stability and task objectives.

The policy is trained using TD-MPC2, a model-based reinforcement learning algorithm that learns a world model and uses it for efficient planning. The training process involves:

1. Collecting trajectory segments
2. Computing stabilizing and task rewards
3. Updating the policy using sampled data from a replay buffer
4. Repeating until convergence

### 5. Experimental Results

FLAM was evaluated on Humanoid-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark featuring diverse locomotion and manipulation tasks. The results demonstrated several key findings:

#### 5.1. Locomotion Tasks

FLAM significantly outperformed state-of-the-art methods (TD-MPC2, SAC, DreamerV3, CQN, CQN-AS) on most locomotion tasks, including:

- **Basic Locomotion**: Walk, Stand, Run tasks showed substantial improvements, with FLAM achieving nearly 2x the performance of baseline methods in some cases.
- **Complex Navigation**: FLAM demonstrated superior performance in Maze, Stair, Slide, and Pole tasks.
- **Challenging Scenarios**: Even in difficult tasks like Sit-Hard, FLAM achieved significantly better results than baseline methods.

However, the method showed limited improvements in non-planar environments (Balance-Hard, Hurdle) and in tasks requiring stability recovery.

#### 5.2. Manipulation Tasks

For manipulation tasks, FLAM consistently outperformed baseline methods, though the absolute performance was lower compared to locomotion tasks. This suggests that controlling dexterous hands with high DoF remains challenging even with improved body stability.

Key findings from manipulation experiments include:

- FLAM achieved significantly better performance on Bookshelf-Simple/Hard, Spoon, and Insert tasks
- The Door task showed comparable performance between FLAM and TD-MPC2
- None of the methods successfully completed manipulation tasks, highlighting the inherent difficulty of these tasks

#### 5.3. Ablation Study on Scaling Factor

The ablation study on the scaling factor λ revealed:

- **λ = 0.0**: Without the stabilizing reward, learning was slower and performance was lower
- **λ = 0.5**: Provided good balance for most tasks
- **λ = 1.0**: Optimal for many tasks, particularly Walk and Stand
- **λ = 10.0**: Too high emphasis on stability hindered task performance in some cases (particularly Run)

### 6. Impact and Significance

FLAM represents a significant advancement in humanoid robot control for several reasons:

1. **Explicit Stability Consideration**: By directly incorporating stability as a learning objective, FLAM addresses a critical gap in conventional reinforcement learning approaches.

2. **Foundation Model Integration**: The work demonstrates how pre-trained foundation models can be effectively leveraged for robotics applications, bridging the gap between human motion understanding and robot control.

3. **General-Purpose Approach**: The method improves performance across a wide range of tasks without task-specific engineering, suggesting a more general solution to humanoid control.

4. **Learning Efficiency**: FLAM achieves faster learning and better final performance compared to baseline methods, indicating more efficient use of training samples.

### 7. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its promising results, FLAM has several limitations that suggest directions for future research:

1. **Stability Recovery**: The method focuses on maintaining stability but doesn't explicitly address recovery from unstable states.

2. **High DoF Manipulation**: While FLAM improves body stability, controlling dexterous hands with high degrees of freedom remains challenging.

3. **Environmental Complexity**: Performance improvements are less pronounced in non-planar environments, suggesting room for improvement in complex terrains.

4. **Reward Balancing**: The linear combination of rewards may not be optimal for all tasks, and more sophisticated reward composition strategies could yield better results.

Future work could explore more advanced foundation models, improved pose mapping techniques, and more sophisticated reward integration methods to address these limitations.

### 8. Conclusion

FLAM introduces a novel approach to humanoid robot control by explicitly incorporating body stability considerations through a foundation model-based reward function. By assessing the stability of robot poses via a human motion reconstruction model, FLAM provides a valuable supplementary signal that accelerates learning and improves performance across a range of locomotion and manipulation tasks.

The research demonstrates that combining task-specific rewards with stability-focused rewards leads to more effective humanoid robot control. This approach holds promise for developing more capable and versatile humanoid robots that can operate reliably in diverse environments and perform a wide range of tasks while maintaining stable body postures.
