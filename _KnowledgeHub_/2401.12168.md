# SpatialVLM: Endowing Vision-Language Models with Spatial Reasoning Capabilities

> [!summary] Summary
> SpatialVLM, from Google DeepMind and Google Research, equips Vision-Language Models with quantitative spatial reasoning abilities, such as estimating distances and object sizes. This is achieved through an automated data synthesis pipeline that generates 2 billion spatial reasoning question-answer pairs, enabling the model to accurately reason about physical space and support applications in robotics.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) frequently struggle with quantitative spatial reasoning tasks, such as understanding distances, object sizes, and precise relative positions in 3D space.
- Existing VLMs primarily process 2D image information and lack inherent 3D depth, making it challenging to ground their understanding in physical metric dimensions.
- Training datasets for VLMs typically lack sufficient examples with accurate distance and size information, and spatial references within questions can be ambiguous without clear referents.

## Method

- Developed an ==automated data synthesis pipeline== to generate a large-scale dataset of **10 million images** with **2 billion** spatial reasoning question-answer pairs, incorporating 3D context from 2D images via ==metric depth estimation==.
- The pipeline includes stages for semantic filtering, 2D context extraction (region captioning, depth, segmentation), conversion to 3D context, ambiguity resolution, and ==human-aligned question-answer synthesis==.
- Trained a VLM based on a modified PaLM-E architecture (using PaLM 2-S and a ViT-L/14 vision encoder), mixing general VQA data with the newly generated spatial reasoning dataset, and found unfreezing the ViT encoder optimal.

## Results

- SpatialVLM demonstrated improved accuracy in both qualitative (e.g., relative positions) and quantitative (e.g., distances in meters, object sizes) spatial reasoning compared to models like GPT-4V, PaLI, and PaLM-E.
- The model successfully learned to estimate distances between objects in meters and measure object sizes, performing consistently well across a range of distances.
- SpatialVLM was applied to robotics tasks, providing accurate **reward annotation** for robot learning and enabling enhanced embodied planning by predicting precise **gripper-to-object distances** for grasping and navigation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large-scale synthetic data generation, leveraging existing computer vision tools for depth and segmentation, can effectively imbue Vision-Language Models with quantitative spatial reasoning skills.
> - Despite reliance on noisy monocular depth estimations in training data, SpatialVLM learned generalizable quantitative spatial understanding that transfers to real-world scenarios.
> - Combining SpatialVLM with a Large Language Model via chain-of-thought prompting enables complex, multi-step spatial reasoning problems to be solved.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in understanding image content and responding to natural language queries. However, these models often struggle with spatial reasoning tasks that humans perform effortlessly, such as understanding distances between objects, object sizes, and relative positions in 3D space. The paper "SpatialVLM: Endowing Vision-Language Models with Spatial Reasoning Capabilities" addresses this critical limitation by developing a methodology to enhance VLMs with quantitative spatial reasoning abilities.

Developed by researchers from Google DeepMind and Google Research, SpatialVLM represents a significant advancement in making VLMs more grounded in physical reality. Unlike previous approaches that primarily focus on qualitative spatial relationships (such as "left of" or "behind"), SpatialVLM equips models with the ability to make quantitative judgments about the physical world, such as measuring distances in meters or estimating object sizes.

### 2. The Spatial Reasoning Challenge

Current VLMs face significant challenges when dealing with spatial reasoning tasks. These limitations become evident when comparing the responses of state-of-the-art models like GPT-4V with SpatialVLM for spatial questions. For example, when asked whether a robot can navigate between furniture, GPT-4V makes approximate visual estimations, while SpatialVLM can provide precise measurements and definitive answers.

The authors identify three key challenges that VLMs face with spatial reasoning:

1. **Lack of 3D information**: VLMs typically process 2D images without depth information, making it difficult to understand true physical distances and sizes.

2. **Insufficient spatial training data**: Most VLM training datasets don't include enough examples with accurate distance and size information.

3. **Ambiguity in spatial references**: Without clear referents, spatial questions can be ambiguous (e.g., "How far is the cup?" lacks a reference point).

### 3. Data Synthesis Pipeline

The cornerstone of SpatialVLM is its automated data synthesis pipeline designed to generate a massive dataset of spatial reasoning questions and answers. This pipeline processes images through several key stages:

#### 3.1. Semantic Filtering

The pipeline begins by filtering a large collection of images to identify those suitable for spatial reasoning tasks. The researchers use CLIP to select scene-level photos where spatial relationships can be meaningfully extracted.

#### 3.2. 2D Context Extraction

For each filtered image, the system extracts contextual information using:
- Region captioning to identify and describe objects
- Metric depth estimation to understand distances
- Coordinate canonicalization to establish a consistent reference frame
- Semantic segmentation to identify object boundaries

#### 3.3. 2D to 3D Context Conversion

The depth information allows the system to convert 2D images into 3D point clouds, providing metric-scale information about object positions, distances, and sizes.

#### 3.4. Ambiguity Resolution

To address the problem of ambiguous references, the pipeline employs embedding-based clustering to disambiguate similar captions and modifies references as needed to ensure clarity.

#### 3.5. Human Alignment

The system also converts raw measurements into more human-friendly units and formats, using common expressions (like "half a meter" instead of "0.5m") and favoring round numbers that align with how humans typically express distances.

#### 3.6. Question-Answer Synthesis

The final step generates both qualitative and quantitative questions about the spatial relationships in the image:

- **Qualitative questions**: Focus on relative positions (left/right, in front/behind)
- **Quantitative questions**: Ask about specific measurements like distances and sizes

This process resulted in an unprecedented dataset of 10 million images with 2 billion spatial reasoning VQA pairs, far exceeding the scale of previous spatial reasoning datasets.

### 4. VLM Training and Architecture

SpatialVLM is built on the foundation of PaLM-E, but with several key modifications:

- The original PaLM backbone is replaced with a smaller PaLM 2-S model (for computational efficiency)
- The vision encoder is based on the ViT-L/14 architecture
- The model is trained with a mixture of the original PaLM-E dataset and the newly generated spatial reasoning dataset

The authors investigated several training strategies, including:

1. **Varying proportions of spatial reasoning data**: Testing different ratios of spatial to general VQA data
2. **Freezing vs. unfreezing the ViT encoder**: Examining whether fine-tuning the vision encoder improves performance
3. **Data canonicalization**: Evaluating whether standardizing the coordinate system helps learning

The results showed that unfreezing the ViT encoder during training and including a substantial proportion of spatial reasoning data in the training mix yielded the best performance.

### 5. Performance and Results

SpatialVLM demonstrated significant improvements in spatial reasoning capabilities compared to state-of-the-art VLMs, including GPT-4V, PaLI, and PaLM-E.

#### 5.1. Qualitative Spatial Reasoning

In qualitative spatial reasoning tasks (understanding relative positions like left/right, in front/behind), SpatialVLM achieved high accuracy across various benchmark datasets. The model was able to correctly interpret spatial relationships between objects in diverse scenes.

Examples of qualitative questions that SpatialVLM can accurately answer include:
- "Is the fireplace screen with red doors smaller than the dog standing on the floor?"
- "Compared to the little boy in a red shirt, which side is the man wearing a blue shirt on?"
- "Are the windows positioned to the left of the black television?"

#### 5.2. Quantitative Spatial Reasoning

The most significant improvements were in quantitative spatial reasoning, where SpatialVLM demonstrated an unprecedented ability to estimate distances and object sizes with high accuracy. The model successfully learned to:

- Estimate distances between objects in meters
- Measure the sizes of objects
- Judge whether objects would fit in spaces or if a person or robot could navigate between obstacles

The researchers found that despite being trained on noisy synthetic data (depth estimation is imperfect), SpatialVLM learned generalizable quantitative estimations that transferred well to real-world scenarios.

### 6. Applications in Robotics and Complex Reasoning

Beyond improving basic spatial reasoning, SpatialVLM enables several advanced applications:

#### 6.1. Chain-of-Thought Spatial Reasoning

By combining SpatialVLM with a Large Language Model (LLM), the researchers demonstrated that complex spatial reasoning problems could be solved through a chain-of-thought approach. The LLM breaks down complex questions into simpler spatial queries that SpatialVLM can answer individually, then synthesizes the final answer.

This approach enables solving multi-step reasoning problems that neither model could solve independently.

#### 6.2. Embodied Planning and Robotics

SpatialVLM's spatial reasoning capabilities make it particularly valuable for robotics applications. The researchers demonstrated two key use cases:

1. **Reward Annotation**: SpatialVLM can serve as a reward annotator for robot learning, providing accurate feedback about spatial objectives.

2. **Embodied Planning**: The model can help robots plan paths and manipulate objects by accurately estimating distances and understanding spatial relationships.

For example, when a robot needs to grasp an object, SpatialVLM can accurately estimate the distance to the target throughout the approach, providing better guidance than previous VLMs. The model can also understand complex spatial commands like "pick up the apple" or "put the apple into the bowl" while accounting for the physical arrangement of objects in the scene.

### 7. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its advances, SpatialVLM has several limitations:

1. **Depth estimation errors**: The pipeline relies on monocular depth estimation, which can be inaccurate, particularly for complex scenes or unusual viewpoints.

2. **Static scene assumption**: The current approach assumes static scenes and doesn't account for object movement or dynamics.

3. **Biased training distribution**: The training data may have biases in the types of scenes, objects, and spatial relationships represented.

4. **Scale limitations**: Even with 2 billion QA pairs, there may be gaps in the coverage of possible spatial scenarios.

Future work could address these limitations by incorporating multi-view information, using more accurate depth sensors, expanding the dataset to cover more diverse scenarios, and integrating temporal reasoning to understand dynamic scenes.

### 8. Conclusion

SpatialVLM represents a significant advancement in endowing Vision-Language Models with robust spatial reasoning capabilities. By generating a massive dataset of spatial reasoning questions and answers and developing effective training strategies, the researchers have created a model that can understand both qualitative and quantitative spatial relationships with unprecedented accuracy.

The ability to reason about distances, sizes, and spatial arrangements opens up new possibilities for VLMs in robotics, augmented reality, and other applications that require understanding of the physical world. This work bridges an important gap between the impressive language and vision capabilities of modern AI systems and the spatial reasoning that humans perform effortlessly.

As VLMs continue to evolve, the spatial reasoning capabilities demonstrated by SpatialVLM will likely become an increasingly important component of AI systems that need to interact with and understand the physical world.
