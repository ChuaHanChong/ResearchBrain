# The Magic Correlations: Understanding Knowledge Transfer from Pretraining to Supervised Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google DeepMind and EPFL systematically analyzed how accuracy and confidence transfer from pretraining to supervised fine-tuning in large language models using correlation protocols. Their findings reveal category-dependent transfer reliability and an inverse scaling dynamic where larger models exhibit stronger accuracy transfer but weaker confidence transfer, indicating significant calibration reorganization.

## Problem

- LLM development often relies on the untested assumption that pretraining performance reliably predicts post-fine-tuning performance.
- Decisions about data curation and resource allocation are frequently based on early-stage evaluations with smaller proxy models, which may lead to suboptimal outcomes if transfer is not stable.
- A lack of systematic understanding of how model capabilities, specifically accuracy and confidence, evolve and transfer across pretraining and fine-tuning stages.

## Method

- Trained ==decoder-only transformer models== (240M and 1B parameters) on 9 pretraining data mixtures (varying proportions of general web, code, and curated knowledge), then fine-tuned all checkpoints on the ==Tulu-v2-mix instruction-following dataset== for 5 epochs.
- Evaluated models on **20** benchmarks across four capability categories (Commonsense, Scientific, NLI, Semantic) using both accuracy and confidence metrics.
- Employed ==five correlation protocols== to quantify the preservation and reorganization of model capabilities across training stages and data mixtures.

## Results

- Science and Commonsense benchmarks show high cross-stage accuracy correlation (mean r > **0.5**), reliably predicting SFT outcomes, while benchmarks like WiC and MultiRC show weak correlations (r < **0.3**), making them unreliable early-stage predictors.
- **1B** models achieve stronger accuracy transfer (r = **0.60**) but substantially weaker confidence transfer (r = **0.39**) compared to **240M** models, which maintain higher confidence transfer (r = **0.68**).
- Persistent miscalibration is observed in Commonsense and Semantic tasks, exhibiting weak or negative accuracy-confidence alignment (r_align ≈ **-0.1** to **-0.2**) even after SFT.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Knowledge transfer from pretraining to supervised fine-tuning is not uniform, varying significantly by capability category and model scale; larger models specifically exhibit stronger accuracy transfer but weaker confidence transfer, suggesting extensive reorganization of calibration patterns during fine-tuning.
> - Pretraining data choices have lasting, scale-dependent effects on model calibration and task performance that supervised fine-tuning does not easily overwrite.
> - Specific benchmarks can be unreliable early-stage predictors of downstream SFT performance, highlighting the need for careful benchmark selection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern Large Language Model (LLM) development typically proceeds through several stages: extensive pretraining on massive, diverse text corpora, followed by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on curated instruction-following data, and often further refinement through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). A critical, yet often unchecked, assumption underlies much of this process: that performance metrics and internal representations established during the pretraining phase reliably predict downstream performance after fine-tuning. This assumption drives crucial decisions in data curation, mixture composition, and resource allocation, particularly when using smaller, proxy models during pretraining to infer capabilities of much larger, more expensive final models.

This paper directly scrutinizes this fundamental assumption. Its contribution lies in providing a systematic, empirical analysis of how language model capabilities, specifically accuracy and confidence, transfer from pretraining to supervised fine-tuning. This work operates at the intersection of several active research areas within the LLM landscape:

- **Scaling Laws and Transfer Learning:** Prior work by Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) established "scaling laws" demonstrating predictable power-law relationships between model performance and factors like parameter count, dataset size, and compute. Hernandez et al. (2021) extended this to transfer learning, suggesting similar scaling for SFT efficiency. However, recent studies by Isik et al. (2025), Lourie et al. (2025), and Schaeffer et al. (2025) have begun to challenge the universal reliability of these scaling laws, particularly for downstream tasks. "The Magic Correlations" contributes to this discourse by analyzing the *dynamics* of transfer—not just overall performance scaling, but the stability and reorganization of specific capabilities (accuracy and confidence) across training stages and different model scales, revealing nuances that simple scaling laws might miss.

- **Benchmark Design and Contamination:** The reliability of evaluation benchmarks is a persistent concern. Issues like benchmark contamination (Sainz et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2024), varying task difficulty (Rodriguez et al., 2021), and construct validity (Bowman and Dahl, 2021) have been widely discussed. Frameworks like HELM (Liang et al., 2023) offer multi-dimensional evaluation. This paper introduces a complementary perspective by assessing *which benchmarks maintain stable rankings* across training stages, making them reliable for early-stage evaluation, rather than just measuring absolute performance. This is crucial for guiding the selection of benchmarks that genuinely predict downstream success.

- **Model Calibration:** The trustworthiness of LLMs depends heavily on their calibration—how well their predicted confidence aligns with their actual accuracy. Studies have shown modern neural networks can be poorly calibrated (Guo et al., 2017), requiring techniques like temperature scaling. For LLMs, research by Kadavath et al. (2022) found larger models improve calibration on factual questions, while Desai and Durrett (2020) highlighted the need for careful calibration for downstream tasks. Surveys by Tian et al. (2023) and Geng et al. (2024) provide comprehensive overviews. This paper significantly extends this by investigating whether *calibration patterns persist* through SFT, offering insights into when and why models might need recalibration post-fine-tuning.

- **Data Mixture and Curation Effects:** The composition of pretraining data profoundly impacts downstream capabilities. Work by Penedo et al. (2023) and Lozhkov et al. (2024a) explored the efficacy of filtered web data and educational content, respectively, while Li et al. (2025) introduced systematic data curation pipelines. Instead of attributing individual samples, "The Magic Correlations" adopts an *aggregate approach*, characterizing how broad data mixture choices (e.g., web source, code proportion) influence the *transferability* of capabilities. This provides high-level actionable guidance for data strategists.

In summary, this work addresses a critical gap in understanding the intricate relationship between pretraining and fine-tuning. By challenging underlying assumptions and providing a novel, correlation-based methodology, it offers a deeper, more nuanced understanding of knowledge transfer, moving beyond simple performance gains to analyze the stability and reorganization of underlying model capabilities and calibration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **understand how language model capabilities transfer from the pretraining stage to the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage.** This understanding is deemed "fundamental to efficient model development and data curation" because current practices often rely on an untested assumption: that pretraining performance reliably predicts post-SFT performance, and that internal model representations remain stable across these critical training stages.

The authors are motivated to "scrutinize this assumption through comprehensive empirical analysis." To achieve this, they articulate four core research questions (RQs) that guide their investigation:

1. **RQ1. Performance Stability:** "To what extent do accuracy and confidence rankings established during pretraining persist after SFT?" This question probes the direct transferability of model proficiency and its certainty estimates from one stage to the next.
2. **RQ2. Benchmark Reliability:** "Which benchmarks serve as robust cross-stage predictors and which are unreliable?" This aims to identify diagnostic tools that can reliably gauge a model's future performance based on early-stage evaluations.
3. **RQ3. Scale Dynamics:** "How do transfer dynamics shift with model scale?" Recognizing the pervasive impact of model size in LLM research, this question explores whether the patterns of knowledge transfer fundamentally change as models grow larger.
4. **RQ4. Calibration Quality:** "How well does model confidence align with accuracy, as a measure of calibration quality? Does this alignment pattern transfer across training stages?" This delves into the more subtle aspect of model trustworthiness, examining the coherence between what a model "knows" (accuracy) and what it "thinks it knows" (confidence), and how this relationship evolves.

The motivation for this rigorous investigation is deeply practical. Decisions about data curation, the composition of data mixtures, and the allocation of vast computational resources are frequently made based on pretraining benchmarks, often using smaller proxy models. If the fundamental assumption of stable transfer is flawed, these decisions could be suboptimal, leading to inefficient development cycles, miscalibrated models, or models that fail to generalize as expected. By providing actionable insights into these transfer dynamics, the authors aim to offer concrete guidance for benchmark selection, data curation strategies, and the overall efficiency of LLM development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

To address their research questions, the authors employ a systematic empirical approach centered on **correlation protocols** applied to accuracy and confidence metrics.

#### 3.1. Training Pipeline

The study investigates two critical phases of LLM development: Pretraining (PT) and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT).

- **Pretraining:**
 - **Models:** Decoder-only transformer models were trained at two distinct scales: 240 million (240M) and 1 billion (1B) parameters.
 - **Data Mixtures:** A suite of 9 distinct pretraining data mixtures was used. These mixtures were systematically constructed to disentangle the effects of data source from data distribution. They combined:
 - **General Web Data:** Sourced from RefinedWeb (minimal filtering), FineWeb-Edu (aggressive educational content filtering), or DCLM (model-based quality scoring).
 - **Code Data:** From StarCoder or The Stack v2.
 - **Curated Knowledge:** From RedPajama-v2 (Wikipedia, ArXiv, Github, StackExchange).
 - **Mixing Proportions:** The proportion of web data was varied (25%, 45%, 65%) relative to code and curated sources, allowing for the study of "web-heavy," "balanced," and "code-heavy" configurations. Models were pretrained for 12B (240M) and 52B (1B) tokens respectively.

- **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT):** All pretrained checkpoints (from the various data mixtures) were then fine-tuned on Tulu-v2-mix, a curated instruction-following dataset. Models underwent 5 epochs of SFT.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Benchmarks

Models were evaluated on 20 benchmarks, organized into four capability categories:

- **Commonsense Reasoning:** (e.g., CommonsenseQA, WinoGrande, HellaSwag, PIQA, SIQA, COPA, BoolQ) — requiring physical world knowledge and social reasoning.
- **Scientific Reasoning (Science):** (e.g., ARC-Challenge/Easy, SciQ, OpenBookQA) — requiring factual and scientific knowledge.
- **Natural Language Inference (NLI):** (e.g., MNLI, QNLI, RTE, CB) — requiring inference about entailment relationships.
- **Semantic Understanding (Semantic):** (e.g., QQP, MRPC, WiC, WSC, MultiRC) — requiring understanding of semantic equivalence, coreference, and textual relationships.

For each benchmark, two primary metrics were reported:
- **Accuracy:** The proportion of correct predictions.
- **Confidence:** The probability assigned to the selected answer, averaged across the evaluation set, serving as a measure of the model's certainty.

#### 3.3. The Lens of Correlations (Correlation Protocols)

The core of the methodology lies in five complementary correlation protocols, designed to quantify the preservation and reorganization of capabilities across training stages and data mixtures.

- **Cross-Stage Correlation:**
 - `r_stage_acc`: Pearson correlation between pretraining accuracy and SFT accuracy for a given benchmark, across the 9 data mixtures. High correlation indicates a reliable early-stage predictor.
 - `r_stage_conf`: Pearson correlation between pretraining confidence and SFT confidence for a given benchmark, across the 9 data mixtures. High correlation suggests persistence of the model's calibration "fingerprint."

- **Intra-Category Correlation:** These protocols measure how coherently benchmarks within the same semantic category respond to changes in data mixtures.
 - `r_PT_intra`: Measures correlation of accuracy between benchmark *i* and *j* within the same category during pretraining.
 - `r_SFT_intra`: Measures correlation of accuracy between benchmark *i* and *j* within the same category after SFT.
 - `r_cross_intra`: Measures correlation between PT accuracy of benchmark *i* and SFT accuracy of benchmark *j* (where *i* ≠ *j* but both are in the same category). This assesses cross-stage, cross-benchmark transfer reliability.

- **Performance-confidence Alignment:** This measures how well a model's confidence reflects its actual accuracy, serving as a proxy for global calibration quality.
 - `r_align`: Pearson correlation between accuracy and confidence scores *across the entire benchmark suite* for a single model configuration. High positive values indicate well-calibrated confidence.

By systematically applying these protocols across different model scales, data mixtures, and training stages, the authors meticulously dissect the complex interplay between pretraining decisions and downstream model behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several crucial findings that illuminate the complex transfer dynamics from pretraining to SFT:

#### 4.1. Cross-Stage Transfer (RQ1)

- **Category-Dependent Transfer Reliability:** Transfer reliability varies significantly by capability category. Science and Commonsense benchmarks consistently show high cross-stage accuracy correlation (mean *r* > 0.5), indicating pretraining performance reliably predicts SFT outcomes. In contrast, NLI and Semantic tasks exhibit weaker transfer, suggesting SFT substantially reorganizes how models approach these tasks.
- **Inverse Scaling of Accuracy and Confidence Transfer (Introduced as main finding, explored in RQ3):** Larger models (1B) show stronger cross-stage accuracy correlation but *weaker* confidence correlation compared to smaller models (240M). This suggests larger models undergo more extensive confidence reorganization during SFT, developing task-specific calibration profiles.
- **Strong Persistence of Confidence Patterns for Reasoning Tasks (Small Models):** For 240M models, confidence calibration patterns from pretraining persist remarkably well, especially for Commonsense (*r* = 0.87) and Science (*r* = 0.82) benchmarks. NLI, however, shows weak confidence persistence, implying SFT re-calibrates uncertainty for these tasks.
- **Persistence of Cross-Benchmark Confidence Structure:** The structural similarity of confidence correlations across different benchmarks within the same category (e.g., the Commonsense-Science "confidence block") persists and even slightly strengthens from pretraining to SFT at 240M, suggesting SFT reinforces shared calibration structures.

#### 4.2. Benchmark Reliability (RQ2)

- **Unreliable Early-Stage Predictors:** Specific benchmarks, such as WiC and MultiRC, show weak or negative accuracy correlations (r < 0.3) between pretraining and SFT, indicating that pretraining performance on these tasks is not a reliable predictor of post-SFT success. WinoGrande and MNLI also show negative transfer at 240M. These tasks often require nuanced linguistic understanding that is heavily reorganized during SFT.
- **Intra-Category Competition:** Commonsense and Semantic categories consistently exhibit negative intra-category coherence on accuracy, meaning that data mixtures improving one benchmark often degrade others within the same semantic category. Science, however, shows positive intra-category coherence in larger models, indicating more unified underlying representations.
- **Incoherent Benchmarks:** Some benchmarks behave incoherently within their semantic categories (e.g., WinoGrande in Commonsense, WSC in Semantic), implying that task-specific improvements might not generalize to semantically related tasks.

#### 4.3. Scaling Dynamics (RQ3)

- **Inverse Scaling of Accuracy and Confidence Transfer:** This is a key finding. The 1B model shows stronger accuracy transfer (mean *r* = 0.60 vs. 0.51 for 240M), but 240M maintains substantially higher confidence transfer (*r* = 0.68 vs. 0.39 for 1B). This indicates that larger models develop more task-specific calibration profiles rather than retaining monolithic uncertainty patterns.
- **Shift from Intra-Category Competition to Synergy in Accuracy:** As models scale to 1B, most capability categories (especially Science) show a shift towards less negative or positive intra-category coherence for accuracy, suggesting larger models develop more unified reasoning representations where knowledge transfers constructively.
- **Confidence Coherence Degrades with Scale:** For confidence calibration, coherence within categories substantially diminishes from 240M (e.g., Commonsense *r* = 0.73) to 1B (e.g., Commonsense *r* = 0.24). This reflects the development of more task-specific uncertainty estimates in larger models.
- **Anomalous Scaling for NLI:** NLI is unique, showing higher confidence coherence at 1B than 240M, the inverse of the general trend. This suggests NLI tasks might engage distinct representational mechanisms in larger models.

#### 4.4. Calibration Quality (RQ4)

- **Category-Dependent Performance-Confidence Alignment:** Science tasks consistently exhibit high alignment (*r_align* ≈ 0.8), indicating well-calibrated confidence (models are confident when correct). In contrast, Commonsense and Semantic tasks show weak or negative alignment (*r_align* ≈ -0.1 to -0.2), often indicating systematic miscalibration (overconfidence on incorrect predictions).
- **Alignment Patterns Persist Through SFT:** The category-level alignment structure remains largely stable from pretraining to SFT. Science maintains high alignment, while the miscalibration in Commonsense and Semantic persists. This suggests pretraining data choices have lasting effects on confidence structure that SFT doesn't easily overwrite.
- **Scale-Dependent Accuracy-Calibration Trade-offs with Educational Filtering:** Educational-filtered data (FineWeb-Edu) has scale-dependent effects. At 240M, FineWeb-Edu improves NLI accuracy (+5.0pp) but severely degrades alignment (Δ*r_align* = -0.80). At 1B, this pattern reverses: NLI accuracy degrades (-4.4pp), and alignment slightly improves. For Science, FineWeb-Edu consistently improves accuracy with slight alignment degradation. This highlights that data curation optimized at small scales may not transfer to larger models and can lead to trade-offs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"The Magic Correlations" provides a rigorous and illuminating analysis that carries significant practical and theoretical implications for the development of Large Language Models.

#### 5.1. Actionable Guidance for Practitioners

The findings offer direct, actionable insights for engineers and researchers in the LLM ecosystem:

- **Strategic Benchmark Selection:** The paper clearly delineates which benchmarks serve as reliable early-stage predictors for post-SFT performance (e.g., HellaSwag, PIQA, COPA, QQP, and Science/Commonsense categories generally) and which are unreliable (e.g., WiC, MultiRC, and sometimes WinoGrande or MNLI at smaller scales). This enables more informed decisions on which benchmarks to trust when evaluating proxy models or making fundamental data and architecture choices, saving considerable computational resources.
- **Leveraging Confidence as an Evaluation Signal:** Confidence metrics provide complementary insights to accuracy. While confidence transfer is reliable at smaller scales, its degradation at larger scales warns practitioners against assuming pretraining calibration will carry over. This emphasizes the need for task- and stage-specific calibration techniques for larger models, especially in high-stakes applications.
- **Scale-Dependent Data Curation Strategies:** The study demonstrates that optimal data curation strategies are highly dependent on model scale. The example of FineWeb-Edu's effects on NLI accuracy and calibration reversing between 240M and 1B models is a powerful warning against extrapolating data mixture decisions from small-scale experiments to larger production models. This advocates for multi-scale validation of data curation choices.
- **Understanding Calibration Limitations:** The persistent miscalibration observed in Commonsense and Semantic tasks (despite SFT) highlights inherent challenges in certain capability domains. This suggests that for these areas, researchers should focus on fundamental architectural or pretraining data changes rather than relying solely on fine-tuning for better calibration, or prepare for mandatory post-hoc calibration.

#### 5.2. Challenging Fundamental Assumptions and New Perspectives

The research fundamentally challenges the common assumption that knowledge and representations learned during pretraining are robustly and stably transferred through SFT. By revealing "inverse scaling dynamics" where accuracy transfer strengthens with scale but confidence transfer weakens, the paper uncovers a previously unappreciated complexity in how LLMs evolve. It demonstrates that what constitutes "transfer" is not monolithic but can manifest differently across various capabilities and evaluation metrics. The introduction of correlation-based protocols provides a novel methodological lens for understanding the *structural* stability and reorganization of knowledge, rather than just changes in absolute performance.

#### 5.3. Enhancing Efficiency and Robustness in LLM Development

By identifying critical blind spots and providing a more nuanced understanding of knowledge transfer, this work can significantly contribute to more efficient and robust LLM development. Reducing wasted compute on unreliable early-stage evaluations, guiding data selection that genuinely improves target capabilities, and ensuring models are appropriately calibrated are all critical for building reliable and trustworthy AI systems. This research paves the way for more principled LLM engineering, moving towards a science of model development rather than relying solely on heuristic practices.

#### 5.4. Future Research Directions

The paper also outlines several limitations that open avenues for future research. These include investigating the observed inverse scaling trends at even larger model scales (e.g., 7B+), exploring different SFT datasets and post-training protocols (like RLHF or DPO), broadening benchmark coverage to domains like long-context reasoning, code generation, and safety, and conducting more granular analysis of data mixture effects, including synthetic or knowledge-enriched data. These proposed directions indicate that "The Magic Correlations" is not just a conclusion but a foundation for further in-depth studies into the intricate learning dynamics of LLMs.

In conclusion, "The Magic Correlations" is a highly impactful paper that offers critical empirical evidence and a sophisticated analytical framework to better understand the crucial transition from pretraining to fine-tuning in large language models. Its findings empower practitioners with actionable insights for more efficient, reliable, and well-calibrated LLM development.
