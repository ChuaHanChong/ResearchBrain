# FailBench: How Reliable are VLMs at Judging Robot Task Success?

> [!summary] Summary
> FailBench is a cross-source benchmark for policy-agnostic robot failure detection: 2,197 manipulation attempts drawn from fourteen public sources (twelve real, two simulated), with 75% organic failures and original provider labels. Thirteen VLM detectors are scored under one protocol by balanced accuracy; the best, Gemini 3 Flash, reaches only 0.77 mean balanced accuracy, and an evidence-localization crop pipeline adds 2.3 points without retraining.

## Problem

- Existing failure benchmarks each come from a single collection campaign, holding robot, camera, scene and labeling procedure fixed, so cross-domain generalization goes unmeasured.
- Most benchmarks construct failures by perturbing successes or relabeling instructions, letting a detector score well by recognizing the generation procedure rather than the physical outcome.
- VLM verdicts are consumed as rewards, data filters, policy rankings and retry triggers as if ground truth, yet the judge itself is rarely validated first.

## Method

- **FailBench** aggregates 2,197 attempts from fourteen public sources into one instruction-observation-label schema, reusing each provider's own labels and tagging failure origin as organic, planned, synthetic or undocumented.
- A single evaluation harness scores thirteen detectors, six general-purpose ==VLMs==, two embodied ==VLMs== and five purpose-built failure detectors, each run with its authors' prompt, decoding and thinking budget.
- An ==evidence-localization pipeline== where a VLM localizer sees four frames plus the instruction, returns one outcome-relevant box without seeing the label, and fixed cameras are cropped before the unchanged detector runs.

## Results

- Gemini 3 Flash leads at **0.77** macro balanced accuracy; all five specialists fall below their own base models, for example ViFailback-8B **0.587** against Qwen3-VL-8B's **0.675**.
- Contact-rich assembly is hardest at **0.52** slice mean with no model above **0.60**, while motion-readable slices reach **0.90**; error-set IoU across detector pairs averages only **0.28**.
- Localized cropping lifts macro accuracy **0.773** to **0.797** (McNemar **p=0.0015**), fixing **223** and breaking **160** samples, whereas a three-model majority vote gains **0.02** at triple cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fine-tuning a judge on one failure corpus can make it worse elsewhere; headline accuracy on class-imbalanced splits often reflects a constant-answer prior, not perception.
> - Detector difficulty tracks the visual evidence a verdict requires, coarse object motion versus established contact, far more than robot embodiment or task family.
> - More reasoning does not cure ambiguity bias: wrong answers carried longer traces (1,868 versus 1,464 characters) and resolved uncertainty toward declaring success.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The broader research landscape in robotics increasingly integrates vision-language models (VLMs) for various decision-making processes, including evaluating the success or failure of robot manipulation attempts. This capability is foundational, as these judgments can serve critical functions such as generating reinforcement learning rewards, filtering training data, ranking policies, or triggering retry mechanisms within autonomous systems. The reliability of these VLM-based judgments is therefore paramount for the development and deployment of robust robot learning and evaluation pipelines.

Current approaches to robot failure detection broadly fall into two categories: policy-dependent monitors and policy-agnostic detectors. Policy-dependent monitors infer failure from a policy’s internal representations, action statistics, or predicted trajectories, thereby tying them to a specific policy and its architecture. In contrast, policy-agnostic detectors, which are the focus of this work, evaluate a completed attempt solely from its recording and instruction. This design principle suggests potential for greater generality and transferability across different policies and robot platforms. However, this generality also introduces the challenge of ensuring reliable transfer across diverse operational contexts, as an inaccurate judgment can propagate errors through the learning pipeline.

Recent advancements in VLMs have led to their application in providing richer outputs beyond a simple binary verdict, including explanations for failures, categorization of errors, suggestions for corrective actions, dense progress signals, or graded success scores. All these advanced functionalities fundamentally depend on the VLM's ability to accurately determine whether a basic task succeeded or failed. Despite this increasing reliance, existing evaluations of VLM performance in this critical binary judgment task have largely been confined to the specific datasets on which the detectors were developed, offering limited evidence of cross-domain generalization.

Existing failure benchmarks typically originate from a single collection effort, maintaining consistency in factors such as the robot, gripper, camera setup, scene, task family, and labeling procedure. Many also construct failures deliberately, for example, by perturbing successful trajectories or mismatching recordings with instructions. While useful for controlled evaluation, such constructions may enable detectors to perform well by recognizing data generation artifacts rather than robustly identifying physical failure states. Real-world robot operations often present more subtle failures, such as incomplete insertions or unestablished contacts, which are less conspicuous. This highlights a gap in the systematic evaluation of policy-agnostic VLM-based failure detectors across a diverse range of independently collected, real-world-relevant data sources with naturally occurring failures. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a multi-source benchmark and conducting a comprehensive cross-domain evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically evaluate the reliability and cross-domain generalization capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) when tasked with judging the success or failure of robot manipulation attempts.

The motivation for undertaking this work stems from several critical observations within the field of robot learning:

- **Increasing Reliance on Unvalidated VLM Judgments:** Recent robot learning systems are increasingly delegating the task of determining manipulation outcome to VLMs. These VLM judgments are then frequently utilized as if they were ground truth—serving as reinforcement learning rewards, filters for training data, signals for policy ranking, or triggers for robot recovery actions. The authors observe that the reliability of these VLM judgments beyond the specific data on which they were developed remains largely unclear.
- **Absence of Cross-Domain Evaluation:** A significant gap exists in the systematic evaluation of VLM-based failure detectors across diverse data sources. Existing benchmarks for robot failure detection, while some are substantial in size, are typically drawn from single collection efforts. This homogeneity limits their ability to assess a detector's capacity for cross-domain generalization, meaning whether a detector trained or evaluated on one set of conditions can perform reliably under different robots, tasks, environments, or failure types.
- **Limitations of Existing Failure Benchmarks:** Many existing benchmarks construct failures deliberately (e.g., through perturbed demonstrations or automatically generated errors). While valuable for controlled studies, such constructed failures may allow models to infer outcomes by recognizing specific data-generation procedures rather than by understanding genuine physical states. The paper argues that failures encountered in natural robot operation are often more subtle and require a deeper understanding of physical interaction (e.g., a peg not fully inserted, a gripper failing to establish contact). There is a need to evaluate detectors against more "organic" failures.
- **Critical Downstream Impact of Unreliable Judgments:** If VLM judgments are unreliable, they can lead to noisy rewards for reinforcement learning, inaccurate policy evaluations, or the retention of flawed trajectories in training datasets. This directly impedes the development of robust and capable robot learning systems.
- **Need for a Common Evaluation Standard:** There is a recognized need for a common and directly comparable framework to assess the fundamental capability of determining task success from a recording and instruction, across various robots, tasks, and environments. This enables meaningful comparisons between different detector models and methodologies.
- **Diagnosis of Failure Modes and Performance Improvement:** Beyond merely quantifying performance, the authors aim to diagnose *where* and *why* these detectors fail. By analyzing error patterns and correlating them with the type of visual evidence required to determine an outcome, they seek to identify fundamental limitations (e.g., distinguishing coarse object motion from fine-grained physical contact) and propose input-level interventions to enhance reliability.
- **Facilitating Reproducible Research:** A key motivation is to release a public benchmark (FailBench) and an accompanying evaluation harness. This aims to standardize the evaluation process for robot failure detection, enabling reproducible research and facilitating community progress toward more reliable VLM-based robot judges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces FailBench, a new benchmark, and uses it to evaluate a panel of VLM-based detectors through a structured experimental setup.

#### 3.1. FailBench Construction

FailBench comprises 2,197 robot manipulation attempts curated from 14 public sources, including 12 real-world and 2 simulated datasets. Each attempt is represented by a task instruction, visual observations, and a binary outcome label (success or failure) provided by the original data creators.

- **Source Selection Criteria:** Candidate corpora were screened based on three rules: (1) the source had to record a robot completing a manipulation task, (2) it had to provide an existing outcome label (no new labels were generated by the authors), and (3) it needed to cover a task family, robot, or failure type not already well-represented in the benchmark.
- **Failure Origin Categorization:** The sources were grouped by how failures occurred:
 - **Organic (75%):** Failures that happened naturally during policy rollouts or human teleoperation (e.g., ur5fail, phail, roboarena, armnetbench, rh20t, reassemble, simplerenv, robometersim).
 - **Planned (11%):** Failures deliberately introduced by human operators (e.g., botfails, robofac, reflect).
 - **Synthetic (9%):** Failures constructed after recording by modifying successful trajectories (e.g., bdv2fail, roboreward).
 - **Undocumented (5%):** Origin not clearly specified (e.g., robometer).
 The benchmark was designed to be majority-organic, reflecting real-world failure scenarios.
- **Task Scope:** FailBench focuses exclusively on *execution failures*, where the outcome can be judged from the recording. It explicitly excludes planning failures (where the intended action is inappropriate) and intermediate task progress evaluation.
- **Data Representation:** Samples include instructions, visual inputs (video clips or start/end frames from various camera views: external, wrist, overhead, front), and a binary outcome label. Multiple views are provided where available in the original source.
- **Sampling Strategy:** Each source was subsampled to maintain diversity across scenes, tasks, robots, and failure types. Failures and successes were balanced to approximately 50/50 where data permitted. Within each source, sampling prioritized coverage of different failure types. Overall, 54% of the benchmark samples are failures.
- **Verification:** Approximately 25% of the benchmark samples underwent manual inspection to identify and replace any mislabeled or ambiguous samples, ensuring label quality.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup

Thirteen VLM-based detectors were evaluated, categorized into three groups:

- **General-purpose VLMs (6 models):** These models (e.g., Gemini 3 Flash, Gemma-4-31B-it, GPT-4o, Qwen3-VL-Thinking variants) had no specific robotics training and included open- and closed-weight models.
- **Embodied Robot Models (2 models):** These models (e.g., Hy-Embodied-VLM-1.0, HY-Embodied-0.5) were trained for general embodied robot tasks but not specifically for failure detection.
- **Purpose-built Failure Detectors (5 models):** These models (e.g., Guardian, RoboReward-8B, ViFailback-8B, RoboFAC-7B, FailSense-Calvin-3B) were explicitly designed and fine-tuned for robot failure detection.

- **Evaluation Protocol:** Each model was evaluated using its authors' officially recommended prompts, inference settings (e.g., decoding strategy, thinking budget), and input formats. Models supporting video input were provided with video clips; others received 32 evenly spaced still frames.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Balanced accuracy was chosen as the primary metric. This accounts for potential class imbalances within subsets, ensuring that models are not rewarded for simply guessing the more common class. Two aggregate scores were reported: macro average (arithmetic mean of subset scores, weighting each subset equally) and micro average (pooling all samples into a single balanced accuracy).

#### 3.3. Analysis Methods

- **Performance Comparison:** Direct comparison of balanced accuracy scores across models and subsets (real vs. simulated, specific task categories).
- **Model Agreement:** Intersection over Union (IoU) of error sets was calculated for all pairs of detectors to quantify the overlap in misclassified samples, indicating whether models fail on similar or different cases.
- **Error Analysis and Reasoning Inspection:** Qualitative manual inspection of incorrect predictions was conducted, primarily using Gemma-4-31B-it due to its accessible reasoning traces. This involved examining recordings, instructions, and model outputs to infer common patterns in misclassifications and systematic biases (e.g., bias towards success under ambiguity). The length of reasoning traces for correct vs. incorrect predictions was also analyzed.
- **Evidence-Localization Pipeline:** An input-level intervention was developed and tested to improve detection performance.
 - **Components:** A "localizer" VLM (given four frames and task instruction, without outcome) generates a bounding box around the outcome-relevant region. A "detector" VLM then processes the visual input cropped to this region.
 - **Ablations:** Experiments were performed to determine the contribution of the localizer and detector components, and to compare different input transformations (cropping, upscaled cropping, masked blackout) with the original full-frame input. Gemini 3 Flash was used as both the localizer and detector for initial testing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VLMs on FailBench yielded several findings regarding their reliability and underlying limitations in robot task success judgment.

- **Overall Performance Limitations:** The best-performing model among the 13 tested detectors, Gemini 3 Flash, achieved a mean balanced accuracy of 0.77. This score suggests that approximately one in four judgments is incorrect, indicating substantial room for improvement in current VLM capabilities for this task. The second-best model, Gemma-4-31B-it, scored within one percentage point of Gemini 3 Flash.
- **Underperformance of Specialized Detectors:** Counterintuitively, models explicitly fine-tuned for robot failure detection (purpose-built detectors) consistently underperformed most general-purpose VLMs. With the exception of the smallest general-purpose model, all specialized detectors scored lower. Furthermore, five of the specialized detectors that allowed a direct comparison performed worse than their corresponding pre-trained base models, suggesting that current fine-tuning strategies for failure detection may degrade performance outside the specific training data.
- **Impact of Simulation vs. Real Environments:** The ranking of detectors was highly consistent between real and simulated environments (Spearman ρ = 0.95), suggesting that simulated data does not fundamentally alter the problem. However, simulated environments exhibited smaller performance dispersion (variance ≈ 0.0089) compared to real environments (variance ≈ 0.0135). This indicates that performance gaps among detectors are less pronounced in simulated settings, potentially masking true performance differences that become apparent in real-world deployment. Simulated tasks were, on average, 1.3 percentage points easier.
- **Dependence on Visual Evidence Type:** A critical finding was the strong correlation between detection performance and the type of visual evidence required to determine the outcome.
 - **Observable Object Motion:** Detectors demonstrated higher reliability when task success or failure could be inferred from coarse, observable object motion (e.g., whether an object moved to the correct location).
 - **Physical Contact Establishment:** Performance significantly degraded, approaching chance levels, when success depended on discerning whether fine-grained physical contact was established (e.g., a peg fully inserted, a gripper successfully grasping an object). No model exceeded 0.60 balanced accuracy on contact-intensive assembly tasks (represented by the `reassemble` dataset), which had the lowest average performance (0.52). This suggests a fundamental difficulty in interpreting subtle physical interactions from visual input.
- **Systematic Bias in Model Reasoning:** Qualitative analysis of model reasoning traces (from Gemma-4-31B-it) revealed a systematic bias towards predicting success when evidence was ambiguous. Models frequently identified uncertain aspects but still concluded success, treating failure as requiring definitive proof. Instances of hallucination (reporting observations not present in frames) and inability to reconcile conflicting views from multiple cameras were also observed. Analysis indicated that incorrect predictions often received *longer* reasoning traces (1,868 characters on average) compared to correct ones (1,464 characters), suggesting that increased reasoning effort does not necessarily alleviate this bias or lead to accurate conclusions.
- **Limited Error Overlap Among Detectors:** The average Intersection over Union (IoU) of error sets across all 78 pairs of detectors was 0.28. This low overlap suggests that different detectors tend to make errors on distinct sets of samples, indicating potential for performance gains through ensemble methods. However, a majority vote ensemble of the three strongest detectors provided only a marginal improvement (+0.01 balanced accuracy) over the best single model, and did not significantly improve performance on contact-rich assembly tasks (0.55 balanced accuracy).
- **Effectiveness of Input-Level Intervention:** An input-level intervention, the evidence-localization pipeline, was found to be effective. By spatially localizing the outcome-relevant region and cropping the visual input before sending it to the detector, the mean balanced accuracy of the strongest detector (Gemini 3 Flash) improved by 2.3 percentage points (from 0.773 to 0.797). This improvement was most pronounced in tasks where the decisive object was small relative to the overall workspace (e.g., `simplerenv` gained +10.7 points, `roboarena` +7 points). Ablation studies showed that both the quality of localization and the detector's ability to interpret the cropped input contributed to this gain, and that full-resolution cropping performed best among tested input transformations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in this research have several significances and potential impacts on the field of robot learning and evaluation.

- **Quantifying VLM Unreliability for Robot Tasks:** This work provides a rigorous, cross-source quantification of the reliability of current VLM-based systems for judging robot task success. The finding that even the best models achieve only 0.77 mean balanced accuracy highlights a substantial gap between current capabilities and the reliability required for critical applications in robot learning pipelines. This challenges the implicit assumption that VLM judgments can be treated as ground truth in many existing systems.
- **Exposing Cross-Domain Generalization Gaps:** By evaluating detectors across 14 diverse sources, FailBench critically demonstrates the limitations of VLM cross-domain generalization. The consistent underperformance of fine-tuned, specialized detectors compared to general-purpose VLMs, and even their own base models, suggests that current fine-tuning methods may lead to overfitting on specific datasets and fail to generalize robustly. This is a crucial insight for developing models that can be deployed across varied robotic platforms and tasks without extensive re-training or re-evaluation.
- **Identifying Fundamental Perceptual Limitations:** The research identifies a key limitation of current VLMs: their difficulty in discerning fine-grained physical contact states compared to gross object motion. This finding is significant because many real-world manipulation tasks depend on precise contact (e.g., successful insertion, firm grasp). This points to a fundamental perceptual or reasoning deficit in current models, indicating that advancements in understanding physical interaction and state changes are necessary.
- **Guiding Future Model Development:** The diagnosis of failure modes, particularly the bias towards predicting success under ambiguity and the struggle with contact-rich tasks, offers clear directions for future model development. It suggests that models need to be explicitly designed with better reasoning capabilities for physical state and contact relationships. The effectiveness of the input-level evidence-localization pipeline further indicates that enhancing models' ability to focus on outcome-relevant regions, or providing such localized information as input, can improve performance. This could lead to the development of more targeted training objectives and architectural designs.
- **Establishing a New Benchmark for Reproducible Research:** The introduction and public release of FailBench, along with its evaluation harness, provide a standardized, cross-source platform for reproducible evaluation of robot failure detectors. This is crucial for enabling the research community to compare new models and methods against a common, challenging, and diverse dataset, accelerating progress in the field.
- **Informing Deployment Strategies:** The distinction in performance between simulated and real environments, with real environments exhibiting greater performance dispersion, is important for deployment. It suggests that models chosen based solely on simulated performance may not translate effectively to real-world robustness. This finding encourages more rigorous real-world evaluation before deployment.
- **Potential for Multi-modal Integration:** The identified difficulty with contact-level failures implicitly suggests that integrating additional modalities, such as force-torque or audio signals (which are available in some of the source datasets), could potentially bridge the gap in understanding physical interactions. This opens avenues for future multi-modal research in failure detection.
- **Enhancing Robot Autonomy and Trust:** Reliable failure detection is a cornerstone for building truly autonomous and trustworthy robotic systems. By clearly articulating the current limitations and providing tools and insights to address them, this work lays foundational groundwork for developing robots that can more accurately monitor their own performance, learn more effectively from their experiences, and recover gracefully from errors in unstructured environments.
