# Real-Time Anomaly Detection and Reactive Planning with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University and NVIDIA developed AESOP, a framework that integrates large language models for real-time anomaly detection and reactive planning in dynamic robotic systems. The framework uses a two-stage reasoning pipeline and a latency-aware Model Predictive Control approach, enabling agile robots to safely leverage LLM capabilities and achieve 100% recovery in simulated anomaly scenarios.

## Problem

- Autonomous robotic systems face reliability challenges when encountering out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios not covered in training data, potentially leading to safety-critical failures.
- Integrating powerful Foundation Models (FMs) like LLMs into safety-critical robotic systems is hindered by their high computational expense and inference latency, limiting real-time application.
- A rigorous methodology for safely grounding FM judgments into closed-loop control systems, without compromising their zero-shot generalization abilities, has been lacking.

## Method

- The **AESOP** framework employs a ==dual-stage runtime monitor==: a 'fast' embedding-based LLM for rapid anomaly detection and a 'slow' generative LLM for deliberative safety assessment and intervention selection.
- A ==multi-contingency Model Predictive Control (MPC)== strategy simultaneously plans nominal and multiple recovery trajectories, with consensus constraints that 'buy time' for the slow LLM's inference latency.
- The MPC ensures that the robot's immediate actions keep all recovery options feasible during the LLM's deliberation period, committing to a specific recovery plan only after the LLM's decision is returned.

## Results

- The fast embedding-based anomaly detector outperforms generative chain-of-thought reasoning for anomaly detection across synthetic domains, with smaller models (e.g., MPNet) proving sufficient.
- The full AESOP framework achieved **100%** successful recovery in simulated quadrotor delivery scenarios when anomalies occurred, significantly outperforming a naive MPC (**15%**) and a fallback-safe MPC (**23%**).
- Real-world hardware experiments on a quadrotor demonstrated real-time feasibility, with the system operating at approximately **10Hz**, successfully managing the latency of cloud-queried generative LLMs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Model (LLM) embedding spaces can be effectively used for fast, computationally efficient anomaly detection by measuring similarity to cached nominal experiences.
> - Smaller, resource-efficient LLMs (e.g., Sentence Transformer MPNet) can achieve comparable or superior performance for embedding-based anomaly detection compared to much larger models.
> - Generative LLMs, particularly when combined with Chain-of-Thought reasoning, are highly effective for deliberative, context-aware assessment of anomalies and selecting appropriate safety interventions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robotic systems are on the verge of widespread deployment, yet a fundamental challenge persists: their reliability when faced with situations not captured in their finite training datasets. These "out-of-distribution" (OOD) scenarios can lead to unexpected and potentially safety-critical failures. The broader research landscape addresses OOD robustness through two main avenues: strengthening model performance in the face of distributional shifts (e.g., robust training, adaptation) and developing OOD detection algorithms (e.g., ensemble variance, energy scores).

Recent advancements in **Foundation Models (FMs)**, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), have introduced a promising new direction. Trained on internet-scale data, these models exhibit powerful zero-shot generalization capabilities, allowing them to reason about novel situations without explicit prior training for those specific scenarios. This has led to their application in various robotic tasks, from manipulation and navigation to long-horizon planning and defining reinforcement learning reward functions. Importantly, prior work has shown that LLMs can provide a more general mechanism for detecting context-dependent safety hazards, especially those difficult to quantify with predefined metrics, such as a drone needing to avoid landing on a building that is on fire.

However, despite this promise, the adoption of FMs in safety-critical robotic systems has faced two significant hurdles, which this paper directly addresses:
1. **Computational Expense and Latency:** FMs, especially large generative LLMs, are computationally intensive and incur considerable inference latencies. Most existing robotic applications of FMs have therefore been confined to quasi-static or offline settings, where time delays are tolerable. This limits their applicability to agile, dynamic robots (like quadrotors or autonomous vehicles) where real-time reactivity is paramount.
2. **Safe Integration into Control Frameworks:** A rigorous methodology for grounding FM judgments into a closed-loop control system, without compromising their generalist zero-shot abilities, has been lacking. Existing OOD detection methods are often tailored to individual components of an autonomy stack, while a general, holistic approach for system-level safety interventions based on FM reasoning has been elusive.

This work critically advances the field by bridging these gaps. It presents a novel framework that *practically integrates* the zero-shot reasoning capabilities of LLMs into a real-time, reactive control loop for dynamic robotic systems. By proposing a dual-stage reasoning architecture that explicitly accounts for LLM latency within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) strategy, the authors enable agile robots to leverage the general intelligence of FMs for anomaly detection and response without sacrificing safety or real-time performance. This is positioned as the first application of FM embeddings for real-time runtime monitoring, significantly improving the trustworthiness of autonomous systems in unstructured, unpredictable environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enhance the **trustworthiness and robustness of dynamic autonomous robotic systems** when encountering unexpected or out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios in real-world environments, all while operating under stringent resource and time constraints.

To achieve this, the paper identifies two core functions that a runtime monitor for an autonomous system must perform:
1. **Detect Anomalous Conditions:** The system must be able to assess the reliability of its machine learning-enabled components at runtime and identify conditions that deviate from its known, nominal operational experiences. These deviations, termed "anomalies," might not always indicate immediate danger but warrant further scrutiny.
2. **Judiciously Enact Safety-Preserving Interventions:** Upon detecting an anomaly, the system must be capable of reasoning about its potential safety implications and, if necessary, selecting and executing an appropriate safety-preserving course of action or "fallback plan."

The primary motivation for employing **Foundation Models (FMs), specifically Large Language Models (LLMs),** for these objectives stems from their remarkable zero-shot generalization capabilities. Pre-trained on vast internet-scale datasets, LLMs possess a form of "common sense" reasoning that enables them to interpret novel, complex, and semantically rich situations. This makes them uniquely suited to:
- **Identify novel OOD failure modes:** Detect situations that were never explicitly seen or trained for by the robot's existing autonomy stack.
- **Reason holistically about context-dependent safety hazards:** Unlike traditional metrics that might only flag low confidence in a perception system, an LLM can understand the broader context—for instance, distinguishing between a landable helipad and a landable helipad that is *on fire*.

However, the authors explicitly acknowledge and aim to overcome two major challenges that have hindered LLM adoption in safety-critical robotics:
- **Real-time Computational Expense:** The considerable computational cost and inference latency associated with LLMs make them challenging to integrate into online, real-time control loops, especially for agile robots with limited onboard compute.
- **Safe Grounding in Control Frameworks:** There is a lack of rigorous methods to seamlessly incorporate LLM judgments into a robot's control framework in a way that guarantees safety without compromising the LLM's generalist reasoning abilities (e.g., through fine-tuning that might degrade OOD performance).

By addressing these challenges, the research seeks to unlock the full potential of FMs for enhancing the safety and reliability of autonomous robots in complex, unpredictable environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named **AESOP**, tackles the challenge of real-time anomaly detection and reactive planning for agile robotic systems by combining a two-stage LLM-based reasoning pipeline with a multi-contingency Model Predictive Control (MPC) strategy.

**A. Runtime Monitor: Fast and Slow Reasoning**
AESOP partitions the runtime monitoring task into two distinct stages, inspired by dual-process theories of cognition ("Thinking, Fast and Slow"):

1. **Fast Anomaly Detection:**
 - **Purpose:** To rapidly identify observations that deviate from the robot's previously experienced nominal (safe and reliable) conditions. This acts as a quick, intuition-based filter.
 - **Mechanism:** It leverages the contextual embedding space of LLMs.
 - **Process:**
 - **Offline Training/Calibration:** A "cache" of embedding vectors (`D_e`) is pre-computed by passing a dataset of nominal robot observations (`D_nom`) through a pre-trained embedding Foundation Model (e.g., `ϕ`). This implicitly grounds the LLM in the robot's typical operational context.
 - **Online Operation:** At runtime, a new observation (`o_t`) is embedded (`e_t = ϕ(o_t)`). An anomaly score (`s(e_t; D_e)`) is then computed, typically measuring the inverse of cosine similarity between `e_t` and the most similar embedding in `D_e`. Intuitively, if `o_t` is very dissimilar from all prior nominal experiences, its score will be high.
 - **Classification:** A pre-calibrated threshold (`τ`), determined as a quantile of scores from `D_nom`, classifies `o_t` as "nominal" or "anomalous."
 - **Advantage:** This process is computationally efficient, enabling high-frequency operation (e.g., 20Hz), crucial for agile robots. It avoids the much slower autoregressive generation process for initial detection.

2. **Slow Generative Reasoning:**
 - **Purpose:** Once an anomaly is detected by the fast reasoner, this stage methodically assesses the anomaly's safety consequences and determines the appropriate safety-preserving intervention. This is the "deliberative" component.
 - **Mechanism:** It employs a larger, generative LLM (e.g., GPT-4) to perform zero-shot reasoning.
 - **Process:**
 - The robot's visual observation (`o_t`) is first converted into a descriptive text (e.g., using a Vision-Language Model like OWL-ViT).
 - This text description is then formatted into a prompt for the generative LLM, providing context about the robot's task and available fallback strategies (e.g., `X^1_R..., X^d_R`).
 - The LLM generates a reasoned output, which is then parsed to yield a discrete decision `y`: `y=0` for continuing nominal operation (if the anomaly is inconsequential) or `y ∈ {1..., d}` to engage a specific recovery strategy (e.g., landing in a designated safe zone).
 - **Advantage:** Leverages the LLM's vast common-sense knowledge to make sophisticated, context-aware safety decisions for novel OOD scenarios, which traditional rule-based or learned classifiers would struggle with.
 - **Latency Management:** The framework explicitly accounts for the inherent latency (`K` timesteps) of this slower generative reasoning process in the control design.

**B. Planning a Tree of Recovery Trajectories (Model Predictive Control)**
The two-stage reasoning pipeline is tightly integrated with the robot's low-level control through a modified receding horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) strategy (Equation 3). This MPC simultaneously plans:
- A **nominal trajectory** to minimize the primary control objective.
- A set of **`d` recovery trajectories**, each designed to safely steer the robot towards a pre-defined "recovery region" (`X^i_R`) within the planning horizon `T`.

The key innovation of this MPC lies in its **consensus constraints**, specifically designed to handle the LLM's latency (`K`):
1. **Initial Input Consensus:** The first control input (`u^0_{t|t}`) of the nominal plan is enforced to be identical to the first input of all *currently available* recovery plans (`u^i_{t|t}`). This ensures that even during nominal operation, the robot's immediate action keeps all recovery options feasible.
2. **Future Input Consensus (Branching Tree):** For the first `K` timesteps into the future (corresponding to the maximum LLM latency), all recovery trajectories are forced to share the same control inputs (`u^i_{t:t+K|t} = u^j_{t:t+K|t}`). This creates a "tree" of trajectories, where the initial `K` steps form a common trunk.

**Algorithm 1 (AESOP)** orchestrates this:
- When operating nominally or when the slow reasoner deems an anomaly inconsequential, the MPC plans, maintaining feasibility of recovery options.
- Upon detection of an anomaly by the *fast* detector, the robot immediately enters a "fallback-safe state." The *slow* reasoner is triggered asynchronously. During the `K`-timestep latency, the robot executes the common trunk of the recovery plans. This usually involves slowing down or gaining altitude to "buy time" and keep all options open.
- Once the slow reasoner returns its decision (`y`), the robot commits to the corresponding recovery trajectory (if `y ≠ 0`) or smoothly transitions back to the nominal plan (if `y = 0`).

**Theoretical Guarantee:** Theorem 1 proves that if the initial MPC problem is feasible, the closed-loop system maintains state and input constraints, always has at least one safety intervention feasible, and reaches the chosen recovery set within `T+1` timesteps after an anomaly is detected. This robust framework ensures safety by anticipating potential needs for intervention and budgeting time for complex reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors systematically evaluate AESOP across synthetic text-based domains, simulated quadrotor experiments, real-world quadrotor hardware, and autonomous vehicle simulations (CARLA). Their findings robustly support their core hypotheses:

- **H1: Fast Embedding-based Anomaly Detection Performance:** The fast embedding-based anomaly detector significantly outperforms generative chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning with high-capacity LLMs like GPT-4 in detecting anomalies across synthetic domains (Warehouse Manipulator, Autonomous Vehicle, VTOL Aircraft). Performance increases logarithmically with sample size and linearly with concept coverage of nominal experiences (Fig. 2, Fig. 5). This validates the utility of grounding LLM analysis in prior experiences for efficient OOD detection.

- **H2: Small Models Suffice for Embedding-based Detection:** Crucially, embedding-based anomaly detection does not necessitate large, computationally expensive generative models. Smaller models, like Sentence Transformer MPNet (110M parameters), often achieve comparable or superior performance to much larger models (e.g., BERT Large, OpenAI embedding models, and even some 7B Llama 2/Mistral variants) (Fig. 2, Fig. 5, Table VI). This finding is critical for deploying the fast reasoner on resource-constrained robotic platforms.

- **H3: Efficacy of Slow Generative Reasoning for Anomaly Assessment:** Generative reasoning is highly effective at assessing whether a detected anomaly warrants a safety-preserving intervention. Higher-capacity models (e.g., GPT-4) generally outperform smaller ones (Llama 2). Furthermore, chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning consistently yields notable improvements in classification accuracy and reduces false positives (Table I, Table VII), demonstrating the value of explicit reasoning steps for complex safety judgments.

- **H4: Real-time Integration and Safety with Hierarchical Planning:** The full AESOP framework successfully integrates both fast and slow reasoners with the MPC for real-time control of an agile quadrotor.
 - **Simulation:** In simulated quadrotor delivery scenarios, AESOP achieved a 100% successful recovery rate when an anomaly occurred, drastically outperforming a naive MPC (15%) and a fallback-safe MPC (FS-MPC) that did not account for LLM latency (23%) (Table II, Fig. 7). The MPC's consensus constraints effectively "bought time" for the slow reasoner, enabling the robot to slow down and maintain feasible fallback options during the LLM's inference period.
 - **Hardware Demonstration:** Experiments on a physical quadrotor equipped with a Jetson AGX Orin validated the real-time feasibility. The fast anomaly detection components (OWL-ViT for object detection and MPNet for embeddings) ran at 18.8Hz. The overall system, including the MPC solver, comfortably operated at approximately 10Hz (Table III). This demonstrated that the latency of cloud-queried generative LLMs (e.g., GPT-3.5 Turbo at 3.1s) can be safely managed within the control loop.

- **H5: Broader Applicability of Embeddings (Vision and Multi-modal):** The approach extends well to high-dimensional visual observations in semantically rich environments (e.g., CARLA autonomous driving simulations).
 - Both text-based (two-step pipeline with object detection + LM embeddings) and vision-based (single-step pipeline with multi-modal CLIP embeddings or GPT-4V) anomaly detection proved effective.
 - Mistral (7B) with ground-truth scene descriptions achieved competitive accuracy (0.95), often surpassing GPT-4V CoT (Table IV), highlighting the importance of high-fidelity scene descriptions for language-based methods and the benefits of larger embedding models for complex scenes (Fig. 10, Fig. 11).
 - CLIP embeddings showed strong performance (0.90 accuracy), comparable to GPT-4V CoT, suggesting that compute-intensive CoT reasoning may not always be necessary for visually-driven anomaly detection (Table IV). However, the authors nuance this finding by noting that CLIP's performance degrades (higher false positive rate) when trained solely on nominal data from routes *without* anomalies (Table IX), indicating that its embeddings mix visual and semantic features, potentially leading to false positives on purely visually novel but semantically benign observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in the field of safe and robust autonomous robotics, with substantial potential impact:

1. **Enabling Trustworthy Autonomous Systems:** By effectively addressing the challenge of out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, this work brings autonomous robots closer to reliable real-world deployment. It acknowledges that no finite dataset can capture the "limitless variety" of reality and provides a mechanism for robots to detect and intelligently react to unforeseen circumstances, thereby enhancing their trustworthiness in unconstrained environments.

2. **Bridging the Gap for Foundation Models in Robotics:** A major hurdle for integrating powerful Foundation Models like LLMs into robotics has been their high computational cost and inference latency, particularly for agile, dynamic systems. AESOP's hierarchical reasoning architecture (fast embedding-based detection + slow generative reasoning) coupled with its latency-aware Model Predictive Control (MPC) design effectively overcomes this. This demonstration of real-time operation on resource-constrained hardware (e.g., Jetson AGX Orin) is a critical step towards making FM-powered robot autonomy practically feasible.

3. **Novel Dual-Process Reasoning Paradigm:** The "Thinking, Fast and Slow" analogy applied to robotic intelligence offers a compelling new paradigm for designing highly reactive yet deliberative autonomous agents. The ability to quickly flag anomalies and then systematically reason about their implications, while the control system "buys time," is a sophisticated approach to managing uncertainty and ensuring safety.

4. **Enhancing Robot Safety and Resilience:** The guaranteed feasibility of fallback plans provided by the MPC, even during the LLM's reasoning period, ensures closed-loop safety. This is vital for safety-critical applications like drone delivery, urban air mobility, and autonomous driving, where delayed responses can lead to catastrophic failures. The ability to distinguish between consequential and inconsequential anomalies also allows for minimal interference with nominal operation, improving efficiency.

5. **Foundational Contribution to Robot Learning and Control:** The work not only demonstrates the power of LLMs but also provides a principled control framework for integrating such high-level, semantic reasoning into low-level reactive control loops. This lays groundwork for future research at the intersection of AI, machine learning, and control theory for intelligent agents.

**Potential Future Impact:**
- **Widespread Adoption of FMs:** The demonstrated feasibility could accelerate the adoption of FMs across various robotic domains, including manufacturing, logistics, exploration, and service robotics, by providing a blueprint for safe integration.
- **More Robust and Adaptive Behaviors:** Robots could become more adaptive to changing environmental conditions, unforeseen obstacles, or novel task variations, moving beyond brittle, pre-programmed behaviors.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By leveraging zero-shot capabilities, the need for extensive, scenario-specific training data collection and complex rule engineering for rare failure modes might be significantly reduced.

The authors acknowledge several promising avenues for future research, including: methods to further reduce LLM inference latency (e.g., constraining output budget), more rigorous analysis of the correctness of LLM-selected fallback plans, programmatic fallback mechanisms in case of LLM timeout, continual learning to refine the fast reasoner's understanding of non-critical anomalies, and techniques to disentangle visual and semantic features in multi-modal embeddings for improved robustness. These future directions underscore the transformative potential of this research for the next generation of autonomous systems.
