# Universal Manipulation Interface: In-The-Wild Robot Teaching Without In-The-Wild Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) presents a framework for efficiently collecting diverse, 'in-the-wild' human demonstrations using a portable, sensorized gripper. It enables the transfer of complex, dynamic, and bimanual human skills to robotic platforms, achieving zero-shot generalization to novel environments and objects.

## Problem

- Traditional robot teleoperation is costly, requires specialized hardware, and is confined to lab settings, limiting the diversity and scale of data collection for robot learning.
- Learning from passive 'in-the-wild' human videos suffers from a large embodiment gap between humans and robots, lacks explicit action information, and presents observation mismatches.
- Prior hand-held gripper systems for data collection struggle with insufficient visual context, action imprecision, latency discrepancies, and inadequate policy representations for complex or dynamic tasks.

## Method

- A portable, low-cost ==hand-held gripper== with a wrist-mounted GoPro camera, featuring a Fisheye lens and side mirrors for ==implicit stereo==, collects information-rich visual and depth data.
- ==IMU-aware tracking== via Inertial-Monocular SLAM accurately captures 6DoF end-effector poses with absolute scale, and continuous gripper width is tracked via fiducial markers.
- A visuomotor policy using **Diffusion Policy** is trained with ==inference-time latency matching== for observations and actions, alongside a ==relative end-effector trajectory== action representation and relative inter-gripper proprioception for bimanual tasks.

## Results

- **UMI** achieved **20/20** success for cup arrangement on a UR5 and **18/20** on a Franka, demonstrating cross-robot generalization. Dynamic tossing reached **87.5%** success, while bimanual cloth folding and dish washing attained **70%** success rates.
- Policies demonstrated zero-shot generalization to novel environments and unseen objects, achieving **71.7%** combined success in challenging, unseen environments with **75%** success on unseen test cups.
- Data collection throughput was significantly improved, being over **3x faster** than traditional Spacemouse teleoperation for cup arrangement, and enabling dynamic tasks that teleoperation failed to produce.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diverse, 'in-the-wild' human demonstration data, combined with a robust policy representation and pre-trained vision models, is the primary driver for achieving zero-shot generalization in robot manipulation tasks.
> - Careful integration of hardware features like a Fisheye lens and side mirrors for implicit stereo, along with IMU-aware SLAM, is essential for capturing rich visual context and precise 6DoF action information.
> - Software-level considerations such as inference-time latency matching and relative end-effector pose representations are critical for successfully transferring dynamic and bimanual human skills to varied robot platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation is rapidly advancing, with a significant focus on developing methods that allow robots to learn complex skills from human demonstrations. This paper, "Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI)," addresses a critical bottleneck in this landscape: the acquisition of diverse, high-quality human demonstration data that can be effectively transferred to real robots.

Historically, robot learning from demonstration (LfD) has primarily explored two main avenues:
1. **Teleoperated Robot Data:** This involves using expert human operators to control physical robots directly, often in a lab setting. While this approach yields data with minimal "embodiment gap" (i.e., the difference between human and robot physical characteristics) to the target robot, it is inherently expensive, requires specialized hardware and expert knowledge, and limits the diversity of environments and tasks. Traditional teleoperation interfaces (e.g., 3D spacemouse, VR/AR controllers, haptic devices) often suffer from high latency and lack intuitiveness. More recent advancements like ALOHA and GELLO offer intuitive, low-cost teleoperation but still rely on physical robots during data collection, restricting "in-the-wild" data acquisition.
2. **Visual Demonstrations from Human Video:** This approach leverages vast amounts of "in-the-wild" human video data (e.g., YouTube videos) to learn robot policies. It offers unparalleled visual diversity but faces significant challenges: lack of explicit action information (requiring inference via hand pose detectors or combined robot data), a substantial embodiment gap between humans and robots that complicates action transfer, and an observation gap between human video perspectives and robot-mounted cameras.

UMI positions itself as a "promising middle-ground alternative" that aims to bridge these two extremes. It belongs to an emerging class of approaches that use **sensorized hand-held grippers**. These devices attempt to minimize the embodiment gap while offering portability and intuitiveness. However, prior hand-held gripper systems have struggled to balance "action diversity" with "transferability." They often collect data for simple grasping or quasi-static pick-and-place tasks, lacking the precision and robustness for dynamic or complex manipulations. Key limitations of these previous approaches included insufficient visual context (due to narrow FoV wrist cameras), action imprecision (from monocular SfM), latency discrepancies between demonstration and inference, and insufficient policy representations to capture multimodal human actions.

UMI directly tackles these identified limitations. By proposing a carefully designed physical and policy interface, it pushes the boundaries of what hand-held grippers can achieve. It distinguishes itself by enabling the collection of **information-rich data** for challenging bimanual and dynamic manipulation, and by facilitating **deployable, hardware-agnostic policies** that can generalize zero-shot to novel environments and objects. This moves beyond the constrained quasi-static actions of previous hand-held gripper systems and offers a path towards truly scalable and generalizable robot skill acquisition. By combining advances in hardware design (e.g., Fisheye lens, side mirrors, IMU-aware tracking) with sophisticated policy learning techniques (e.g., Diffusion Policy, latency matching, relative trajectory representation), UMI seeks to democratize robot data collection and accelerate the development of versatile robotic manipulation capabilities in real-world, unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) project is to **enable direct skill transfer from "in-the-wild" human demonstrations to deployable robot policies.** The authors recognize that current methods for robot learning from demonstration (LfD) are insufficient for building general-purpose manipulation skills. Traditional teleoperation is costly and confined to labs, while passive human videos suffer from a large embodiment gap and lack explicit action information.

The core motivation behind UMI is to overcome these limitations by developing a framework that is simultaneously:

- **Portable:** The hand-held UMI grippers are designed to be easily transported and set up in any environment with minimal effort, facilitating widespread, decentralized data collection outside of controlled lab settings. This addresses the high setup costs and infrastructure requirements of traditional teleoperation systems.
- **Capable:** UMI aims to capture and transfer complex, natural human manipulation skills that go beyond simple pick-and-place. This includes dynamic actions (like tossing), bimanual coordination, precise manipulations, and long-horizon tasks, which are often difficult or impossible with existing LfD paradigms.
- **Sufficient:** The collected data must be rich enough to enable effective robot policy learning. This means capturing not only visual context but also precise action information, critical depth cues, and subtle adjustments in gripper control, all while minimizing information specific to the human embodiment that would hinder transfer to robots.
- **Reproducible:** The framework is designed to allow researchers and enthusiasts to consistently build UMI grippers and use the collected data to train their own robots, irrespective of the specific robot arm being used. This promotes accessibility and collaborative research.

To achieve these ambitious goals, the authors identify and aim to address four critical issues that have hampered previous hand-held gripper approaches:
1. **Insufficient visual context:** Wrist-mounted cameras, while portable, often lead to occlusions and limited scene coverage. UMI addresses this with a wide Field-of-View (FoV) Fisheye lens and side mirrors for implicit stereo.
2. **Action imprecision:** Methods relying on monocular Structure-from-Motion (SfM) often struggle with scale ambiguity, motion blur, and insufficient texture, leading to imprecise global action recovery. UMI incorporates IMU-aware tracking for robust 6DoF pose estimation with absolute scale.
3. **Latency discrepancies:** The timing mismatch between data collection (zero latency) and inference (sensor, inference, execution latencies) causes out-of-distribution inputs and out-of-sync actions, especially for dynamic tasks. UMI introduces inference-time latency matching.
4. **Insufficient policy representation:** Simple policy representations struggle to capture the complex, multimodal action distributions inherent in human demonstrations. UMI leverages Diffusion Policy and a relative trajectory action representation to model these complexities effectively.

In essence, UMI is motivated by the vision of unlocking new robot manipulation capabilities by making it practical to collect vast, diverse datasets of complex human skills in real-world environments, and then reliably transferring these skills to a variety of robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) framework is meticulously designed with two core components: a physical **Demonstration Interface** for data collection and a sophisticated **Policy Interface** for skill transfer and deployment.

**A. Demonstration Interface Design (Hardware)**

The UMI data collection hardware is a portable, low-cost, and intuitive system centered around a hand-held, trigger-activated 3D-printed parallel-jaw gripper with soft fingers. The **only sensor** and recording device is a **GoPro camera** mounted on the wrist, which can be trivially extended for bimanual tasks. The design focuses on capturing maximum information with minimal setup:

- **HD1. Wrist-mounted cameras as input observation:** This design choice inherently minimizes the observation embodiment gap between human demonstration and robot deployment, as the robot is equipped with an identical setup. It also offers mechanical robustness (no need for camera-robot-world calibration), high portability, and naturally diversifies data during training by providing a moving camera view.
- **HD2. Fisheye Lens for visual context:** A 155-degree Fisheye lens on the GoPro provides an ultra-wide FoV, offering sufficient visual context for diverse tasks and improving SLAM robustness. Crucially, raw Fisheye images are used as input, as rectification can distort peripheral views.
- **HD3. Side mirrors for implicit stereo:** A pair of physical mirrors strategically placed in the camera's peripheral view create "implicit stereo" views within the same image. These mirrors simulate additional virtual cameras, providing critical depth information. Digital reflection of the mirror content is applied to ensure consistent object orientation for policy learning.
- **HD4. IMU-aware tracking:** Leveraging the GoPro's built-in IMU data and a robust Inertial-Monocular SLAM system (based on ORB-SLAM3), UMI accurately captures 6DoF end-effector (EE) poses with absolute scale, even under fast motion or temporary visual occlusions. This is vital for precise actions and bimanual coordination. For bimanual setups, a "map-then-localize" scheme ensures a shared global coordinate system between grippers.
- **HD5. Continuous gripper control:** Unlike binary open/close actions, UMI continuously tracks gripper width via fiducial markers, allowing for fine-grained control over grasping (e.g., precise timing for tossing, implicit force control via soft finger deformation).
- **HD6. Kinematic-based data filtering:** While data collection is robot-agnostic, trajectories are filtered based on the known kinematics of the target robot, ensuring the learned policies are feasible within the robot's physical constraints.

**B. Policy Interface Design (Software/Learning)**

The UMI framework trains a visuomotor policy using **Diffusion Policy**, chosen for its ability to model complex multimodal action distributions. The policy takes a sequence of synchronized observations (RGB image, relative EE pose, gripper width) and outputs a sequence of desired relative EE poses and gripper widths. Key design principles here ensure hardware-agnosticism and effective skill transfer:

- **PD1. Inference-time latency matching:** This addresses critical timing discrepancies between training (zero latency in demonstration) and real-world robot deployment (variable sensor, inference, and execution latencies).
 - **PD1.1) Observation latency matching:** Individually measured latencies for each observation stream (camera, EE pose, gripper width) are used to align all observations to the slowest stream (typically the camera) at inference time via temporal downsampling and linear interpolation.
 - **PD1.2) Action latency matching:** Robot and gripper execution latencies are measured. Action commands are then sent "ahead of time" to ensure the robot reaches the desired pose at the predicted time, compensating for these delays. Outdated predicted actions are discarded.
- **PD2. Relative end-effector pose:** To make the policy independent of specific robot embodiments or base placements, all EE poses are represented relative to the gripper's current EE pose.
 - **PD2.1) Relative EE trajectory as action representation:** Actions are represented as a sequence of SE(3) transforms relative to the initial EE pose for each inference step. This provides robustness against tracking errors and camera displacements, outperforming absolute and delta action representations.
 - **PD2.2) Relative EE trajectory as proprioception:** Historical EE poses are also represented as relative trajectories, providing velocity information and making the system calibration-free (e.g., moving the robot base during execution does not affect performance).
 - **PD2.3) Relative inter-gripper proprioception:** For bimanual tasks, the relative pose between the two grippers is provided as proprioception. This is critical for robust bimanual coordination, especially when visual overlap between cameras is limited, enabled by the shared global coordinate system from the SLAM mapping.

This comprehensive methodology, integrating robust hardware design with intelligent policy interface considerations, allows UMI to collect diverse, high-quality human manipulation data and translate it into versatile, deployable robot skills.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The UMI framework was evaluated extensively across three key aspects: Capability, Generalization, and Data Collection Efficiency, demonstrating its versatility and effectiveness.

#### 4.1. Capability Experiments

UMI demonstrates the ability to capture and transfer complex, dynamic, bimanual, and long-horizon manipulation skills.

- **Cup Arrangement:**
 - **Success:** Achieved 20/20 success on a UR5 robot and 18/20 (90%) on a Franka Emika FR2 robot, showcasing strong cross-robot generalization.
 - **Ablations:**
 - Without Fisheye lens (narrow FoV): Success rate dropped significantly to 11/20 (55%), highlighting the importance of visual context.
 - Absolute action representation: Performed poorly at 5/20 (25%), underscoring the challenge of precise global calibration and the robustness of relative trajectory. Delta action achieved 16/20 (80%).
 - Side mirrors: Crucially, *digitally reflecting and swapping* mirror images was necessary for optimal performance (20/20). Without this processing, performance decreased (85-90%), indicating confusion from opposite motions in mirrored views.
- **Dynamic Tossing:**
 - **Success:** Achieved 105/120 (87.5%) success in sorting objects into bins placed beyond robot reach, showcasing fluid, rapid motions and precise hand-eye coordination.
 - **Ablation (No Latency Matching):** Success rate dropped to 69/120 (57.5%). This confirms that inference-time latency matching is critical for dynamic tasks, preventing jittery motions and suboptimal object release timing.
- **Bimanual Cloth Folding:**
 - **Success:** Achieved 14/20 (70%) success on a complex multi-step folding task, demonstrating tight two-arm coordination for deformable objects.
 - **Ablation (No Relative Inter-gripper Proprioception):** Success rate plummeted to 6/20 (30%). This highlights the necessity of providing relative pose information between the two grippers for synchronized bimanual actions, preventing failures like asynchronous grasps.
- **Dish Washing:**
 - **Success:** Achieved 14/20 (70%) success on an ultra-long-horizon, 7-step task involving fluid manipulation, deformable tools, and articulated objects. The policy was robust to added ketchup and different sauce types, demonstrating semantic understanding of "cleanliness."
 - **Ablation (No CLIP-pretrained ViT):** A ResNet-34 trained from scratch achieved 0% success, indicating that large pre-trained vision models (like CLIP ViT) are essential for visually complex tasks and learning reactive behaviors.

#### 4.2. In-the-Wild Generalization Experiments

UMI policies demonstrated zero-shot generalization to novel environments and objects, a rare achievement for behavior cloning frameworks.

- **Dataset:** 1400 demonstrations collected by 3 human demonstrators across 30 diverse physical locations (homes, offices, restaurants, outdoors) using 15 different espresso cups. A CLIP-pretrained ViT-L/14 was used as the vision encoder.
- **Performance:** In two *unseen* and challenging environments (a busy cafe table and a black, flowing water fountain), UMI achieved a combined success rate of 43/60 (71.7%). This included 28/40 (70%) success on training cups and an impressive 15/20 (75%) success on *unseen* testing cups (different color/texture).
- **Ablation (No In-the-Wild Data):** A model trained only on narrow-domain lab data (even with the same pretrained ViT backbone) achieved 0% success in the unseen environments, proving that the **diversity of in-the-wild data is the primary driver of generalization**, not just the large pre-trained vision backbone.

#### 4.3. Data Collection Throughput and Accuracy

- **Throughput:** UMI significantly improves data collection efficiency compared to traditional robot teleoperation.
 - For the cup arrangement task, UMI was >3x faster than Spacemouse-based teleoperation and 48% as fast as bare human hand demonstration.
 - For dynamic tossing, UMI achieved 64% the speed of human hand, while teleoperation *failed to produce any successful demonstrations* in 15 minutes.
- **SLAM Accuracy:** The SLAM-based tracking system showed high accuracy, critical for precise manipulation:
 - Mean Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE): 6.1mm for position and 3.5° for rotation.
 - Mean Relative Pose Error (RPE) between two grippers: 10.1mm for position and 0.8° for rotation.

These results collectively validate UMI's ability to facilitate the learning of highly capable, generalizable, and complex robot manipulation skills from easily collected human demonstrations, representing a significant advancement in data-driven robotics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) framework represents a significant step forward in robot learning from demonstration, addressing long-standing challenges in data acquisition and skill transfer. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

1. **Unlocking New Robotic Capabilities:** UMI enables robots to learn and perform complex manipulation tasks that were previously difficult or impossible with traditional methods. This includes dynamic actions like tossing, precise fine-grained control, intricate bimanual coordination, and multi-step, long-horizon tasks such as washing a dirty dish. By capturing the fluidity and nuance of human motion, UMI opens doors for robots to tackle a wider array of real-world problems.

2. **Achieving Unprecedented Generalization:** A standout achievement of UMI is its demonstration of zero-shot generalization to novel environments and unseen objects. This is a critical breakthrough for behavior cloning, which typically struggles with out-of-distribution scenarios. The success is attributed to UMI's ability to facilitate the collection of truly diverse, "in-the-wild" human demonstration data, combined with robust policy representations and pre-trained vision models. This generalization capability means learned policies are more adaptable and deployable in unstructured, dynamic real-world settings (e.g., homes, offices, outdoor environments), reducing the need for costly environment-specific fine-tuning.

3. **Democratization and Accessibility of Robot Data Collection:** UMI lowers the barrier to entry for collecting high-quality robot training data. The hardware is portable, low-cost (BoM cost of $73 for the 3D-printed gripper, plus $298 for a GoPro), and intuitive to use, requiring minimal setup time. This contrasts sharply with expensive, complex teleoperation rigs that require expert operators and dedicated lab space. The open-sourcing of UMI's hardware and software is a crucial step towards fostering a "vast, diverse, and decentralized dataset to emerge from the robotics community," enabling a broader range of researchers and even non-experts to contribute to robot learning datasets.

4. **Advancing the State of Imitation Learning:** UMI systematically addresses critical issues identified in prior hand-held gripper approaches, such as insufficient visual context, action imprecision, latency discrepancies, and inadequate policy representations. By integrating a wide-FoV Fisheye lens, side mirrors for implicit stereo, IMU-aware SLAM, continuous gripper control, and sophisticated latency matching with Diffusion Policy and relative action representations, UMI significantly improves the robustness and transferability of learned skills. This methodological rigor provides valuable lessons for future developments in imitation learning.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robot Development:** By making data collection more efficient and accessible, UMI can significantly accelerate the research and development cycles for robot manipulation, fostering rapid progress in the field.
- **Wider Adoption of Robotics:** More capable and adaptable robots, trainable by diverse human demonstrations, could lead to broader adoption in various sectors, from manufacturing and logistics to healthcare and domestic assistance.
- **Foundation for Large-Scale Datasets:** UMI's design facilitates the creation of large-scale, diverse datasets, akin to those that have revolutionized other areas of AI (e.g., ImageNet for computer vision, large text corpora for NLP). Such datasets are essential for training the next generation of highly competent and general-purpose robotic agents.

**Limitations and Future Work (as acknowledged by the authors):**

Despite its strengths, UMI has acknowledged limitations that point to important future research directions:
- **Kinematic Feasibility:** Currently relies on data filtering to ensure learned policies are within the target robot's kinematic limits. Future work could explore embodiment-aware policy learning frameworks that inherently handle hardware constraints.
- **SLAM Robustness:** The SLAM-based action recovery system requires sufficient visual texture in the environment. Future work could integrate static third-person-view cameras or additional fiducial markers to improve robustness in texture-deficient areas.
- **Data Collection Efficiency vs. Human Hand:** While faster than teleoperation, UMI is still less efficient than bare human hand demonstrations due to gripper weight/bulkiness and reduced degrees of freedom. Future improvements in mechanical design, ergonomics, and potentially more dexterous robotic hands could bridge this gap further.

In conclusion, UMI is more than just a new robot gripper; it's a holistic framework designed to fundamentally change how robots learn complex manipulation skills. By making "in-the-wild" human demonstrations directly transferable and generalizable to diverse robotic platforms, UMI lays crucial groundwork for the future of adaptable, general-purpose robots.
