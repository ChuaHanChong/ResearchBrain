# Reevaluating Policy Gradient Methods for Imperfect-Information Games

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers re-evaluated policy gradient methods for imperfect-information games, demonstrating that appropriately tuned generic algorithms like PPO and PPG achieve competitive or superior performance compared to specialized game-theoretic DRL approaches. The study also introduced new benchmarks for exact exploitability computation in large-scale imperfect-information games.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for imperfect-information games (IIGs) primarily adapted classical tabular game-theoretic algorithms (e.g., Fictitious Play, Double Oracle, CFR), leading to computationally expensive and slow convergence issues in DRL contexts.
- Generic policy gradient (PG) methods were widely considered unsuitable for adversarial IIGs due to perceived failures and cyclical best-response dynamics, often serving as weak baselines in prior research.
- A lack of accessible, large-scale benchmarks with exact exploitability computations hindered rigorous evaluation and comparison of DRL algorithms in IIGs, forcing reliance on small benchmarks or approximate metrics.

## Method

- The study developed and released `**exp-a-spiel**`, a Python package enabling efficient and exact ==exploitability== computations for five new large-scale imperfect-information games, providing robust evaluation benchmarks.
- A diverse set of seven DRL algorithms, including generic policy gradient methods (PPO, PPG, MMD) and game-theoretic adaptations (NFSP, PSRO, ESCHER), were selected and implemented for comprehensive evaluation.
- A two-stage experimental design, involving an extensive hyperparameter tuning phase (over 7,000 runs, 345,000 CPU hours) followed by an unbiased evaluation phase with fresh random seeds, was executed to rigorously test the Policy Gradient Hypothesis.

## Results

- Generic policy gradient methods (MMD, PPO, PPG) consistently achieved the lowest exploitability values and demonstrated strong head-to-head performance across all five large-scale imperfect-information games tested.
- Specialized game-theoretic DRL algorithms, including ESCHER (CFR-based) and PSRO (DO-based), generally showed poor exploitability and were largely uncompetitive compared to the generic policy gradient methods.
- The optimal entropy coefficient for generic policy gradient methods in IIGs was found to be between **0.05** and **0.2**, a range notably higher than typical default values, indicating that previous underperformance may have stemmed from suboptimal entropy regularization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Appropriately tuned generic policy gradient methods (MMD, PPO, PPG) are competitive with or superior to model-free deep reinforcement learning approaches based on fictitious play, double oracle, or counterfactual regret minimization in two-player zero-sum imperfect-information games.
> - The effectiveness of generic policy gradient methods in IIGs is significantly influenced by hyperparameter tuning, with optimal performance observed for entropy coefficients in a higher range (0.05 to 0.2) than typically used in single-agent DRL.
> - Exact exploitability can be efficiently computed for large imperfect-information games using sequence-form representations and optimized dynamic programming, providing a robust metric for evaluating DRL algorithms in these settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for imperfect-information games (IIGs) has largely focused on adapting classical tabular game-theoretic algorithms to DRL contexts. This approach was driven by the observation that naive self-play DRL methods often failed in adversarial IIGs due to the cyclical best-response dynamics inherent in such settings, leading to highly exploitable policies. Consequently, much of the existing literature has developed DRL algorithms based on principles such as Fictitious Play (FP), Double Oracle (DO), and Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR), which possess established equilibrium convergence guarantees in tabular settings.

However, the adaptation of these tabular algorithms to DRL has presented challenges. FP and DO variants typically require computationally expensive best-response computations at each iteration, often involving solving an entire reinforcement learning problem, and can exhibit slow convergence rates. CFR-based DRL methods, while offering faster tabular convergence, often necessitate importance sampling over trajectories, which can introduce high variance feedback that is problematic for function approximators. Additionally, none of these methods guarantee last-iterate convergence, complicating policy extraction.

A recent development has been the exploration of regularization-based policy gradient (PG) methods. Specifically, the Magnetic Mirror Descent (MMD) algorithm demonstrated competitive performance with CFR in tabular settings and exhibited promising results as a DRL algorithm for IIGs. The current work builds upon this observation by noting the functional similarities between MMD and other generic deep PG methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Phasic Policy Gradient (PPG). Both MMD and these generic PG methods aim to maximize expected value, regularize policies, and control update sizes, albeit through different mechanisms (e.g., reverse KL divergence in MMD versus gradient clipping and forward KL divergence in PPO).

This paper positions itself as a re-evaluation of the long-held belief that generic PG methods are inherently unsuitable for IIGs, often serving merely as underperforming baselines in prior research. It hypothesizes that with appropriate tuning, these simpler, general-purpose DRL algorithms may be more effective than previously assumed, potentially challenging the dominant paradigm of adapting complex game-theoretic algorithms. A critical enabling factor for this re-evaluation is the lack of accessible, large-scale benchmarks with exact exploitability computations, which this work addresses by introducing new tools and datasets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to test the **Policy Gradient Hypothesis**, which states: "Appropriately tuned policy gradient methods that share an ethos with magnetic mirror descent are competitive with or superior to model-free deep reinforcement learning approaches based on fictitious play, double oracle, or counterfactual regret minimization in two-player zero-sum imperfect-information games."

This hypothesis is motivated by several considerations:

- **Empirical Success of MMD:** The recent observation that Magnetic Mirror Descent (MMD), a policy gradient method, achieved competitive performance with sophisticated CFR algorithms in IIGs suggests that other generic policy gradient methods, which share similar core mechanisms (maximizing expected value, policy regularization, and update size control), might also be effective.
- **Re-evaluating Perceived Failures:** Generic policy gradient methods, particularly PPO, have frequently been dismissed in the literature on DRL for IIGs, often appearing only as weak baselines. The authors propose that this perceived failure may not stem from inherent limitations of these methods but rather from factors such as:
 - **Inadequate Hyperparameter Tuning:** The expense and difficulty of thorough tuning, coupled with potential confirmation bias against generic baselines, might have prevented researchers from discovering optimal configurations.
 - **Reliance on Head-to-Head Metrics:** Head-to-head evaluations, often used in large games, are problematic due to the intransitive dynamics of IIGs, making true comparative strength difficult to ascertain and tuning more complex.
 - **Suboptimal Entropy Regularization:** The specific hyperparameter regime where MMD was effective involved higher entropy regularization than typically applied in single-agent PG methods, suggesting that effective configurations for IIGs might have been overlooked if tuning was restricted to conventional ranges.
- **Need for Robust Benchmarks:** A significant impediment to rigorous evaluation in DRL for IIGs is the absence of broadly accessible and adequately sized benchmarks that allow for exact exploitability computations. Existing benchmarks are either too small to be representative of real-world DRL problems or rely on approximate or head-to-head metrics, which are less definitive for assessing Nash equilibrium convergence. Addressing this gap is a critical objective to enable reliable comparative studies.

The confirmation of the Policy Gradient Hypothesis could have substantial implications for both research and practice. For researchers, it would necessitate a re-evaluation of fundamental assumptions about DRL in IIGs and potentially shift focus towards simpler, more generalizable algorithms. For practitioners, it could simplify DRL implementations and lead to performance improvements in various application domains, ranging from autonomous systems to cybersecurity and game AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted methodology encompassing benchmark development, algorithm selection, and a two-stage experimental design.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Development and Exploitability Computation

To address the lack of suitable evaluation metrics, the authors implemented exact exploitability computations for five "large" imperfect-information games:
1. **2D5F Liar's Dice (LD2D5F)**
2. **3x3 Dark Hex (DH3)**
3. **Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe (PTTT)**
4. **3x3 Abrupt Dark Hex (ADH3)**
5. **Abrupt Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe (APTTT)**

These games were selected for their compatibility with OpenSpiel (a standard DRL library for games), existing use in head-to-head evaluations, and their scale, possessing millions of information states, making them considerably larger than common benchmarks like Leduc Hold'em while remaining tractable for exact exploitability calculations.

The exploitability computation relies on:
- **Sequence-form representations:** These are approximately three orders of magnitude more compact than explicit game trees, enabling computation on commodity hardware.
- **Dynamic payoff matrix generation:** This avoids explicit storage of large matrices.
- **Optimization for specific games:**
 - For LD2D5F, dynamic programming is used to calculate payoffs at public states in linear time, leveraging the public nature of actions and root-located chance outcomes.
 - For the 3x3 games, computation is distributed across multiple threads after the first two moves, using 18 buffers to manage concurrency conflicts across the 81 possible opening sequences.
- **Release of `exp-a-spiel`:** A Python package with OpenSpiel-compatible bindings is released to facilitate community use of these benchmarks.

#### 3.2. Algorithm Selection and Implementation

Seven DRL algorithms were selected to represent different classes of approaches for IIGs:
- **FP-based:** NFSP (Heinrich and Silver, 2016)
- **DO-based:** PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017)
- **CFR-based:** ESCHER (McAleer et al., 2023)
- **Generic PG methods:** PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), PPG (Cobbe et al., 2021), and MMD (Sokota et al., 2023)
- **Non-standard PG method:** R-NaD (Perolat et al., 2022)

Implementations were primarily sourced from OpenSpiel, CleanRL, or the original ESCHER repository to minimize implementation errors. Modifications included moving NFSP to PyTorch, enabling multi-agent self-play for PPO and PPG, and implementing MMD as a PPO modification. Correctness of these modifications was corroborated by reproducing known results for NFSP on Leduc Hold'em and MMD on ADH3 and PTTT.

#### 3.3. Experimental Design

The experiments comprised two main launches:

1. **Hyperparameter Tuning Launch:**
 - **Scope:** All 5 games, 7 algorithms, 50 distinct hyperparameter configurations per algorithm/game pair, each run with 3 random seeds. This totaled over 7,000 runs and required more than 345,000 CPU hours.
 - **Hyperparameter Search:** Real-valued hyperparameters were varied by multiplying their default values by powers of 2 (2^N, where N is uniformly sampled from {-3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3}). Hyperparameters upper-bounded by 1 were exponentiated to the power of 2^N.
 - **Standardization:** A consistent neural network architecture (3-layer, 512-hidden unit fully connected network) and the Adam optimizer were used across all algorithms. The entropy coefficient for generic PG methods was initially set based on MMD's effective regime, but was part of the sweep for PPO/PPG/MMD.
 - **Duration:** All runs were trained for 10 million environment steps.

2. **Evaluation Launch:**
 - **Selection:** For each game and algorithm, the 5 hyperparameter configurations yielding the lowest average final exploitability from the tuning launch were selected.
 - **Reruns:** These configurations were then re-run with 10 fresh random seeds each for 10 million steps to provide a maximization-bias-free evaluation.
 - **Metrics:** Final exploitability and head-to-head performance were measured. For head-to-head comparisons, the policy with the lower median final exploitability over the 10 seeds was selected, along with an approximate Nash equilibrium policy from discounted CFR, and compared against other selected policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results primarily support the Policy Gradient Hypothesis.

#### 4.1. Exploitability Performance

- **Generic Policy Gradient Methods (MMD, PPO, PPG):** These methods exhibited the strongest performance across the board, consistently achieving the lowest exploitability values. They demonstrated comparable performance to each other, suggesting that the specific type of KL divergence or regularization strategy (e.g., reverse KL in MMD vs. clipping in PPO) might be less critical in deep learning settings than the general principles of value maximization, policy regularization, and update size control.
- **ESCHER (CFR-based):** ESCHER was consistently uncompetitive, failing to achieve low exploitability across the tested games.
- **PSRO (DO-based):** PSRO largely underperformed, showing poor results in ADH3, PTTT, and APTTT. While it achieved sporadic success in DH3 (some configurations found Nash equilibria on certain seeds), its overall performance was inconsistent and generally inferior.
- **NFSP (FP-based) and R-NaD (Non-standard PG):** These algorithms occasionally approached or matched the performance of generic PG methods in specific cases, but more frequently exhibited lower performance compared to MMD, PPO, and PPG.

#### 4.2. Head-to-Head Comparisons

- The head-to-head evaluation results corroborated the exploitability findings. Generic PG methods (MMD, PPO, PPG) generally matched or outperformed the other algorithms. Within the group of generic PGs, they showed rough parity.
- R-NaD's performance in head-to-head scenarios varied, sometimes competing effectively with generic PGs and other times falling short.
- NFSP, ESCHER, and PSRO were generally uncompetitive in head-to-head matchups against the generic PG methods.

#### 4.3. Entropy Coefficient Analysis (Supplementary)

An important subsidiary finding, derived from the hyperparameter tuning data, concerned the optimal range for the entropy coefficient in policy gradient methods. The study found that entropy coefficients between 0.05 and 0.2 yielded the best average performance for generic PG methods. This range is notably higher than the default entropy coefficient values (typically 0 to 0.01) often used in popular DRL libraries for single-agent problems. This observation suggests that previous underperformance of generic PG methods in IIGs might indeed have been attributable to the use of suboptimal (too low) entropy regularization, as hypothesized.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several implications for the field of deep reinforcement learning in imperfect-information games:

- **Challenging Established Beliefs:** The primary significance lies in providing empirical evidence that challenges the prevailing assumption that generic deep policy gradient methods are ineffective for adversarial IIGs. The results suggest that, with proper tuning, simpler PG algorithms can be competitive with or superior to more complex, game-theory-specific DRL approaches derived from fictitious play, double oracle, and counterfactual regret minimization.
- **Simplification of DRL Architectures:** If generic PG methods prove widely effective, it could lead to a simplification of DRL algorithm design for IIGs. This might reduce the need for specialized, game-theory-informed adaptations, potentially resulting in cleaner implementations and a lower barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners.
- **Broader Applicability:** The findings could encourage a re-evaluation of generic PG methods across a wider range of game-theoretic settings, potentially unifying approaches for DRL in games. This aligns with a growing body of evidence, including work on cooperative IIGs, suggesting the possibility of a "universal" PG algorithm for diverse game types.
- **Enhanced Research Rigor:** The development and release of the `exp-a-spiel` package, offering efficient and exact exploitability computations for a set of large, representative IIGs, addresses a significant gap in the community. This resource is poised to enable more rigorous, standardized, and comparable evaluations of DRL algorithms in IIGs, moving beyond the limitations of small benchmarks or intransitive head-to-head metrics.
- **Impact on Applications:** Improved performance and simpler implementation of DRL algorithms could accelerate advancements in real-world applications that involve adversarial imperfect information, such as autonomous driving, network security, robot combat, and language model alignment. The work encourages practitioners to consider well-tuned generic PG methods as viable, and potentially superior, options for these domains.
- **Hyperparameter Awareness:** The specific finding regarding the importance of higher entropy coefficients for PG methods in IIGs provides concrete guidance for future research and practical deployment, highlighting that optimal hyperparameter regimes for multi-agent adversarial settings may differ substantially from single-agent contexts.
