# Idiobionics: The Unification of Privacy and Intelligent Robotic Prostheses

> [!summary] Summary
> This research defines "idiobionics" as an interdisciplinary field to investigate privacy in advanced robotic prostheses, empirically demonstrating that sensor data from these devices can be used to infer sensitive user activities with an average accuracy of 83%. The work advocates for a privacy-by-design approach to ensure user trust and accelerate the adoption of next-generation bionic limbs.

## Problem

- Existing research on privacy in the Internet of Bodies (IoB) has not adequately addressed the unique privacy risks associated with intelligent robotic prostheses, which are medical devices deeply integrated with the human body.
- The advanced sensing and AI capabilities in modern bionic limbs, while enhancing functionality, also introduce new "threat vectors" that could be exploited to infer sensitive user information.
- A lack of trust due to potential privacy violations could hinder the widespread adoption of innovative bionic limbs, limiting their therapeutic and functional benefits for users.

## Method

- The authors formally introduce and define **"idiobionics"** as a new interdisciplinary research domain dedicated to enhancing privacy in autonomous and adaptive bionic limbs.
- An empirical study demonstrates the feasibility of ==Activity Inference Attacks (AIA)== by using accelerometer data from forearm-mounted sensors to infer user activities with machine learning.
- The work advocates for a proactive "privacy-by-design" philosophy, integrating privacy protections from the initial development stages of bionic prostheses.

## Results

- A supervised learning approach achieved an average attack accuracy of **83%** in inferring user activities (walking, jogging, sitting, standing) from accelerometer data, with individual participant accuracies ranging from **57% to 96%**.
- Unsupervised clustering methods successfully separated different activities in a feature space, with Gaussian Mixture Models performing best, demonstrating the inherent separability of activities from bionic limb sensor data.
- Adversary interpretation was shown feasible by demonstrating similar clustering patterns from consumer-grade smartphone data compared to clinical-grade sensor data, suggesting an adversary could interpret bionic limb activity without ground truth access.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sensor data from bionic limbs, even from common accelerometers, can readily reveal sensitive user information such as activity patterns, posing a concrete privacy threat.
> - The unique characteristics of bionic limbs (medical device classification, high cost, non-replaceability) necessitate a dedicated privacy research focus distinct from other consumer IoB devices.
> - Addressing privacy concerns through a systematic field like idiobionics is crucial for fostering user trust, accelerating technological progress, and broadening the adoption of advanced bionic technologies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is positioned within the evolving landscape of technologies that tightly couple biological and digital systems, commonly referred to as the Internet of Bodies (IoB). The IoB encompasses a wide array of devices, from implantable medical devices to popular external wearables such as fitness trackers. Robotic prostheses, or bionic limbs, represent a specific and increasingly sophisticated example of IoB devices, designed for long-term daily use by individuals with limb differences.

Contemporary bionic limbs are integrating advanced sensors and artificial intelligence (AI)-based control approaches, rendering them more perceptive, responsive, autonomous, and adaptive. While these advancements promise significant functional benefits, the paper identifies a critical gap in the existing research: a lack of comprehensive investigation into the privacy risks specifically associated with these intelligent bionic limbs. Prior research has addressed privacy concerns in more common IoB devices like smartwatches and fitness trackers. However, bionic limbs possess unique characteristics, being classified as medical devices that are often persistently and sometimes invasively affixed to the human body. Their high cost, customization, limited commercial alternatives, and clinical/regulatory considerations often make them non-replaceable, unlike consumer electronic devices.

The paper argues that this distinction necessitates a dedicated line of inquiry to understand and address privacy vulnerabilities inherent in bionic limb designs. By introducing the concept of "idiobionics," this work seeks to establish a framework for holistically investigating these issues, advocating for a proactive "privacy-by-design" approach for future prosthetic technologies. This positions the research as foundational in bridging the gap between technological innovation in bionics and the critical need for user privacy and trust, aiming to unlock the full potential of these devices by mitigating adoption barriers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that the very technological advancements enhancing the capabilities of robotic prostheses also introduce significant privacy vulnerabilities. While AI and advanced sensing enable bionic limbs to be more adaptive and functional, they simultaneously create potential "threat vectors" that could be exploited by malicious entities. These exploits could lead to unauthorized deductions of sensitive user information, such as daily activities, demographic attributes, or health status, thereby violating personal privacy.

The authors contend that such privacy violations could have serious adverse consequences for users, including discriminatory practices (e.g., higher insurance premiums based on inferred health risks), targeted physical crimes (e.g., burglaries based on inferred routines), and broader erosion of trust in adaptive technologies. This lack of trust is a known barrier to the widespread adoption of innovative devices, ultimately hindering users from fully realizing the functional benefits promised by next-generation bionic limbs.

Given the unique nature of bionic limbs as medical devices that are deeply integrated and difficult to replace, the authors emphasize that existing privacy discussions around other IoB devices are insufficient. There is an urgent need for proactive measures to address these specific privacy risks from the outset of design and development.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the paper are detailed as follows:

- **Formal Definition of Idiobionics:** To introduce and formally define "idiobionics" as a new, interdisciplinary field of research. This field is dedicated to comprehensively investigating and enhancing privacy in autonomous, semiautonomous, and adaptive bionic limbs.
- **Grounding in Literature:** To contextualize idiobionics within relevant existing literature on IoB privacy and the technological advancements in robotic prostheses.
- **Empirical Demonstration of Privacy Risks:** To provide preliminary empirical evidence demonstrating the feasibility of specific adversarial attacks that could exploit intelligent bionic limb designs. This involves showcasing how data from common sensors can be used to infer sensitive user information.
- **Discussion of Implications and Risks:** To discuss the broader implications of these findings, outlining various potential privacy risks that could arise from unrestricted access to bionic limb sensor data.
- **Articulation of Open Research Questions:** To contribute a curated list of open research questions within idiobionics. These questions are intended to guide future technical and socio-technical investigations for researchers in wearable robotics and other human-facing autonomous systems.
- **Promotion of Trust and Adoption:** Ultimately, to foster an environment where users can trust and confidently adopt advanced bionic limbs, thereby accelerating technological progress and commercialization by preemptively addressing privacy concerns and promoting a privacy-by-design philosophy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a two-pronged approach: first, formally defining a new research domain, "idiobionics," and second, providing empirical evidence to substantiate the necessity of this new field by demonstrating a privacy threat vector.

#### 3.1. Defining Idiobionics

The paper defines idiobionics as an interdisciplinary research domain focused on enhancing privacy in autonomous, semiautonomous, or adaptive bionic limbs. It is posited as a means to accelerate progress in intelligent bionic devices and facilitate commercialization by preemptively addressing privacy concerns. The approach for idiobionics research is described as involving:
- **Technical Assessments:** Identification of vulnerabilities and threat vectors within bionic limbs that could lead to privacy violations, enabling proactive mitigation.
- **Integrated Research:** Extensive qualitative and quantitative research engaging all relevant stakeholders, including end-users, to understand privacy perceptions and requirements.

#### 3.2. Empirical Evidence: Activity Inference Attack (AIA)

As a first step within idiobionics research, the paper presents preliminary experimentation to demonstrate the feasibility of an Activity Inference Attack (AIA) on upper-limb bionic devices, specifically in a context congruent with transradial bionic limb use.

##### 3.2.1. Data Collection

- **Sensor:** A tri-axial accelerometer, integrated within a clinical-grade electromyography (EMG) sensor, was used to capture movement data along three axes in 3D space.
- **Participants:** Twelve individuals, aged between eighteen and sixty, self-selected, provided informed consent, and reported no physical limitations.
- **Placement:** The accelerometer was affixed to each participant's inner forearm, specifically above the wrist flexor muscle belly, just below the elbow. This placement simulates a sensor location on an upper-limb bionic prosthesis.
- **Sampling Rate:** Data was recorded at 148 Hz.
- **Activities:** Participants performed four controlled activities: walking, jogging in place, sitting, and standing. Each activity lasted thirty seconds and was performed sequentially within a single laboratory session, with separate data recorded for each.

##### 3.2.2. Analytical Methods

Both supervised and unsupervised machine learning approaches were employed to assess the feasibility of activity inference.

- **Supervised Learning Approach (Transfer Learning):**
 - **Model:** The HarNet10 model, a publicly available pre-trained model for human activity recognition, was utilized.
 - **Strategy:** A leave-one-participant-out cross-validation strategy was implemented over twelve experimental runs. In each run, data from one participant was reserved for testing (attacking), while data from the remaining eleven participants were used to finetune the HarNet10 model.
 - **Evaluation:** The model produced a prediction accuracy score for each of the twelve test participants. The average of these twelve accuracy scores was then computed to provide a robust overall performance metric for the attack.

- **Unsupervised Learning Approach (Clustering):**
 - **Objective:** To determine the inherent separability of different activities based on forearm-mounted accelerometer data in a feature space.
 - **Feature Extraction:**
 - The magnitude of the raw tri-axial accelerometer data was computed.
 - Data was segmented into non-overlapping two-second windows.
 - From each window, a set of features was extracted:
 - **Time-domain features:** Mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, minimum, maximum, and median.
 - **Frequency-domain features:** Dominant frequencies (top three peaks using Welch's method), spectral centroid, and band powers.
 - These features were concatenated into feature vectors.
 - **Preprocessing:** The extracted features were standardized, and then dimensionality reduction was performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce the feature vectors to two dimensions for visualization.
 - **Clustering Algorithms:** Five different clustering algorithms were applied to the processed feature vectors: K-Means, DBSCAN, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), OPTICS, and Agglomerative Clustering.
 - **Adversary Interpretation Demonstration:** To illustrate how an adversary might interpret the clusters without ground truth, the authors collected additional accelerometer data using a consumer-grade smartphone placed at a similar forearm location while participants performed the same four activities. The clustering patterns from the smartphone data were then compared to those obtained from the clinical-grade sensor.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical investigation provided evidence for the feasibility of Activity Inference Attacks (AIA) against bionic limb users, employing both supervised and unsupervised machine learning techniques on accelerometer data.

#### 4.1. Supervised Learning Results (Activity Inference Attack Accuracy)

- The supervised learning approach, utilizing a transfer learning strategy with the pre-trained HarNet10 model, yielded an average attack accuracy of 83% across the twelve experimental runs for predicting the user's activity (walking, jogging in place, sitting, or standing).
- This accuracy level was deemed sufficiently high to conclude that an adversary could potentially leverage a publicly available model, pre-trained on similar accelerometer data, to successfully infer a bionic limb user's activities.
- Analysis of individual participant results revealed variability in attack accuracy, ranging from 57% for one participant to 96% for others. This indicates that certain individuals' activity patterns are more distinct and thus more susceptible to inference attacks.

#### 4.2. Unsupervised Learning Results (Activity Clustering)

- Among the five clustering algorithms tested, Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) demonstrated the best performance in grouping similar data points based on the extracted features.
- **Tri-axial Accelerometer Data Clustering (Ground Truth):** Visualizations of the data in the reduced two-dimensional feature space showed distinct clustering patterns for different activities (Figure 2). Specifically, jogging exhibited clear separability from the other activities. Walking data, however, showed some partial overlap with the clusters representing sitting and standing activities.
- **Smartphone Data for Adversary Interpretation:** To illustrate how an adversary might interpret these clusters, data was collected using a common smartphone placed at a similar forearm location. The clustering patterns observed from the smartphone's accelerometer data (Figure 3) were found to be similar to those obtained from the clinical-grade tri-axial accelerometer. Jogging again formed the most distinct cluster, while walking showed slight overlap with sitting and standing activities.
- This finding suggests that an adversary could potentially interpret activity patterns inferred from bionic limb sensor data by comparing them with activity clusters derived from easily accessible consumer-grade devices or publicly available datasets, even without direct access to ground truth labels for the specific bionic limb user.

In summary, the empirical section successfully demonstrated that sensitive information about a user's activities can be inferred from commonly embedded bionic limb sensors, thereby highlighting a concrete privacy vulnerability and underscoring the necessity of idiobionics research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces "idiobionics" as a critical and emergent field, addressing a significant gap in the intersection of advanced robotic prostheses and user privacy. The work carries several important significances and potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Validation of Privacy Concerns in Bionic Limbs

The empirical demonstration of Activity Inference Attacks validates the core premise that the advanced sensing capabilities of modern bionic limbs, while functionally beneficial, inherently create privacy vulnerabilities. The high accuracy achieved in inferring activities from accelerometer data, even with publicly available models, underscores that these are not theoretical risks but feasible attack vectors.

#### 5.2. Broadening the Scope of Privacy Risks in Human-Machine Systems

The paper expands the understanding of privacy risks beyond simple activity inference. It highlights how sensor data from bionic limbs could be exploited to deduce:
- **Sensitive Activity Patterns:** Leading to physical crimes (e.g., targeted burglaries) or inference of health issues (e.g., cardiovascular disease risk) which could be monetized by third parties.
- **Demographic Attributes:** Such as age, inferred from gait patterns or activity changes over time, particularly relevant for lower-limb prostheses.
- **Speech-Related Information:** Through the exploitation of vibration-sensitive sensors (accelerometers, actuators) to discern spoken words, speaker identity, or gender, paralleling known vulnerabilities in smartphones. This emphasizes the need for caution when integrating sound-related features into bionic limbs.
- **Other Personal Data:** Drawing parallels with smartphone and smartwatch domains, risks include inference of location, keystrokes (PINs, passwords), and user height.
- **Risks from Other Sensors:** The paper explicitly points out that gyroscopes (for identity/authentication via gait) and EMG sensors (for muscle activity, potentially leading to "keystroke" attacks via residual muscles) also pose direct privacy risks.

#### 5.3. Proactive Approach to Privacy-by-Design

By defining idiobionics and demonstrating risks early, the research advocates for a "privacy-by-design" approach. This means incorporating privacy protections into the core architecture of intelligent bionic limbs from the initial design stages, rather than as an afterthought. This proactive stance can streamline regulatory approval and accelerate the commercial viability of innovative prosthetic technologies.

#### 5.4. Fostering User Trust and Adoption

A key impact of idiobionics research is the potential to build and maintain user trust. If users are confident that their privacy is protected, they are more likely to adopt and fully utilize advanced bionic limbs. This can unlock the full therapeutic and functional potential of these devices, preventing user distrust from becoming a barrier to access and benefit.

#### 5.5. Guiding Future Research Directions

The curated list of open research questions provides a structured roadmap for future technical and socio-technical investigations within idiobionics. These questions prompt researchers to:
- Identify vulnerabilities in other embedded components (sensors, actuators, computing, algorithms).
- Investigate potential data leakage from machine learning models in commercial prostheses.
- Develop new hardware and software paradigms that offer robust privacy guarantees.
- Explore the applicability and development of Privacy-Preserving Machine Learning (PPML) techniques tailored to the unique requirements of bionic devices (balancing accuracy with privacy).
- Engage with end-users to understand their perceptions of privacy and how to meet their expectations, balancing functionality with data privacy.

#### 5.6. Transferability to Related Technologies

The paper highlights that the privacy concerns are not limited to bionic limbs but extend to closely related fields, such as exoskeletons. With the expanding use of exoskeletons in medical, recreational, and industrial applications, and their increasing accessibility, the societal impact of addressing these privacy risks through idiobionics becomes even broader.

In conclusion, this research marks a significant step towards ensuring that the advancements in intelligent robotic prostheses are realized responsibly. By establishing idiobionics, the authors lay the groundwork for a systematic, interdisciplinary effort to protect user privacy, which is crucial for building trust, driving innovation, and enabling the widespread, beneficial adoption of next-generation human-machine integration technologies.
