# Discrete-Time Hybrid Automata Learning: Legged Locomotion Meets Skateboarding

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Michigan and SUSTech developed a Discrete-time Hybrid Automata Learning (DHAL) framework that enables a quadrupedal robot to learn complex, contact-rich behaviors like skateboarding. The method combines unsupervised hybrid dynamics learning with a multi-critic reinforcement learning approach and a Beta distribution policy, achieving robust real-world performance across various terrains and disturbances.

## Problem

- Existing model-based robot control methods often rely on pre-defined gaits and simplified models, limiting their adaptability to novel or disturbed scenarios.
- Model-free reinforcement learning typically treats the entire system as a single dynamic model, overlooking explicit mode-switching knowledge inherent in hybrid systems and struggling with high-dimensional rigid body dynamics.
- Learning complex, contact-rich robot behaviors, such as skateboarding, is challenging due to the discontinuous nature of contact dynamics and the difficulty of specifying sparse rewards in high-dimensional action spaces.

## Method

- A **Discrete Neural Hybrid Automata (DHA)** is developed to identify the active discrete mode of the system online, using an ==unsupervised== learning approach with a ==Beta-Variational Autoencoder (β-VAE)== for state and contact prediction.
- A ==multi-critic reinforcement learning== framework is implemented with separate critics for gliding, pushing, and sim-to-real regularization rewards, whose normalized advantages are weighted and combined to update the actor's policy.
- A ==Beta distribution policy== is employed for action generation, directly outputting shape parameters for each joint to ensure actions remain within physical bounds and prevent issues common with Gaussian policies in bounded spaces.

## Results

- DHAL demonstrated that using hybrid dynamics (**K>=2** modes) significantly reduced prediction loss, with **three modes** (gliding, airborne/transition, pushing) proving sufficient to capture skateboarding dynamics.
- The framework successfully identified intuitive modes in real-world skateboarding (e.g., Mode 3 for gliding, Mode 2 for pushing), confirmed by LED indicators and t-SNE visualization of controller latent states.
- DHAL achieved a **100%** success rate on various indoor terrains, **80%** on slopes, and **60%** on uneven terrain in real-world quadruped skateboarding, outperforming single-critic or Gaussian policy baselines which often resulted in failures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling hybrid dynamics with multiple discrete modes (K>=2) significantly reduces prediction error compared to single dynamic models, effectively capturing complex contact-rich behaviors.
> - A Beta distribution policy, instead of a Gaussian one, is crucial for learning tasks with strictly bounded action spaces, preventing issues like biased gradients or excessive variance exploration.
> - A multi-critic reinforcement learning architecture effectively addresses sparse rewards and enables the robot to learn distinct, multi-style motions required for complex tasks like skateboarding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper operates at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: **hybrid dynamical systems, legged robot control, reinforcement learning, and sim-to-real transfer**. It addresses critical limitations in existing approaches by proposing a novel framework for learning complex, contact-rich robot behaviors.

**Hybrid Dynamical Systems:** The paper explicitly acknowledges that many real-world systems, especially in robotics (e.g., legged locomotion with contact events), are inherently hybrid, combining continuous flow with discrete mode switching. Traditional model-based approaches, like the Hybrid Automaton framework, have been used for planning and control but typically rely on simplified models and pre-defined contact events or gaits. More recent data-driven and learning-based methods for hybrid dynamics have primarily focused on low-dimensional or simple systems, often requiring explicit trajectory segmentation or labels for different modes. This work differentiates itself by developing a method that operates in high-dimensional systems and avoids the need for such explicit labels, making it more practical for complex robotic tasks. It specifically adopts a discrete-time formulation, arguing for its better compatibility with digital control systems compared to continuous-flow approaches.

**Legged Robot Control:** The control of legged robots has traditionally seen success with Model Predictive Control (MPC), especially for robust locomotion on flat ground. However, MPC often relies on accurate state estimation and manually designed gait patterns, limiting its scalability to complex, unstructured scenarios. Model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling high-speed locomotion, complex terrain traversal, and manipulation. A major focus in RL-based quadruped control is sim-to-real transfer, safe RL, and gait control. This paper contributes to the RL paradigm by focusing on "sparse motion patterns" and "embedding hybrid dynamics learning," which are less explored aspects.

**Contact-Guided Locomotion:** Synthesizing optimal motions for contact-rich tasks is challenging due to the discontinuities introduced by contact dynamics. Model-based methods often employ techniques like linear complementarity problems (LCP) or hybrid dynamic systems. In RL, specifying sparse or contact-guided motion patterns is difficult, as sparse rewards in high-dimensional spaces can hinder exploration. The paper addresses this by integrating a multi-critic framework and a Beta distribution policy, contrasting with typical approaches that rely on key-frame tracking or energy minimization, which may not naturally induce complex contact behaviors like skateboarding.

**Learning Hybrid Dynamics with RL:** Previous efforts in learning hybrid dynamics, such as those by Ly and Lipson or Poli et al., have explored symbolic representations or neural event functions for continuous-flow systems. This paper advances the field by integrating hybrid dynamics directly into an online reinforcement learning pipeline, allowing the robot to learn explicit mode switching and control without requiring human-provided labels or pre-segmentation of trajectories. This unsupervised learning of hybrid automata, coupled with powerful RL techniques, represents a significant step towards more autonomous and adaptable robot control for complex, discontinuous tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the inherent hybrid nature of many robotic tasks, particularly legged locomotion, which involves both continuous body movements and discrete contact events. Current state-of-the-art methods struggle with this duality in different ways:
- **Model-based approaches** often rely on pre-defined gaits and simplified dynamic models, limiting their adaptability to novel, complex scenarios or disturbances.
- **Model-free reinforcement learning methods**, while powerful, typically treat the entire system as a single dynamic model, overlooking explicit mode-switching knowledge. Learning high-dimensional rigid body dynamics in hybrid systems without clear trajectory labels or segmentation remains a significant open problem.

To address these limitations, the paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives, explicitly stated as contributions:

1. **Develop a Discrete Hybrid Automata Framework:** The primary objective is to propose a framework for online reinforcement learning that can identify and execute mode-switching in hybrid dynamical systems without needing explicit trajectory segmentation or event function learning. This aims to bridge the gap between model-based hybrid systems and model-free RL, allowing for more intuitive and effective control of systems with distinct operational modes.
2. **Design an Effective Contact-Guided Task Learning Scheme:** For tasks involving complex sequences of contact events, such as skateboarding, learning with sparse rewards is particularly challenging. The objective is to combine a multi-critic architecture with a Beta distribution policy to effectively handle these contact-guided problems within hybrid systems, promoting stable and natural motion.
3. **Achieve Robust Sim-to-Real Performance for Underactuated Skateboarding:** The paper aims to validate its approach on a highly challenging, dynamic, and underactuated task: enabling a quadrupedal robot to skateboard. This task serves as a strong testbed for the framework's ability to handle precise mode transitions and contact events, demonstrating agile and robust sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to create a more generalizable and robust learning approach for complex robotic behaviors that inherently involve discrete mode changes, moving beyond methods that require extensive manual pre-definition or struggle with the discontinuous nature of such tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **Discrete-time Hybrid Automata Learning (DHAL)**, integrates hybrid dynamics within a reinforcement learning pipeline to enable a robot to learn complex, contact-guided motions like skateboarding. The methodology comprises several interconnected components:

**A. Discrete Neural Hybrid Automata (DHA):**
At the core of DHAL is the DHA, designed to identify the active discrete mode of the system at each time step.
- **Modeling Hybrid Dynamics:** The system models legged robot dynamics as a switched affine system, although it uses a set of nonlinear neural networks to represent the dynamics within each mode. This discrete-time formulation simplifies the integration with RL and digital control.
- **Mode Identification:** The DHA takes a window of past observations ($o_{t-k:t}$) and actions ($a_{t-k-1:t-1}$) as input. It then outputs a probability distribution over a predefined maximum number of modes (K) using a softmax classifier. A one-hot latent vector ($\delta$) representing the selected mode is obtained via categorical sampling from this distribution.
- **Unsupervised Learning:** Crucially, the mode labels are not provided explicitly. Instead, the DHA is trained simultaneously with a Beta-Variational Autoencoder (β-VAE) using an unsupervised learning approach. The system learns the correct mode by minimizing the prediction error of the next partial state ($o_{t+1}$) and contact information ($c_{t+1}$). The loss function for the VAE includes Mean Squared Error (MSE) for state prediction, Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) for contact prediction, and a KL divergence term to regularize the latent space.
- **Mode Distinction:** To ensure distinct modes and prevent confusion, an information entropy term ($H(p)$) is added to the loss function, encouraging the mode probabilities to be as distinct as possible.
- **Gradient Flow:** Given the discrete nature of categorical sampling, the Straight-Through-Gradient method is employed to enable backpropagation during training, allowing gradients to flow through the discrete mode selection.

**B. Dynamics Encoder and Decoder (β-VAE):**
- **Mode-Specific Dynamics:** For each of the K modes identified by the DHA, a corresponding β-VAE is established.
- **Encoder:** An encoder, implemented as a Transformer architecture to capture temporal dependencies, takes the same historical state-action sequence input as the DHA. It processes this information to extract a compact latent representation ($z_t$) specific to the currently selected mode.
- **Decoder:** The decoder then uses this latent representation ($z_t$) to predict the next partial state ($o_{t+1}$) and the contact probabilities for each foot ($c_{t+1}$). The predicted contact information aids in learning hybrid switching.
- **Controller Input:** The extracted latent representation ($z_t$) from the encoder, rich in dynamics information, is then passed to the robot's controller (actor) to inform its actions.

**C. Multi-Critic Reinforcement Learning:**
To effectively handle the challenging sparse rewards and multi-style motions inherent in the skateboarding task, the framework employs a multi-critic architecture.
- **Reward Groups:** Three distinct reward groups are defined, each with its own dedicated critic:
 - **Gliding Critic:** Focuses on rewards related to maintaining balance and smooth gliding on the skateboard (e.g., feet on board, feet distance, joint positions).
 - **Pushing Critic:** Addresses sparse, contact-related rewards during the pushing phase (e.g., tracking linear/angular velocity, hip positions, orientation).
 - **Sim2Real Critic:** Manages regularization rewards for effective sim-to-real transfer (e.g., wheel contact, body height, joint acceleration, collisions, action/torque rates, torques, linear/angular velocity, base orientation).
- **Separate Value Estimation:** Each critic estimates its value function and calculates its advantage (using GAE) independently for its specific reward group.
- **Weighted Summation:** The normalized advantages from all three critics are combined through a weighted summation to form the final advantage signal used for updating the actor's policy in the PPO algorithm. This approach reduces sensitivity to reward tuning and allows for balanced learning of different motion styles.

**D. Beta Distribution Policy:**
- **Bounded Action Space:** Unlike common Gaussian policies in PPO, DHAL utilizes a Beta distribution for its policy. This is crucial for tasks with strict action space bounds (e.g., robot joint limits), where Gaussian policies can introduce bias through clipping or lead to excessive variance exploration, potentially causing unstable or dangerous movements.
- **Parameterization:** The policy directly outputs the shape parameters ($\alpha$, $\beta$) of the Beta distribution for each joint independently.
- **Unimodality:** A custom activation function `SoftplusWithOffset(x) = log(1 + e^x) + 1 + 10^-6` ensures that $\alpha, \beta > 1$, maintaining a unimodal Beta distribution.
- **Action Scaling:** The Beta distribution, naturally defined over, is scaled to the robot's action space (e.g., $[-a_{max}, a_{max}]$), allowing the policy to produce valid, bounded actions while fully utilizing the available range.

**E. Environment Design and Sim2Real Considerations:**
- **Unitree Go1 and Skateboard:** The framework is validated on a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot tasked with skateboarding.
- **Simulation Details:** In simulation, the robot's left forelimb is connected to the skateboard via a spherical joint for stability. The skateboard's truck mechanism is simulated using a position PD controller, and rolling friction is approximated with a compensation force. Contact detection is a combination of relative position error and collision forces, accounting for simulation inaccuracies.
- **Domain Randomization:** The paper implies the use of standard sim-to-real techniques like domain randomization, although specific details (e.g., Table VI in appendix) are in the supplementary material, covering aspects like friction, added mass, COM, and sensor noise.

This comprehensive methodology allows DHAL to learn complex, hybrid behaviors in a data-driven, end-to-end manner, addressing long-standing challenges in robot control and hybrid system identification.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments presented in the paper effectively validate the DHAL framework across three key questions, providing strong evidence for its efficacy and advantages over existing methods.

**Q1: Why Hybrid Dynamics System is better than Single Dynamics modeling?**
The results clearly demonstrate the benefit of explicitly modeling hybrid dynamics. When comparing the dynamics prediction loss (MSE of next observation) for different numbers of discrete modes (K):
- A single dynamics model (K=1) yielded the highest prediction loss, indicating its inadequacy in capturing the robot's hybrid behavior.
- Incorporating hybrid dynamics (K >= 2) significantly reduced the reconstruction loss, with performance plateauing around K=3. This suggests that three distinct modes are sufficient to represent the skateboarding dynamics (likely corresponding to "on the skateboard," "pushing," and "airborne/transition" states).
- Real-world tests showed that the learned dynamics could accurately predict the robot's joint trajectories and discrete jumps (e.g., right front leg joint motor position) even when exposed to different cyclic signals than those used during training, confirming the accuracy of the learned hybrid dynamics.

**Q2: Can our method identify the mode of skateboarding?**
The mode identification capability of DHAL was validated through both qualitative and quantitative analyses:
- **Intuitive Mode Identification:** In real-world deployment, the robot's onboard LEDs changed color according to the active mode selected by the hybrid automata. This visualization confirmed that the mode transitions were smooth and highly consistent with human intuition:
 - **Mode 3 (Red):** Identified when the robot was in the gliding phase, with all four feet firmly on the skateboard.
 - **Mode 1 (Green):** Activated during the airborne/transition phase, such as when the right front foot lifted off the ground.
 - **Mode 2 (Blue):** Corresponded to the pushing phase, when the robot's right legs were in contact with the ground to propel the skateboard.
- **Latent Space Visualization:** A t-SNE dimensionality reduction of the controller's hidden layer outputs revealed distinct clusters corresponding to the identified modes (green for Mode 1, blue for Mode 2, red for Mode 3). This quantitative evidence supports that the DHAL module effectively differentiates between various modes and that the controller learns distinct control policies for each.

**Q3: Can our method achieve skateboarding in the real-world with disturbances?**
The paper presents extensive ablation studies and real-world experiments to demonstrate the robustness and performance of DHAL.

- **Comparison of Training Returns:** DHAL achieved training performance comparable to a PPO-oracle-beta policy, which had access to privileged skateboard information. In contrast, baseline methods using Gaussian distributions (DreamWaq, PPO-curiosity) failed to learn the skateboarding task, often resulting in dangerous robot motions. This strongly supports the paper's claim that the Beta distribution policy is crucial for successful learning in complex, bounded action spaces with sparse rewards.
- **Comparison with Single-Critic:**
 - A single-critic approach without transferred weights led to aggressive, erratic, and often failing motions, indicating difficulty in balancing different reward components.
 - A single-critic approach with transferred weights could mount the skateboard but exhibited unnatural pushing motions (relying primarily on hind legs).
 - DHAL's multi-critic framework, by contrast, enabled smooth, natural, and efficient skateboarding, demonstrating its effectiveness for multi-style learning.
- **Quantitative Real-World Success Rate:** DHAL showed remarkable robustness across various indoor and outdoor terrains and under disturbances:
 - **100% success rate** on smooth ceramic flooring, soft carpeted flooring, and under external disturbances, and for single-step terrains.
 - **80% success rate** on slopes.
 - **60% success rate** on uneven terrain.
 - In stark contrast, baseline methods (Our-wo-MC, Ours-wo-Beta, DreamWaq) mostly resulted in complete failures or highly unsafe behaviors (marked as '✗' in the table, indicating joint protection states due to excessive torque).
 - The robot also demonstrated surprising stability and recovery capabilities in an "extreme skateboard park" (Fig. 12), navigating multi-level stair sets and U-shaped bowls.
- **Validation on Other Hybrid Tasks (Appendix):** The framework's generalizability was further shown on other contact-rich problems like robot hand-stand locomotion (identifying gaits) and dexterous manipulation hand-over (identifying task stages), as well as on classic switching linear dynamical systems, where its identification matched analytical solutions.

Overall, the findings robustly support that DHAL successfully addresses the challenges of learning hybrid dynamics in complex robotic tasks, providing intuitive mode identification and achieving highly robust real-world performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Discrete-time Hybrid Automata Learning" (DHAL) framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotics and reinforcement learning for complex, contact-rich tasks. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Bridging Model-Based and Model-Free Control:** DHAL successfully integrates the structured knowledge of hybrid dynamical systems (discrete modes and continuous flow) with the adaptive power of model-free reinforcement learning. This provides a compelling alternative to traditional model-based approaches that require extensive manual design of gaits and event functions, while simultaneously addressing a key limitation of purely model-free RL (lack of explicit mode awareness). This integration allows for robust control policies that are both adaptable and interpretable.

2. **Unsupervised Learning of Hybrid Dynamics:** A major contribution is the elimination of the need for explicit trajectory segmentation or event function learning. By using an unsupervised learning approach to train the hybrid automata and VAE simultaneously, DHAL significantly reduces the human effort and data labeling requirements often associated with learning hybrid systems. This makes the framework more scalable and practical for real-world applications where ground truth mode labels are difficult or impossible to obtain.

3. **Robust Learning in Complex, Contact-Rich Environments:** The paper tackles the challenging problem of contact-guided locomotion with remarkable success, particularly demonstrated by the quadrupedal robot skateboarding task. The introduction of a multi-critic architecture effectively addresses the issue of sparse rewards and enables the learning of distinct motion styles (gliding vs. pushing). This is crucial for real-world robotics where complex interactions and intermittent contacts are common.

4. **Enhanced Policy Robustness with Beta Distribution:** The adoption of the Beta distribution policy for bounded action spaces is a subtle yet powerful innovation. It prevents the issues of biased gradients and variance explosion that often plague Gaussian policies when actions are clipped at physical limits. This contributes directly to the stability and safety of learned robot behaviors, making sim-to-real transfer more reliable and hardware deployment less risky.

5. **Improved Interpretability and Diagnostics:** The ability of DHAL to identify distinct modes that align with human intuition (e.g., gliding, airborne, pushing phases in skateboarding) is highly significant. This interpretability not only provides insights into how the robot solves the task but also offers a valuable diagnostic tool for understanding and debugging complex learned behaviors. It could pave the way for more explainable AI in robotics.

6. **Broad Applicability and Generalizability:** While validated on quadrupedal skateboarding, the underlying DHAL framework is general. Its successful application to other hybrid tasks, such as robot hand-stands, dexterous manipulation hand-over, and classical switching linear systems (as shown in the appendix), underscores its potential to generalize to a wide range of robotic problems involving discrete mode changes and contact interactions. This opens avenues for developing more versatile and intelligent robots capable of performing multi-modal behaviors.

In summary, DHAL offers a promising pathway toward creating autonomous systems that can intelligently infer and adapt to their underlying hybrid dynamics. This research moves beyond current limitations, providing a robust, data-efficient, and interpretable framework for learning complex robotic skills in the real world, thus pushing the boundaries of what autonomous robots can achieve.
