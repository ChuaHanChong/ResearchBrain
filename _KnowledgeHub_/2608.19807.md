# Answer-Level Trust Selection for Physical Vision-Language Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Answer-Level Trust Selection (ATS) is a post-hoc, model-agnostic selective-prediction framework that decides whether to accept or reject a single VLM numerical prediction (duration, speed, acceleration) without ground truth, by aggregating eight behavioral diagnostic scores from repeated queries and prior-perturbation interventions into one trust score. Across 20 VLM backbones it rejects far more stable-but-wrong and prior-tracking failures than self-consistency, at the cost of retaining fewer correct answers.

## Problem

- Existing physical-reasoning benchmarks only measure aggregate model accuracy against ground truth, giving no way to judge whether one specific deployed prediction is trustworthy when ground truth is absent.
- Self-consistency and repeated-sampling agreement can assign high confidence to answers that are stable but numerically wrong, since agreement alone cannot detect this failure mode.
- Existing black-box uncertainty methods do not test whether a VLM's numerical estimate is being driven by textual priors rather than actual visual evidence.

## Method

- ==Controlled VLM probing== queries the same model under five conditions (Video+Prior, Prior-Only, Video+Counterfactual-Prior, CoT-Prompted, Self-Consistency Sampling) to generate comparison signals without modifying the model.
- ==Eight diagnostic scores== (video-prior consistency, counterfactual prior stability, sampling stability, sample agreement, prompt consistency, scale plausibility, category familiarity, CoT non-refusal) quantify distinct behavioral phenomena from those probes.
- ==Trust-score aggregation== combines the eight scores into intervention/stability/validity groups via prior-weighted, equal-weight, or learned logistic-regression weighting, then an ==event-conditional selector== with ==Hoeffding-style threshold screening== accepts or rejects each answer.

## Results

- On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, prior-weighted ATS reaches MRA **0.4889** at 50% coverage versus 0.4734 when the video-prior consistency score is removed, and AURC worsens from 0.5300 to **0.5406** without it.
- Averaged over 20 backbones at 50% coverage, prior-weighted ATS rejects **40.9%** of stable-but-wrong cases and **95.8%** of prior-tracking cases, versus 1.3% and 65.4% for self-consistency alone.
- Across 20 backbones ATS shows a mean AURC improvement of **+0.0133** over self-consistency (positive on **14 of 20** backbones), while fixed-coverage MRA gains are small and backbone-dependent (+0.0192 at 30%, -0.0037 at 70%).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Repeated-sampling agreement is not sufficient evidence of correctness; a model can be highly self-consistent while systematically wrong, so reliability estimation needs orthogonal behavioral probes beyond consistency.
> - Deliberately perturbing an input's stated prior while holding visual evidence fixed is a cheap, general diagnostic for exposing whether a model's numeric output is grounded in perception or in textual anchoring.
> - Higher failure-case rejection rates trade off against retention of correct answers, so selective-prediction gains must be reported as risk-coverage trade-offs, not unqualified accuracy improvements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in interpreting and reasoning about visual information, extending from basic object recognition and scene description to more complex tasks involving physical reasoning. Recent advancements indicate that VLMs can estimate physical quantities such as duration, speed, and acceleration from visual observations. This capability positions VLMs as potential perception and reasoning modules in systems that interact with the physical world, where their numerical predictions could interface with downstream planning, monitoring, and decision-support systems.

The broader research landscape for VLMs in physical reasoning includes benchmarks like CLEVRER (Yi et al. 2020) and Physion (Bear et al. 2021), which investigate collision reasoning and physical scene evolution. Other benchmarks, such as ComPhy (Chen et al. 2022) and CRIPP-VQA (Patel et al. 2022), focus on inferring latent physical properties (e.g., mass, charge, friction, initial velocity) from observed motion. More recently, PhysBench (Chow et al. 2025) and QuantiPhy (Puyin et al. 2026) have extended this evaluation to general-purpose VLMs, with QuantiPhy specifically focusing on video-based numerical estimation, often incorporating explicit textual physical priors. These existing benchmarks primarily assess VLM capabilities by evaluating overall model performance against annotated ground truth across entire datasets, typically using aggregate metrics like accuracy.

However, a gap exists in assessing the reliability of individual VLM predictions at deployment time, where ground truth is unavailable. This is particularly critical for quantitative physical reasoning, where numerical outputs directly influence downstream decisions. Conventional uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods or reliance on self-consistency (Wang et al. 2023) may not adequately address specific failure modes in VLMs. For instance, a VLM might consistently produce stable but incorrect numerical estimates (stable-but-wrong predictions) or might be overly influenced by textual priors, generating predictions that track these priors rather than deriving from visual evidence (prior-tracking predictions). These hidden failure modes are not typically revealed by aggregate performance metrics or simple measures of output agreement.

The present work contributes to the field of behavioral reliability estimation and selective prediction. Behavioral reliability methods estimate uncertainty based on model behavior rather than internal confidence scores. This includes approaches based on repeated sampling and response inconsistency (Manakul, Liusie, and Gales 2023; Kuhn, Gal, and Farquhar 2023) or multimodal perturbations (Zhang, Zhang, and Zheng 2024; Padhi et al. 2025). Selective prediction frameworks (El-Yaniv and Wiener 2010; Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017, 2019) aim to retain predictions only when their estimated reliability is sufficient, abstaining otherwise. This paper builds on this behavioral perspective by introducing diagnostic scores specifically tailored for quantitative physical reasoning and applying them in a post-hoc, black-box selective prediction framework for numerical VLM outputs. It distinguishes itself by focusing on answer-level trust for specific numerical predictions, complementing the existing model-level capability evaluations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the practical challenge of determining the trustworthiness of individual numerical predictions made by Vision-Language Models (VLMs) during real-world deployment. While VLMs are increasingly capable of estimating physical quantities from visual observations, existing evaluation paradigms predominantly focus on aggregate model performance metrics across datasets. This approach, while essential for measuring overall capability, does not provide a mechanism to assess the reliability of a single prediction when its ground truth is unknown in an operational setting.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations regarding VLM behavior in quantitative physical reasoning:
- **Need for Answer-Level Reliability**: In deployment scenarios, numerical VLM outputs can serve as critical inputs for downstream systems (e.g., planning, monitoring, decision support). The reliability of each individual estimate directly impacts the robustness and safety of these applications. An overall high benchmark score does not guarantee the trustworthiness of any particular prediction.
- **Limitations of Self-Consistency**: A common approach to estimate uncertainty is to check for consistency across multiple generations from a VLM. However, as illustrated in the paper, VLMs can exhibit "stable-but-wrong" behavior, consistently producing similar numerical estimates that are far from the actual ground truth. In such cases, self-consistency would incorrectly suggest high confidence.
- **Susceptibility to Textual Priors**: VLMs may sometimes over-rely on numerical information provided in textual priors within the prompt, rather than extracting the quantity directly from visual evidence. This "prior-tracking" behavior can lead to predictions shifting significantly when the textual prior is perturbed, even if the visual input remains constant. Standard evaluation metrics or self-consistency alone may fail to identify predictions influenced by such biases.
- **Gap in Current Benchmarks**: Existing benchmarks for quantitative VLM reasoning primarily evaluate model-level performance, focusing on how well models perform on average. They do not offer tools or methodologies for real-time, answer-level reliability assessment that can inform an accept-or-reject decision for an individual VLM output.

To address these motivations, the research establishes the following key objectives:
1. **Formulate Answer-Level Selective Prediction**: Define the problem of selective prediction specifically for quantitative physical reasoning with VLMs, where the goal is to decide whether to accept or reject an individual numerical answer without modifying it.
2. **Propose a Post-Hoc, Black-Box Framework**: Develop a framework, named Answer-Level Trust Selection (ATS), that can evaluate prediction reliability without requiring fine-tuning of the VLM, access to its internal logits, or an auxiliary neural verifier. This framework is designed to be model-agnostic and minimally invasive.
3. **Provide an Interpretable Basis for Selection**: Construct ATS to use eight behavior-based diagnostic scores. Each score is intended to capture a distinct and interpretable aspect of the VLM’s predictive behavior under controlled conditions, thereby providing a basis for assessing trustworthiness.
4. **Evaluate Framework Performance**: Conduct an in-depth evaluation of ATS using a representative VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) to assess its selective performance, the contribution of individual diagnostic components, and its effectiveness in identifying targeted failure modes.
5. **Characterize VLM Capabilities**: Benchmark 20 diverse VLM backbones to provide a broad characterization of their numerical reasoning capabilities and diagnostic profiles.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond aggregate performance metrics to enable practical, real-time trust assessment of individual numerical VLM predictions in physical reasoning tasks, specifically targeting subtle failure modes that existing methods overlook.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Answer-Level Trust Selection (ATS), a post-hoc, black-box framework for evaluating the trustworthiness of individual numerical predictions from Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in quantitative physical reasoning tasks.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

Each example is represented as a tuple $(v_i, q_i, p_i)$, consisting of a visual observation ($v_i$), a question ($q_i$), and textual context with a numerical prior ($p_i$). The VLM produces an original numerical prediction $\hat{y}_i = f(v_i, q_i, p_i)$, for which the ground truth is $y_i$. The objective is to perform post-hoc selection, generating a trust score $s_i = S(x_i, \hat{y}_i; f)$ for each prediction. A selector $g_\tau(x_i, \hat{y}_i) = I[s_i \geq \tau]$ then accepts or rejects the prediction based on a threshold $\tau$.

Prediction quality is measured using Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA), which averages correctness across multiple relative-error tolerances. Performance of the selector is assessed by coverage (fraction of accepted predictions) and retained-answer MRA (or selective risk, $1 - \text{MRA}_{\text{ret}}$). A desirable selector achieves lower selective risk at a given coverage.

#### 3.2. Overview of ATS

ATS operates in several steps:
1. **Controlled VLM Queries**: The VLM is probed under five distinct conditions for each example.
2. **Diagnostic Score Construction**: Responses from these queries are converted into eight behavioral diagnostic scores.
3. **Trust Score Aggregation**: These scores are combined into a unified trust score.
4. **Selector Construction**: A selector is trained on a validation set to determine acceptance or rejection based on the trust score.
5. **Decision**: For new predictions, the selector accepts or rejects the original numerical answer.

#### 3.3. Controlled VLM Probing

For each example $x=(v, q, p)$, ATS performs the following queries:
- **Video + Prior**: Standard query $f(v, q, p)$, yielding the original prediction $\hat{y}_{\text{full}}$.
- **Prior Only**: Query $f(\emptyset, q, p)$ without visual input, providing $\hat{y}_{\text{prior}}$.
- **Video + Counterfactual Prior**: Query $f(v, q, p_{\text{cf}})$ where $p_{\text{cf}}$ has a perturbed numerical prior, yielding $\hat{y}_{\text{cf}}$. This tests sensitivity to textual numerical priors.
- **CoT-Prompted**: Query $f(v, q, p; \pi_{\text{cot}})$ with a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting instruction, providing $\hat{y}_{\text{cot}}$.
- **Self-Consistency Sampling**: $M$ stochastic generations $\hat{y}_{\text{sc}}^{(m)} \sim f(v, q, p; \xi_m)$ from the standard input, used to assess output stability.

#### 3.4. Diagnostic Score Construction

Eight behavioral diagnostic scores are constructed from the VLM responses:
1. **Video-Prior Consistency ($s_{\text{vp}}$)**: Measures agreement in log-magnitude space between $\hat{y}_{\text{full}}$ and $\hat{y}_{\text{prior}}$. Higher values indicate closer agreement.
2. **Counterfactual Prior Stability ($s_{\text{cps}}$)**: Evaluates whether $\hat{y}_{\text{cf}}$ remains closer to $\hat{y}_{\text{full}}$ or to an idealized prior-tracking reference (scaled $\hat{y}_{\text{full}}$ by the prior perturbation ratio). Higher values suggest less prior tracking.
3. **Sampling Stability ($s_{\text{rep}}$)**: Quantifies the inverse of the log-space dispersion (standard deviation) of valid numerical predictions from self-consistency samples. Higher values indicate tighter concentration.
4. **Sample Agreement ($s_{\text{agr}}$)**: Represents the proportion of self-consistency samples that produce a valid positive numerical prediction. Higher values imply more frequent valid outputs.
5. **Prompt Consistency ($s_{\text{prompt}}$)**: Measures numerical consistency (inverse of standard deviation in log space) across $\hat{y}_{\text{prior}}$, $\hat{y}_{\text{full}}$, and $\hat{y}_{\text{cot}}$. Higher values indicate greater consistency.
6. **Scale Plausibility ($s_{\text{scale}}$)**: Assesses if $\hat{y}_{\text{full}}$ has an extreme log-magnitude relative to a fixed scale constant. Higher values indicate less extreme magnitudes.
7. **Category Familiarity ($s_{\text{fam}}$)**: Based on the empirical frequency of the example's event family in a reference pool. Higher values suggest greater familiarity.
8. **CoT Non-Refusal ($s_{\text{valid}}$)**: A binary score indicating whether the CoT-prompted response avoids predefined refusal patterns. $s_{\text{valid}}=1$ means non-refusal.

#### 3.5. Trust-Score Aggregation

The eight scores are grouped into three categories: intervention-related ($s_{\text{vp}}, s_{\text{cps}}$), stability-related ($s_{\text{rep}}, s_{\text{agr}}, s_{\text{prompt}}$), and validity-related ($s_{\text{fam}}, s_{\text{scale}}, s_{\text{valid}}$). An aggregate trust score $\tau_i$ is computed as a weighted sum of these scores. Three aggregation strategies are explored:
- **Prior-weighted ATS**: Uses manually specified, heuristically determined weights.
- **Equal-weight ATS**: Assigns uniform weights to all eight scores.
- **Learned ATS**: Employs a lightweight logistic regression model trained on the validation set to learn the optimal combination weights.

#### 3.6. Selector Construction

ATS uses the validation set to construct event-conditional selection rules. For each event family $h$, a specific set of acceptance thresholds ($\alpha_h, \beta_h, \eta_h, \lambda_h$) is determined. The final selector $g_{\hat{\gamma}_{h_i}}(x_i, \hat{y}_i)$ accepts a prediction if its individual diagnostic scores ($s_{i, \text{vp}}$, $s_{i, \text{cps}}$, $r_{i, \text{unk}}$) and the aggregate trust score $\tau_i$ all meet their respective family-specific thresholds. A Hoeffding-style upper-confidence screening statistic is used during validation to penalize rules supported by fewer accepted examples, promoting robustness.

#### 3.7. Test-Time Selection

During testing, the pre-determined family-specific selectors are applied to the original numerical predictions. No test labels are used, and ATS does not modify or replace the VLM's original output.

#### 3.8. Theoretical Motivations

The paper provides two theoretical propositions:
- **Conditional finite-class selective-risk bound**: This proposition offers a Hoeffding-style bound on conditional selective risk for a finite collection of prespecified rules, explaining the sample-size penalty used in validation. While not a formal guarantee for the adaptive validation procedure, it informs the conservative screening statistic.
- **Monotonicity of counterfactual prior stability**: This proposition demonstrates that $s_{\text{cps}}$ strictly decreases with the degree of prior tracking, confirming its intended behavior as a diagnostic for prior sensitivity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ATS was conducted on the QuantiPhy (Puyin et al. 2026) validation split, measuring numerical answer quality using Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA). The primary model used for in-depth analysis was Qwen2.5-VL-7B, with additional benchmarking across 20 VLM backbones.

#### 4.1. Cross-backbone Diagnostic Profiles

Figure 3 presents a summary of the 8 ATS diagnostic scores across 20 VLM backbones. The results indicate that repeated-output stability (e.g., $s_{\text{rep}}$, $s_{\text{agr}}$) and validity-related scores (e.g., $s_{\text{scale}}$, $s_{\text{valid}}$) are generally high across models. In contrast, intervention-related scores ($s_{\text{vp}}$, $s_{\text{cps}}$) and prompt consistency ($s_{\text{prompt}}$) exhibit greater variation between different VLM backbones. This variation suggests that models differ in their sensitivity to prior interventions and prompting styles, providing empirical evidence that these diagnostic probes capture distinct behavioral aspects.

#### 4.2. Cross-backbone Selective Comparison

When comparing Prior-weighted ATS to SC-only (self-consistency only) across 20 backbones, the mean paired effects in MRA were +0.0192 at 30% coverage, +0.0023 at 50% coverage, and -0.0037 at 70% coverage. For the Area Under the Risk-Coverage (AURC) curve, a metric where positive differences favor ATS, the mean effect was +0.0133, with positive effects observed for 14 out of 20 backbones. These results suggest that ATS provides consistent, albeit modest, advantages in selective performance on aggregate metrics across a diverse set of VLMs, particularly at higher coverage rates.

#### 4.3. Targeted Failure-mode Analysis

A key finding concerns ATS's ability to identify specific failure modes that other baselines may miss, as detailed in Table 1. At 50% coverage, Prior-weighted ATS rejected 40.9% of "stable-but-wrong" cases, significantly outperforming Generic UQ (27.1%) and SC-only (1.3%). Similarly, Prior-weighted ATS rejected 95.8% of "prior-tracking" cases. This indicates that the intervention-based diagnostics (e.g., $s_{\text{vp}}$, $s_{\text{cps}}$) effectively identify predictions that are consistently incorrect or unduly influenced by textual priors.
However, these gains in rejecting failure cases came with a trade-off: lower retention of matched correct predictions. For instance, while Prior-weighted ATS showed high rejection rates for "stable-but-wrong" and "prior-tracking" cases, its retention of correct predictions in these categories was lower compared to some baselines. This highlights that improved failure-case rejection can sometimes reduce the overall set of accepted correct predictions, representing a rejection-retention trade-off.

#### 4.4. Representative Qwen2.5-VL-7B Results

On the Qwen2.5-VL-7B model, SC-only demonstrated competitive performance at 30% and 50% coverage levels in terms of retained MRA (Table 2, Figure 4). Generic UQ with rank fusion achieved the lowest AURC. Prior-weighted ATS remained competitive across various operating points and exhibited stability under random weight perturbations.
Ablation studies on Qwen2.5-VL-7B demonstrated the contribution of specific diagnostic scores. Removing the video-prior consistency score ($s_{\text{vp}}$) from Prior-weighted ATS reduced MRA@50 from 0.4889 to 0.4734 and increased AURC from 0.5300 to 0.5406. This suggests that $s_{\text{vp}}$ contributes positively to ATS's performance on this specific backbone. These results underscore that ATS, particularly with its intervention-based diagnostics, provides complementary evidence for evaluating VLM prediction reliability, especially in detecting nuanced failure modes not fully captured by generic uncertainty estimation or self-consistency methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Answer-Level Trust Selection (ATS), a framework that holds significance for both the deployment and ongoing development of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) engaged in quantitative physical reasoning.

**Practical Utility and Deployment Implications:**
The primary significance lies in addressing a critical practical need: assessing the trustworthiness of individual numerical VLM predictions in real-world deployment scenarios where ground truth is unavailable. Currently, systems relying on VLMs for quantitative outputs often lack a robust mechanism to decide whether to accept or reject a specific numerical estimate. ATS provides a post-hoc, black-box solution that does not require fine-tuning the VLM, access to its internal architecture or logits, or the use of auxiliary neural verifiers. This model-agnostic and minimally invasive nature makes ATS highly adaptable to a wide range of existing VLMs without extensive modifications, facilitating its integration into practical applications. For applications like robotics, autonomous systems, or industrial monitoring, where physical quantity estimates are crucial for safety and performance, ATS offers a mechanism to enhance decision reliability by identifying potentially untrustworthy predictions.

**Enhanced Understanding of VLM Failures:**
The research significantly contributes to a deeper understanding of VLM failure modes in quantitative reasoning. By introducing intervention-based diagnostics, ATS specifically highlights "stable-but-wrong" and "prior-tracking" predictions. These are critical failure types that traditional aggregate performance metrics or even self-consistency checks often fail to expose. The ability to systematically identify when a VLM consistently produces incorrect but stable answers, or when it over-relies on textual priors instead of visual evidence, provides valuable insights for VLM developers aiming to improve model robustness and grounding. This diagnostic capability moves beyond a simple "right or wrong" assessment to explain *why* a prediction might be unreliable.

**Complementary to Model-Level Evaluation:**
ATS complements existing model-level capability evaluations (e.g., benchmarks like QuantiPhy). While benchmarks assess overall VLM performance, ATS provides an answer-level reliability assessment crucial for operational decisions. This dual perspective is essential: high aggregate accuracy does not guarantee the reliability of every individual output, and ATS bridges this gap by offering a decision-support tool for specific predictions.

**Foundation for Future Research:**
The findings motivate future research directions:
- **Improved Aggregation Strategies**: While ATS demonstrates efficacy, the observed trade-off between failure-case rejection and correct-case retention suggests room for more sophisticated aggregation strategies that could balance these aspects more effectively to improve overall selective performance.
- **Novel Diagnostic Probes**: The success of intervention-based diagnostics in uncovering specific failure modes encourages the development of further specialized behavioral probes for other types of VLM reasoning.
- **Integration with Model Development**: The diagnostic insights provided by ATS could inform the design and training of future VLMs, guiding efforts to make them less susceptible to issues like prior-tracking or stable-but-wrong predictions.

In summary, ATS represents a significant step towards building more reliable and trustworthy AI systems that interact with the physical world. By focusing on the critical, yet often overlooked, challenge of answer-level trust, this work enhances the deployability and interpretability of advanced VLMs.
