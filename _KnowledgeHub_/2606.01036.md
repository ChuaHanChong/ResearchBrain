# Position: Good Embodied Reward Models Need Bad Behavior Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Embodied reward models systematically over-reward suboptimal robot behaviors, especially as task complexity increases, due to a lack of negative data in training. Incorporating real robot failure videos with dense reward labels significantly improves model calibration and alignment with human preferences, yielding up to a 10% accuracy increase in distinguishing good from bad robot actions.

## Problem

- Current embodied reward models systematically over-reward suboptimal, unsafe, or low-quality robot behaviors that human evaluators would penalize.
- Model performance degrades with increasing task complexity, indicating a misalignment with human preferences for nuanced execution and safety.
- Embodied datasets typically lack 'bad' robot behavior data (failures, errors), as it is difficult to collect and often filtered out, leading to biased reward models.

## Method

- The study evaluates three state-of-the-art embodied reward models (ReWind, GVL, Dopamine) using a human-centric evaluation protocol on real robot behaviors.
- It demonstrates the impact of intentionally injecting 'bad' robot behavior data, including text descriptions, robot videos, and dense reward labels of failures, into a VLM-based reward model (GVL).
- The approach specifically leverages in-context learning to provide negative examples without retraining, focusing on different granularities of negative data.

## Results

- All evaluated reward models systematically over-rewarded suboptimal behaviors, with Preference Ordering Accuracy dropping from **0.72**-**0.77** for simple tasks to **0.52**-**0.62** for complex tasks like Tool Use.
- Injecting text descriptions combined with actual robot failure videos improved accuracy by approximately **8%** for simpler tasks like Pick/Place and Push/Pull.
- The most substantial gains, an approximate **10%** accuracy improvement for complex tasks such as Tool Use and Pour Liquid, were achieved by providing dense, per-timestep reward labels for negative behavior examples.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art embodied reward models exhibit systematic over-optimism, over-rewarding behaviors that humans perceive as unsatisfactory or unsafe.
> - Text-only descriptions of failures are insufficient for improving VLM-based reward models, as they struggle to ground abstract criteria in concrete physical reality.
> - Explicit, visually grounded negative feedback, particularly dense, per-timestep reward labels associated with failure examples, is crucial for effectively calibrating embodied reward models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, specifically at the intersection of foundation models and embodied AI. Foundation models, such such as large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), have demonstrated capabilities across various domains, often relying on learned reward models for tasks like open-ended generation, dialogue, and summarization. Reward models are integral to the lifecycle of these models, facilitating reinforcement learning-based post-training, informing test-time compute, and enabling large-scale evaluation.

In recent years, embodied domains, including autonomous driving and household robotics, have begun developing their own foundation models, often mirroring the architectures and training paradigms of LLMs and VLMs. Vision–language–action (VLA) models are a prominent example in this context. The evaluation of robotic behavior is frequently subjective, difficult to specify precisely, and involves fine-grained physical consequences. Consequently, there is an increasing demand for general-purpose reward models capable of evaluating a physical agent's behavior directly from visual observations and textual task specifications. Such models aim to reduce the need for costly human evaluation while providing essential signals for post-training policy refinement and guiding actions during deployment.

However, the authors identify a discrepancy between the performance of current embodied reward models and human evaluators. Existing embodied reward models primarily leverage datasets biased towards successful behaviors. This paper addresses this gap by arguing for the necessity of incorporating "bad" robot data—comprising failed, suboptimal, error-prone, or hazardous behaviors—to improve the calibration and reliability of embodied reward models. This perspective contrasts with the prevailing imitation learning paradigm, which often prioritizes expert demonstrations and filters out negative data, thus contributing to models that exhibit systematic over-optimism. The research contributes to the discussion on data diversity and calibration challenges in embodied AI, proposing a strategic shift in data collection and model training for more robust and human-aligned robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this paper is to advocate for and demonstrate the necessity of integrating "bad" robot behavior data into the training and evaluation of embodied reward models. The authors propose that reliable embodied reward models require a deliberate shift away from datasets composed solely of successful or expert demonstrations towards the intentional collection and release of diverse failure data.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed limitations and challenges in the current state of embodied AI:

1. **Systematic Over-Optimism in Current Reward Models:** The paper identifies that contemporary embodied reward models systematically over-reward behaviors that human evaluators would deem unsatisfactory or unacceptable. These include low-quality execution, unsafe interactions with the environment, and "shortcut" strategies that appear to complete a task superficially but violate implicit human preferences.
2. **Increased Failure with Task Complexity:** This over-optimism becomes more pronounced as the complexity of physical tasks increases. For simple pick-and-place operations, models may perform adequately, but their alignment with human preferences degrades significantly for more complex tasks involving nuanced execution, temporal coordination, or implicit safety constraints (e.g., bi-manual tool use or pouring tasks).
3. **Data Gap in Negative Embodied Behavior:** The authors attribute these failures to a critical data gap. Unlike non-embodied domains (like LLMs), where diverse "bad" data (e.g., toxic language, logical fallacies) is abundant and relatively inexpensive to generate, embodied data collection is constrained by real-world factors such as wall-clock time and hardware safety. This makes the large-scale collection of failures, errors, or dangerous behaviors challenging and undesirable.
4. **Filtering of Negative Data:** Even when failures occur during robot operation, they are rarely preserved or released in public datasets. The dominant imitation-based learning paradigm tends to focus on expert demonstrations, actively filtering out or withholding negative data, leading to datasets that are heavily biased towards successful outcomes. This bias results in reward models that lack the necessary calibration to distinguish between genuinely good and subtly flawed or unsafe behaviors.
5. **Impact on Foundation Model Development:** As reward models define the optimization objective for reinforcement learning-based post-training and influence action selection during test-time compute, their calibration directly determines the upper bound for embodied foundation model performance and reliable evaluation. A poorly calibrated reward model can steer a foundation model towards undesirable or unsafe behaviors, despite appearing to make progress.

To address these issues, the paper aims to:
- Experimentally demonstrate the systematic over-rewarding behavior of state-of-the-art embodied reward models using real robot videos and human evaluation data.
- Show that even modest exposure to real "bad" robot behavior data can improve the alignment of reward models with human preferences and reduce the occurrence of high-impact false positives.
- Advocate for specific community actions to facilitate the collection, generation, and evaluation of "bad" embodied data, thereby improving the reliability and safety of future embodied AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the performance of current embodied reward models and the impact of injecting "bad" robot data. The methodology involves evaluating three distinct types of state-of-the-art reward models using a human-centric evaluation protocol and then analyzing how their performance changes with exposure to different forms of negative data.

#### 3.1. Embodied Reward Models Evaluated

The study evaluates three general-purpose, open-sourced embodied reward models, representing distinct paradigms:

1. **Preference-based Reward Models Trained with Synthetic Negatives (ReWind):** This category includes models like ReWind (Zhang et al., 2025), which are trained using preference-learning objectives. These models construct pseudo-negative or failure-like rollouts by perturbing or shuffling successful expert trajectories from large datasets (e.g., Open-X embodiment dataset). The model is trained to distinguish these synthetic negatives from successful executions.
2. **Zero-shot VLM Rewards (GVL):** This approach leverages vision-language models (VLMs) as zero-shot reward predictors. It frames reward prediction as an embodied reasoning task where the VLM first articulates evaluative reasoning (e.g., frame-level analysis) and then generates a dense reward sequence. The paper specifically uses GVL (Ma et al., 2024), querying GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) as the VLM backbone.
3. **Fine-tuned VLM Rewards (Dopamine):** These models fine-tune pre-trained VLMs to act as reward predictors, transferring semantic priors into task-specific embodied scoring functions. The fine-tuning signal typically comes from automatically or heuristically constructed task progress labels. The paper uses Dopamine (Tan et al., 2025), an 8B parameter model, in a zero-shot setting.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Protocol

The evaluation protocol focuses on quantifying the "goodness" of predicted rewards against human judgments, addressing the limitations of metrics like Value-Order Correlation (VOC) that primarily assess temporal progress.

- **Robot Behavior & Human Evaluation Dataset:** The study utilizes real robot behaviors and human evaluations from the RoboArena benchmark (Atreya et al., 2025). This dataset comprises a small number of real robot rollouts for various tasks, along with human-assigned continuous performance scores (0-100) and explicit pairwise preference judgments, accompanied by rationales.
- **Task Categories:** The evaluation covers seven task categories of increasing complexity: Pick/Place, Push/Pull, Open/Close, Stack Objects, Reorient Objects, Pour, and Tool Use, spanning rigid-body, soft-object manipulation, and multi-step tasks.
- **Ground Truth:** Human judgments are considered the ground truth. For each task, human-annotated rollout scores are used to induce an ordinal preference ranking over trajectories.
- **Reward Model Scoring:** For each reward model, a score for a rollout is computed by accumulating its predicted per-step rewards. This accumulated score then induces a corresponding ranking of rollouts.
- **Evaluation Metric:** The primary metric is **Preference Ordering Accuracy (A)**. This measures the agreement between the reward model's induced ranking and the human-derived ranking for all strictly preferred pairs of rollouts within a task context. A score of 1 indicates perfect agreement, while 0.5 suggests random guessing.

#### 3.3. Injecting "Bad" Robot Data into Embodied Reward Models

To demonstrate the impact of negative data, the researchers focus on the GVL model due to its in-context learning capabilities, allowing for negative example injection without retraining. Three levels of negative data exposure are explored:

1. **Text Descriptions of Failure:** GVL is prompted with concise, general summaries of common failure behaviors for a given task. These descriptions are distilled from the free-form text feedback provided by RoboArena evaluators.
2. **Text Descriptions & Robot Videos of Failures:** This method enhances the previous approach by pairing each failure description with a real robot video snippet that visually exhibits the described failure mode. These text-image pairs are provided as in-context examples to GVL. The hypothesis is that visual information offers finer-grained features beyond language alone.
3. **Text Descriptions, Robot Videos, and Dense Reward Labels of Failures:** This most comprehensive approach further augments the text and video inputs with dense, per-timestep reward labels associated with negative behavior examples. Since explicit dense oracle rewards are unavailable, proxy dense rewards are obtained through a preference-guided self-distillation process. This involves querying GVL multiple times for dense reward sequences for a human-preferred A/B test pair, retaining the sequence whose induced ordering matches human preference, and then using the dense reward sequence of the *less-preferred* robot behavior as an in-context example of how negative behavior should be evaluated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the limitations of current embodied reward models and the beneficial impact of integrating "bad" robot behavior data.

#### 4.1. Limitations of Current Embodied Reward Models (Section 3)

- **Systematic Over-Reward:** All three state-of-the-art embodied reward models (ReWind, GVL, Dopamine) were found to systematically over-reward behaviors that human evaluators would penalize. This includes instances of low-quality execution, unsafe interactions, and "shortcut" strategies that achieve superficial task completion without aligning with human preferences.
- **Degradation with Task Complexity:** The models' performance, measured by Preference Ordering Accuracy, significantly decreased as task complexity increased. While models achieved relatively high accuracy (0.72–0.77) on simple tasks like Pick/Place, their performance dropped to the low-to-mid 0.6 range for more nuanced tasks like Reorient Object and Pour. For the most complex task, Tool Use, accuracy was only modestly above random guessing (0.52–0.62).
- **Qualitative Examples:** Visual analysis of reward predictions showed models assigning high reward values even during clear failure events, such as a robot colliding with an object while lifting a lid or spilling nuts outside a plate during a pouring task. The models appeared to prioritize "progress-like" signals that correlate with success in their training data, while failing to sufficiently penalize critical negative behaviors.
- **Analysis of Failure Causes:** The paper attributes these failures to the lack of explicit negative embodied failure data. ReWind, which uses synthetic negatives by perturbing successful rollouts, struggles to capture realistic closed-loop failure modes. GVL inherits generic scoring from VLM pretraining but is poorly calibrated for safety- and quality-critical failures. Dopamine, relying on heuristic progress labels, overlooks fine-grained execution quality and safety.

#### 4.2. Impact of Injecting "Bad" Robot Data (Section 4)

- **Limited Impact of Text-Only Negatives:** Providing GVL with only textual descriptions of failures, distilled from human feedback, resulted in little to no significant improvement in preference ordering accuracy. This suggests that even powerful LLM backbones in VLMs struggle to ground abstract textual criteria into concrete physical, embodiment-specific robot behaviors.
- **Improvement with Text and Robot Videos:** Injecting both textual descriptions and actual robot failure videos as in-context examples led to an approximate 8% improvement in accuracy for simpler tasks, such as Pick/Place and Push/Pull. For these tasks, failures are often visually distinct (e.g., missing a grasp, placing an object incorrectly). Grounded visual counterexamples enabled GVL to more effectively down-weight undesirable behaviors.
- **Significant Gains with Dense Reward Labels of Failures:** The most substantial improvements, particularly for nuanced and complex tasks, were observed when GVL was provided with text descriptions, robot failure videos, and dense, per-timestep reward labels associated with negative behavior examples. This approach resulted in an approximate 10% accuracy improvement even in challenging tasks like Tool Use and Pour Liquid. Dense negative reward examples provided a concrete calibration signal, enabling the model to penalize subtle quality or safety violations that might otherwise be overlooked by coarse progress cues or visually ambiguous negatives. For instance, GVL was able to sharply down-weight a trajectory when a robot dropped a wiper, a subtle error, only when provided with dense negative reward examples.

These results indicate that the type and granularity of "bad" behavior data are crucial. Explicit, temporally resolved, and visually grounded negative feedback, especially in the form of dense reward labels, significantly enhances the calibration and human alignment of embodied reward models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of embodied AI by identifying a critical limitation in current reward modeling paradigms and proposing a direct, evidence-based solution. The systematic over-optimism of state-of-the-art embodied reward models, particularly in complex and nuanced tasks, underscores a fundamental misalignment with human preferences and safety considerations. The core finding—that reliable embodied reward models necessitate exposure to "bad" robot behavior data—challenges the prevailing practice of focusing primarily on successful demonstrations and highlights a crucial data gap.

The potential impact of this work is substantial and extends across several dimensions of embodied AI:

- **Improved Robot Safety and Reliability:** By enabling reward models to accurately identify and penalize unsafe interactions, poor execution, and shortcut behaviors, robots can be trained and deployed with higher levels of safety and reliability. This is particularly critical for applications in human-centered environments (e.g., household robotics, healthcare, autonomous driving) where failures can have severe consequences.
- **Enhanced Policy Learning and Evaluation:** Reward models serve as the optimization objective for reinforcement learning-based post-training and influence test-time action selection in embodied foundation models. A more calibrated reward model, informed by diverse failure data, can provide a more accurate learning signal, leading to the development of more robust and human-aligned robot policies. It also establishes a more realistic upper bound for performance evaluation, ensuring that models are assessed against human-relevant criteria rather than proxy metrics that can be misleading.
- **Shift in Data Collection Paradigms:** The paper's findings advocate for a deliberate shift in how embodied AI datasets are curated. It calls for the intentional collection and release of negative robot behavior data—failures, errors, and hazardous events—instead of discarding them. This could lead to the development of new data collection methodologies and infrastructure specifically designed to capture and categorize such critical information, moving beyond expert-only datasets.
- **Advancements in Synthetic Data Generation:** Recognizing the practical challenges and safety concerns associated with collecting real-world "bad" data at scale, the research highlights the need for advanced methods in synthetic failure generation. This could drive innovation in high-fidelity simulators, physically interactive real-to-sim models, and action-conditioned video world models capable of generating diverse and realistic failure trajectories. Such synthetic data could complement real-world failures, forming a virtuous cycle of data generation and model refinement.
- **Development of Robust Evaluation Benchmarks:** The paper underscores the inadequacy of existing evaluation benchmarks that rely on sparse labels or heuristic proxies. It calls for the development of new benchmarks for general-purpose embodied reward models that provide dense, human-annotated reward labels on real robot data, deeply grounded in human stakeholder values. This would ensure that progress in reward modeling is measured against truly meaningful and human-centric criteria.
- **Refinement of Vision-Language Models for Embodied AI:** The observation that text-only failure descriptions are insufficient for improving VLM-based reward models suggests that current VLMs, despite their reasoning capabilities, struggle with grounding abstract textual criteria in fine-grained physical reality. This finding can stimulate further research into enhancing the embodiment and physical grounding capabilities of VLMs for robotic applications.

In summary, this research provides a compelling argument and empirical evidence for the necessity of "bad" behavior data in embodied reward modeling. Its significance lies in guiding the development of safer, more reliable, and human-aligned embodied AI systems by addressing a fundamental data and calibration challenge.
