# Wavelet Phase Diffusion for Structurally and Semantically Consistent Sim-to-Real Translation

> [!summary] Summary
> ψ-PD is a phase-preserving diffusion framework for sim-to-real translation. It builds structured noise in the Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Packet Transform domain, injecting source phase per localized packet, and randomizes the low-frequency packet to shed the synthetic illumination prior. Trained on unpaired open-domain data with no conditioning modules, it tops realism and semantic consistency on vKITTI→KITTI and cuts VLM planner ADE 5.4% on CARLA video.

## Problem

- Conditioning-based translators align structure via dense depth, edge, or segmentation signals, but need dedicated control modules, heavy inference cost, and still leak synthetic artifacts.
- Paired-data editors reach high realism through expensive multi-stage synthesis pipelines that inject generator bias and freely alter geometry, weather, traffic-light state, and lane semantics.
- Training-free editors lack a learned appearance prior, while Fourier phase-preserving diffusion suffers global spectral coupling causing ringing and boundary leakage that corrupt structure.

## Method

- ==DT-CWPT phase injection== decomposes source and noise latents into one low-frequency and many localized complex high-frequency packets, applying packet-wise phase masks from a normalized cutoff map.
- ==Low-Frequency Randomization== swaps the source low-frequency packet for the noise one, decoupling generation from the synthetic global illumination prior so real-world lighting stays in-distribution.
- A spatially varying radius map turns the scalar cutoff into a per-pixel control, enabling **ψ-PD**'s zero-shot instance-level translation; backbones FLUX.1-dev and Wan 2.2-14B are fine-tuned unpaired.

## Results

- On vKITTI→KITTI, ψ-PD leads on KID **4.41**, CLIP-IQA **0.561**, and mIoU **43.50**, versus NeuralRemaster's **4.87**/**0.527**/**38.32** and Cosmos Transfer 2.5's **4.52**/**0.469**/**39.36**.
- On CARLA video it achieves best sKID **1.35** and second-best sFID **36.58**, cutting planner average ADE **5.4%** and FDE **5.1%**, while Ditto worsens ADE **2.0%**.
- Ablations shift the realism-consistency Pareto frontier outward: wavelet over Fourier raises mIoU at equal KID, and LFR gives monotone realism gains; noise construction costs **0.073s** per image.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Localized basis functions matter for structural constraints: global Fourier support turns filtering artifacts into spurious geometry cues that a strong diffusion prior faithfully hallucinates into.
> - Realism metrics alone mislead for sim-to-real; a downstream planner metric exposes methods that gain photorealism by silently rewriting traffic lights, lanes, or weather.
> - Coarse illumination lives in the low-frequency band, so randomizing it alone releases a model from source-domain lighting without touching structure-bearing higher bands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of sim-to-real translation, a critical area for deploying AI systems trained in synthetic environments to real-world applications such as autonomous driving and robotics. The core challenge in sim-to-real translation is to bridge the visual appearance gap between synthetic and real domains without compromising the structural and semantic integrity of the scene, which is essential for downstream perception and planning systems.

Current approaches to sim-to-real translation exhibit distinct limitations:
- **Conditioning-based methods** (e.g., Cosmos Transfer 2.5, VACE) achieve structural alignment by injecting dense control signals (e.g., depth maps, edge maps) into the generative process. However, these methods typically require dedicated control modules, incur significant computational overhead during inference, and may struggle to fully suppress synthetic artifacts if the control signals themselves originate from simulation.
- **Paired-data methods** (e.g., Ditto) offer high perceptual realism by training on synthetically generated paired data. The primary drawback here is the resource-intensive nature of creating such datasets, often relying on complex, multi-stage synthesis pipelines that can introduce generator-specific biases and limit generalization to novel environments. Furthermore, without explicit structural constraints, they are prone to altering scene geometry and semantics.
- **Training-free editing methods** (e.g., FlowEdit, DNAEdit) avoid the need for extensive training or conditioning. Despite this flexibility, they generally lack a learned appearance prior, which restricts their ability to achieve the high perceptual quality necessary to effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- **Phase-preserving diffusion methods** (e.g., NeuralRemaster) represent a more recent development. These methods are founded on the principle that the phase component of a signal primarily encodes geometric structure, while its magnitude governs texture statistics. By preserving the phase from the source image in a domain-invariant frequency band and randomizing the magnitude, these models aim to synthesize realistic textures without distorting structural layout. However, existing implementations, often utilizing Fourier transforms, suffer from global spectral coupling due to the global support of Fourier basis functions. This coupling can lead to spatial artifacts like ringing and boundary leakage, particularly in scenes with varied structural elements, thereby degrading the desired structural and semantic consistency.

The present work, Wavelet Phase Diffusion (ψ-PD), positions itself as an advancement within the phase-preserving diffusion paradigm. It aims to overcome the limitations of Fourier-domain approaches by leveraging a localized frequency representation and addressing the issue of synthetic illumination priors. This allows it to achieve high realism and consistency without requiring paired training data, heavy inference-time conditioning, or suffering from the global artifacts of previous phase-preserving methods. The proposed method also introduces capabilities for instance-level translation and broad generalization beyond simulation data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust sim-to-real translation framework that can bridge the visual appearance gap between synthetic and real domains while maintaining strict structural and semantic consistency. This goal is motivated by the critical dependence of downstream perception and planning systems (such as those in autonomous vehicles) on accurate and unaltered scene information. For such applications, a visually realistic translation that, for instance, hallucinates lane geometry or changes traffic sign semantics, is detrimental regardless of its photographic quality.

The specific motivations driving the development of ψ-PD are detailed as follows:
- **Addressing the computational and architectural overhead of conditioning-based methods:** The authors aim to avoid the need for dedicated control modules and substantial inference-time computation typically associated with conditioning-based translation techniques.
- **Eliminating the reliance on paired training data:** The high cost, complexity, and potential for domain bias introduced by synthetic paired dataset generation in methods like Ditto are a significant constraint that this work seeks to circumvent.
- **Achieving superior perceptual quality compared to training-free methods:** Recognizing that training-free approaches often lack the learned appearance priors necessary for high-fidelity realism, a key motivation is to integrate a generative prior capable of producing photorealistic outputs.
- **Overcoming the global spectral coupling inherent in Fourier-domain phase-preserving diffusion:** This is a central technical motivation. Prior Fourier-based phase preservation methods suffer from artifacts such as ringing and boundary leakage due to the global nature of Fourier basis functions. These artifacts can impose "spurious structural constraints," leading generative models to produce realistic but geometrically incorrect elements. The objective is to mitigate these issues to ensure accurate structural preservation.
- **Decoupling the generative model from synthetic global illumination priors:** Synthetic environments often possess characteristic lighting and illumination properties that differentiate them from real-world scenes. A goal is to enable the translation model to generate in-distribution real-world lighting, rather than being biased by the source's synthetic illumination.
- **Enabling spatially adaptive control and instance-level translation:** To enhance usability and flexibility, the method aims to allow for localized translation, where specific objects or regions within a scene can be translated to realism independently, while the rest of the scene remains unaltered.
- **Ensuring negligible inference overhead and architectural compatibility:** The new components should integrate seamlessly into existing diffusion backbones without requiring architectural modifications and introduce minimal additional computational cost during inference, facilitating practical adoption.

In essence, the research seeks to develop a sim-to-real translation method that concurrently achieves high realism, preserves structural and semantic integrity, is data-efficient (unpaired data), computationally efficient (low inference overhead), and offers fine-grained control over the translation process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ψ-PD operates within a phase-preserving diffusion framework, building on the principle that signal phase encodes structural information while magnitude carries texture statistics. The methodology introduces two primary innovations: operating in the Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Packet Transform (DT-CWPT) domain for localized phase injection, and Low-Frequency Randomization (LFR) to decouple from synthetic illumination priors.

**Preliminary: Phase-Preserving Diffusion Fundamentals**
The foundational concept involves decomposing a latent representation `x` using a complex-valued transform `T` into its magnitude `|T(x)|` and phase `∠T(x)`. Structured noise `ˆϵ` is then constructed by combining the phase of the source latent `x` with the magnitude of a Gaussian noise sample `ϵ`. A mask `M` dictates the spatial or spectral regions where source phase is injected. The combined representation `ˆy` is formed as `ˆy = |T(ϵ)| * e^(j * (M ⊙ ∠T(x) + (1-M) ⊙ ∠T(ϵ)))`, and the structured noise `ˆϵ` is obtained via the inverse transform `T⁻¹(ˆy)`. For video translation, this process is applied per meta-frame, with a two-stage pipeline where an initial frame's translation sets the appearance for subsequent frames to ensure temporal and visual consistency.

**Innovation 1: Dual-Tree Complex Wavelet Packet Transform (DT-CWPT) for Spatially Adaptive Phase Injection**
- **Problem with Fourier Transform:** When the Fourier Transform is used as `T`, its global basis support causes spectral modifications in one region to affect the entire image. This leads to non-local interference artifacts like ringing and boundary leakage, particularly around high-contrast edges. These artifacts can act as spurious structural constraints, causing the diffusion model to synthesize incorrect geometry.
- **ψ-PD's Solution:** The Fourier Transform `F` is replaced with the DT-CWPT as the transform `T`. DT-CWPT decomposes the latent `x` into a single real-valued low-frequency packet `L` and multiple complex-valued high-frequency packets `{H_l}`. Crucially, DT-CWPT coefficients are spatially localized, which substantially reduces non-local interference compared to Fourier bases. This localization is key to preventing global spectral coupling.
- **Phase Extraction:** DT-CWPT is applied to both the source latent and a Gaussian noise sample, yielding their respective `L` and `{H_l}` packets. The complex-valued high-frequency packets `{H_l}` allow for direct magnitude-phase decomposition.

**Innovation 2: Low-Frequency Randomization (LFR)**
- **Problem:** The low-frequency packet `L^(x)` derived from synthetic inputs often encodes the global illumination characteristics of the simulation domain. If preserved, this biases the generative model towards synthetic lighting.
- **ψ-PD's Solution:** LFR replaces the source's low-frequency packet `L^(x)` with the low-frequency packet derived from the Gaussian noise sample, `ˆL = L^(ϵ)`. This randomization decouples the generative model from the synthetic global illumination prior, allowing it to produce in-distribution, realistic coarse-scale appearance more faithfully aligned with the target real domain.
- **Application:** During training, LFR is applied with a probability `p=0.8`.

**Packet-wise Local Phase Injection**
- For each high-frequency packet `H_l`, phase injection is controlled by a cutoff map `ˆF`. This `ˆF` can be a global scalar radius `r` or a spatially varying radius map `R`, normalized to a unitless cutoff map indicating the highest normalized frequency up to which phase should be preserved at each location.
- The phase for `ˆH_l` is determined as `∠ˆH_l = M_l ⊙ ∠H^(x)_l + (1 − M_l) ⊙ ∠H^(ϵ)_l`, where `M_l` is an element-wise indicator function that determines whether source phase or noise phase is injected, based on `ˆF` and the packet's midpoint frequency. This packet-wise, localized control enables spatially adaptive phase injection, crucial for features like instance-level translation.

**Noise Reconstruction**
- After LFR for the low-frequency packet and packet-wise phase injection for high-frequency packets, the final structured noise `ˆϵ` is reconstructed using the inverse DT-CWPT: `ˆϵ = DT-CWPT⁻¹(ˆL, {ˆH_l}^L_{l=1})`.

**Implementation Details**
- **Training:** The model is trained on unpaired, open-domain image and video datasets. The cutoff radius `r` is dynamically sampled from a shifted exponential distribution. The decomposition depth `J` for DT-CWPT is also dynamically sampled.
- **Inference:** By default, a single global scalar cutoff radius `r` (half the Nyquist frequency) and `J=4` are used. Spatially varying cutoff maps (`R`) are used for instance-level translation.
- **Backbones:** FLUX.1-dev serves as the image backbone, and Wan 2.2-14B as the video backbone, both fine-tuned.
- **Video-specific consistency:** An initial frame is translated by the image stage to establish a photorealistic reference, which then conditions the video stage to ensure consistent appearance across subsequent frames.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that ψ-PD achieves improvements across multiple metrics for both image and video sim-to-real translation, particularly in realism, semantic consistency, and downstream utility.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

- **vKITTI → KITTI Image Translation (Table 1):**
 - ψ-PD achieved the best realism (KID 4.41) and perceptual quality (CLIP-IQA 0.561).
 - It also showed the highest semantic consistency (mIoU 43.50) among translation methods, outperforming competitors like NeuralRemaster (mIoU 38.32) and Cosmos Transfer 2.5 (mIoU 39.36).
 - Structural alignment metrics (DepSSIM 0.839, AbsRel 0.229) were competitive, approaching the best conditioning-based method (Cosmos Transfer 2.5, DepSSIM 0.870, AbsRel 0.202), which achieved structural alignment at the cost of lower perceptual quality and mIoU.
 - Training-free methods (FlowEdit, DNAEdit) showed limited improvements in realism and perceptual quality. NeuralRemaster, while having competitive perceptual quality, showed the worst structural alignment (AbsRel 0.344) and lowest mIoU, supporting the authors' claim about global spectral coupling issues in Fourier-domain methods.

- **CARLA Video Translation (Table 2):**
 - ψ-PD achieved the best realism (sKID 1.35) and second-best sFID (36.58), comparable to the paired-data method Ditto.
 - It was the only evaluated method to concurrently improve realism, perceptual quality (CLIP-IQA 0.495), and crucial downstream planning utility. Specifically, ψ-PD reduced the VLM planner's average ADE by 5.4% and FDE by 5.1%, outperforming all baselines including those that achieve high realism (NeuralRemaster, Ditto) but degrade planning metrics due to inconsistencies.
 - Temporal coherence (MS 98.58%) was preserved at the level of the input.

- **Inference Overhead:** The computational overhead introduced by ψ-PD's noise construction was found to be negligible. For images, it added 0.073 seconds (less than 0.15% of FLUX inference time), and for video, 0.096 seconds (less than 0.15% of Wan inference time). This contrasts with conditioning-based methods that add substantial per-step costs.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results

- **vKITTI → KITTI (Fig. 4):** Qualitative comparisons showed that ψ-PD produced realistic outputs devoid of common artifacts observed in other methods. FlowEdit and NeuralRemaster sometimes hallucinated structural elements (e.g., an ego-vehicle dashboard) or reversed vehicle orientations. Cosmos Transfer 2.5 avoided these but introduced incorrect lane semantics and desaturated appearances.
- **CARLA Video (Fig. 5, Fig. 6):** ψ-PD generated photorealistic textures comparable to Ditto while preserving critical scene semantics. Baselines either retained a synthetic appearance (DNAEdit) or introduced significant artifacts (VACE's lens flare) or altered fundamental scene elements (Ditto changing weather conditions, traffic light states, and lane markings). In a VLM planning task, ψ-PD's translated frames led to predicted waypoints closely aligned with ground truth. In contrast, Ditto's translation of a green traffic light to red caused the VLM planner to drastically alter its trajectory, resulting in large deviations from the ground truth.

#### 4.3. Zero-Shot Instance-Level Translation

- The spatially adaptive cutoff map feature of ψ-PD enables zero-shot instance-level translation without requiring specific instance-level supervision. This allows for selective translation of individual objects or regions to a photorealistic appearance, while the surrounding scene remains untranslated.
- This capability was demonstrated not only on simulation data but also on non-simulated sources like pixel-art comic scenes (Fig. 7), highlighting its generalization ability. Unlike methods such as Ditto, which indiscriminately translate entire frames, ψ-PD maintains per-pixel selectivity.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study (Fig. 8)

- **DT-CWPT vs. Fourier Phase Injection:** A comparison between ψ-PD without LFR and NeuralRemaster demonstrated that DT-CWPT significantly improved the realism-consistency Pareto frontier. At comparable realism (KID), ψ-PD w/o LFR achieved higher semantic consistency (mIoU) than NeuralRemaster. This supports the argument that the localized support of wavelet packets reduces spurious structural constraints caused by global spectral coupling in Fourier-domain methods, leading to better structural preservation.
- **Low-Frequency Randomization (LFR):** The full ψ-PD model (with LFR) further extended the Pareto frontier towards greater realism (lower KID and higher CLIP-IQA). This indicates that randomizing the low-frequency packet effectively decouples the generative process from the synthetic global illumination prior of the source, enabling more realistic in-distribution appearance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Wavelet Phase Diffusion (ψ-PD) represents a notable advancement in the field of sim-to-real translation, with several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Reliability for Downstream AI Systems:** The most significant impact is the demonstrated ability of ψ-PD to not only generate photorealistic imagery but also to rigorously preserve structural and semantic consistency. This is crucial for real-world applications such as autonomous driving, where downstream perception, planning, and control systems rely heavily on accurate geometric and semantic cues. The direct improvement in VLM planner performance (reduced ADE and FDE) illustrates a tangible benefit for functional utility beyond mere visual appeal.
- **Increased Efficiency and Accessibility:** By circumventing the need for computationally expensive paired training data and heavy inference-time conditioning, ψ-PD offers a more efficient and accessible solution for sim-to-real translation. Its negligible inference overhead makes it suitable for integration into real-time or near real-time operational pipelines. Furthermore, its ability to integrate into arbitrary diffusion backbones without architectural modifications lowers the barrier to adoption for developers.
- **Fine-Grained Control and Flexibility:** The introduction of spatially adaptive phase injection through DT-CWPT enables unprecedented fine-grained control over the translation process. This is exemplified by the zero-shot instance-level translation capability, allowing users to selectively translate individual objects or regions within a scene. This feature has considerable potential for targeted data augmentation, interactive scene editing, and controlled experimentation in simulation environments.
- **Broader Applicability and Generalization:** The method's ability to generalize zero-shot beyond traditional simulation data to other non-photorealistic sources, such as comic illustrations, suggests a wider range of applications beyond its primary sim-to-real focus. This adaptability could extend its utility to various image and video editing tasks requiring domain translation with structural fidelity.
- **Foundational Contribution to Generative Modeling:** The technical innovations, particularly the use of DT-CWPT for localized spectral manipulation and the concept of Low-Frequency Randomization, offer new strategies for controlling generative processes. These principles could inspire further research in structured generative synthesis, particularly in areas where precise control over frequency-domain features is critical for maintaining content integrity.

In summary, ψ-PD advances the state-of-the-art in sim-to-real translation by providing a robust, efficient, and controllable framework that addresses key limitations of prior methods. Its direct positive impact on downstream planning performance and its versatile application capabilities underscore its potential to significantly benefit the development and deployment of intelligent systems.
