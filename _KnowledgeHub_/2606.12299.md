# Learning What to Say to Your VLA: Mostly Harmless Vision Language Action Model Steering

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework developed by Carnegie Mellon University enhances the reliability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by learning a language feedback policy that steers frozen VLAs through their language input. This system improves task success rates (e.g., 12.7 percentage points in simulation) and robustness to out-of-distribution scenarios, concurrently controlling the false positive rate of harmful steering interventions.

## Problem

- VLA models exhibit brittle and unpredictable language-to-action mapping, where minor semantic variations in instructions can lead to erroneous robot behaviors.
- Users struggle to intuit how to rephrase instructions to achieve desired behaviors, and certain VLA capabilities may not be consistently elicitable through simple prompting.
- Existing methods for VLA robustness often involve fine-tuning the VLA policy or relying on external feedback, which can be computationally expensive or modify core capabilities.

## Method

- A framework is developed to learn a language feedback policy (LFP) that steers a frozen pre-trained **VLA** purely through its language input, without modifying the base VLA.
- The solution integrates narrated fine-tuning of a smaller VLM to create a prior for plausible language sequences, followed by interactive language search using an LLM to perturb these sequences and identify beneficial steering.
- A "conformalized improvement head" is trained to predict when language steering will be beneficial and, critically, to abstain from intervening when harm is predicted, providing statistical guarantees on the false positive rate of harmful steering.

## Results

- The language feedback policies demonstrated superior success rates (e.g., π_RFT improved performance by **12.7** percentage points in the training environment over the base VLA) and robustness across various in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions in simulation and hardware.
- Conformalization significantly reduced the false positive rate of harmful steering from **38.92%** to **9.31%** in simulation and from **61.11%** to **2.22%** in hardware, effectively controlling the rate of undesirable interventions.
- The closed-loop language feedback approach proved more sample-efficient than direct VLA fine-tuning (e.g., comparable performance with one-fifth of the data) and enabled dynamic recovery from mid-task environmental perturbations, outperforming open-loop prompt search.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective steering of frozen VLAs can be achieved by learning a closed-loop language feedback policy that adapts to evolving scene states and corrects robot behavior.
> - Leveraging narrated video data to establish a prior for language sequences and then performing interactive search over trajectory-level language perturbations makes the vast language search space tractable.
> - Conformal prediction can be applied to language steering to provide principled statistical guarantees of harmlessness, allowing the system to refuse interventions that are likely to degrade performance, especially in out-of-distribution settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant advancement in robotics, enabling robots to interpret natural language instructions and translate them into physical actions based on visual observations. These models, often trained on extensive datasets, offer a flexible interface for controlling complex robotic behaviors without requiring direct programming of low-level motor commands. However, a prevalent challenge in the field is the inherent brittleness and unpredictability of the language-to-action mapping within these models. Minor semantic variations in instructions can lead to substantially different or erroneous robot behaviors, and certain capabilities may not be elicitable through simple prompting. This unpredictability limits the reliability of both human instructions and zero-shot language models in consistently achieving desired task outcomes with VLAs.

Existing research in robot steering and natural language control has explored various approaches, including hierarchical control structures, human feedback, demonstrations, and prompt optimization techniques. Some methods guide VLA behavior through residual policies, external reward models, or verification signals, while others focus on refining or quantifying language-following capabilities. Separately, efforts to improve VLA robustness have concentrated on low-level action guardrails, latent activation steering, or failure prediction mechanisms to ensure safe and reliable deployment.

This work contributes to this landscape by addressing the brittleness of VLA language interfaces specifically. Unlike methods that fine-tune the underlying VLA policy or rely on external feedback modalities beyond language, this research proposes a framework to *learn a language feedback policy* that steers a *frozen* pre-trained VLA purely through its language input. A key distinguishing aspect is the introduction of a "conformalized improvement head," which allows the system to predict *when* to intervene with language steering and, crucially, *when to abstain* to prevent harmful actions, especially in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. This focus on principled refusal and harmlessness, achieved without modifying the core VLA, positions the work at the intersection of VLA steering, interactive learning, and robustness guarantees through conformal prediction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. While VLAs offer the promise of intuitive natural language control, their practical deployment is hindered by the unpredictable and non-robust nature of their language-to-behavior mapping. This unpredictability manifests in several ways:
1. **Brittleness to Semantic Variation**: Instructions that are semantically similar can lead to drastically different robot behaviors, making it difficult for users to formulate reliable commands.
2. **Unintuitive Responses**: It is often unclear a priori how to rephrase an instruction to elicit a desired behavior when the initial prompt fails. The vast, open-vocabulary nature of language makes exhaustive search for effective prompts intractable.
3. **Limited Steerability**: In some cases, the desired behavior may not be achievable through language conditioning at all, either because the VLA ignores language input or because the required low-level skill is outside its behavioral repertoire.

Given these challenges, the overarching objective of this research is to develop a framework that enhances the reliability and robustness of natural language steering for frozen, pre-trained VLA models. The framework aims to enable consistent task execution by learning to provide effective language feedback.

More specific objectives include:
- **Learning an Effective Language Feedback Policy (LFP)**: To develop a mechanism that can interactively identify and distill effective language sequences into a policy that guides the VLA's behavior over time.
- **Addressing the Large Search Space**: To structure the search for beneficial language sequences in a computationally tractable manner, avoiding exhaustive exploration of the open-vocabulary language space.
- **Predicting Steering Efficacy**: To learn an "improvement head" that can predict, for a given initial state and task, whether applying language steering is likely to improve performance over simply using the original task instruction.
- **Ensuring Harmlessness**: To introduce a mechanism that prevents language steering from degrading task performance, particularly in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. This involves calibrating the improvement head to control the rate of harmful interventions.
- **Operating on Frozen VLAs**: To achieve these objectives without requiring access to the VLA's original training distribution or fine-tuning the underlying VLA model itself, thereby maintaining its pre-trained capabilities and reducing computational overhead.

By addressing these objectives, the researchers aim to make VLA models more predictable, robust, and dependable for real-world robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, termed "Mostly Harmless Vision Language Action Model Steering," is designed to learn a language feedback policy (LFP) that steers a frozen VLA. It frames language steering as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where natural language strings serve as actions, conditioned on observations and task instructions. The dynamics are induced by the frozen VLA interacting with the real world, and the reward is task success. The methodology proceeds in three distinct phases: Narrated Fine-Tuning, Interactive Language Search, and Conformalizing the Improvement Head.

**Phase 1: Narrated Fine-Tuning**
To address the combinatorial vastness of the language search space, the framework first establishes a structured proposal distribution over task-relevant language sequences. This is achieved by:
- **Data Collection**: Gathering observation-only videos of robot behavior (D_exp), each paired with a task description (ℓ_task).
- **Narration**: Utilizing a Vision Language Model (VLM), Molmo2-8B, to caption these videos frame-by-frame, generating a sequence of language descriptions (ℓ_0:T) that detail the robot's subtasks at each observation (o). This creates a narrated dataset (D_nar) of (o, ℓ_task, ℓ) tuples.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: The base VLM, Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, is then fine-tuned on D_nar using a cross-entropy objective to produce π_SFT. This policy serves as a strong prior for generating plausible, context-aware language actions, making subsequent local search tractable.

**Phase 2: Interactive Language Search**
This phase focuses on identifying language sequences that empirically improve performance and distilling them into a robust LFP, while also learning to predict the benefit of steering.
- **Seed Sequence Generation**: For a given task instruction (ℓ_task) and environment context (e), π_SFT generates an initial language sequence (ℓ_0:T) by interacting with the VLA and the environment.
- **Trajectory-Level Perturbations**: A Large Language Model (LLM), GPT-5.4, is used to generate N=16 semantic perturbations of this seed sequence. These perturbations involve rewriting the entire sequence, varying verbs, nouns, and compositional phrasing while preserving task semantics. This defines a local language set (L_local).
- **Improvement Estimation**: Each perturbed language sequence in L_local is evaluated through closed-loop VLA rollouts. Monte Carlo estimates of "language improvement" (b_∆) are computed, comparing the success rate of the VLA steered by the perturbed sequence against the base VLA conditioned only on the original ℓ_task.
- **Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT)**: The language sequence (ℓ*) yielding the highest estimated improvement (b_∆ > 0) is selected. These successful (o, ℓ_task, ℓ*) tuples are collected into a rejection fine-tuning dataset (D_RFT). The LFP (π_RFT) is then trained (via LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct) on D_RFT, effectively distilling the beneficial steering behaviors.
- **Improvement Head Training**: Concurrently, an "improvement head" (ψ), a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) built on the frozen π_RFT backbone's final hidden state, is trained on a dataset (D_∆) of (o_0, ℓ_task, e, b_∆) tuples. This head uses square loss regression to predict the estimated improvement (b_∆) of steering at an initial state.

**Phase 3: Conformalizing the Improvement Head**
To prevent harmful steering interventions, especially in OOD scenarios where the improvement head might be unreliable, a conformal prediction technique is applied.
- **Calibration Data**: A calibration set (D_cal) is constructed from held-out visual and semantic OOD perturbations. For each sample (X_i, e_i, b_∆_i), a binary label Y_i = I{b_∆_i ≥ 0} is assigned, indicating whether steering was beneficial (Y=1) or harmful (Y=0).
- **Threshold Setting**: A steering threshold (ˆq_α) is determined by computing the empirical (1-α) quantile of the improvement scores (s_i = ψ(X_i)) specifically for the *harmful* examples (Y_i=0) within D_cal.
- **Deployment**: At deployment, the system employs language steering from π_RFT *only if* the improvement head's prediction for the current state (ψ(X)) exceeds the calibrated threshold (ˆq_α). Otherwise, the system abstains from steering and reverts to providing the original task instruction (ℓ_task) to the VLA.
This conformalization provides a statistical guarantee that the false positive rate of harmful steering is controlled at a user-specified level α, thereby ensuring "mostly harmless" interventions. The VLA remains frozen throughout, preserving its original capabilities while the LFP learns to interactively steer it effectively and safely.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research addressed three primary questions through extensive simulation and hardware experiments.

**Q1: How does language steering compare to the base VLA and direct VLA fine-tuning in terms of in-distribution performance, out-of-distribution performance, and sample complexity?**

- **Robustness to Perturbations**: The language feedback policies (π_RFT and π_CP) demonstrated superior performance across various conditions.
 - In simulation, π_RFT and π_CP achieved the highest success rates in all four evaluated conditions (training environment, ID scene/OOD distractor, OOD scene/ID distractor, OOD scene/OOD distractor). For example, π_RFT improved performance in the training environment by 12.7 percentage points (pp) and in the ID Scene/OOD Distractor by 8.3 pp over the base VLA (π_VLA).
 - Narration alone (π_SFT) improved in-distribution (ID) performance but led to degraded out-of-distribution (OOD) performance.
 - Direct VLA fine-tuning (π_VLA-SFT) showed improvements in training-like conditions but performed worse in OOD scene conditions, indicating a lack of generalization compared to language steering.
 - In hardware, π_RFT improved over π_VLA on tasks like CubeSort, CubeMug, and MarkerBlock. Critically, it also showed positive transfer to novel, unseen tasks such as MarkerBowl and ChipsCup, suggesting that language steering can leverage existing VLA skills in new contexts.
- **Novel Behavior Composition**: π_RFT successfully steered the VLA to perform unseen behavior compositions, outperforming direct VLA fine-tuning which sometimes degraded performance compared to the base VLA.
- **Sample Efficiency**: A data scaling study revealed that π_RFT learned more efficiently from on-policy rollouts. π_RFT trained with only 10 successful rollouts per task achieved performance comparable to or better than π_VLA-SFT trained with 50 rollouts, using one-fifth of the data. Furthermore, π_RFT continued to improve with additional data, whereas π_VLA-SFT plateaued.

**Q2: Can conformalization prevent harmful steering?**

- **Performance Improvement with Refusal**: Refusal mechanisms, both uncalibrated (π_RFT) and conformalized (π_CP), consistently improved overall success rates in simulation. Moving from no refusal (π_NR) to uncalibrated refusal (π_RFT) increased success from 70.93% to 74.96%. Conformalization (π_CP) further improved this to 75.96%.
- **Control over Harmful Steering**: Conformalization significantly reduced the False Positive Rate (FPR), which represents instances where harmful steering was incorrectly applied.
 - In simulation, the FPR dropped from 38.92% for π_RFT to 9.31% for π_CP (at a target α=0.1), aligning closely with the target error rate. The True Negative Rate (TNR), representing correct refusal of harmful steering, increased from 61.08% to 90.69%.
 - In hardware, the FPR decreased from 61.11% to 2.22%, with accuracy rising from 84.72% to 99.17%, while only slightly reducing the True Positive Rate (TPR) for beneficial steering.
- **Reliability**: Empirical FPR was observed to track the target α across different conformalization levels, demonstrating effective control over harmful interventions. This was particularly evident on tasks like Microwave in hardware, where π_CP mitigated performance degradation under semantic perturbation.

**Q3: Is closed-loop language feedback necessary, or is open-loop prompt search sufficient?**

- **Necessity of Closed-Loop Feedback**: Trajectory-level closed-loop language feedback outperformed open-loop prompt search strategies. This suggests that allowing the language policy to adapt its prompts as the scene evolves is crucial for improved performance.
- **Recovery from Perturbations**: Closed-loop steering enabled the robot to recover from dynamic environmental perturbations mid-task. For instance, when a human removed a previously placed object, the closed-loop LFP (π_RFT) observed the changed state and generated a corrective language instruction, guiding the frozen VLA to re-attempt and successfully complete the subtask. The base VLA, in contrast, continued with its original plan and failed to recover.
- **Search Space Structure**: Trajectory-level language sequence perturbations were found to be more effective than phrase-level perturbations of individual language actions. This implies that designing edits at the sequence level, which maintains contextual consistency, makes the search space more tractable while yielding high-quality candidates.

**Additional Findings (from Appendix):**
- **Inference Latency**: The addition of the language feedback policy (π_RFT) increased the per-replanning step latency from 68ms (base VLA) to 274ms, reducing the effective replanning frequency from 14.7 Hz to 3.6 Hz. The primary bottleneck was identified as autoregressive language generation (204ms).
- **Successful Trajectory Length**: In several hardware tasks, π_RFT not only improved success rates but also led to shorter successful trajectories compared to the base VLA, suggesting more efficient execution strategies (e.g., 35.4% reduction on ChipsCup).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a framework that significantly advances the reliability and safety of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic applications. The core contribution lies in developing a closed-loop language feedback policy (LFP) that can effectively steer arbitrary *frozen* pre-trained VLAs, crucially incorporating a mechanism to prevent harmful interventions, particularly in unseen or out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios.

The significance of this work can be highlighted through several points:

- **Enhanced Reliability and Robustness**: By learning to interactively identify and apply beneficial language edits, the framework substantially improves VLA performance and generalization across visual, semantic, and novel task composition perturbations. This addresses a major limitation of current VLAs, making them more dependable in varied and dynamic environments.
- **Principled Harmlessness Guarantees**: The integration of class-conditional conformal prediction provides a robust statistical guarantee against harmful steering interventions. This "mostly harmless" approach is critical for the safe deployment of robotic systems, as it allows the system to refuse interventions when the predicted benefit is uncertain or likely negative. This moves beyond heuristic safety measures by offering quantifiable error rate control.
- **Sample-Efficient Adaptation**: The language steering approach is shown to be significantly more sample-efficient than directly fine-tuning the underlying VLA action policy. This is a practical advantage, as collecting and labeling large-scale robot interaction data is expensive and time-consuming. Adapting the language interface, rather than the low-level policy, offers a higher-level abstraction for learning.
- **Leveraging Existing Models without Retraining**: The ability to operate on frozen, pre-trained VLAs without requiring access to their original training data or fine-tuning them is a major practical benefit. It allows for the widespread adoption of this steering mechanism with existing foundation models, extending their capabilities without incurring the high computational and data costs of full model retraining.
- **Enabling Adaptive Recovery Behaviors**: The closed-loop nature of the LFP enables dynamic adaptation and recovery from unexpected mid-task perturbations. This is a crucial capability for real-world robustness, allowing robots to adjust their internal interpretation of the task based on evolving environmental states.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction**: By making VLAs more predictable and robust, the framework contributes to more intuitive and effective human-robot interaction. Users can have greater confidence that their instructions, or subtle rephrasing, will lead to desired outcomes, reducing frustration and increasing usability.

**Potential Impact:**
The potential impact of this research is substantial for robotic learning and deployment. It offers a practical and theoretically grounded method for making advanced VLA models more robust and safer for real-world applications ranging from industrial automation to domestic assistance. The principle of learning a safe and adaptive interface, rather than constantly retraining the core model, could become a standard paradigm for deploying large foundation models in robotics.

**Limitations:**
Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge several limitations. Language steering is one of many potential control modalities; future work could explore the complementary roles of editing observations, steering model latents, or directly manipulating action generation. The current language search strategy is local, and more efficient methods for exploring diverse and broader linguistic perturbations could be beneficial. Finally, the inference latency introduced by autoregressive language generation remains a bottleneck, suggesting opportunities for optimizing the LFP's computational efficiency through distillation or asynchronous updates.
