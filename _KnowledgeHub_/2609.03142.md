# Sensing Which Modality Matters: Evidence-Gated Regularization for Robust VLA Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Evidence-Gated Regularization (EGR) is a training-time objective for Vision-Language-Action policies that computes a per-frame, per-sensor task-relevance evidence score from task structure, then gates two consistency losses: invariance to corrupting low-evidence sensors, and single-sensor sufficiency on high-evidence ones. It adds no inference overhead, lifting BEHAVIOR-1K manipulation success from 12.5% to 16.4% and real bi-manual distractor success from 30% to 85%.

## Problem

- Early multimodal fusion on small, homogeneous robot demonstrations makes VLA policies latch onto spurious inter-sensor correlations instead of task-relevant signal, a failure termed modality entanglement.
- Random modality dropout and adversarial multi-sensor consistency treat all sensors and timesteps uniformly, improving generic robustness but never performing state-dependent modality selection.
- Learned attention inherits the same spurious co-occurrences from limited demonstrations, and architectural gates such as contact-triggered tactile gating are tied to one modality and cue.

## Method

- ==Evidence score==: per-frame, per-sensor task relevance derived from task structure, focal and gated interaction-object pixel area for cameras, contact indicator or task-feature similarity for tactile.
- Two ==hinge-gated consistency losses== on ==flow-matching velocities==: ==invariance== when a low-evidence sensor is corrupted, ==sufficiency== when only a high-evidence sensor survives, added to the base ==imitation loss==.
- ==BEHAVIOR-1K modality-entanglement benchmark==: 47 evidence-filtered segments (11 navigation, 36 manipulation), an inference-only action-deviation suite and a 20-rollout success suite.

## Results

- On the rollout suite, manipulation success rose **12.5%** to **16.4%** clean, **9.4%** to **16.5%** under useless-sensor corruption, **2.8%** to **6.1%** single-sensor; navigation fallback **15.5%** to **37.3%**.
- Bi-manual Kinova platform: physical-distractor success **30%** to **85%**, UsefulOnly **25%** to **72.5%**; the vision-tactile MELFA setup improved SingleUseful **40%** to **90%** and distractors **55%** to **70%**.
- Action-deviation ratios fell from **+31.4%** to **+15.8%** (navigation) and **+158.5%** to **+121.8%** (manipulation), while modality dropout did not eliminate entanglement and hurt clean manipulation success (**12.5%** to **7.5%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robustness regularizers grounded in task structure beat uniform dropout, which spreads capacity across sensors that carry no signal at the current state.
> - Separating modality-specific evidence construction from modality-agnostic regularization lets one objective cover cameras and tactile sensors without changing the policy architecture.
> - Gains need evidence specifiable a priori; tasks whose relevant signal must be learned, like in-hand reorientation, stay out of reach, and absolute success rates remain low.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has advanced significantly, enabling embodied agents to execute increasingly complex tasks. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies represent a prominent paradigm, often built upon large model backbones that integrate visual, linguistic, and action-relevant information through early multimodal fusion.

However, multimodal learning, particularly with early fusion architectures, is susceptible to "shortcut learning," a phenomenon where models exploit spurious cross-modal correlations present in the training data rather than true task-relevant signals. This issue has been characterized in general multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and is amplified in VLA policies due to the typically smaller and less diverse nature of robot demonstration datasets compared to web-scale corpora. Existing research has explored various forms of shortcut learning, but the specific problem of VLA policies developing spurious dependencies on sensory modalities that are not task-relevant at a given state – termed "modality entanglement" by the authors – has not been explicitly isolated and addressed.

Prior work on modality robustness in multi-sensor policies includes techniques like random modality dropout and adversarial multi-sensor consistency, which aim for generic robustness but do not perform state-dependent modality selection. Attention-based methods allow policies to learn such selection, but their effectiveness can be limited by the same homogeneous demonstration data that causes entanglement. Explicit architectural gates, such as those used for tactile sensing activation, are often specialized to a single modality and cue, requiring re-design for broader applicability.

This research aims to bridge a gap in the literature by proposing a modality-selection method that is explicit, grounded in task structure, and decoupled from any particular sensor modality, addressing the limitations of existing approaches that rely on architectural changes, run-time interventions, or end-to-end learned attention without explicit task grounding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies against "modality entanglement," a failure mode arising from training on limited and homogeneous robot demonstration data. Modality entanglement leads policies to develop spurious dependencies on sensory modalities that are irrelevant at a given state, rather than relying on task-relevant signals.

The authors identify and formalize two state-dependent failure modes that exemplify modality entanglement:

1. **Nuisance Sensitivity:** This occurs when a sensory modality, `m`, is task-irrelevant at a particular frame, `t`. Perturbing this irrelevant modality should ideally not affect the policy's action distribution or task success rate. However, under nuisance sensitivity, such perturbations cause a degradation in the policy's behavior. An illustrative example provided is a navigation task where a head camera provides task-relevant information, while downward-facing wrist cameras observe only the floor. If the policy spuriously associates the floor appearance with the correct path, adding physical distractors to the wrist-camera views alone can stall the policy, even if the head-camera view remains unchanged. This indicates an undue dependence on uninformative inputs.

2. **Single-Modality Insufficiency:** This failure mode arises when a single modality, `m`, is sufficient on its own to guide the task at a given frame, `t`, but other task-relevant modalities are also present. If all other task-relevant modalities become corrupted, the policy should ideally be able to fall back to the intact, sufficient modality without a change in its action distribution or a degradation in task success. However, under single-modality insufficiency, the policy fails to effectively utilize the remaining single informative sensor, leading to erratic behavior or task failure. An example is a reaching task where both head and wrist cameras initially observe a target. If the target is placed deeper, occluding the head camera while the wrist camera still sees the target, the policy might stop or behave erratically, suggesting it has not learned to rely solely on the wrist camera as sufficient evidence.

The motivation for addressing these issues stems from the practical challenges faced by VLA policies in real-world scenarios. Real-world environments are characterized by occlusions, novel distractors, and varying sensor availability, which can expose these vulnerabilities. Current VLA policies, especially those relying on early multimodal fusion, are prone to exploiting superficial cross-modal correlations from constrained training data. The absence of a mechanism to discern the state-dependent task relevance of individual sensors makes these policies fragile.

The key objective, therefore, is to develop a training objective that enables VLA policies to dynamically determine which sensors provide task-relevant evidence at each frame. This solution should be:
- **Explicit:** Grounded in task structure rather than relying solely on fragile demonstration statistics.
- **Modality-agnostic:** Applicable consistently across heterogeneous sensor types (e.g., vision, tactile) without requiring specific architectural re-designs for each.
- **Zero inference-time overhead:** Imposed only during training, ensuring no computational burden during deployment.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to improve the robustness and reliability of VLA policies, enabling them to generalize better to unseen conditions and operate effectively despite sensor corruptions or distractors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces Evidence-Gated Regularization (EGR), a training objective designed to mitigate modality entanglement in VLA policies. EGR operates by explicitly estimating the task-relevance of each sensor per frame and using this "evidence score" to gate two complementary consistency objectives during training. The core design principle is the decoupling of per-frame sensor relevance computation from its effect on policy learning.

**Problem Setup (Section 3.1):**
A VLA policy, denoted πθ(at | ot), maps a multi-sensor observation ot = {om t }m∈M to an action distribution. The set M comprises all sensor modalities. `o m t` denotes an observation where only modality `m` is retained, and all other modalities are corrupted. Modality entanglement is defined as a misalignment between a policy's behavioral dependence on modality `m` and its actual importance for task completion at (t, m). This misalignment manifests as nuisance sensitivity (perturbing an irrelevant `m` changes the policy's action) and single-modality insufficiency (policy fails to use a sufficient `m` when others are corrupted).

**Evidence as a Task-Relevance Indicator (Section 3.2):**
For each frame `t` and sensor `m`, an evidence score `Et,m ∈` is assigned, indicating the task-relevant signal carried by that sensor. This score is derived from task properties rather than being learned end-to-end. Scores are normalized across modalities at each frame.

- **Vision Instantiation (Section 3.2.1):** Vision evidence measures the visibility of task-relevant geometry. This involves identifying "focal objects" (being manipulated) and "interaction objects" (receptacles/targets) from per-frame instance segmentation. For camera `m` at frame `t`, `A focal t,m,k` and `A inter t,m,k` denote the pixel-area ratios of focal object `k` and interaction object `k`, respectively. These are aggregated into `a focal t,m` and `a inter t,m`. To prevent large, irrelevant interaction objects from skewing evidence, interaction objects are included only if a focal object is visible in the same camera (`g t,m = ⊮[a focal t,m > ϵ focal]`). The per-camera interaction score `s t,m` combines focal and gated interaction contributions: `s t,m = a focal t,m + α·g t,m·a inter t,m`. Cross-camera normalization yields the final evidence `Et,m = s t,m / (max m′ s t,m′ + ε)`. A global gate `G t = ⊮[max m s t,m > ϵ global]` disables regularization on frames where no camera has meaningful evidence.

- **Tactile Instantiation (Section 3.2.2):** Tactile evidence is contact-based. In sparse-contact regimes (grasping, pressing), evidence is a binary contact indicator `E t,tactile = ⊮[contact at frame t]`. In rich-contact regimes (contact throughout sub-task), a task-feature operator `φtask` maps the tactile observation `otactile t` to a similarity score against a target feature, `E t,tactile = φtask (otactile t ) ∈`.

**Evidence-Gated Regularization Objectives (Section 3.3):**
The evidence score `Et,m` gates two consistency objectives using hinge weights `w inv(Et,m)` and `w suff(Et,m)`, anchored at thresholds `τ low < τ high`. An intermediate "ambiguous zone" exists where neither loss applies. A corruption operator `Cm(·)` (implemented as random erasing for visual inputs) is used to create corrupted observations: `˜o (m) t` (modality `m` corrupted, others intact) and `o m t` (only modality `m` retained, others corrupted).

- **Invariance Loss (`L inv`):** Addresses nuisance sensitivity. When `Et,m` is low, perturbations to `m` should not alter the policy's action. `L inv = ∑ t, m∈M G t · w inv(Et,m) · ||vθ (ot) − vθ (˜o (m) t )|| 2 2`. `vθ` is the flow-matching velocity prediction of the `π0.5` policy. This loss activates only on low-evidence sensors, weighted by `w inv`.

- **Single-Sensor Sufficiency Loss (`L suff`):** Addresses single-modality insufficiency. When `Et,m` is high, the policy should produce the same action when only `m` is preserved (`o m t`) as when all sensors are available (`ot`). `L suff = ∑ t, m∈M G t · w suff(Et,m) · ||vθ (ot) − vθ (o m t )|| 2 2`. This loss activates only on high-evidence sensors, weighted by `w suff`.

The final training objective combines the underlying flow-matching imitation loss `L flow` with the two evidence-gated objectives: `L = L flow + λ inv L inv + λ suff L suff`.

**Efficient Training via Importance Sampling (Section 3.4):**
To reduce the computational overhead of `2|M|` additional forward passes per training step, an unbiased importance-sampled estimator is used. For each batch sample, a single modality `m⋆ b` is sampled proportionally to its hinge weight `w inv(Eb,m)` or `w suff(Eb,m)`. The loss term is evaluated only on `m⋆ b` and rescaled by the sum of weights, `W inv b` or `W suff b`. This reduces the overhead to two extra forward passes per step, independent of the number of modalities.

**Implementation Details (Section 4.1, Appendix A):**
EGR is instantiated on `π0.5`, with vision evidence computed from BEHAVIOR-1K's instance segmentation in simulation and from Grounding DINO v2 + SAM2 for real-robot data (used offline during training). The corruption operator `C m` is random erasing during training. For simulation evaluation, center erasing is used to approximate large physical occlusions. For real-robot deployment, physical object distractors are employed. Training starts from a pre-trained `π0.5` checkpoint, updating only LoRA adapter weights.

**Benchmark (Section 4.2):**
A benchmark based on BEHAVIOR-1K is constructed, comprising 47 curated skill segments (11 navigation (NAV) and 36 manipulation (MAN)).
- **Evaluation Suites:**
 - **Suite 1 (Inference-only diagnostic):** Measures per-frame action deviation (∆) under modality perturbations without execution.
 - **Suite 2 (Rollout-based evaluation):** Measures task success rate (SR) through real executions.
- **Corruption Regimes:**
 - **Clean:** All sensors intact.
 - **NoUseless:** Useless modalities corrupted (tests nuisance sensitivity).
 - **UsefulOnly:** All modalities except a single useful one corrupted (tests single-modality insufficiency).
 - **SingleUseful:** Used for setups with exactly one useful sensor; corrupts all other sensors, combining NoUseless and UsefulOnly.
- **Baselines:** Vanilla `π0.5` and `ModDrop` (random modality token replacement).
- **Metrics:** `∆` for Suite 1; SR, end-effector contact rate (EC), and minimum end-effector distance to target (Dist) for Suite 2.
- **Benchmark Construction:** Segments are filtered based on vision evidence, ensuring head cameras are useful and wrist camera conditions (useless or useful/useless contrast) align with evaluation protocols.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Evidence-Gated Regularization (EGR) addresses three core questions regarding modality entanglement and its mitigation across simulation and real-robot platforms.

**Q1: Diagnosis of Modality Entanglement**
The results from the vanilla `π0.5` baseline confirm the presence of modality entanglement:
- **Action Deviation (Suite 1, Table 1):** In navigation tasks (NAV), under the `SingleUseful` condition (corrupting useless wrist cameras while keeping the useful head camera intact), vanilla `π0.5` showed a +31.4% increase in action deviation relative to full modalities. For manipulation tasks (MAN), corrupting only useless modalities (`NoUseless`) resulted in a +12.7% action deviation, while keeping only a single useful modality intact (`UsefulOnly`) led to a substantial +158.5% deviation. This indicates that the policy's action distribution is significantly affected by perturbations to irrelevant sensors and struggles when relying on a single, sufficient sensor.
- **Task Success Rate (Suite 2, Table 2):** Task-completion level metrics corroborated these findings. For MAN tasks, vanilla `π0.5` success rate dropped from 12.5% (Full) to 9.4% (NoUseless) and further to 2.8% (UsefulOnly). In NAV tasks, success rate decreased from 42.3% (Full) to 15.5% (SingleUseful). The `ModDrop` baseline exhibited comparable patterns, suggesting that simple modality dropout does not effectively eliminate this entanglement.

These findings collectively demonstrate that modality entanglement manifests at both the action prediction level and the ultimate task-completion level in vanilla VLA policies.

**Q2: EGR Mitigation of Modality Entanglement**
EGR consistently demonstrated an ability to reduce modality entanglement:
- **Simulation Success Rates (Suite 2, Table 2):**
 - On MAN tasks, EGR improved `Full` success rate from 12.5% to 16.4% (+31% relative).
 - Under `NoUseless` corruption, SR increased from 9.4% to 16.5% (+75%).
 - Under `UsefulOnly` corruption, SR improved from 2.8% to 6.1% (+120%).
 - On NAV tasks, `Full` performance was largely preserved (42.3% for vanilla vs. 40.9% for EGR), while `SingleUseful` SR significantly increased from 15.5% to 37.3% (+141%).
- **Action Deviation (Suite 1, Table 1):** EGR also resulted in lower action-deviation ratios compared to baselines. For NAV `SingleUseful`, the ratio dropped from 31.4% (vanilla) to 15.8% (EGR). For MAN `UsefulOnly`, it decreased from 158.5% (vanilla) to 121.8% (EGR).

These improvements were achieved without compromising performance on clean inputs, and in some cases, such as MAN `Full` SR, performance even improved, suggesting that evidence-gated regularization facilitates learning of more task-relevant features. Significance tests (Appendix C.3) support these improvements, with EGR's performance under corruption conditions being significantly better than baselines in most cases.

**Q3: Real-Robot Validation**
EGR's benefits transferred to two distinct real-robot platforms:

- **Bi-Manual Vision Platform (Kinova arms + 3 RGB cameras):** Evaluated on three short-horizon and one long-horizon task. Averaged across tasks (Fig. 4):
 - `Clean` success rate improved from 70% (baseline) to 80% (+14%).
 - `NoUseless` SR increased from 52.5% to 90% (+71%).
 - `UsefulOnly` SR rose from 25% to 72.5% (+190%).
 - Crucially, `RealDistractor` (physical out-of-distribution objects) SR boosted from 30% to 85% (+183%), indicating strong robustness to real-world occlusions.
- **Vision-Tactile Platform (MELFA ASSISTA arm + RGB camera + 2 GelSight tactile sensors):** Evaluated on two tactile feature-localization tasks. Averaged across tasks (Fig. 4):
 - `Clean` SR was largely maintained (90% to 85%, -6%).
 - `SingleUseful` SR improved from 40% to 90% (+125%).
 - `RealDistractor` SR increased from 55% to 70% (+27%).

The real-robot experiments confirm that EGR's framework effectively transfers to different sensor combinations and physical environments, providing robustness against both synthetic corruptions and novel physical distractors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces Evidence-Gated Regularization (EGR) as a methodology for addressing modality entanglement in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. Modality entanglement, characterized by nuisance sensitivity and single-modality insufficiency, represents a significant challenge for the deployment of VLA policies in dynamic and unstructured real-world environments.

The significance of EGR lies in its principled approach to enhancing multimodal robustness:
- **Task-Grounded Modality Selection:** EGR provides an explicit, per-frame, per-sensor task-relevance signal derived from task structure rather than relying solely on the potentially spurious correlations present in limited demonstration data. This grounding provides a more robust basis for determining sensor importance.
- **Modality-Agnostic Regularization:** The framework's design separates modality-specific evidence computation from modality-agnostic regularization, allowing it to be applied consistently across diverse sensor types (e.g., vision, tactile) without architectural modifications to the base VLA policy. This generalizability is a notable advantage over specialized gating mechanisms.
- **Zero Inference-Time Overhead:** Because EGR is applied exclusively during training, it incurs no additional computational cost or latency during policy deployment, which is critical for real-time robotic control.
- **Demonstrated Robustness:** The validation across a new BEHAVIOR-1K benchmark and two distinct real-robot platforms shows substantial improvements in success rates under various corruption conditions, including physical distractors. This suggests that EGR can contribute to more reliable and generalizable robot behaviors.

The potential impact of this work is in fostering the development of more robust and trustworthy VLA policies. By enabling robots to correctly identify and prioritize relevant sensory information while ignoring distractors or adapting to sensor failures, EGR could facilitate the broader adoption of VLA models in applications requiring high reliability, such as manufacturing, service robotics, and assistive technologies. The ability to generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios and manage inherent sensor variability in real-world settings is crucial for the continued progress of embodied AI.

The authors acknowledge limitations, such as the derivation of evidence instantiations from pre-specified task structure, which may not cover scenarios requiring learned evidence signals (e.g., in-hand object reorientation). These limitations also indicate potential avenues for future research, such as integrating information-theoretic objectives or self-supervised feature discovery to enable policies to learn task-relevant evidence from data. Expanding the simulation benchmark to cover a wider range of tasks is also identified as straightforward future work. These considerations do not diminish the current contributions but rather contextualize them within the ongoing research trajectory toward truly adaptive and robust robotic systems.
