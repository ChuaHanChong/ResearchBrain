# Optimal Sparsity of Mixture-of-Experts Language Models for Reasoning Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from the Institute of Science Tokyo systematically investigates how Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) sparsity influences both memorization and reasoning capabilities in large language models. The study reveals divergent scaling behaviors for these tasks, with reasoning performance showing a non-monotonic, U-shaped relationship with increasing total parameters, and identifies optimal sparsity levels dependent on active compute and tokens per parameter.

## Problem

- Existing empirical scaling laws for dense LLMs do not fully capture the behavior of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, which introduce an additional dimension of sparsity.
- There is an incomplete understanding of how MoE sparsity, total parameters, active parameters, and top-k routing affect distinct cognitive capabilities (memorization vs. reasoning) under fixed compute budgets.
- The direct relationship between decreasing pre-training loss and improved downstream task performance is not always consistent across diverse tasks, particularly for reasoning abilities.

## Method

- A systematic experimental approach involved training numerous ==MoE language models== with varied architectural hyperparameters, including model width, number of experts (E), and top-k experts (k).
- Models were pre-trained on a fixed 125B-token corpus and rigorously evaluated across both memorization-centric (TriviaQA, HellaSwag) and reasoning-intensive (GSM8K, GSM-Plus) benchmarks.
- The study also assessed whether post-training adaptation (Group Relative Policy Optimization - GRPO) or increased test-time compute (Self-Consistency decoding) could mitigate observed performance trends related to MoE sparsity.

## Results

- Pre-training loss decreased monotonically with increasing total parameters, which consistently improved performance on memorization tasks, but led to a U-shaped performance trend (initial improvement followed by degradation) for reasoning tasks like GSM8K and GSM-Plus.
- Reasoning ability on GSM8K and GSM-Plus peaked around a Total Tokens Per Parameter (TPP) ratio of approximately **20**, indicating that reasoning is "data-hungry" and requires an optimal balance rather than simply more parameters or more data.
- Neither applying GRPO post-training nor employing Self-Consistency decoding eliminated the inverse U-shaped relationship between pre-training loss and reasoning accuracy, though both methods generally improved overall performance across models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Memorization and reasoning capabilities exhibit fundamentally different scaling behaviors in MoE models, with reasoning performance often showing a non-monotonic response to increasing total model capacity.
> - Optimal MoE sparsity is highly dependent on the task type and compute budget, with memorization tasks generally benefiting from higher sparsity and reasoning tasks requiring an optimal balance of active compute and tokens per parameter.
> - Pre-training architectural choices, specifically concerning MoE sparsity, are foundational for reasoning ability and are not fully overcome or mitigated by post-training fine-tuning or increased test-time inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has been significantly influenced by empirical scaling laws, which establish relationships between training loss and factors such as model size, dataset size, and computational budget. Initial work by Kaplan et al. (2020) and subsequent refinements by Hoffmann et al. (2022) provided a framework for compute-optimal model planning for dense Transformer architectures. These classical scaling laws, however, have been observed to have coefficients that vary with architectural changes or data pipeline modifications (Liu et al., 2023a; Brandfonbrener et al., 2025).

A notable architectural advancement is the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2021), which allows for high model capacity at a relatively fixed computational cost by sparsely activating experts. MoE models are now integrated into state-of-the-art LLMs, including Gemini 2.5 Pro (Gemini Team, 2025), DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025b), and Qwen3 (Qwen Team, 2025). Prior research has begun to extend scaling laws to MoE architectures, exploring parameter-versus-FLOPs frontiers and identifying optimal sparsity for given compute budgets (Abnar et al., 2025; Frantar et al., 2024; Kimi Team, 2025). These studies suggest that the traditional dense-model scaling picture is incomplete, necessitating consideration of architectural variables such as MoE sparsity and top-k routing.

Further complexity arises in the relationship between pre-training loss and downstream task performance. Research indicates that the next-token prediction loss, typically used in scaling laws, does not always reliably predict performance on diverse downstream tasks (Grattafiori et al., 2024). Different tasks exhibit distinct scaling behaviors; for instance, knowledge-based tasks may be "capacity-hungry," while code-related tasks may be "data-hungry" (Roberts et al., 2025). Specifically for MoE models, it has been observed that adding experts tends to improve memorization more than reasoning skills (Jelassi et al., 2025), and that models with identical training loss can show varied reasoning performance (GLM-4.5 Team, 2025).

Additionally, post-training adaptation methods, such as reinforcement learning techniques like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) and its variants like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025a), are standard for enhancing reasoning abilities. Test-time compute (TTC) strategies, which involve allocating more inference resources without updating model parameters (e.g., self-consistency decoding, longer chains of thought) (OpenAI, 2024b; Wang et al., 2023), also contribute to improving model outputs on complex reasoning tasks.

This work contributes to this landscape by systematically investigating how MoE sparsity, defined as the fraction of inactive parameters (1 - Top-k / Experts), influences both memorization and reasoning capabilities. It aims to disentangle the relationships between total parameters, active parameters, top-k routing, pre-training loss, and downstream accuracy under fixed compute budgets. The study further examines whether post-training (GRPO) or increased test-time compute can mitigate any observed trade-offs related to MoE sparsity, thereby refining the understanding of optimal scaling practices for modern LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to address the limitations of existing scaling laws for large language models (LLMs) when applied to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, particularly concerning their performance on distinct cognitive capabilities: memorization versus reasoning. While empirical scaling laws have guided the development of dense LLMs, their applicability to MoE models, which introduce an additional dimension of sparsity, remains incompletely understood.

The key objectives of this study are:

1. **Investigate the influence of MoE sparsity on distinct capability regimes:** The work aims to understand how MoE sparsity impacts two categories of tasks: memorization skills (e.g., TriviaQA, HellaSwag) and reasoning skills (e.g., GSM8K, GSM-Plus). This involves systematically varying MoE configurations and evaluating their performance across these task types.
2. **Disentangle pre-training loss from downstream accuracy:** A core objective is to move beyond the assumption that lower pre-training loss universally translates to improved downstream task performance. The researchers seek to analyze the generalization gap between training loss and task loss, and further between task loss and accuracy, especially as MoE sparsity changes.
3. **Analyze the roles of total parameters, active parameters, and top-k routing under fixed compute budgets:** The study aims to determine how variations in these architectural parameters—total parameters (number of experts), active parameters (number of top-k experts), and the top-k routing mechanism—affect model performance under controlled computational budgets. This involves training families of MoE models with specific constraints to isolate the effects of these variables.
4. **Evaluate the impact of post-training and test-time compute:** A further objective is to assess whether common post-training adaptation methods (specifically, Group Relative Policy Optimization, GRPO) or increased test-time compute (TTC) can alter or mitigate the performance trends and trade-offs observed with different MoE sparsity levels. This addresses whether architectural choices made during pre-training are fundamental or can be overcome by subsequent optimizations.
5. **Characterize optimal MoE sparsity based on specific metrics:** The research intends to characterize the divergences in performance along two proposed axes: "Active FLOPs" (downstream reasoning performance correlates with active compute, not just training loss) and "Total tokens per parameter (TPP)" (memorization is parameter-hungry, reasoning benefits from optimal TPP). This aims to revise classical compute-optimal scaling perspectives by integrating MoE-specific considerations.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the increasing adoption of MoE architectures in frontier LLMs and the need for more nuanced scaling laws that account for architectural sparsity. Understanding these relationships is crucial for efficiently allocating computational resources, designing more effective MoE models for specific tasks, and guiding future research into how model architecture, data, and compute interact to develop advanced LLM capabilities, particularly for complex reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic experimental approach involving the training and evaluation of numerous Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models with varied architectural configurations.

#### 3.1. Model Architecture

The base architecture utilized was Mixtral (Jiang et al., 2024), a Transformer backbone incorporating RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019), SwiGLU activations (Shazeer, 2020), and rotary positional embeddings (Su et al., 2024). All models had a fixed depth of L=16 layers. Each feed-forward block was implemented as a sparsely gated MoE layer, employing dropless token-choice top-k routing (Gale et al., 2023). Each individual feed-forward network within an expert had a hidden dimension of 2d.

#### 3.2. Architectural Hyperparameter Sweeps

Three primary architectural hyperparameters were varied:
- **Model width (d):** {512, 1024, 2048}
- **Number of experts per layer (E):** {8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}. For d=2048, the search was limited to E ≤ 128 due to computational constraints.
- **Top-k experts per token (k):** {2, 4, 8, 16}.
The term "sparsity" was defined as 1 - (Top-k / Experts), representing the fraction of inactive parameters.

#### 3.3. Training Details

- **Optimizer:** AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) was used for training.
- **Learning Rate Schedule:** A peak learning rate of 4 × 10⁻⁴ was applied, followed by a 2k-step linear warm-up and cosine decay.
- **Regularization:** A weight decay of 0.1 was used.
- **Auxiliary Losses:** Load-balancing loss (L_LB) and router z-loss (L_RZ) were included, with coefficients of 10⁻² and 10⁻³ respectively, consistent with prior work (Xue et al., 2024; Zoph et al., 2022).

#### 3.4. Hyperparameter Study for Optimization Effects

To distinguish architectural effects from optimization artifacts, a separate study was conducted where E=16 and k=2 were fixed for widths d={512, 1024}. The following optimization hyperparameters were varied:
- LM-head initialization schemes.
- Peak learning rate.
- AdamW ϵ.

#### 3.5. Pre-training Datasets

All models were pre-trained on a fixed 125B-token corpus, ensuring consistency with token-budget-based scaling law analyses. The corpus comprised a balanced mixture of high-quality web text (43B tokens), mathematics corpora (32B tokens), STEM literature and reference (49B tokens), and code (1B tokens). An ablation study was also performed where GSM8K-related datasets (original training data and synthetic derivatives) were removed from the pre-training corpus to assess their impact on observed trends.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Protocol

Models were evaluated using standard few-shot prompts across several benchmarks:
- **Mathematical Reasoning:** GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) with 4-shot prompting, and GSM-Plus (Li et al., 2024) with 5-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting.
- **Reading Comprehension:** TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) with 4-shot prompting.
- **Commonsense Reasoning:** HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019) with 4-shot prompting.
- **Coding Tasks (Ablation):** HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) (0-shot) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) (3-shot), both evaluated using the Pass@1 metric.
Metrics included pre-training loss, validation loss, task loss (cross-entropy over ground-truth answer tokens), and accuracy on respective benchmarks.

#### 3.7. Post-training (GRPO)

- **Algorithm:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) was applied.
- **Dataset:** Fine-tuning was performed on the GSM8K training dataset, and an ablation used the MATH 500 dataset (Lightman et al., 2024).
- **Parameters:** Training for 15 epochs (105 steps), batch size 1024, actor's learning rate fixed at 5 × 10⁻⁶, temperature 1.0, KL-penalty coefficient 10⁻³, 5 samples per prompt.

#### 3.8. Test-Time Compute (TTC)

- **Method:** Self-Consistency (SC) decoding (Wang et al., 2023) was used for GSM8K in a zero-shot setting.
- **Procedure:** 128 independent continuations (up to 1,024 tokens each) were generated per problem (temperature 0.6, top-p=0.95), and the most frequent answer was selected.
- **Ablations:** The number of prompt shots and sampling temperature were varied to assess their influence. Inference-time top-k was also varied in some cases.

#### 3.9. Computational Environment

Pre-training runs were conducted on the ABCI 3.0 supercomputer (Takano et al., 2024) using NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Test-Time Compute experiments utilized the TSUBAME 4.0 supercomputer at Institute of Science Tokyo with NVIDIA H100 SXM5 GPUs. The Megatron-LM codebase was extended for MoE training, and GRPO was implemented using the veRL framework. Evaluations were performed using lm-evaluation-harness and LargeLanguageMonkeys.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning the optimal sparsity of MoE language models and their performance on memorization versus reasoning tasks.

#### 4.1. Divergent Scaling for Memorization vs. Reasoning

- **Pre-training Loss:** Across all configurations, training and validation losses decreased monotonically as the total number of parameters increased (by adding more experts while keeping top-k fixed).
- **Memorization Tasks:** For memorization-centric benchmarks such as TriviaQA and HellaSwag, task loss and accuracy consistently improved as pre-training loss decreased (i.e., with increasing total parameters). This indicates a continuous benefit from larger model capacity for these tasks.
- **Reasoning Tasks:** In contrast, for mathematical reasoning tasks like GSM8K and GSM-Plus, a non-monotonic, U-shaped trend was observed. Task loss initially decreased with falling pre-training loss, but then began to increase beyond a certain threshold of total parameters, leading to a degradation in reasoning accuracy. This suggests an "inverse scaling" regime where larger models (and lower pre-training loss) can be detrimental to reasoning.

#### 4.2. Dependence on Active Parameters

Models with a greater number of active parameters (achieved by increasing `k` in top-k routing) were found to reach lower minimum task losses on GSM8K and GSM-Plus. These models could also tolerate lower pre-training losses before exhibiting the performance degradation observed for reasoning tasks. This indicates that while total parameter count (related to `E`) matters, the actively used compute (related to `k`) plays a significant role in mitigating the inverse scaling effect for reasoning.

#### 4.3. Optimal Sparsity under Iso-FLOP Budgets

- When evaluating models under a fixed FLOPs budget (which implicitly increases with active parameters), optimal sparsity varied by task type.
- **Memorization Tasks:** For TriviaQA and HellaSwag, higher sparsity (lower density, i.e., larger `E` for a fixed `k`) consistently led to lower task loss and higher accuracy. This aligns with prior observations that sparse models can outperform dense ones on QA tasks under fixed FLOPs.
- **Reasoning Tasks:** For GSM8K and GSM-Plus, the trend reversed at larger active parameter counts (higher FLOPs). Initially, increasing sparsity might improve performance, but beyond a certain FLOPs budget, denser models (lower sparsity) began to achieve better results (lower loss and higher accuracy). This suggests that reasoning skills have a compute-dependent optimal sparsity, sometimes favoring denser configurations in higher compute regimes.

#### 4.4. Tokens Per Parameter (TPP) and Task-Specific Scaling

The study found that the Total Tokens Per Parameter (TPP) ratio had a distinct impact on different task types:
- **Memorization Tasks:** Performance for memorization skills improved monotonically as TPP decreased (i.e., with more parameters relative to tokens), categorizing these tasks as "parameter-hungry."
- **Reasoning Tasks:** Reasoning ability (on GSM8K and GSM-Plus) peaked around a TPP of approximately 20. Performance degraded if TPP was either too low (too many parameters relative to data) or too high (too few parameters relative to data), indicating that reasoning is "data-hungry" and requires an optimal balance.
- **Interaction with Active Compute:** Even at fixed TPP, models with larger `top-k` values consistently outperformed those with smaller `top-k` on reasoning tasks. This highlights that reasoning performance in MoE models depends on both the total parameter-to-token ratio and the active compute per token.

#### 4.5. Impact of Post-training and Test-Time Compute

Neither applying Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) post-training nor employing additional Test-Time Compute (Self-Consistency decoding) eliminated the observed inverse U-shaped relationship between pre-training loss and reasoning task accuracy on GSM8K. While both methods generally improved overall performance, they did not fundamentally alter the underlying trade-offs linked to MoE sparsity. This implies that the pre-training architectural choices, particularly concerning sparsity, are a dominant factor for reasoning ability under fixed budgets.

#### 4.6. Coding Task Ablations

Similar to mathematical reasoning, coding tasks (HumanEval, MBPP) exhibited non-monotonic performance with increasing sparsity. Beyond a certain task-dependent threshold, higher sparsity degraded Pass@1 accuracy, with denser configurations showing better procedural reasoning for code synthesis in high-compute regimes.

#### 4.7. Influence of Optimization Hyperparameters

The study also revealed that optimization hyperparameters, such as learning rate, influenced downstream reasoning performance even when models converged to similar pre-training losses. Lower learning rates and smaller initialization scales resulted in superior downstream accuracy for reasoning tasks, suggesting that implicit biases shaped by optimization dynamics play a role in generalization.

#### 4.8. Token Budget Ablation

An ablation study comparing models trained on 125B tokens versus 1T tokens indicated that while the larger corpus significantly improved memorization-intensive tasks, it provided only marginal gains for reasoning tasks. This was attributed to the scarcity of high-quality mathematical and reasoning corpora in the upsampled 1T dataset, justifying the 125B token budget for prioritizing architectural trade-off analysis.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of scaling laws for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models, particularly by differentiating their behavior on memorization versus reasoning tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Refinement of MoE Scaling Laws:** The study refines existing scaling laws by demonstrating that "Active FLOPs" and "Total tokens per parameter (TPP)" are crucial dimensions that influence optimal MoE sparsity. This moves beyond the classical dense-model scaling picture, providing a more granular framework for understanding the efficiency and performance of sparse architectures.
2. **Challenging Monotonic Scaling Assumptions:** It challenges the assumption that continuously decreasing pre-training loss always correlates with improved downstream performance, especially for reasoning tasks. The discovery of an inverse-U relationship, where reasoning performance can degrade beyond a certain optimal sparsity or model size, highlights the complexity of generalization in MoE LLMs.
3. **Guidance for MoE Model Design and Training:**
 - For **memorization-intensive tasks**, the findings suggest that higher sparsity and a greater number of experts are beneficial, consistent with these tasks being "parameter-hungry."
 - For **reasoning-intensive tasks**, the research indicates a need for a careful balance between active FLOPs and TPP. Optimal reasoning performance peaks around TPP ≈ 20, and in high-compute/limited-data regimes, denser MoE layers might be more advantageous, while greater sparsity could be beneficial with abundant data. This provides practical guidance for architects and trainers on how to configure MoE models based on desired capabilities and available resources.
4. **Implications for Post-training and Test-Time Optimization:** The observation that neither reinforcement learning post-training (GRPO) nor increased test-time compute (TTC) fully mitigates the sparsity-induced trade-offs for reasoning tasks underscores the fundamental importance of architectural and pre-training choices. This suggests that achieving optimal reasoning capabilities requires addressing sparsity configurations during the initial pre-training phase, rather than solely relying on post-hoc refinements.
5. **Understanding Implicit Biases in Generalization:** The finding that optimization hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate) influence reasoning accuracy even at identical pre-training losses points to the role of implicit inductive biases. This implies that comprehensive generalization studies for LLMs should incorporate rigorous reasoning benchmarks alongside traditional loss curves.
6. **Broader Applicability:** The extension of these non-monotonic trends to coding tasks suggests that the observed phenomena are not unique to mathematical reasoning but might apply more broadly to complex procedural reasoning capabilities.
7. **Promotion of Reproducibility and Further Research:** The open-sourcing of model checkpoints, code, and logs provides a foundation for other researchers to reproduce these findings, explore different architectural settings, or investigate the identified trade-offs in other contexts, fostering continued advancement in MoE research.

Overall, this work provides empirical evidence that MoE sparsity is not a universally beneficial scaling knob but rather a task- and compute-dependent variable. It advances the understanding of how to build more efficient and capable LLMs, particularly for complex reasoning, by emphasizing the joint consideration of active compute and data-per-parameter ratios in architectural planning.
