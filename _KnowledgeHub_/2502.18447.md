# Supervised Reward Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> Supervised Reward Inference (SRI) learns a direct mapping from behavior trajectories to reward functions via supervised regression on a dataset of behaviors paired with known rewards, instead of assuming a demonstrator behavior model. It is proven asymptotically Bayes-optimal and empirically infers correct rewards from gestures, noisy, and systematically wrong-goal demonstrations on Meta-World manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Prior IRL methods assume a specific behavior model (noisy-optimal, Boltzmann-rational, bounded-reasoning) that never matches the full diversity of real human suboptimality and communicative gestures.
- Assuming an incorrect behavior model is theoretically shown to yield badly wrong reward functions, yet most reward-inference algorithms still hard-code one such model.
- Existing behavior-model-learning approaches (e.g. value iteration network based inverse planning) require full demonstrator policies at inference time and do not extend to continuous, arbitrary suboptimal behavior classes.

## Method

- **Supervised Reward Inference** (**SRI**): a ==regression objective== that directly maps sets of behavior trajectories and a query state to a scalar reward, avoiding any explicit behavior-model assumption.
- Trajectory/task encoder architecture: a ==transformer== encodes each trajectory, a ==set transformer== aggregates them into one task embedding, decoupled from a per-timestep ==state-encoder MLP== for efficient reward queries.
- Dataset construction pipeline that pairs simulated suboptimal demonstrations (gestures, noisy actions, goal offsets) with independently sampled state-reward labels from known ground-truth rewards for training.

## Results

- On pick-place tasks with pure gesture demonstrations, SRI reaches **0.822** average goal proximity versus about **-0.001** for GAIL, AIRL, and BC, near the ground-truth RL policy's **0.903**.
- Under systematically wrong-goal (PSYCHIC) demonstrations, optimality-assuming baselines degrade to zero proximity while SRI stays near-optimal across all offset values.
- On the hardest HARD reach task, SRI reaches **0.930** proximity with **1280** tasks and **10000** labeled observations, versus **-1.808** (GAIL), **-1.582** (AIRL), and **-0.930** (BC).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning the behavior-to-reward mapping directly via supervised learning sidesteps the need to specify a demonstrator behavior model, generalizing across arbitrarily suboptimal or communicative behaviors.
> - Asymptotic Bayes-optimality under mild compactness and continuity assumptions gives a principled bound on how well any reward-inference method can perform given behavior data.
> - Data quality and coverage of the state distribution matter more than raw quantity; sparse state coverage can cause the learned reward to admit hackable, spurious local optima.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), faces a fundamental challenge in enabling agents to understand and achieve human-specified goals. Traditionally, this involves explicit reward specification, which can be complex and prone to errors. Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) emerged as an alternative, inferring reward functions from human demonstrations.

However, existing IRL methods typically operate under the assumption that human demonstrations are generated according to specific, often idealized, models of behavior. These models range from noisy optimality (where actions are mostly optimal but with some random deviations) to bounded rationality (accounting for cognitive limitations). A significant limitation of this approach is that real-world human behavior frequently deviates from these specific models. Human actions can be highly suboptimal due to poor planning or execution, or they might be explicitly non-optimal, such as gestures intended to communicate a goal rather than achieve it directly.

The research landscape has identified "behavior model misspecification" as a critical problem. It has been theoretically shown that simultaneously inferring a demonstrator’s reward function and their behavior model is generally impossible without additional assumptions. Furthermore, incorrect assumptions about the behavior model can lead to substantially incorrect inferred reward functions, even for small discrepancies. Prior work has attempted to address specific types of suboptimality in demonstrations, such as hyperbolic discounting or mistaken beliefs, but these methods are typically tailored to predefined categories of suboptimal behavior. Other approaches have explored learning from arbitrarily suboptimal demonstrations, often by relying on additional information like success/failure labels or preference rankings.

This paper positions itself as a response to the inherent limitations of these model-driven IRL approaches. It proposes Supervised Reward Inference (SRI) as a unified framework that does not assume a specific model of human behavior. Instead, it frames human actions broadly as *indications of goals* rather than direct attempts at optimization. By using a supervised learning paradigm to directly map behaviors to reward functions, given a dataset of behaviors paired with ground-truth rewards, SRI aims to circumvent the challenges of behavior model misspecification and expand the range of human behaviors from which goals can be inferred. This contrasts with approaches that first learn a behavior model and then invert it to find a reward. The work by Shah et al. (2019) is noted as conceptually close, in that it learns behavior models from known rewards and policies, but it requires full policies at inference time and has not been extended to continuous domains. SRI offers a direct, data-driven alternative.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that allows artificial agents to accurately infer human goals from a wide spectrum of human behaviors, including those that are suboptimal or communicative rather than purely optimizing.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current methods for goal communication and reward inference:
1. **Difficulty of Reward Specification**: Explicitly defining reward functions for complex tasks is challenging and can lead to reward misspecification, potentially causing agents to pursue unintended behaviors. This highlights the need for alternative, more intuitive goal communication modalities.
2. **Restrictive Assumptions in IRL**: Existing Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) techniques typically assume that human demonstrations are generated by a specific, often rational or near-optimal, behavioral model. These assumptions simplify the learning problem but are often inaccurate descriptions of real-world human behavior.
3. **Real-world Human Behavior Complexity**: Human behavior is diverse and often suboptimal due to factors like poor planning, execution errors, cognitive biases, or the intent to communicate rather than strictly optimize. For instance, a human might make a large, exaggerated gesture to indicate a target location, which is not an optimal path to that location but effectively conveys the goal. Current IRL methods struggle to interpret such behaviors effectively.
4. **Consequences of Behavior Model Misspecification**: Theoretical and empirical evidence suggests that assuming an incorrect behavior model in IRL can lead to the inference of substantially erroneous reward functions. This undermines the reliability of agents attempting to learn from human input.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of the paper are:
- To propose Supervised Reward Inference (SRI) as a novel framework that directly learns a mapping from observed behaviors to reward functions using supervised learning, thereby bypassing the need to explicitly model human behavior.
- To theoretically demonstrate that SRI, under reasonable assumptions, is asymptotically Bayes-optimal, implying that with sufficient data, it can infer the best possible reward function.
- To empirically validate SRI's ability to infer accurate reward functions from a variety of arbitrarily suboptimal demonstrations, including highly noisy actions, systematically incorrect trajectories, and communicative gestures.
- To evaluate SRI's performance in learning complex, discontinuous reward functions.
- To assess the data efficiency of SRI with respect to both the size of the training dataset and the number of demonstrations available at inference time.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to provide a more robust and flexible approach to reward inference, enabling artificial agents to align more effectively with human intentions in diverse and unconstrained environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this work is Supervised Reward Inference (SRI), which re-frames the problem of reward inference as a supervised learning task. Instead of explicitly modeling the human behavior that generates demonstrations, SRI directly learns a mapping from observed behaviors to reward functions using a dataset where behaviors are paired with their corresponding ground-truth rewards.

**Definition of Supervised Reward Inference (SRI):**
SRI is formalized as a minimization problem:
`arg min_θf,θg E[ L(g_θg(S, f_θf({T_n})), R(S)) ]`
where:
- `{T_n}` is a set of `N` behavior trajectories.
- `R` is the ground-truth reward function.
- `S` is a state sample.
- `D_T` is the joint distribution of `R` and `{T_n}`.
- `D_S` is a distribution over states.
- `f_θf : T^N → Ψ` is a parameterized *task encoder* that maps multiple trajectories into a compact task representation `ψ`.
- `g_θg : S × Ψ → R` is a parameterized *reward model* that takes a state `S` and a task representation `ψ` to predict the reward `r(S)`.
- `L` is a regression loss function (specifically, mean-squared error (MSE) in this work).

This architecture allows for efficient inference: the task encoder processes behaviors once per task, generating an embedding `ψ` that is then combined with real-time state observations by the reward model.

**Example Architecture:**
The experimental setup utilizes a neural network architecture with distinct components:
- **Trajectory Encoder**: A 3-head transformer (two 256-dimensional layers) followed by a 2-layer MLP (50 hidden dimensions) to process individual trajectories into 100-dimensional representations.
- **Task Encoder**: A set transformer with a hidden dimension of 128, which aggregates the individual trajectory representations into a 256-dimensional task embedding `ψ`. This computation is performed once per task.
- **State Encoder**: A 2-layer MLP (256 hidden dimensions) to transform the current state `s` into a 100-dimensional representation `ϕ`. This is computed at each timestep.
- **Reward MLP**: A 2-layer MLP (256 hidden dimensions) that takes the task embedding `ψ` and state representation `ϕ` to output a scalar reward `r`. All networks use leaky ReLU activations.

**Dataset Construction:**
For training SRI, a dataset of `K` tasks is required, where each task includes `N` behavior trajectories and `M` state-reward pairs. In the simulated experiments, ground-truth rewards are computationally accessible. This allows for:
- **Behavior Trajectories**: Generated by simulating suboptimal human behavior for known reward functions.
- **State-Reward Pairs**: States are sampled from a distribution `D_S` (e.g., states encountered by a robot trying to reach random locations) and computationally labeled with the true reward `R(s)`. This allows for a large number of state-reward pairs independent of the number of behavior samples.

The paper acknowledges that in real-world scenarios, computational access to ground-truth rewards is rare. It suggests alternatives like human labeling of goals/preferences or computational reward estimation from speech or facial expressions.

**Bayes Optimality of SRI:**
The paper provides a theoretical argument for SRI's asymptotic Bayes-optimality. Under a set of assumptions (compact state/action spaces, continuous/bounded reward functions, compact and equicontinuous model family, use of MSE loss, and the assumption that the model family can represent the Bayes-optimal estimator, and full support data sampling), SRI is proven to be asymptotically Bayes-optimal in two respects:
1. **Reward Function Convergence**: As the size of the training dataset increases, the inferred reward functions `R_θK` almost surely converge uniformly over the state space to the expectation of the posterior distribution of the true reward `R` given the observed trajectories.
2. **Policy Convergence**: Consequently, the returns of the optimal policies derived from these inferred reward functions almost surely converge to the maximum expected return achievable under this posterior distribution.
The proof sketch involves using Glivenko-Cantelli and Arzelà-Ascoli theorems for uniform convergence of the reward function and generalizing a result by Ramachandran and Amir (2007) to show that maximizing return under the expectation of the posterior reward is equivalent to maximizing expected return under the posterior.

**Experimental Design:**
- **Tasks**: Simulated robotic manipulation tasks from Meta-World (pick-place and reach tasks) with randomly distributed goals. An additional discontinuous reward was added for successful completion to test SRI's ability to infer sharp reward functions.
- **Demonstration Classes**: Five distinct classes of behavior trajectories were generated by oracular policies for reach goals, each representing different forms of suboptimality or communication:
 - `GESTURE`: Robot hand moves towards the goal for 50 timesteps from a random start, primarily for communication, not optimality.
 - `NOISYε`: Hand reaches towards the goal but with a probability `ε` of taking a random action.
 - `NOISY GESTUREε`: Combines random actions with gesture-like initial movements.
 - `PSYCHICα`: Hand deterministically reaches towards an incorrect position, offset from the true goal.
 - `HARD`: Hand draws a circle around a deterministically incorrect, mirrored goal.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Average normalized goal proximity, calculated as 1 minus the scaled distance to the goal, averaged across timesteps and trials.
- **Reinforcement Learning for Policy Training**: After reward inference, policies were trained using Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) for reach tasks and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for pick-place tasks, implemented in Stable-Baselines3. For reach tasks, multi-task policies conditioned on the task representation (`ψ`) were trained.
- **Baselines**: Behavioral Cloning (BC), Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (AIRL), and Ground-Truth RL (GT RL).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on simulated robotic manipulation tasks demonstrated SRI's capability to infer reward functions from diverse and suboptimal behaviors, distinguishing its performance from traditional imitation learning methods.

**1. Inference of Complex, Discontinuous Reward Functions:**
SRI exhibited the ability to infer sharp and discontinuous reward functions accurately. In pick-place tasks using `GESTURE` demonstrations, SRI achieved an average normalized goal proximity of 0.822 ± 0.051 (Table 2). This performance was substantially higher than the baselines (GAIL, AIRL, BC), which registered approximately -0.001, indicating near-total failure to identify the goal. SRI's performance approached that of the Ground-Truth RL baseline (0.903 ± 0.011), which had direct access to the true reward. This finding indicates SRI's effectiveness in modeling rewards for tasks with clear success/failure states and complex underlying shaped rewards.

**2. Inference from Indirectly Communicative Behaviors (Gestures):**
SRI successfully inferred goals from `GESTURE` demonstrations, which are behaviors primarily intended to communicate a goal rather than optimally achieve it. As shown in Table 2, SRI's policy achieved high goal proximity, whereas the optimality-assuming baselines failed to learn a relevant policy from these non-optimal, communicative inputs.

**3. Performance with Increasing Demonstration Suboptimality:**
- **Noisy Actions (`NOISY GESTUREε`, Figure 2)**: SRI demonstrated greater robustness to noise in demonstrations compared to other methods. Its performance gracefully degraded as the proportion of random actions (ε) increased. While it approached Ground-Truth RL performance with low noise, it maintained a higher average scaled goal proximity even when demonstrations were highly noisy (e.g., ε = 0.87), where baselines' performance significantly dropped.
- **Systematically Incorrect Behavior (`PSYCHICα`, Figure 3)**: A key result showed SRI's ability to infer correct goals even when demonstrations deterministically indicated the *wrong* location. As the goal position offset factor (α) varied, indicating systematically incorrect or mirrored goals, SRI's learned policies consistently achieved near-optimal performance. In contrast, GAIL, AIRL, and BC exhibited a sharp decline in performance, approaching zero goal proximity, as the offset factor deviated from an optimal setting (α=1.0), demonstrating their sensitivity to behavior model misspecification.

**4. Impact of Data Quantity:**
- **Training Tasks and Labeled Observations (Tables 3 & 4)**:
 - **Reach Tasks (`HARD` class)**: SRI learned reasonable reward functions even with relatively modest amounts of training data. For instance, with 80 training tasks and 1,000 labeled state-reward observations per task, SRI achieved an average goal proximity of 0.792 ± 0.008, a substantial improvement over baselines (e.g., GAIL: -1.808 ± 0.368) and a notable fraction of Ground-Truth RL (0.957 ± 0.005). Performance generally improved with more tasks and observations.
 - **Pick-Place Tasks (`HARD` class)**: This task was observed to be more challenging, requiring a larger dataset for strong performance. SRI achieved a proximity of 0.784 ± 0.052 with 1,280 tasks and 10,000 observations, indicating that complex tasks may still demand significant labeled data.
- **Number of Inference Demonstrations (`NOISY0.87`, Figure 4)**: SRI consistently outperformed optimality-assuming methods regardless of whether it received 1, 10, or 100 noisy demonstrations at inference time. This indicates good few-shot inference capabilities for reward functions, especially when compared to baselines that struggled even with more demonstrations.

**Challenges Encountered:**
The researchers noted that data quality was critical, and insufficient sampling of the state space could lead to "hackable" reward functions with unintended local optima. For pick-place tasks, a trade-off emerged between modeling the primary success reward and providing effective pre-grasping shaping rewards, which was sometimes exacerbated by limited model capacity. This highlights a need for careful dataset construction and potentially more sophisticated model architectures or training strategies for complex, multi-faceted reward functions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Supervised Reward Inference (SRI), an approach that has the potential to significantly advance how artificial agents learn human goals. Its significance stems from both its theoretical underpinnings and empirical performance in addressing key limitations of existing reward inference paradigms.

**Fundamental Contribution:**
SRI is presented as the first algorithm capable of inferring accurate reward functions from *arbitrary* behavior. This moves beyond the restrictive assumptions of existing Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods, which typically require demonstrations to adhere to specific models of optimal or near-optimal behavior. By directly learning a mapping from behavior to reward through supervised learning, SRI circumvents the challenging problem of behavior model misspecification, which has been identified as a major impediment to robust reward inference.

**Theoretical Robustness:**
The theoretical proof of SRI's asymptotic Bayes-optimality provides a strong foundation for its reliability. This indicates that, given sufficient training data and model capacity, SRI can converge to the optimal reward function in a Bayesian sense, leading to policies that maximize the expected return given the uncertainty in the reward function. This theoretical guarantee supports its applicability to a broad range of reward inference problems.

**Enhanced Practical Applicability:**
- **Flexible Goal Communication**: SRI's ability to infer goals from highly suboptimal, systematically incorrect, and even purely communicative behaviors (like gestures) enables more natural and flexible human-AI interaction. Humans are no longer constrained to providing "optimal" demonstrations, allowing for a wider range of intuitive goal communication methods.
- **Robustness in Real-World Scenarios**: The demonstrated robustness to various forms of suboptimality suggests that SRI could be more effective in real-world robotic applications where human demonstrations are inherently imperfect due to human error, cognitive biases, or different communicative intentions.
- **Data Efficiency for Inference**: The results indicating strong performance even with a small number of inference-time demonstrations are important for practical deployment, as collecting large numbers of demonstrations for every new task can be resource-intensive.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancing AI Alignment**: By allowing agents to more accurately infer human goals from diverse behaviors, SRI contributes to the critical challenge of aligning artificial agents with human values and intentions. This can lead to more trustworthy and beneficial AI systems.
- **Robotics and Human-Robot Interaction**: SRI could enable robots to learn tasks more intuitively from human partners, by interpreting a broader spectrum of human actions as goal-indicative. This would reduce the burden on human users to provide perfectly executed demonstrations.
- **Foundation Models for Behavior**: The paper highlights SRI's compatibility with self-supervised foundation models (e.g., for visual features or general behaviors). This suggests a path towards leveraging large, unlabeled datasets to improve reward inference, mirroring successes seen in natural language processing with massive datasets. This integration could potentially address the current challenge of collecting sufficient labeled data for complex real-world tasks.

**Future Directions and Challenges:**
The authors acknowledge several areas for future work. Integrating image encoders into SRI models is necessary for visual real-world tasks. Scaling SRI to highly complex real-world tasks will likely require advancements in labeled data efficiency, potentially through self-supervised and weakly-supervised learning methods. Addressing issues like "hackable" reward functions due to insufficient state sampling, perhaps through techniques like regularization or iterative data labeling, remains an important area of research. These efforts are crucial for SRI to realize its full potential in enabling AI agents to understand and achieve human goals more effectively.
