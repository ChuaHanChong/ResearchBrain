# WorldLine: Action-Driven Visual Simulation for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> WorldLine is an action-driven video world simulator for robot manipulation. It learns robot-object dynamics from over 10,000 hours of action-free robot video, then learns action control from over 2,000 hours of trajectories across more than ten embodiments. Controls are expressed as camera-projected action maps. It predicts trajectory success with 74% accuracy and raises out-of-domain RoboTwin best-of-N planning success by up to 21.4 points.

## Problem

- Video generation models favor visual plausibility over accurate action following, so their rollouts drift from the commanded direction, timing, and gripper state, and from coherent robot-object dynamics.
- Action-conditioned simulators depend on scarce, embodiment-specific action data whose native control spaces differ in dimensionality, kinematics, and coordinates, so trajectories cannot be pooled across robots.
- Prior simulators learn visual dynamics and action semantics jointly from the same trajectories and mostly train on successes, so coverage is capped by action labels and failure outcomes are poorly modeled.

## Method

- Three-stage decoupled training: Stage I adapts ==Cosmos3-Nano== with ==flow matching== on action-free robot video, Stage II adds action grounding, and Stage III distills a ==block-autoregressive causal student==.
- Cross-embodiment spatial action conditioning projects the end-effector pose into each camera as a nine-channel ==Gaussian-heatmap action map== (position, depth, Rot6D, gripper), added to the video tokens through a ==residual Conv3D branch==.
- Multi-view, failure-enriched training uses head and wrist views plus about 200 hours of failure trajectories, with ==V-JEPA2== relational regularization and robot-mask-weighted losses during four-step distillation.

## Results

- On held-out AgiBotWorld failure trajectories WorldLine leads all metrics, raising robot-mask IoU by 0.1626 over the best baseline. On out-of-domain DROID it reaches the highest IoU, 0.2739.
- Policy evaluation reaches **74%** mean success-classification accuracy on RoboTwin and AgiBot. At N=32, RoboTwin selection lifts pi0.5 by **19.1 points** and LingBot-VLA by **21.4 points** over direct execution.
- Causal distillation cuts sampling from 35 to 4 steps (0.698 to **0.163 s/frame**, **4.3x** faster). Native action vectors collapse DROID IoU to 0.1171, and success-only data costs LingBot-VLA **10.8 points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamics and action grounding can be learned separately. Cheap action-free video still helps under matched compute, which points to added interaction coverage rather than extra optimization.
> - Expressing actions by their image-space effects, not as native vectors, gives one control interface across different robots and strongly improves both motion fidelity and the ranking of candidate actions.
> - Visual-fidelity gains do not automatically improve planning. Planning needs the correct ranking of competing futures, so failure data matters more than regularizers that only sharpen geometry.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Robot learning demands large-scale interaction, and real-world collection is costly. A robot policy can propose a manipulation, but testing every candidate in the real world uses time and hardware. A video forecast could help, but only if it follows the commanded movement and shows what happens to the object. A video model that looks plausible may still fail to follow the robot's controls or preserve object interactions.

WorldLine first learns broad robot–object motion from many videos that have no action labels. It then adds a visual cue that shows where each robot is commanded to move. In the illustrated held-out and out-of-domain examples, WorldLine's predicted robot motion and gripper–object configurations better match the recorded future, particularly under DROID's unseen environments and embodiments.

Held-out AgiBotWorld examples (top) and out-of-domain DROID examples (bottom). Columns show the initial scene, ground-truth future, WorldLine, Masked Visual Actions, GE-Sim-V2, and OpenDW-0.5. The authors report that WorldLine's causal predictions align more closely in these examples. These are qualitative examples. They do not measure task success.

### 2. Learn motion, then ground controls

In Stage I, WorldLine adapts a pretrained video model to more than 10,000 hours of robot video. Action and state signals are not used as model inputs or conditioning in this stage. Some source videos may still contain those annotations.

Stage II uses more than 2,000 hours of action trajectories across more than ten embodiments. Robot kinematics and camera calibration project several control signals into camera-aligned action maps:

- end-effector position
- depth
- orientation
- gripper state

These maps let different robots condition the video model through a shared visual interface. This preserves action geometry across embodiments.

Training uses synchronized head and wrist views. It also includes approximately 200 hours of failure trajectories, treated as ordinary action–video examples without explicit outcome labels.

Stage III distills generation into a causal, block-by-block model that can accept updated controls.

The closest comparison is [[2605.27491|GE-Sim-V2]], which already uses image-space action maps. WorldLine does not claim that camera-space actions are new. Its claim is that transferable dynamics can be learned separately from control grounding and then shared across robots.

### 3. Testing the control interface and the decisions it enables

The central motion metric is robot-mask IoU. It measures overlap between independently segmented robot masks in generated and recorded video, on the head-camera view. It measures image-space motion agreement, not physical accuracy in general.

**Mechanism test.** The ablation replaces image-space action maps with FiLM-injected native action vectors. This reduces held-out AgiBotWorld robot IoU from 0.5622 to 0.3639. The authors also report worse LPIPS and lower planning success for both tested policies. This supports the map interface. It does not establish that image-space grounding is the only cause of the full system's gains.

Ablation of WorldLine components. Columns give LPIPS (lower is better) and robot IoU (higher is better) for held-out AgiBotWorld and DROID. They also give RoboTwin success and gain over direct execution for π0.5 and LingBot-VLA. Planning uses 32 rollouts. Compare the full WorldLine row with the native action vectors row.

**Downstream planning.** On RoboTwin, the simulator evaluates policy-proposed candidates. A Qwen3VL-8B selector then chooses one, and the highest-scoring trajectory is executed. The test covers 10 tasks with five scenes each. At 32 candidates, selection using WorldLine rollouts improves LingBot-VLA's RoboTwin task success by 21.4 percentage points over direct execution.

WorldLine had no RoboTwin training, adaptation, or checkpoint selection. The policy checkpoint itself was trained on RoboTwin. This is a simulated benchmark result, not a real-robot deployment result. The achievable improvement is also bounded by the policy-proposed candidates and the quality of the multimodal selector.

### 4. What the evidence supports

A forecast can help compare robot actions only if it preserves the robot's commanded motion and the resulting object interaction. In the reported visual and RoboTwin tests, WorldLine's image-space grounding supports that link. The authors present WorldLine as an action-driven visual simulator useful for downstream robotic decisions. The evidence remains bounded to those datasets and simulated outcomes. Testing whether simulated gains translate to more reliable real-robot behavior is left for future work.
