# MemER: Scaling Up Memory for Robot Control via Experience Retrieval

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed MemER, a hierarchical policy framework that equips existing generalist robot policies with long-term visual memory through intelligent experience retrieval. This method allows robots to successfully perform complex, multi-step, partially observable manipulation tasks requiring minutes of memory, outperforming memory-less and short-context baselines on real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Most current robot policies lack long-term memory, struggling with tasks requiring recall of information gathered minutes ago in partially observable environments.
- Naively extending observation context windows for end-to-end policies is computationally prohibitive and can lead to brittle generalization due to irrelevant or redundant information.
- Existing memory-based solutions for robotics often scale only to short horizons or are ill-suited for dynamic manipulation tasks, while proprietary VLMs suffer from high latency and insufficient robot-specific reasoning.

## Method

- A hierarchical policy framework is introduced, comprising a high-level VLM-based policy for subtask generation and keyframe nomination, and a low-level generalist robot policy for action execution.
- An intelligent experience retrieval mechanism, utilizing 1D single-linkage clustering on nominated keyframes, dynamically builds and maintains a compact, task-relevant visual memory over long horizons.
- Open-source Vision-Language Models (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) and generalist robot policies (π0.5) are finetuned and merged with pretrained weights to optimize for robot-specific memory and task performance.

## Results

- MemER achieved 59 object retrievals (out of 60 possible) in 'Object Search' and only 1 wrong scoop in 'Counting Scoops', significantly outperforming memory-less and short-context baselines on all three complex long-horizon tasks.
- Performance of MemER was on par with a 'Human HL' policy (human-provided subtasks), indicating that the memory mechanism is highly effective and primarily limited by low-level policy execution.
- The approach demonstrated efficient real-world deployment, with the high-level policy operating at ~1Hz and the low-level policy at ~2Hz, enabling practical closed-loop control with minutes of memory.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual memory alone proved to be the most robust representation for long-term robot memory in manipulation tasks, outperforming and showing more resilience than text-based memory or multimodal combinations including text.
> - Proprietary VLMs, without fine-tuning, lack the robot-specific perceptual grounding to identify truly meaningful keyframes and are too slow for real-time closed-loop robot deployment.
> - Linear interpolation (model merging) of finetuned weights with pretrained VLM weights effectively preserves general robustness while adapting to specific robot tasks, and multi-task training enhances cross-task object generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has witnessed rapid advancements, particularly with the emergence of vision-language-action (VLA) models that leverage web-scale pretraining to enable impressive language-following and generalization capabilities in robots. These models, such as RT-2, $\pi_0$, DROID, and OpenVLA, have significantly broadened the scope of tasks robots can perform in real-world settings.

However, a critical limitation persists: the **absence of long-term memory**. While humans routinely rely on memory to navigate partially observable environments and solve complex, multi-step tasks (e.g., recalling where an object was last seen), most current robot policies lack this crucial ability. Existing generalist end-to-end policies often operate with little to no visual history due to the prohibitive computational expense of conditioning on long sequences of high-dimensional image and video data. Naively extending context windows also leads to brittleness under covariate shift and the inclusion of irrelevant or redundant information, hindering generalization.

Prior works have attempted to expand observation contexts through auxiliary losses (Torne et al., 2025) or by finetuning foundation models with native memory capabilities (Fang et al., 2025). However, these methods typically scale only to relatively short horizons (e.g., a few frames to at most two dozen), falling short of the "hundreds of frames" or "minutes of memory" required for truly long-horizon tasks. Other approaches, such as compressing images in pixel space (Li et al., 2025a) or into 2D visual traces (Zheng et al., 2025a), have limitations with wrist-mounted cameras or for very long-term reasoning. Memory-based solutions in robotic navigation, often relying on explicit geometric/semantic maps (Henry et al., 2012; Yu et al., 2024), are ill-suited for manipulation tasks where the environment is dynamically modified by the robot. Furthermore, directly prompting API-based VLMs for complex manipulation (Chiang et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2023) often suffers from high latency and insufficient robot-specific affordance reasoning for real-world deployment.

MemER addresses this fundamental gap by introducing a novel, hierarchical framework designed to **efficiently scale up memory for robot control via experience retrieval**. It builds upon the existing VLA paradigm, specifically the hierarchical approach where a high-level policy plans subtasks for a low-level controller (Shi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b). The key innovation lies in empowering the high-level policy to intelligently select and consolidate task-relevant visual "keyframes" from its extensive experience, enabling it to reason over much longer horizons (minutes of memory) without incurring prohibitive computational costs or sacrificing reactivity. By adapting open-source VLMs with strong video understanding priors (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) and generalist robot policies ($\pi_{0.5}$), MemER offers a practical and scalable solution to a critical bottleneck in achieving truly general-purpose robotic intelligence. This positions MemER as a significant step forward in enabling robots to perform complex, partially observable tasks that were previously intractable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to **endow robot policies with the ability to leverage memory for performing tasks, akin to humans.** This ambitious objective is motivated by the recognized human capacity to recall relevant past experiences to navigate partially observable environments and achieve multi-step goals, a capability largely absent in contemporary robot control systems.

The specific objectives of the MemER project are:
1. **To provide an effective and scalable method for equipping existing generalist robot policies with long-term visual memory.** The emphasis is on enabling current state-of-the-art VLA models to tackle tasks that extend beyond their typical short-context limitations.
2. **To enable robots to solve complex, multi-step, long-horizon tasks** that explicitly require recalling information gathered minutes ago.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several critical limitations and challenges faced by current robotic systems:

- **Partial Observability**: Real-world environments are inherently partially observable. Robots often need to remember where an item was last seen, what actions have already been performed, or the state of objects outside their immediate field of view to successfully complete tasks.
- **Computational Expense**: Naively increasing the observation history (e.g., conditioning on long sequences of high-dimensional images) for end-to-end policies is computationally exorbitant, leading to prohibitively expensive training and slow inference during deployment.
- **Brittle Generalization**: Long observation histories can cause policies to overfit to spurious correlations, leading to "shortcut reliance" and poor generalization under covariate shift. As the robot operates, its state distribution can drift from the training data, making long, undifferentiated histories detrimental.
- **Irrelevant and Redundant Information**: A long, undifferentiated history is likely to contain much irrelevant or redundant information, making it difficult for the policy to extract truly useful cues for decision-making. Robots need to learn to *filter* and *store* only task-relevant information.
- **Scalability for Long Horizons**: While some prior works extend context to a few dozen frames, these are insufficient for tasks requiring minutes of memory, which can span hundreds or even thousands of frames. The challenge is to maintain a compact yet informative memory representation over such extended periods.
- **Limitations of Existing Foundation Models**: While powerful, off-the-shelf VLMs and robot foundation models are often either too slow (due to API latency for large models) or lack the robot-specific perceptual grounding required to identify and utilize meaningful long-term memory cues for complex manipulation tasks without explicit fine-tuning.

MemER aims to overcome these hurdles by introducing a memory mechanism that is not only effective but also computationally efficient and compatible with existing successful VLA architectures, thereby pushing the frontier of autonomous robot capabilities for truly complex, real-world tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MemER proposes a **hierarchical policy framework** to scale up memory for robotic control. This framework decomposes the robot's control into a high-level policy responsible for memory and planning, and a low-level policy for executing granular actions.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Policy Structure

- **High-Level Policy ($\pi_h$)**: This policy is based on a finetuned **Vision-Language Model (VLM)**, specifically Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct. Its role is twofold:
 1. **Subtask Generation**: Given the current situation, it generates a language subtask ($l'_t$) for the low-level policy to execute.
 2. **Keyframe Nomination**: It identifies and nominates candidate keyframes ($J_t$) from the robot's recent visual context that represent important information to be remembered for future reasoning.
 - **Inputs to $\pi_h$**: The high-level policy conditions on:
 - $R_t$: The last $N$ frames (recent context window, e.g., $N=8$) from multiple cameras (base and wrist-mounted).
 - $l_t$: The overarching high-level task instruction.
 - $K_t$: A set of previously selected keyframes (episodic memory, $|K_t| \le 8$).
- **Low-Level Policy ($\pi_l$)**: This policy is a finetuned generalist robot policy, specifically a version of $\pi_{0.5}$ trained on the DROID dataset. Its role is to execute the immediate language subtask provided by the high-level policy.
 - **Inputs to $\pi_l$**: It conditions on:
 - $I_t$: Current images from cameras.
 - $q_t$: Robot proprioceptive inputs (joint angles, gripper state).
 - $l'_t$: The language subtask generated by $\pi_h$.
 - **Output of $\pi_l$**: It predicts a chunk of actions ($A_t$) for the robot (e.g., joint velocities).

#### 3.2. Keyframe Selection and Visual Memory Building

The core novelty of MemER lies in its mechanism for dynamically building and maintaining a compact visual memory ($K_t$). This process, called **Experience Retrieval**, ensures that only task-relevant information is stored over long horizons.

- **Candidate Keyframe Nomination**: The high-level policy $\pi_h$ predicts $J_t$, a subset of frames from its recent context $R_t$, that it deems important.
- **Keyframe Filter (1D Single-Linkage Clustering)**: Candidate keyframes are not simply added to memory; they undergo a filtering and consolidation process.
 1. **Temporal Index Aggregation**: All candidate keyframe indices (temporal timestamps) nominated from the start of the trajectory ($J'_{0:t}$) are collected into a single, temporally ordered list ($G_{0:t}$). Duplicates are preserved.
 2. **Clustering**: A 1D single-linkage clustering algorithm is applied. Keyframe indices that are within a predefined "merge distance" $d$ (e.g., $d=5$ frames) of each other are grouped into clusters ($C_i$). This effectively merges closely nominated frames.
 3. **Representative Selection**: For each cluster $C_i$, the *median index* is selected as its representative keyframe. This median selection aggregates repeated nominations and yields the most representative frame within a cluster.
 4. **Memory Update**: The frames corresponding to these median indices form $K_{t+1}$, the selected keyframes for the next timestep's high-level inference. For efficiency, clusters with indices significantly older than the current recent context are not recalculated.

#### 3.3. Practical Implementation Details

- **Data Collection**: 50 teleoperated demonstrations per task were collected. Each demonstration was manually segmented into subtasks ($l'_t$) with corresponding language annotations. A semi-automatic procedure was used for keyframe labeling, where human annotators defined simple rules (e.g., "select last frame of 'look inside bin'") for subtask segments, and these rules were then applied automatically. Additionally, 10-15 "intervention demonstrations" were collected for the low-level policy to improve robustness to failures.
- **Training**:
 - **Low-Level Policy**: $\pi_l$ was finetuned from a public $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint on the DROID dataset using the collected (image, proprioception, subtask, action) trajectories. A single $\pi_l$ was trained for all three tasks.
 - **High-Level Policy**: $\pi_h$ (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) was finetuned to predict both $l'_t$ and $J_t$. Vision encoder and projection layer weights were frozen for training efficiency. A single $\pi_h$ was also trained for all three tasks to enhance object generalization.
- **Model Merging**: To preserve the robustness and generalization capabilities of the pretrained VLM while adapting it to specific robot tasks, a linear interpolation strategy was used: $\theta = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \theta_{\text{pre}} + \alpha \cdot \theta_{\text{ft}}$, where $\theta_{\text{pre}}$ are the original Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct weights and $\theta_{\text{ft}}$ are the finetuned weights, with $\alpha=0.8$.
- **Closed-Loop Deployment**: The policies run asynchronously for improved responsiveness. $\pi_h$ operates at approximately 1Hz, predicting subtasks and keyframes, while $\pi_l$ operates at approximately 2Hz, executing action chunks. Images are subsampled at 2Hz for input to the hierarchical policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

MemER was rigorously evaluated on three complex, real-world long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks, each designed to necessitate minutes of memory for successful completion: "Object Search," "Counting Scoops," and "Dust & Replace."

#### 4.1. Outperformance on Long-Horizon Memory-Based Tasks

- **Comparison to Baselines**: MemER consistently and significantly outperformed memory-less ("No History"), short-context ("Short History" with 8 frames), and naively extended-context ("Long History" with 32 frames) baselines across all three tasks.
 - On **Object Search**, MemER achieved 59 object retrievals and 57 optimal paths (out of 60 maximum across 20 trials), far surpassing "No History" (32/25) and "Long History" (47/41).
 - For **Counting Scoops**, MemER had only 1 wrong scoop (lower is better), demonstrating precise long-term counting, whereas "No History" had 61 and "Long History" 12.
 - In **Dust & Replace**, MemER showed high success rates across all sub-components (20/20 bottom shelf dusted, 19/20 top shelf dusted, 18/20 bottom object replaced, 20/20 top object replaced), contrasting sharply with the significantly lower performance of all baselines.
- **Near Human-Level Performance**: MemER's performance was on par with a "Human HL" policy (where a human provides correct subtasks), suggesting that the remaining failures primarily stem from the low-level policy's execution rather than the high-level memory and planning. This indicates the memory mechanism is highly effective.

#### 4.2. Superiority to Proprietary VLMs for Robot-Specific Memory

- **Offline Evaluation**: When comparing the finetuned Qwen2.5-VL-based $\pi_h$ with zero-shot proprietary VLMs (GPT-5 and Gemini Robotics–ER 1.5) in an offline setting, MemER demonstrated substantially higher trajectory and boundary accuracy for subtask predictions.
- **Latencies and Robot-Specific Reasoning**: The API latency (10-15 seconds) of proprietary models rendered them unusable in real-time closed-loop deployment. Even offline, they struggled, often predicting too many non-informative keyframes. This highlights that general VLMs, without fine-tuning, lack the robot-specific perceptual cues and the capacity to identify truly meaningful keyframes necessary for complex manipulation tasks.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Visual Memory Modality

- **Visual Dominance**: Ablation studies on memory modalities revealed that visual memory alone is the most robust representation for these tasks.
 - Replacing visual memory with text-based memory ("Short History + Text") led to a significant performance drop.
 - Adding text alongside visual memory ("MemER + Text") provided no benefits and often degraded performance, particularly in the "Counting" task.
- **Over-reliance on Text**: The authors hypothesize that the high-level policy tended to "over-index" on text tokens in memory, ignoring critical visual details. Textual memory also proved brittle when policy retries or freezes caused out-of-distribution shifts in the recent context, as the model overfit to canonical subtask orderings. Visual memory provided more direct and robust grounding.

#### 4.4. Benefits of Model Merging and Multi-Task Training

- **Robustness from Model Merging**: Linear interpolation of the finetuned high-level policy weights with the pretrained Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct weights (model merging) consistently improved or maintained performance across all tasks. This technique effectively preserves the general robustness and generalization capabilities of the large foundation model while allowing it to adapt to the specific robot manipulation domain.
- **Improved Object Generalization**: Training a single high-level policy on all three memory-based tasks (multi-task training) significantly enhanced cross-task object generalization. When objects were swapped between tasks (creating novel object-task combinations not seen during training), the multi-task MemER generalized much more effectively than single-task variants. This highlights the scalability benefit of the approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of MemER represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of enabling robots to effectively utilize long-term memory. Its potential impact is multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Complex, Long-Horizon Tasks**: MemER directly tackles the limitation of current robot policies that struggle with tasks requiring minutes of recall. By demonstrating success on real-world tasks like "Object Search," "Counting Scoops," and "Dust & Replace," it opens the door for robots to perform more intricate, multi-step, and partially observable tasks in unstructured environments, moving beyond short-term reactive behaviors.
- **Practical Scalability and Efficiency**: The framework achieves long-term memory without incurring the prohibitive computational costs of naively extending context windows. Its hierarchical design, combined with intelligent keyframe selection and consolidation, allows for low-latency inference, making it practical for real-world closed-loop robotic control. This is a crucial step towards deployable, generalist robots.
- **Leveraging Existing VLA Models**: MemER's compatibility with off-the-shelf, open-source VLA backbones (like Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and $\pi_{0.5}$) is a major strength. It demonstrates how to augment existing powerful models with a critical missing capability, accelerating progress without requiring entirely new model architectures. The findings on model merging further provide a practical strategy for effectively adapting large pretrained models.
- **Insights into Memory Modalities**: The ablation studies offer valuable insights into the efficacy of different memory modalities for robot control. The finding that visual memory outperforms and is more robust than textual memory (or multimodal combinations including text) in these specific manipulation contexts challenges assumptions and guides future research in designing memory representations.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: MemER lays a robust foundation for several promising future directions:
 - **Dynamic Memory Management**: The current system continuously accumulates keyframes. Future work can explore mechanisms for actively pruning or forgetting less relevant memories, which will be essential for tasks requiring hours or days of recall.
 - **Higher-Frequency Control**: Further optimizations in model caching and tokenization could reduce inference latency, enabling even higher-frequency control and reactivity to rapid environmental changes.
 - **Multimodal Memory**: Integrating other sensory modalities like tactile or audio information into the memory framework could enhance task performance and environmental understanding.
 - **Mobile Manipulation and Spatial Reasoning**: Extending MemER to mobile manipulation and multi-room tasks would require interleaving episodic recall with spatial mapping, moving closer to human-like comprehensive memory.
- **Advancing Generalist Robotics**: By providing a scalable and effective memory mechanism, MemER contributes significantly to the long-term vision of creating truly generalist, autonomous robots that can learn, adapt, and operate intelligently over extended periods in complex human environments. It represents a concrete step towards systems that can "decide what to remember and leverage those memories when needed for effective long-horizon control."
