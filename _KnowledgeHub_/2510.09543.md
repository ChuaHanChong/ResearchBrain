# Guiding Energy-Efficient Locomotion through Impact Mitigation Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northeastern University and Caltech introduce the Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF) as a physics-informed reward signal for reinforcement learning, guiding legged robots to learn energy-efficient locomotion by optimizing passive impact absorption. The method reduces the Cost of Transport by 18-32% and peak mechanical power consumption by 28-35% in simulation.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) methods, especially those using kinematic imitation like Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), often overlook the implicit passive dynamics crucial for energy-efficient locomotion.
- Traditional handcrafted reward functions are laborious to tune for complex biological dynamics such as passive impact absorption and compliance.
- Current RL reward structures for legged robots typically lack explicit physics-informed signals to promote energy-efficient interaction with the environment.

## Method

- Introduces the **Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF)**, a ==physics-informed metric== quantifying a robot's passive ability to mitigate impacts based on its configuration, as a direct ==reward signal==.
- Develops a ==unified reward framework== that integrates the IMF with both ==AMP-based (kinematic fidelity)== and traditional handcrafted reward structures.
- Formulates the IMF by comparing the actual ==contact impulse== to a ==locked-case impulse==, where joints are rigid, to incentivize configurations that passively absorb impact energy.

## Results

- IMF-augmented policies reduced the Cost of Transport (CoT) by **18.4%** at 1 m/s and **2.8%** at 2 m/s on flat ground, and by **19.0%** at 1.5 m/s on rough terrain, compared to baselines.
- Policies leveraging IMF consistently showed reduced peak mechanical power consumption, achieving a **35%** reduction for AMP+IMF and a **28%** reduction for handcrafted+IMF policies.
- The approach led to a **18-32%** reduction in cumulative joint torques across various joints, indicating lower mechanical stress and improved dynamic resilience on challenging terrains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating physics-informed metrics like the IMF into RL reward functions effectively guides policies to discover more energy-efficient gaits by implicitly leveraging passive dynamics for impact absorption.
> - Combining kinematic imitation with explicit rewards for passive dynamics creates a more comprehensive learning approach, addressing both motion style and underlying physical principles of biological locomotion.
> - Optimizing for impact mitigation leads to quantifiable reductions in mechanical power consumption and joint torques, potentially enhancing robot longevity and robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics, particularly legged locomotion, has long sought to emulate the energy efficiency and dynamic resilience observed in biological systems. This aspiration often involves complex control strategies and sophisticated mechanical designs. Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (RL) have provided powerful tools for discovering locomotion policies, allowing robots to learn complex behaviors through trial-and-error by maximizing defined reward functions.

Within RL for locomotion, imitation learning techniques, such as those employing Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), have shown promise in enabling robots to replicate naturalistic animal motions by distilling kinematic patterns from motion capture (MoCap) data. AMP simplifies the reward engineering process by focusing on the kinematic fidelity of the generated motion to a reference. However, a limitation of these imitation-based approaches, as well as many traditional handcrafted reward structures, is their predominant focus on explicit kinematic patterns, often overlooking the implicit passive dynamics crucial for energy efficiency and robust interaction with the environment. Biological systems inherently leverage these passive dynamics—such as compliance and impact absorption during ground contact—to mitigate forces, conserve energy, and achieve agile movements.

This research aims to bridge the gap between kinematic imitation and dynamic resilience. It addresses the challenge that existing RL methods, while capable of producing visually convincing gaits, may not inherently optimize for the underlying physical principles of energy-efficient impact mitigation. By integrating a physics-informed metric, the Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF), into the reward structure, the work seeks to guide RL policies to learn both the explicit motion trajectories (kinematics) and the implicit passive dynamics essential for energy-efficient and robust legged locomotion, thereby moving closer to replicating the full spectrum of biological motion principles.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the energy efficiency and dynamic resilience of legged robot locomotion by integrating a physics-informed metric into reinforcement learning reward functions. The authors state a long-standing aspiration in academia to mimic bio-level energy-efficient locomotion, which is currently more attainable with deep reinforcement learning.

The motivation for this work stems from identified limitations in current reinforcement learning approaches for legged robots:

1. **Limitations of Kinematic Imitation:** While Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) simplify reward engineering by distilling kinematic styles from reference motion, they primarily capture explicit kinematic patterns (gaits). These approaches tend to overlook the implicit passive dynamics, such as energy-efficient impact absorption and compliance, that are fundamental to biological locomotion and contribute significantly to energy efficiency and resilience. The paper highlights that AMP, by design, focuses on motion style rather than intrinsic physical interaction principles like energy transfer during impacts.
2. **Challenges with Handcrafted Rewards:** Traditional handcrafted reward functions, despite their ability to enforce task-specific stability, require extensive and laborious tuning to approximate energy-efficient gaits that mirror complex biological dynamics. Crafting rewards that explicitly encode passive impact absorption and compliance is challenging.
3. **Absence of Physics-Informed Passive Dynamics in Learning Signals:** The implicit passive dynamics—how a robot's configuration and mechanical properties allow it to absorb impacts—are critical for reducing energy consumption and increasing robustness, especially during high-speed or rough-terrain locomotion. Existing reward structures typically do not explicitly account for these dynamics as a learning signal.

To address these motivations, the key objectives and contributions of this work are articulated as:

- **IMF as a Physics-Informed Reward Signal:** To introduce the Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF) as a reward component that explicitly encodes passive compliance and impact resilience into the learning process. The IMF is a physics-informed metric quantifying a robot's passive ability to mitigate impacts based on its configuration.
- **Hybrid Reward Framework:** To develop a unified reward framework that combines the kinematic fidelity derived from AMP (for motion style) with the physics-informed IMF (for biomechanical passive dynamics). This framework also extends to augmenting traditional handcrafted rewards with IMF.
- **Experimental Validation:** To demonstrate through simulation that augmenting both AMP and handcrafted reward structures with the IMF leads to quantifiable improvements in the Cost of Transport (CoT), specifically by 18%-32%. This objective aims to provide empirical evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach in enhancing energy efficiency.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to enable RL policies to learn not only the desired explicit motion trajectories but also the implicit dynamic principles of biological systems, leading to more energy-efficient, robust, and naturalistic robot locomotion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this work involves integrating the Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF) as a physics-informed reward signal into various reinforcement learning frameworks to guide policies towards energy-efficient locomotion by encouraging passive impact mitigation.

**A. Hardware Overview (Husky-v.2)**
The research utilizes the Husky-v.2 robot, a quadrupedal platform designed for robust locomotion and potential aerial mobility. Key design features include a lightweight structure (3D-printed Onyx polymer, carbon fiber), high torque-to-weight actuators (Dynamixel XH540-W270-T servos), and compact propulsion units for flight. For the scope of this work, the authors state that the robot's flight capability is not utilized, and the focus remains exclusively on low-level locomotion policy learning.

**B. Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF) Formulation**
The IMF (ξ) quantifies a robot's ability to passively mitigate impact forces at its end-effector based on its configuration. It serves as a metric to encourage the robot to adopt configurations that enable efficient energy conversion during impacts.

1. **System Description:** A floating-base robot with configuration vector $q = [q_b^T, q_j^T]^T \in R^{n+6}$, where $q_b \in R^6$ describes base position/orientation and $q_j \in R^n$ describes joint coordinates. The system dynamics are described by $H\ddot{q} + h(q, \dot{q}) = S^T\tau + J^Tf$, involving the inertia matrix $H$, Coriolis/centrifugal/gravitational forces $h$, joint torques $\tau$, end-effector forces $f$, and Jacobian $J$.
2. **Rigid-Body Impact Dynamics:** The end-effector velocity is $v = J\dot{q}$. Upon impact, the resulting contact impulse $\rho$ is related to $v$ by $\rho = -\Lambda v$, where $\Lambda = (JH^{-1}J^T)^{-1}$ is the Operational-Space Inertia Matrix (OSIM), representing the effective inertia at the end-effector.
3. **Locked-Case:** This scenario models the robot as if its joints are completely locked, meaning only the base contributes to motion. The locked-case operational-space inertia $\Lambda_L = (J_b H_{bb}^{-1} J_b^T)^{-1}$ is derived using only the base Jacobian $J_b$ and base inertia submatrix $H_{bb}$. The corresponding locked impulse is $\rho_L = -\Lambda_L v$. This locked-case provides a benchmark for maximal rigidity.
4. **IMF Derivation:** The IMF is derived by comparing the actual impulse $\rho$ with the locked-case impulse $\rho_L$. The impact mitigation matrix is defined as $\Xi = I - \Lambda\Lambda_L^{-1}$. The scalar IMF, $\xi = det(\Xi)$, ranges from 0 to 1. $\xi = 1$ implies perfect impact absorption (zero impulse compared to the locked case), while $\xi = 0$ indicates no mitigation (impulse matches the locked case).
5. **IMF as Reward:** The IMF is integrated into the RL reward function as $R_{IMF} = -\log(1 - \xi)$. This formulation ensures that as $\xi$ approaches 1 (higher mitigation), $R_{IMF}$ increases significantly, thereby incentivizing the policy to adopt configurations that enhance passive impact absorption.

**C. Reinforcement Learning Formulation**
The problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Four distinct reward structures are defined and evaluated:

1. **Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP)-Based Reward:** This baseline combines a style reward from AMP with a task reward.
 - **Reference Data Pre-processing:** Unstructured dog motion clips from are retargeted to the Husky-v.2 morphology using Pinocchio for kinematic mapping.
 - **Discriminator:** A neural network $D_{\phi}$ is trained using a least squares GAN formulation to distinguish between reference motion transitions and policy-generated transitions. The loss function $L_D$ includes a gradient penalty for stability.
 - **Style Reward ($r_{Style}$):** Derived from the discriminator output, $r_{Style} = \max(0, 1 - 0.25 (D_{\phi}(s_t, s_{t+1}) - 1)^2)$, rewarding motions similar to the reference.
 - **Task Reward ($r_{Task}$):** Encourages velocity tracking, defined as $r_{Task} = w_v \exp(-\|\hat{v}_{xy,t} - v_{xy,t}\|) + w_{\omega} \exp(-|\hat{\omega}_{z,t} - \omega_{z,t}|)$, for linear and angular velocities.
 - **AMP with IMF Reward:** The total reward is $r_t = w_{Task} r_{Task} + w_{Style} r_{Style} + w_{IMF} r_{IMF}$.

2. **Handcrafted Reward:** This baseline uses a traditionally engineered reward structure without relying on reference motion. The reward components (Table I) include terms for linear and angular velocity tracking, penalties for vertical velocity, roll/pitch angles, height deviation, torque effort, acceleration effort, position limits, action smoothness, limb contact, and rewards for foot air time and stand-still joint deviation.
 - **Handcrafted with IMF Reward:** The total reward is $r_t = w_{IMF} r_{IMF} + \sum_i w_i r_{i,t}$.

**D. Model Representation**
- **Observation Space ($O \in R^{56}$):** Includes linear and angular velocities, gravity vector (all in body frame), desired velocity commands, joint positions and velocities (16 joints, 12 actuated, 4 underactuated), and previous actions. For AMP-guided policies, base linear and angular velocities are excluded.
- **Discriminator State Input ($[s_t, s_{t+1}] \in R^{102}$):** Comprises joint positions and velocities, leg end positions relative to the robot body, linear and angular base velocities, and base height.
- **Action Space ($A \in R^{12}$):** Joint position commands for the 12 actuated joints.

**E. Experiment Setup and Training**
Experiments are conducted in the IsaacLab simulation environment.
- **Reward Weights:** For AMP, weights are 2.0 (AMP), 2.5 (linear velocity), 1.5 (angular velocity). When IMF is added, its weight is 10.0. For handcrafted rewards, IMF weight is 0.15 on flat ground and 1.5 on rough terrain.
- **Neural Networks:**
 - Discriminator: Two fully connected layers (1024, 512 neurons) with LeakyReLU, followed by a linear output layer.
 - RL Policy (PPO): Actor and critic networks with three hidden layers (512, 256, 128 neurons) with ELU activations.
- **Metrics:** Cost of Transport (CoT, defined as $\tau \cdot \dot{q} / mgv$), Velocity Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), mechanical power consumption ($\tau \cdot \dot{q}$), and cumulative joint torques.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that incorporating the Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF) into both Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) and handcrafted reward structures consistently improves energy efficiency and motion fidelity across different terrains.

**A. Cost of Transport (CoT) and Velocity Tracking**
- **Flat Ground:**
 - The IMF-augmented AMP policy reduced the Cost of Transport (CoT) by 18.4% at 1 m/s and 2.8% at 2 m/s, compared to the baseline AMP policy without IMF.
 - The handcrafted policy augmented with IMF achieved a 20% CoT reduction at 2 m/s.
- **Rough Terrain:**
 - The handcrafted policy with IMF demonstrated a 19.0% CoT reduction at 1.5 m/s.
 - It also maintained stable velocity tracking, with a Roll RMSE of 0.59 compared to 0.68 in its baseline counterpart.
 - The AMP+IMF policy showed improvements in dynamic resilience, indicated by a 20.3% decrease in Pitch RMSE on rough terrain.
- Table II provides detailed comparative data for CoT and Velocity RMSE across policies and terrains. For instance, at 1.5 m/s on rough terrain, the handcrafted+IMF policy achieved a CoT of 0.72, which was lower than the handcrafted w/o IMF (0.88), AMP w/ IMF (0.99), and AMP w/o IMF (1.24).

**B. Mechanical Power Consumption**
- Policies leveraging the IMF consistently exhibited reduced mechanical power consumption, calculated as $\tau \cdot \dot{q}$ (torque times joint velocity).
- On flat terrain at velocities ranging from 1.5 m/s to 2 m/s:
 - The AMP+IMF policy reduced peak mechanical power by 35%, from 100 W to 65 W, compared to its baseline.
 - The handcrafted+IMF policy achieved a 28% reduction in peak mechanical power, from 70 W to 50 W, compared to its baseline.
- Figure 4 visually supports these findings, illustrating the reduced average mechanical power for IMF-augmented policies.

**C. Joint Torques**
- The efficiency gains correlated with a reduction in joint loading.
- The handcrafted+IMF policy reduced cumulative joint torques by 25–32% across various joints (e.g., 33% reduction for the rear left knee, 31% for the front right knee) compared to its baseline.
- The AMP+IMF policy achieved an 18–25% reduction in cumulative joint torques.

These results indicate that integrating IMF as a reward signal successfully guides reinforcement learning policies to discover more energy-efficient gaits by implicitly learning to leverage passive dynamics for impact absorption. Figure 3 provides a visual comparison of locomotion, suggesting smoother, more compliant motion in the IMF-augmented policies on rough terrain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates a method for integrating physics-informed metrics into reinforcement learning, offering several areas of significance and potential impact for robotic locomotion.

**A. Advancing Energy-Efficient Locomotion**
The primary significance lies in the ability to significantly improve the energy efficiency of legged robots, as quantified by reductions in Cost of Transport (CoT) by up to 32% and mechanical power consumption by up to 35%. By explicitly rewarding configurations that facilitate passive impact mitigation, the research moves beyond purely kinematic imitation to embed a fundamental principle of biological energy efficiency into robotic control. This is crucial for extending robot operating times and enabling more practical, long-duration autonomous missions.

**B. Bridging Kinematic Imitation and Passive Dynamics**
The research successfully bridges a gap in current reinforcement learning approaches for legged locomotion. While Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) excel at replicating the explicit kinematic patterns of biological motion, they often overlook the implicit passive dynamics. By combining AMP with the Impact Mitigation Factor (IMF), the developed hybrid reward framework enables policies to learn both the "style" of motion and the underlying physical principles of energy-efficient interaction with the environment. This represents a step towards creating more holistic and biologically plausible robot behaviors.

**C. Enhancing Dynamic Resilience and Robustness**
The observed improvements in motion fidelity and reduced RMSE for pitch on rough terrain indicate that IMF-augmented policies lead to increased dynamic resilience. By encouraging passive impact absorption, the robot becomes better equipped to handle ground irregularities and impacts, leading to more stable and robust locomotion. This is particularly important for robots operating in unstructured or unpredictable environments where perfect terrain knowledge is unavailable.

**D. Reducing Mechanical Wear and Tear**
The findings show a substantial reduction in cumulative joint torques (25-32% for handcrafted+IMF, 18-25% for AMP+IMF). This directly translates to lower stress on robot actuators and mechanical components. Reduced joint loading can significantly prolong the lifespan of robotic hardware, decrease maintenance requirements, and potentially allow for the use of lighter or less powerful actuators while maintaining performance.

**E. Simplifying Reward Engineering**
By introducing a physics-informed metric like IMF as an effective reward signal, the work offers a pathway to potentially simplify reward engineering for complex dynamic tasks. Instead of laboriously tuning multiple kinematic and penalty terms to implicitly encourage energy efficiency, a single, theoretically grounded term directly promotes the desired physical interaction principle. This can make the process of developing control policies for new robots or tasks more efficient.

**F. Future Directions**
The reported results are from simulations. The authors suggest future work will expand this concept to broader contexts, including real-world hardware experiments and more aggressive maneuvers. Successful transfer to physical robots would validate the practical utility of the IMF-guided approach and pave the way for real-world applications requiring efficient and robust legged locomotion.
