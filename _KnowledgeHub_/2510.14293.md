# Learning Human-Humanoid Coordination for Collaborative Object Carrying

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed COLA, a framework enabling humanoid robots to compliantly and coordinately carry objects with humans, leveraging a proprioception-only policy learned through a three-step training process. The system achieved a 24.7% reduction in human effort and demonstrated stable performance across diverse objects and terrains in real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Existing human-humanoid collaboration methods are often rigid, rely on predefined heuristic rules, or lack whole-body coordination capabilities for complex tasks like object carrying.
- Integrating advanced humanoid locomotion and manipulation skills into truly compliant collaboration with humans, especially in dynamic environments, remains challenging.
- Prior approaches frequently depend on external sensors (e.g., cameras, force sensors) or explicit models of human intention, complicating real-world deployment and limiting robustness.

## Method

- A three-step learning pipeline starts with training a foundational ==Whole-Body Control (WBC)== policy for robust locomotion and general manipulation.
- A residual teacher policy is trained in a closed-loop simulation environment with privileged object information, implicitly learning collaborative dynamics and human intentions.
- The teacher's expertise is distilled into a proprioception-only student policy using behavioral cloning, enabling robust and practical real-world deployment without external sensors.

## Results

- COLA achieved superior performance in simulation, demonstrating the lowest linear velocity tracking error (**0.102** m/s) and angular velocity tracking error (**0.098** rad/s), alongside enhanced object stability.
- The framework reduced human effort by **24.7%** in simulation, achieving the lowest average external force (**12.298** N), which was further corroborated by real-world human user studies.
- Real-world experiments successfully validated COLA's ability to carry diverse objects across varied terrains and movement patterns, exhibiting implicit learning of human intention and flexible leader/follower role switching.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Joint state offsets can effectively proxy interaction forces, allowing a humanoid to implicitly estimate and respond to physical interactions using only proprioceptive feedback.
> - The state of the carried object implicitly encodes collaboration constraints, which can be leveraged by a learning agent to infer optimal collaborative behaviors.
> - Training a residual policy on top of a pre-trained whole-body control policy efficiently enables complex collaborative skills by learning corrective adjustments rather than entirely new actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of human-robot collaboration (HRC) has garnered increasing attention, driven by the potential for robots to assist humans in diverse domains such as healthcare, domestic tasks, and manufacturing. While significant progress has been made in compliant collaboration involving robotic arms, enabling truly compliant and effective human-humanoid collaboration remains a formidable challenge. Humanoid robots, with their complex whole-body dynamics and human-like morphology, offer greater versatility and mobility in open environments compared to static robotic arms. However, this complexity also introduces unique hurdles for seamless collaboration.

Prior research in human-robot collaboration has largely focused on several distinct areas:
- **Robotic Arm Collaboration:** Extensively studied, often in confined workspaces, utilizing explicit force sensing and model-based control to achieve compliant interaction.
- **Humanoid Locomotion and Manipulation:** Significant advancements have enabled humanoids to achieve agile locomotion, teleoperation, and dexterous manipulation. However, integrating these skills into effective collaboration with humans has been limited.
- **Model-Based Approaches for Humanoid Collaboration:** Existing human-humanoid collaboration methods often rely on predefined heuristic rules or explicit models of human intention and robot behavior. These approaches tend to be rigid, struggle with dynamic interactions, and lack the ability for whole-body coordination, limiting their applicability to complex tasks like picking up objects from the ground or navigating varied terrains while carrying objects. For example, some methods predict human intention from multi-modal data or use haptic cues for limited scope tasks, but often neglect full-body dynamics.
- **Force and Compliance Control:** Research has explored explicit force estimation and integration into control policies for contact-rich manipulation. More recent approaches have learned force characteristics implicitly for compliant behaviors in legged robots. However, applying these insights comprehensively to whole-body human-humanoid collaboration, especially for object carrying, has been underexplored.

COLA distinguishes itself by addressing the limitations of prior work in several key ways. It moves beyond model-based or heuristic approaches by employing a data-driven reinforcement learning framework. Crucially, it tackles the challenge of **whole-body coordination** in dynamic object carrying tasks, which previous methods often neglected. Unlike approaches that rely on external sensors or explicit force sensing, COLA proposes a **proprioception-only policy** for real-world deployment, making it highly practical and robust. Furthermore, it introduces the concept of **implicitly learning human intentions and object dynamics** within a closed-loop training environment, allowing the humanoid to adapt compliantly and flexibly switch between leader and follower roles without explicit commands. This holistic approach, combining robust control, implicit interaction learning, and sensor-agnostic deployment, marks a significant step forward in realizing the potential of humanoid robots for truly collaborative assistance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind COLA is to unlock the significant promise of human-humanoid collaboration for real-world applications by enabling humanoids to assist humans in complex, shared tasks like object carrying. The authors identify the current limitations in this domain, particularly the lack of compliant, whole-body coordination capabilities in humanoids, as a critical barrier to their widespread adoption.

The key objectives of the COLA research can be summarized as follows:

1. **Develop a Compliant and Coordinated Policy:** To enable humanoid robots to collaborate seamlessly with human partners in object carrying tasks, exhibiting compliant behavior that adapts to human motion and maintains object stability.
2. **Achieve Whole-Body Coordination:** To overcome the limitations of prior work that neglects full-body dynamics, allowing the humanoid to perform complex collaborative actions such as lifting, lowering, and traversing varied terrains (e.g., slopes) while carrying diverse objects.
3. **Facilitate Flexible Role Allocation:** To empower the humanoid to flexibly switch between leader and follower roles, optimizing the efficiency and naturalness of collaboration.
4. **Reduce Human Effort:** A core objective is to minimize the physical burden on the human partner during collaborative carrying, thereby enhancing the practical value of humanoid assistance.
5. **Enable Proprioception-Only Deployment:** To create a robust policy that relies solely on the robot's internal sensor feedback (proprioception) for real-world operation, eliminating the need for external sensors (e.g., cameras, force sensors at contact points) or complex interaction models, thus simplifying deployment and increasing robustness to environmental variations.
6. **Implicitly Infer Human Intent and Object Dynamics:** To allow the humanoid to implicitly learn object motion patterns and human intentions directly from physical interactions and dynamic feedback, rather than requiring explicit commands or pre-programmed behaviors.
7. **Ensure Generalizability and Robustness:** To demonstrate the effectiveness, generalization, and robustness of the developed model across various terrains, object types, and movement patterns in both simulation and real-world scenarios.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between advanced humanoid locomotion/manipulation and practical, intuitive human-humanoid collaboration by providing a unified, learning-based framework that emphasizes compliance, whole-body control, and real-world applicability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

COLA proposes a novel, learning-based approach structured around a three-step training pipeline designed to achieve compliant and coordinated human-humanoid collaboration using only proprioceptive feedback. The core insights guiding this design are that (i) joint state offsets can proxy interaction forces, and (ii) the carried object's state implicitly encodes collaboration constraints.

The methodology comprises the following key steps:

#### 3.1. A. Whole-body Control (WBC) Policy Training

- **Foundation:** The first step involves training a foundational Whole-Body Control (WBC) policy in a simulated environment. This policy is responsible for robust locomotion and general manipulation.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** The WBC policy takes as input a combination of lower-body locomotion commands (linear velocity, angular velocity, root height) and upper-body end-effector pose commands (position and rotation). Its observations include a history of joint positions, joint velocities, root orientation, gravity vector, and previous actions. The output is a set of target joint positions for the robot's 29 joints (excluding fingers).
- **Actuation and Algorithm:** PD position control is used for actuation. The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is employed for reinforcement learning.
- **Robustness:** To enhance force-adaptive capabilities, external forces are applied to the humanoid's end-effectors during this initial training phase.

#### 3.2. B. Residual Teacher Policy Training for Collaboration

- **Closed-Loop Environment:** A crucial component of this step is a specialized closed-loop training environment. This environment explicitly models the dynamic interactions between the human (simulated by a "supporting base body"), the object, and the humanoid. The object is connected to the support body via a 6-DoF joint, allowing for realistic transmission of forces and movements.
- **Residual Learning:** Instead of training a new policy from scratch, a "residual teacher policy" is trained on top of the pre-trained WBC policy. This allows the teacher to build collaborative skills by outputting corrective adjustments (residual actions) to the WBC policy's outputs. This implicitly infers collaborative intent directly from physical interaction, as explicitly encoding such nuances with manual commands is challenging.
- **Privileged Information:** During training, the teacher policy has access to "privileged information" (O_priv), which includes the ground-truth pose and velocity history of the carried object. This privileged access allows the teacher to learn optimal collaborative strategies more effectively.
- **Reward Function:** The teacher's learning is guided by a composite reward function (detailed in Table I) that combines standard whole-body control rewards with task-specific rewards. These include rewards for linear and angular velocity tracking, penalties for Z-axis object velocity, height differences between object ends, and support forces.
- **Role Allocation:** Role allocation (leader/follower) is implicitly controlled via a velocity command. A zero velocity command corresponds to the humanoid acting as a follower, adapting to the human's motion.

#### 3.3. C. Student Policy Distillation

- **Real-World Deployment Focus:** The teacher policy, relying on privileged information, is not suitable for real-world deployment. Therefore, its expertise is distilled into a "student policy."
- **Proprioception-Only:** The student policy is designed to operate solely on proprioceptive observations (O_wbc), mirroring the input space of the initial WBC policy, making it practical for real-world applications without external sensors.
- **Behavioral Cloning:** Knowledge transfer is achieved through behavioral cloning. The student policy is trained to mimic the actions of the teacher policy by minimizing the mean squared error between their outputs during interactions with the environment.
- **Collaboration Modes (COLA-F and COLA-L):** Two experimental settings are defined: COLA-F (Follower), where the humanoid receives a zero-goal command and adapts to the human; and COLA-L (Leader), where the policy is provided with a sampled goal command, enabling it to initiate and lead movements.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Training Platform:** Isaac Lab on an RTX 4090D GPU with PPO and 4096 parallel environments.
- **Network Architecture:** Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) are used for actor and critic networks, with the residual teacher and student policies employing additional MLPs.
- **Command Sampling:** Whole-body control and collaborative carrying commands are sampled from predefined ranges, including end-effector pose commands near a default grasping pose for fine-scale arm adjustments.

This multi-stage, learning-based approach, especially the implicit learning of interaction dynamics within a closed-loop environment and the distillation to a proprioception-only student, forms the core of COLA's innovative contribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of COLA was rigorously evaluated through comprehensive simulation experiments, real-world deployments, and human user studies. The results consistently demonstrate its superiority over baseline methods in achieving compliant, coordinated, and generalizable human-humanoid collaboration.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments

- **Superior Performance:** COLA significantly outperformed all baseline methods (Vanilla MLP, Explicit Goal Estimation, Transformer) across key quantitative metrics (Table III).
 - **Trajectory Coordination:** COLA-L achieved the lowest linear velocity tracking error (0.102 m/s) and angular velocity tracking error (0.098 rad/s), indicating precise coordination with human motion.
 - **Object Stability:** COLA-F and COLA-L showed superior height error (0.031 m and 0.038 m respectively), demonstrating better maintenance of object stability during carrying.
 - **Human Effort Reduction:** COLA-L achieved the lowest average external force (12.298 N), signifying a substantial reduction in human effort. The abstract mentions a 24.7% reduction compared to baselines.
- **Effectiveness of Teacher-Student Framework:** The "Explicit Goal Estimation" baseline performed poorly, highlighting that collaboration is not just about predicting control commands but implicitly learning dynamic interactions. This validates COLA's approach of learning object dynamics and human movements within a closed-loop environment.
- **Architecture Design Validation:**
 - **MLP vs. Transformer:** COLA's MLP-based student policy outperformed a Transformer-based baseline, which required twice the training steps. This suggests that for dynamic collaborative tasks, prompt adaptation to current object motion (achieved by MLPs) is more crucial than long-term temporal processing.
 - **History Length:** An ablation study showed that a history length of 25 frames provided optimal performance, balancing sufficient information with efficient adaptation, indicating that the task is not overly sensitive to very long-term joint position changes.
- **Compliance to External Forces:** Simulation tests (Fig. 5a, 5b) showed COLA's effective response to external forces. Unlike baselines that remained stationary or merely resisted, COLA began to follow external forces above a certain threshold (e.g., 15N) and demonstrated agile full-body compliance to vertical disturbances, actively supporting the load while adapting its height. COLA-L consistently outperformed COLA-F, suggesting that explicit goal commands provide beneficial cues for collaboration in dynamic environments.

#### 4.2. B. Real-World Experiments

- **Qualitative Demonstrations:** Real-world experiments (Fig. 4) validated COLA's practical value. The humanoid successfully carried diverse objects (e.g., boxes, carts, stretchers) across various terrains (including slopes) and movement patterns (straight lines, turns). It also demonstrated capabilities in object lifting and lowering.
- **Implicit Human Intention Learning:** COLA implicitly learned to interpret human intentions. When humans applied directional forces, the humanoid inferred the desired movement and autonomously continued the motion, showcasing force-aware adaptation without explicit commands.
- **Quantitative Metrics in Real-World:**
 - **Reduced Effort and Improved Stability:** COLA reduced height-tracking error by approximately three-quarters and exhibited stronger compliance to external forces compared to the baseline (Fig. 5c, 5d). This confirms practical assistance by maintaining stable object pose and reducing human physical effort.
- **Human User Studies:** A study with 23 participants rated COLA's performance on Height Tracking and Smoothness (scale 1-5). COLA achieved the highest average scores (3.96 for both metrics) compared to baselines, further confirming its perceived effectiveness and compliance in real-world human-robot interaction.

#### 4.3. C. Implicit Estimation of Interaction Forces

- **Joint-Specific Sensitivity:** Analysis of humanoid behavior (Fig. 6) revealed that the robot's response was sensitive to the point of force application. Forces on the hand or arm (end-effectors) caused the humanoid to follow, while forces on the torso or legs resulted in resistance and maintenance of a stable stance. This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that COLA effectively learns interaction dynamics and implicitly estimates interaction forces from the offsets between joint states and their targets, enabling context-aware compliant behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The COLA framework represents a significant advancement in the field of human-humanoid collaboration, offering several key contributions and promising a substantial impact:

1. **Proprioception-Only, Whole-Body Collaboration:** COLA's most significant contribution is the development of a unified residual model that enables compliant, coordinated, and generalizable whole-body collaboration relying solely on proprioceptive feedback. This eliminates the need for external sensors or complex interaction models, which are often fragile or costly in real-world deployments. This sensor-agnostic approach drastically increases the practicality and robustness of human-humanoid systems.
2. **Implicit Learning of Interaction Dynamics:** The innovative three-step training framework, particularly the closed-loop environment and the use of a privileged teacher policy, allows the robot to implicitly learn complex object movements and human intentions from dynamic interactions. This move away from explicit command parsing or predefined behaviors makes the robot's responses more natural, intuitive, and adaptive to unforeseen variations in human actions and environmental conditions.
3. **Flexible Leader/Follower Roles:** The ability for the humanoid to seamlessly switch between leader and follower roles, guided by simple velocity commands or inferred human intent, provides unprecedented flexibility in collaborative tasks. This adaptability allows for more efficient task execution and better integration into human workflows.
4. **Demonstrated Practical Value:** The extensive validation through simulation, real-world experiments with diverse objects and terrains, and human user studies strongly confirms COLA's practical effectiveness. The quantifiable reduction in human effort (24.7% in simulation, confirmed by user studies) and improved object stability underscore its direct benefit for assistive robotics in various domains, from logistics and manufacturing to healthcare and domestic assistance.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** COLA provides a robust baseline and insightful methodology for future research in human-humanoid interaction. While acknowledging limitations such as the absence of multi-modal perception (visual, tactile) and autonomous planning capabilities, the authors explicitly point to these as valuable avenues for future work. This suggests that COLA could serve as a springboard for integrating richer sensory information and higher-level cognitive abilities into collaborative humanoid systems.

In essence, COLA pushes the boundaries of human-humanoid collaboration by offering a robust, practical, and intuitive solution for object carrying tasks. Its focus on proprioception-only control and implicit learning lays a strong foundation for the deployment of truly helpful and adaptable humanoid robots in complex real-world environments, significantly broadening their potential applications and their role in supporting human life.
