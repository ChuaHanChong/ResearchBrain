# Hierarchical Latent Prediction for Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Hierarchical Latent Prediction (HiLP) introduces a pre-training objective for language models that incorporates multi-scale predictive learning within the latent space, aiming to improve long-range generation, reasoning, and planning. It achieved a HumanEval `pass@1` of 11.33 and improved speculative decoding efficiency, all while maintaining zero inference-time overhead.

## Problem

- Standard Next-Token Prediction (NTP) in language models suffers from "exposure bias," leading to compounding errors in long-range generation and reasoning tasks.
- Existing solutions like Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) have limited prediction horizons, and Next-Latent prediction (NextLat) primarily provides local learning signals, struggling to capture long-range temporal structures.
- The inherent hierarchical structure of natural language is not fully leveraged by current pre-training objectives, limiting models' ability to encode multi-scale predictive structure.

## Method

- Developed **Hierarchical Latent Prediction (HiLP)** by augmenting a transformer with a ==Sliding-Window Attention (SWA)== module to create higher-level abstract latents from lower-level latents.
- Trained separate ==dynamics models== for both lower-level (single-step) and higher-level (multi-step) latent predictions, alongside a ==combined NTP head== using both latent granularities.
- Utilized a weighted sum of five loss terms during training, but discarded all auxiliary components at inference time, ensuring zero architectural overhead and no increase in inference latency.

## Results

- HiLP models achieved higher accuracy on code generation, scoring **11.33** `pass@1` on HumanEval, surpassing NextLat (**10.58**), MTP (**9.21**), and the NTP baseline (**8.77**).
- Enhanced speculative decoding efficiency, with HiLP's NTP head accepting an average of **3.41** tokens per step on code data validation, compared to NextLat's **3.26** and MTP's **2.57**.
- Demonstrated lower future prediction error at longer horizons in the latent space, indicating more stable and coherent long-horizon belief state representations compared to previous methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating multi-scale self-predictive learning signals into the latent space during pre-training significantly improves a language model's capacity for long-horizon planning and reasoning.
> - The use of a higher-level abstract latent and a multi-step prediction objective effectively mitigates the accumulation of errors in multi-step latent rollouts, fostering more stable and coherent long-horizon representations.
> - It is possible to achieve improved representation quality and downstream performance benefits from auxiliary pre-training objectives without incurring any additional computational cost or architectural complexity during inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has largely been driven by the Next-Token Prediction (NTP) pre-training objective. While effective for generating coherent local sequences, NTP with teacher-forcing inherently introduces an "exposure bias" during inference. In this auto-regressive process, the model generates tokens based on its own potentially error-prone previous outputs, which can lead to compounding errors and degraded quality in long-range generation, reasoning, and planning tasks.

The current work by Shi et al. is positioned within an active research area focused on mitigating this limitation by exploring pre-training objectives beyond standard NTP. Prior attempts to address the myopic nature of NTP include:
- **Multi-Token Prediction (MTP)**: Aims to predict multiple future tokens, but often with a limited prediction horizon.
- **Next-Latent prediction (NextLat)** (Teoh et al., 2025): Introduces a self-supervised prediction loss in the latent space, encouraging local transition consistency. However, NextLat's single-step latent dynamics model still primarily provides local learning signals, making it less effective at capturing long-range temporal structures without extensive sequential unrolling, which can suffer from compounding errors.

The paper draws inspiration from the long history of multi-scale temporal abstraction in sequence modeling and reinforcement learning (Sutton et al., 1999; Bacon et al., 2017), and the observation that natural language itself incorporates hierarchical structures (e.g., characters, words, phrases, sentences, paragraphs, discourses). This context suggests that incorporating hierarchical learning signals at different granularities could lead to richer latent transition dynamics and improve long-horizon capabilities.

This work specifically distinguishes itself from other related efforts:
- **Other pretraining objectives**: While some works introduce auxiliary signals for future prediction (Gloeckle et al., 2024; Ahn et al., 2025), they typically operate on a single granularity scale. HiLP introduces two distinct latent prediction pathways at different temporal scales.
- **Latent-space language modeling**: Approaches like Large Concept Models (Barrault et al., 2024), CALM (Shao et al., 2025), and Coconut (Shao et al., 2025) modify the inference-time generation process to operate on latents, often requiring bespoke decoding procedures. HiLP, in contrast, uses latent prediction purely as a training signal, with standard token-level autoregressive decoding at inference, incurring zero additional latency.
- **Hierarchical models**: While hierarchical designs exist in various domains, including language modeling (Yu et al., 2023; Pagnoni et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2025), reinforcement learning (Zhang et al., 2026), and time series analysis (Tao et al., 2024), HiLP is presented as the first to introduce hierarchical latent prediction as a pre-training objective for autoregressive language models where the hierarchical components are entirely removed at inference time.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of standard Next-Token Prediction (NTP) in language models, particularly its inadequacy for tasks requiring long-horizon reasoning and planning. The "exposure bias" inherent in NTP's teacher-forced training paradigm leads to a training-inference mismatch where compounding errors accumulate during auto-regressive generation. Existing auxiliary objectives, such as Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) and NextLat, have attempted to mitigate this. However, MTP often has a limited prediction horizon, while NextLat's single-step latent dynamics model provides only local consistency, offering indirect gradient pressure for representations capturing long-range structure. Sequential unrolling of single-step dynamics in NextLat still suffers from error accumulation.

The researchers hypothesize that language's inherent hierarchical structure, from character to discourse levels, suggests that multi-scale learning signals could be beneficial. This observation, combined with the historical success of multi-scale temporal abstraction in sequence modeling and reinforcement learning, motivates the exploration of whether temporal hierarchy can be directly integrated into the latent-space self-prediction objective. The goal is to explicitly shape the representation of a language model transformer to encode multi-scale predictive structure during pretraining.

The key objectives of this work are articulated through the following contributions:
1. **Proposal of Hierarchical Latent Prediction (HiLP):** To introduce a novel hierarchical representation training method that incorporates multi-scale self-predictive learning into transformer pretraining. This involves utilizing a sliding-window attention mechanism over lower-level latents to generate higher-level abstract latents, training a separate higher-level dynamics model for longer-horizon predictions, and integrating a combined NTP head for comprehensive token prediction.
2. **Evaluation of Performance:** To empirically assess the effectiveness of HiLP by evaluating the resulting models on established downstream benchmarks. Specifically, the objectives include comparing HiLP's accuracy against MTP and NextLat on coding and multi-step reasoning benchmarks, and evaluating its efficiency in speculative decoding.
3. **Demonstration of Inference-Time Removal:** To show that the entire hierarchical apparatus introduced during training can be completely removed at inference time. This objective aims to preserve the zero architectural overhead and inference speed characteristic of approaches like NextLat, ensuring that the benefits of hierarchical pretraining do not come at the cost of deployment efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HiLP extends the NextLat framework by introducing a multi-scale hierarchy into the latent prediction objective. The approach involves augmenting a standard transformer architecture with additional components and training objectives that operate in the latent space at different temporal granularities.

**Architectural Components:**
1. **Backbone Transformer ($G_{\theta}$):** This is the core language model, which processes an input token sequence $X_{1:T}$ and produces lower-level latents $h_t = G_{\theta}(X_{1:t})$ for each token.
2. **Sliding-Window Attention (SWA) Module:** This component takes a window of $k$ consecutive lower-level latents ($h_{t-k+1:t}$) and computes a higher-level abstract latent $u_t = \text{SWA}(h_{t-k+1:t})$. This $u_t$ serves as a coarser-scale representation summarizing the information over the window. The window size $k$ is a hyperparameter, set to 4 in the experiments.
3. **Higher-level Dynamics Model ($p_{\phi}$):** This model is trained to predict $k$ steps ahead in the abstract latent space. Specifically, it maps $u_t$ to $\hat{u}_{t+k}$. This explicit objective encourages lookahead planning and captures longer-horizon structures beyond single-step transitions.
4. **Lower-level Dynamics Model ($p_{\psi}$):** Similar to NextLat, this model predicts the next lower-level latent ($h_{t+1}$) based on the current lower-level latent ($h_t$) and the next token ($X_{t+1}$). This enforces local transition consistency.
5. **Standard NTP Head ($p_{\theta}$):** This is the conventional output head of the transformer, predicting the next token $X_{t+1}$ conditioned solely on the lower-level latent $h_t$.
6. **Combined NTP Head ($p_{\rho}$):** This is an auxiliary output head that predicts the next token $X_{t+1}$ conditioned on both the lower-level latent $h_t$ and the higher-level abstract latent $\tilde{u}_t$.

**Training Objectives:**
The total training objective is a weighted sum of five distinct loss terms, designed to jointly learn the parameters of $G_{\theta}$, $p_{\theta}$, $p_{\psi}$, $p_{\phi}$, and $p_{\rho}$, along with the SWA module.

1. **Standard NTP Loss ($L_{ntp}$):** The cross-entropy loss for predicting $X_{t+1}$ from $h_t$.
 $L_{ntp} = E_{t<T} [-\log p_{\theta}(X_{t+1} | h_t)]$
2. **Lower-level Transition Consistency Loss ($L_h$):** A SmoothL1 loss that encourages the predicted lower-level latents ($\hat{h}_{t+i}$) to match the actual lower-level latents ($h_{t+i}$) over a rollout depth $d$ (set to 8 in experiments). A `stop-gradient (sg)` operator is applied to $h_{t+i}$ to prevent gradients from flowing back from the loss target.
 $L_h = E_t [\frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^d \text{SL1}(\text{sg}[h_{t+i}], \hat{h}_{t+i})]$
3. **KL Divergence Term ($L_{KL}$):** This term ensures that the token distribution predicted from a generated latent ($\hat{h}_{t+i}$) is consistent with the distribution from the true latent ($h_{t+i}$). The `stop-gradient` operator is applied to the true latent's distribution.
 $L_{KL} = E_t [\frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^d \text{D}_{KL}(p^{\text{sg}}_{\theta}(\cdot | \text{sg}[h_{t+i}]) || p^{\text{sg}}_{\theta}(\cdot | \hat{h}_{t+i}))]$
4. **Higher-level Transition Consistency Loss ($L_u$):** A SmoothL1 loss that forces the predicted higher-level latent ($\hat{u}_{t+k}$) to match the actual higher-level latent ($u_{t+k}$). `Stop-gradient` is applied to $u_{t+k}$.
 $L_u = E_t [\text{SmoothL1}(\text{sg}[u_{t+k}], \hat{u}_{t+k})]$
5. **Combined NTP Loss ($L_{cntp}$):** A cross-entropy loss for predicting $X_{t+1}$ from both $h_t$ and $\tilde{u}_t$. Crucially, $\tilde{u}_t := \text{SWA}(\text{sg}[h_{t-k+1:t}])$ incorporates a `stop-gradient` inside the SWA computation. This prevents the combined NTP head from updating the lower latents $h_t$ via the SWA module, ensuring that $h_t$ remains primarily governed by the standard NTP and lower-level consistency losses. This separation avoids competing optimization signals for $h_t$.

**Consistency Conditions:** The losses are designed to enforce specific conditions at optimality, ensuring next-token consistency ($p_{\theta}(X_{t+1}|h_t) = P(X_{1:t})$ and $p_{\rho}(X_{t+1}|h_t, u_t) = P(X_{1:t})$) and transition consistency in both lower ($p_{\psi}(h_{t+1}|h_t, X_{t+1}) = P(h_{t+1}|X_{1:t+1})$) and higher latent spaces ($p_{\phi}(u_{t+k}|u_t) = P(u_{t+k}|X_{1:t})$).

**Inference Procedure:** A key aspect of HiLP is that all auxiliary components – the SWA module, the lower and higher dynamics models, and the combined NTP head – are discarded at inference time. Only the backbone transformer $G_{\theta}$ and the standard NTP head $p_{\theta}$ are retained. This design ensures that HiLP does not introduce any architectural overhead or increase inference latency, making it a purely training-time intervention to shape representations.

**Implementation Details:** Models were trained with 1 billion parameters on 100 billion tokens using 8 NVIDIA B200 GPUs. The `glu_cross` input-combination mode was used for latent prediction modules and the combined NTP head, which was found to be more parameter-efficient than a simple `concat` mode, particularly for large vocabularies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HiLP focused on its performance on downstream benchmarks for coding and multi-step reasoning, its efficiency in speculative decoding, and its ability to maintain long-horizon predictive coherence in the latent space.

#### 4.1. Coding and Multi-step Reasoning Benchmarks

- **HumanEval (Code Generation):** HiLP demonstrated improved code-generation accuracy. On the HumanEval `pass@1` metric, HiLP achieved 11.33, outperforming NextLat (10.58), MTP (9.21), and the NTP baseline (8.77). This indicates that the hierarchical latent prediction mechanism contributes to a better understanding and generation of structured code.
- **DataComp for LLMs (DCLM - Symbolic/Multi-step Evaluation):** HiLP showed an improvement in performance on a set of symbolic and multi-step reasoning benchmarks from DCLM. While specific numerical results are not provided for DCLM in the excerpt, the graphical representation in Figure 2 suggests a consistent positive effect compared to MTP and NextLat.

#### 4.2. Speculative Decoding

HiLP was evaluated for its efficiency in speculative decoding, a technique used to accelerate language model inference. The metrics included the average number of accepted tokens per drafting step and the average match (per-position draft-verifier argmax agreement).
- **Code Data Validation Split:**
 - HiLP (NTP head) achieved an average of 3.41 accepted tokens per step, which was higher than NextLat (3.26) and MTP (2.57).
 - The average match score for HiLP was 0.592, also surpassing NextLat (0.580) and MTP (0.545).
 - Notably, HiLP showed improved match consistency at longer horizons (e.g., K=8), with a score of 0.340 compared to NextLat's 0.234.
- **Nemotron-ClimbMix Data Validation Split:**
 - Similar trends were observed on this dataset. HiLP (NTP head) had an average of 2.59 accepted tokens, slightly above NextLat (2.57) and MTP (2.11).
 - HiLP's average match was 0.491, compared to NextLat's 0.483 and MTP's 0.162, indicating a more reliable draft.

#### 4.3. Longer Horizon Prediction

Analysis of latent prediction cross-entropy across various rollout horizons (up to 8 steps ahead) provided insights into the internal dynamics of HiLP.
- HiLP maintained near-term cross-entropy comparable to NextLat, suggesting it did not compromise short-range predictive accuracy.
- Critically, HiLP exhibited lower future prediction error at longer horizons. This finding indicates that the auxiliary higher-level latent mechanism effectively mitigates the accumulation of errors during multi-step latent rollouts, thereby fostering more stable and coherent long-horizon belief state representations.

#### 4.4. Training Efficiency and Parameters

- **Parameter Count:** All models shared the same 1.06 billion-parameter backbone. HiLP introduced approximately 210 million additional parameters (total 1.27B) for its auxiliary components (SWA, dynamics models, combined head). However, only the 1.06 billion parameters of the backbone and standard NTP head are used during inference, ensuring no increase in inference-time model size.
- **Training Throughput:** The additional complexity of HiLP's training objectives led to a reduction in training throughput compared to the NTP baseline (126,278 tokens/s/GPU) and NextLat (104,505 tokens/s/GPU). HiLP achieved 81,653 tokens/s/GPU, which was still more efficient than MTP (61,789 tokens/s/GPU). This indicates a trade-off where improved representation quality incurs a higher training computational cost, but without impact on inference speed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Hierarchical Latent Prediction (HiLP) represents a contribution to the field of language model pre-training, particularly concerning the challenges of long-horizon reasoning and planning.

One primary significance of HiLP lies in its demonstrated ability to **mitigate the compounding error effect** commonly associated with autoregressive generation and single-scale latent prediction. By incorporating a higher-level abstract latent and a multi-step prediction objective, HiLP encourages the formation of more stable and coherent belief state representations over extended temporal scales. This is substantiated by the lower long-horizon future and excess cross-entropy observed in HiLP models.

The empirical results indicate that HiLP leads to **enhanced performance on downstream tasks requiring complex reasoning**. Improvements on the HumanEval coding benchmark and DCLM multi-step reasoning tasks suggest that the learned hierarchical representations enable language models to better handle structured and multi-step problems. This could have implications for the development of more capable AI assistants for programming, scientific discovery, and complex problem-solving.

Furthermore, HiLP contributes to **improved speculative decoding efficiency**. The higher average number of accepted tokens and increased match rates across various horizons indicate that models trained with HiLP can generate more reliable "drafts" for verification. This translates to faster inference for LLMs without compromising generation quality, which is crucial for real-world applications where latency is a critical factor.

A key design principle and significant impact of HiLP is its **zero inference-time overhead**. The entire hierarchical apparatus, including the SWA module, dynamics models, and combined head, is designed to be removed after pre-training. This ensures that the benefits of improved long-horizon capabilities and representation quality are gained during training without any architectural complexity or latency penalty at deployment. This addresses a common limitation of many auxiliary training objectives that often introduce additional computational costs during inference.

The work also contributes to the broader understanding of **latent-space language modeling** by demonstrating that hierarchical learning signals can enrich latent transition dynamics effectively without altering the fundamental autoregressive token generation process at inference. This offers a different design point compared to approaches that modify inference to operate purely in the latent space.

Finally, HiLP opens avenues for **future research directions**, such as dynamically choosing the lookahead horizon for abstract latent prediction or exploring trade-offs by selectively retaining parts of the combined NTP head during inference to balance speed and accuracy. The success of HiLP suggests that explicitly modeling the hierarchical nature of language within latent spaces during pre-training can lead to more robust and capable language models for a variety of demanding applications.
