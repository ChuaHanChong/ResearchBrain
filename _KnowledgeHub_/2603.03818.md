# Pretrained Vision-Language-Action Models are Surprisingly Resistant to Forgetting in Continual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Large pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models show surprising resistance to catastrophic forgetting in continual robot learning, maintaining performance on past tasks with minimal Experience Replay. The research from The University of Texas at Austin and Microsoft demonstrates that pretraining fundamentally alters continual learning dynamics, enabling rapid recovery of seemingly forgotten knowledge.

## Problem

- Robots must continually acquire new skills without catastrophically forgetting previously learned abilities, a challenge exacerbated by the stability-plasticity dilemma.
- Traditional continual learning approaches for smaller, non-pretrained policy models have relied on complex algorithms (e.g., large replay buffers, regularization, architectural isolation) to mitigate forgetting.
- The behavior of large-scale pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models regarding catastrophic forgetting was largely unexplored, especially in robot policy learning.

## Method

- Empirically investigated the continual learning dynamics of pretrained VLAs (==Pi0==, ==GR00T N1.5==) on sequential robot manipulation tasks using the ==LIBERO== benchmark.
- Utilized ==Experience Replay (ER)== as the primary continual learning mechanism, evaluating its effectiveness with varying and often small replay buffer sizes.
- Conducted ablations to isolate the role of pretraining, analyzed knowledge retention through ==component swapping==, and measured the efficiency of recovering 'forgotten' skills via finetuning.

## Results

- Pretrained VLAs achieved near-zero to positive Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) across multiple LIBERO benchmarks, outperforming non-pretrained models by **2-4x**, even with only **2%** of data in the replay buffer.
- The vision-language backbone was identified as the dominant source of apparent forgetting when swapping components, correlating with task diversity.
- Seemingly forgotten skills in VLAs were rapidly recovered, with models like Pi0 regaining peak performance in less than **10%** of the original training steps, indicating knowledge retention.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pretrained VLAs exhibit remarkable inherent resistance to catastrophic forgetting, achieving near-zero or even positive backward transfer with simple Experience Replay.
> - Large-scale pretraining is integral to mitigating forgetting, particularly in low-data replay regimes, enabling high forward transfer while preserving old skills.
> - Apparent performance degradation on previous tasks in VLAs often signifies inaccessible rather than entirely lost knowledge, allowing for rapid recovery with minimal finetuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenge of continual learning in robot policy acquisition, a domain where agents are required to learn new skills sequentially without experiencing catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired knowledge. Historically, research in this area, particularly for robotic manipulation, has focused on relatively small behavior cloning (BC) policy models. These smaller models, often trained from scratch or with limited pretraining, commonly exhibit significant catastrophic forgetting, necessitating complex mitigation strategies such as large experience replay buffers or sophisticated regularization techniques (e.g., Elastic Weight Consolidation, Synaptic Intelligence). The established understanding in this context often involves a fundamental stability-plasticity dilemma, where improving the ability to learn new tasks (plasticity) conflicts with preserving old knowledge (stability).

In recent years, the field of robotics has seen the emergence of large-scale pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, developed by extending large vision-language models (VLMs) with training on extensive robot demonstration datasets, have demonstrated capabilities in generalization and downstream task transfer across diverse robotic manipulation scenarios. They achieve this by leveraging knowledge acquired from pretraining on vast multimodal data (internet-scale image-text data and robot trajectories), which suggests they might adapt to new tasks by reconfiguring existing representations rather than extensively modifying core parameters.

The current work positions itself at the intersection of these two trends. It notes that the continual learning behavior of these modern, large-scale pretrained VLAs remains underexplored. Previous studies on continual learning in large pretrained models (e.g., language models) have indicated that pretraining can reduce, though not eliminate, forgetting and can alter transfer patterns, suggesting that conclusions drawn from smaller, scratch-trained models may not directly apply. This paper specifically investigates whether the dynamics of continual learning, particularly concerning forgetting, differ significantly when applied to pretrained VLAs compared to traditional smaller robot policies. It systematically explores how pretraining influences knowledge retention and transfer in the context of sequential task learning for robotic control, aiming to establish whether VLAs constitute a distinct regime within continual learning, potentially altering the perceived necessity for complex algorithmic solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically investigate the continual learning behavior of large-scale pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic manipulation. This investigation aims to determine whether VLAs exhibit different dynamics regarding catastrophic forgetting compared to smaller robot policy models trained without extensive pretraining.

The motivation for this work stems from a long-standing challenge in robot policy learning: continual learning. In this paradigm, a robot policy must acquire a sequence of new skills over time while concurrently retaining the ability to perform previously learned tasks. The phenomenon of "catastrophic forgetting" often hinders this process, where the acquisition of new skills leads to a significant degradation or complete loss of proficiency in older, learned behaviors. Prior research efforts to address catastrophic forgetting in robotics have largely concentrated on relatively small behavior cloning (BC) policy models. In these settings, forgetting is prevalent, necessitating the application of mitigation strategies such as large experience replay buffers or parameter regularization techniques, which can be computationally and data-intensive.

The recent development and deployment of large-scale pretrained VLAs have introduced a new variable into this landscape. These models, pretrained on diverse and extensive multimodal datasets, have demonstrated enhanced generalization and transfer capabilities for robotic manipulation tasks. This characteristic raises a central question: does the extensive pretraining, which provides VLAs with a rich initial representation, fundamentally alter their susceptibility to catastrophic forgetting during sequential task learning? Specifically, the authors are motivated to explore:
1. Whether pretrained VLAs exhibit increased resistance to forgetting compared to smaller, non-pretrained models.
2. How standard continual learning techniques, such as Experience Replay (ER), perform with VLAs.
3. The specific role of large-scale pretraining in influencing both the retention of old knowledge (backward transfer) and the acquisition of new skills (forward transfer).
4. The nature of knowledge retention in VLAs, particularly whether seemingly forgotten skills are truly erased or merely become inaccessible and can be recovered.

By addressing these questions, the research seeks to provide empirical insights into the stability-plasticity trade-off in the context of advanced, pretrained robotic models, with implications for the design of future lifelong robot learning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed an empirical study design to compare the continual learning performance of pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models against smaller, non-pretrained policy models.

**Problem Formulation:**
Robotic tasks were modeled as finite-horizon Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). Continual learning involved a robot sequentially acquiring *K* tasks ({T_k}_(k=1)^K) using a single task-conditioned policy π(· | s, T). Each task T_k was defined by an initial-state distribution μ_k^0 and a goal predicate g_k. The learning paradigm was imitation learning, where policies were trained on expert demonstrations D_k for each task. The objective was to minimize the behavior cloning loss across all tasks learned up to a given point. The primary continual learning strategy utilized was Experience Replay (ER), where a subset of samples from previous tasks was stored in a replay buffer and combined with the current task's training data.

**Models Evaluated:**
- **Pretrained VLAs:**
 - **Pi0 (Black et al., 2026):** A VLA model pretrained on large-scale robot datasets, built upon a PaliGemma (Beyer et al., 2024) VLM backbone.
 - **GR00T N1.5 (NVIDIA et al., 2025):** Another large VLA model, differing in architecture, parameter count, and pretraining data compared to Pi0.
- **Non-Pretrained Small Policies (Baselines):**
 - **BC-Transformer (Liu et al., 2023a):** A small policy model trained from scratch.
 - **BC-Diffusion Policy**
 - **BC-ViT**
The architecture and pretraining details of these models, including trainable and frozen components, were provided (Table 8).

**Benchmarks and Datasets:**
The study utilized the LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023b) benchmark, designed for lifelong learning in robotic manipulation. Four distinct task suites were used to assess knowledge transfer across different properties:
- LIBERO-Spatial
- LIBERO-10
- LIBERO-Object
- LIBERO-Goal
A filtered dataset (Kim et al., 2024) was used, and a consistent task ordering was maintained across all experiments.

**Training and Evaluation Procedures:**
- A separate model was trained for each task suite.
- Models were trained for a fixed number of optimization steps per stage (task), with weights carried over sequentially.
- For ER, a fixed replay buffer size of *M* randomly sampled transitions per task was maintained. The default was *M* = 1000 transitions (approximately 15-20% of the full dataset per task), with additional experiments at *M* = 100 (2%) and *M* = 10 (0.2%). Data from the replay buffer and the current task were sampled in a 1:1 ratio.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Average Success Rate (SR):** The mean success rate across all tasks.
 - **Negative Backward Transfer (NBT):** Quantified knowledge loss on previously learned tasks. Calculated as the average difference between a task's success rate immediately after learning it and its success rate after subsequent tasks were learned. Lower NBT indicates less forgetting. A normalized variant (NBT_norm) was also used for LIBERO-10 to account for initial performance differences.
 - **Knowledge Transfer (KT):** The aggregate sum of success rates across all tasks, used to measure overall learning progress (reflecting both stability and plasticity).

**Controlled Experiments for Pretraining's Role (Section 4):**
To isolate the impact of pretraining, three variants of the Pi0 architecture were compared:
1. **Pi0 from VL + Action:** The full Pi0 model, pretrained on robot data in addition to its VLM backbone.
2. **Pi0 from VL:** Initialized only from the PaliGemma VLM backbone, without additional robot data pretraining.
3. **Pi0 from scratch:** The Pi0 architecture with weights initialized randomly and trained entirely from scratch.
These variants were subjected to continual learning with varying replay buffer sizes to analyze changes in forward and backward transfer behaviors.

**Investigation of Knowledge Retention (Section 5):**
To understand the nature of "forgetting" in VLAs, a multi-pronged approach was used:
1. **Component Swapping:** The model's knowledge was decomposed into vision-language (VL) backbone and action head components. By swapping components between models trained on successive tasks (e.g., combining VL backbone from Task k+1 with action head from Task k), the researchers assessed which component contributed more to performance degradation on Task k.
2. **Task Performance Recovery (Finetuning):** For tasks where performance had degraded, the VLA was finetuned again on that specific task's data, starting from its state after learning subsequent tasks. The speed of recovery (compared to original learning speed) was measured to determine if underlying knowledge was retained.
3. **Recovery Efficiency:** This metric quantified the ratio of finetuning steps required to regain peak success rate (Tf) to the original training steps (To), providing a numerical measure of knowledge retention. This was compared between Pi0 and BC-Transformer.
Further ablations on model size and training objective were conducted in the appendix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the continual learning performance of pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models:

#### 4.1. Resistance to Forgetting with Experience Replay (ER)

- **Near-Zero Forgetting:** Pretrained VLAs, specifically Pi0 and GR00T N1.5, when trained with Experience Replay (ER), demonstrated low levels of negative backward transfer (NBT), often approaching zero or exhibiting positive backward transfer across multiple LIBERO benchmarks (Table 1, Figure 1). Positive backward transfer indicates that learning new tasks could, in some instances, improve performance on previously learned ones. This behavior contrasts with smaller policy models, which generally experienced significant forgetting under similar continual learning conditions.
- **Consistency Across VLAs:** The observed resistance to forgetting was consistent across different VLA architectures (Pi0, GR00T N1.5), despite variations in parameter count and pretraining methods, suggesting this is a general property of pretrained VLAs.
- **Effectiveness in Low Replay Data Regimes:** Pretrained VLAs maintained relatively low NBT (0.1–0.2) even with small replay buffer sizes (e.g., 2% of training data, 100 samples per task). In contrast, non-pretrained baselines showed significantly higher forgetting (0.4–0.5 NBT) and required substantially larger replay buffers (>20%) to achieve comparable NBT values (Figure 2).
- **ER's Role:** Comparisons with non-ER baselines (Sequential, EWC) indicated that explicit re-use of past-task data through ER was crucial for preserving (and sometimes improving) prior task performance in pretrained VLAs. Neither Sequential nor EWC consistently reduced forgetting for these models (Table 2).

#### 4.2. Integral Role of Pretraining

- **Mitigation of Forgetting:** Large-scale pretraining was identified as a key factor in mitigating forgetting, particularly in scenarios with small replay buffer sizes. As the buffer size decreased, the performance gap between pretrained and non-pretrained models widened (Figure 4).
- **Balanced Stability and Plasticity:** Pretraining improved continual learning by both reducing forgetting (lower NBT) and maintaining high success rates on newly learned tasks (high forward transfer). This suggests pretraining helps VLAs avoid the trade-off where low forgetting might result from insufficient plasticity (i.e., inability to learn new tasks) (Table 3, Figure 3).
- **Enhanced Aggregate Knowledge Transfer:** Pretrained Pi0 variants (Pi0 from VL + Action, Pi0 from VL) showed steadily increasing aggregate knowledge transfer over time, indicating effective acquisition of new tasks while preserving prior knowledge. In contrast, Pi0 trained from scratch exhibited slower growth in knowledge transfer (Figure 5).

#### 4.3. Retention of Seemingly Forgotten Knowledge

- **Compartmentalized Knowledge Loss:** Analysis through component swapping revealed that knowledge loss in VLAs was not monolithic across the model's architecture. Both the vision-language (VL) backbone and the action head contributed to performance degradation on previous tasks. Swapping the VL backbone resulted in a larger performance drop compared to swapping only the action head, indicating that the VL backbone was a more dominant source of apparent forgetting (Figure 7).
- **Correlation with Task Diversity:** The degree of performance degradation was observed to correlate with the diversity of tasks, with benchmarks featuring more varied visual backgrounds (e.g., LIBERO-10) showing greater impact from VL backbone updates.
- **Rapid Knowledge Recovery:** A significant finding was that even when performance on previous tasks appeared to degrade (suggesting catastrophic forgetting), the underlying task-relevant knowledge was often retained within the VLA's internal representations. Pi0 models were able to rapidly recover peak performance on these "forgotten" tasks with minimal finetuning, typically requiring less than 10% of the original training steps (Figure 8, Table 4). In contrast, the non-pretrained BC-Transformer often required a comparable or greater number of steps to relearn tasks, indicating a more complete erasure of prior knowledge.

In summary, the research demonstrates that pretrained VLAs exhibit substantial resistance to catastrophic forgetting in continual learning, performing effectively even with small replay buffers. This resistance is attributed primarily to their large-scale pretraining, which facilitates both knowledge preservation and new skill acquisition. Furthermore, the study indicates that observed performance degradation in VLAs often represents an accessibility issue rather than complete knowledge erasure, as forgotten skills can be rapidly recovered through targeted finetuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several implications for the fields of continual learning and robotic policy development, particularly concerning the capabilities of modern large-scale pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

**Re-evaluation of Continual Learning Paradigms:** The findings challenge the conventional understanding of catastrophic forgetting, which largely originated from studies on smaller neural networks. The observed resistance of pretrained VLAs to forgetting, even with minimal experience replay data, suggests that existing complex continual learning algorithms (e.g., elaborate regularization or distillation methods) may be less critical or even unnecessary for these large foundation models. This could simplify the algorithmic requirements for developing lifelong learning robots.

**Enhanced Understanding of Pretraining's Role:** The study underscores the fundamental importance of large-scale pretraining beyond initial task performance and generalization. Pretraining is identified as a key factor that fundamentally alters the stability-plasticity dynamics, enabling VLAs to maintain a balance between retaining past knowledge and acquiring new skills more effectively than models trained from scratch. This insight can guide future pretraining strategies for robotic foundation models, emphasizing architectural choices and data scales that promote continual learning capabilities.

**Implications for Lifelong Robot Learning Systems:** The capacity of VLAs to continually acquire new skills with simple replay, coupled with their ability to rapidly recover seemingly forgotten knowledge, has practical implications for building more robust and adaptable robotic systems. Robots equipped with such VLAs could be deployed in dynamic environments, learning new tasks on the fly without suffering severe performance degradation on previously mastered skills. This reduces the need for extensive re-training from scratch, potentially leading to more efficient and scalable robot deployment.

**Nature of Forgetting in Large Models:** The research provides a nuanced perspective on "forgetting" in large pretrained models. It suggests that apparent performance degradation does not necessarily equate to the complete erasure of underlying task knowledge. Instead, knowledge may be retained in internal representations, becoming temporarily inaccessible but rapidly recoverable through targeted finetuning. This understanding could inform future research into knowledge representation, modularity, and memory mechanisms within large neural networks.

**Future Research Directions:** The work opens several avenues for further investigation. For instance, exploring the specific mechanisms within VLA architectures that contribute to this resilience, optimizing replay buffer strategies (e.g., intelligent sampling) for minimal data usage, and understanding how model scale, different pretraining data distributions, and varying architectures interact to influence continual learning behavior could be fruitful. Further, the finding that the vision-language backbone is a dominant source of forgetting suggests concentrating mitigation efforts on this component during sequential learning.

In conclusion, this research indicates that pretrained VLAs represent a distinct and advantageous paradigm for continual learning in robotics. Their inherent resistance to forgetting, driven by pretraining, suggests a shift in focus from complex forgetting-mitigation algorithms to leveraging and intelligently reusing the robust knowledge encoded within these large models.
