# Achieving Olympia-Level Geometry Large Language Model Agent via Complexity Boosting Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> InternGeometry, an LLM agent, achieved medalist-level performance on International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) geometry problems, solving 44 out of 50 tasks from IMO 2000-2024. The system demonstrated high data efficiency, using significantly less training data than prior expert models, and leveraged a complexity-boosting reinforcement learning framework for curriculum generation.

## Problem

- The application of Large Language Model (LLM) agents to IMO-level geometry problems, particularly those requiring creative auxiliary constructions, remained an underexplored area.
- IMO-level geometry problems demand proofs with extended steps, incorporation of numerous theorems, and the generation of non-obvious auxiliary constructions which often lack strong heuristic guidance.
- Existing state-of-the-art expert models like AlphaGeometry 2 and SeedGeometry rely on vast amounts of synthesized data and guided large-scale search, suggesting potential for more efficient and generalized LLM agent solutions.

## Method

- Developed **InternGeometry**, an LLM agent (based on ==InternThinker==-32B) integrated with InternGeometry-DDAR, an extended symbolic geometric proof engine supporting advanced definition strategies, double points, and an expanded theorem library.
- Implemented a geometric proof agent with a long-horizon iterative reasoning loop, dynamic memory compression, and prior-guided rejection sampling to manage interaction history, prevent action collapse, and encourage diverse exploration.
- Introduced Complexity-Boosting Reinforcement Learning (CBRL), a multi-stage curriculum reinforcement learning pipeline that dynamically generates geometry problems of controllable difficulty based on the agent's current performance.

## Results

- InternGeometry solved 44 out of 50 IMO geometry problems (IMO 2000-2024), surpassing the average score of IMO gold medalists and outperforming AlphaGeometry 2 (42/50) and SeedGeometry (43/50).
- The system achieved this performance with approximately **13K** training examples, demonstrating significant data efficiency by using **0.004%** of AlphaGeometry 2's data and **0.006%** of SeedGeometry's data.
- Ablation studies confirmed that long-horizon components (proposition proposal, 'slow thinking', dynamic context compression, and prior-guided rejection sampling) and the CBRL curriculum were critical for achieving IMO-level performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Long-horizon iterative reasoning, reflection, and dynamic memory management are crucial for LLM agents to effectively explore and solve complex IMO-level geometry problems, proving more effective than simply increasing sample numbers.
> - An automated curriculum learning approach (CBRL) that progressively increases problem complexity based on the agent's performance accelerates learning, improves generalization, and prevents convergence issues from sparse rewards on hard tasks.
> - LLM agents, when properly integrated with robust symbolic engines and trained with long-horizon interaction, can discover novel auxiliary constructions and generalize geometric concepts, indicating a capacity for geometric creativity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of artificial intelligence capable of solving complex mathematical problems, particularly those encountered in competitions like the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO), represents a significant area of research. Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated considerable capabilities in diverse problem-solving domains such as programming and mathematics. By integrating with external tools like code interpreters or formal proof systems (e.g., LEAN), LLM agents can engage in iterative reasoning and reflection based on tool feedback, achieving medalist-level performance in various mathematical tasks. This approach is often noted for its potential for generalization across problem types.

However, the application of LLM agents to geometry problems, especially at the IMO level, has remained an underexplored area. Geometry problems often require proofs with extended steps, incorporating numerous theorems, and crucially, involve the generation of creative auxiliary constructions. These constructions typically lack strong heuristic guidance, necessitating extensive trial-and-error. Consequently, the state-of-the-art in geometry problem-solving has largely been dominated by expert models, such as AlphaGeometry 2 and SeedGeometry. These systems are characterized by their reliance on vast amounts of synthesized data for training and a guided large-scale search through a symbolic engine to discover proofs.

This research addresses this gap by investigating the viability of LLM agents for solving IMO-level geometry problems. It contrasts with existing expert models by aiming for higher efficiency and generalization through an agentic LLM approach, rather than solely depending on massive data synthesis and search. In the context of reinforcement learning (RL) agents in mathematics, prior works have focused on informal proofs using general-purpose tools or formal proofs with Interactive Theorem Provers (ITPs) for non-geometric domains. While curriculum learning is also explored in RL, many existing methods rely on structured task types or manually designed curricula. This work distinguishes itself by developing a system that automates large-scale data synthesis with controllable difficulty, allowing for an unrestricted and unbiased curriculum adjustment, particularly for more complex tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an LLM agent, named InternGeometry, capable of achieving medalist-level performance in solving International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) geometry problems.

This objective is motivated by several factors:

- **Underexplored Potential of LLM Agents in Geometry:** While LLM agents have exhibited strong general problem-solving abilities in other mathematical and programming domains, their application to complex geometry problems, particularly those requiring creative auxiliary constructions, has been limited. Existing expert models for geometry rely on extensive data and search, suggesting a potential for LLM agents to offer more generalized and efficient solutions.
- **Challenges of IMO-Level Geometry:** IMO-level geometry problems are characterized by their requirement for long proving steps, the integration of diverse geometric theorems, and the need for non-obvious auxiliary constructions. These auxiliary constructions often lack clear heuristics, making their discovery a significant challenge for automated systems.
- **Limitations of Current State-of-the-Art:** Current leading approaches like AlphaGeometry 2 and SeedGeometry primarily employ expert models trained on large-scale synthetic data. These models then guide extensive search processes with symbolic engines to find proofs. While effective, this paradigm often demands substantial data resources and computational effort for search. The researchers aim to explore if an LLM agent can achieve comparable or superior performance with higher efficiency and better generalization.
- **Need for Long-Horizon Reasoning and Reflection:** Solving complex geometry problems, akin to human experts, often involves iterative exploration, proposing ideas, verifying them, and adapting strategies based on feedback. Traditional expert models might not intrinsically support this type of long-horizon, reflective interaction in the same manner as LLM agents.
- **Demand for Efficient Learning:** The high data requirements of previous expert models motivate the development of more data-efficient learning paradigms. The researchers hypothesize that a curriculum learning approach, specifically one that adapts problem complexity, could accelerate the acquisition of expert-level capabilities with reduced data.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire to push the boundaries of LLM agent capabilities into the domain of expert-level geometry, addressing the challenges of auxiliary constructions and long-horizon reasoning, while also seeking to improve learning efficiency compared to existing data-intensive expert systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The InternGeometry system integrates a Large Language Model (LLM) agent with a specialized geometric proof engine and employs a novel reinforcement learning framework.

#### 3.1. Geometry Proof Language and Environment (InternGeometry-DDAR)

InternGeometry-DDAR serves as the interactive geometric proof engine, built upon the open-source Deductive Database Arithmetic Reasoning (DDAR) system Newclid. It extends previous symbolic engines (like Newclid and AlphaGeometry) with enhanced capabilities to handle complex geometric structures and interactions.

- **Advanced Definition Strategies:** The system introduces methods for globally optimizing point placements to satisfy multiple geometric constraints simultaneously, addressing scenarios where points must adhere to specific conditions (e.g., a line being tangent to a circle) that cannot be enforced through simple point-by-point construction. This is achieved using gradient descent to adjust points.
- **Support for Double Points:** To handle cases where distinctively named points might geometrically coincide—a common technique in geometry proofs—new syntax and rules are introduced. A `!` prefix allows creating points even if they share coordinates, and a predicate `idc x y` is defined to indicate geometric equivalence of points, with associated inference rules.
- **Expanded Theorem Library:** InternGeometry-DDAR incorporates additional common geometry theorems, such as the Power of a Point and Menelaus’ theorem, into its rule set to broaden its deductive capabilities.
- **Interactive Functionality:** The engine allows the LLM agent to interact using a Domain-Specific Language (DSL) for problem specification, auxiliary point construction, and proposing sub-proof goals. It maintains a persistent state, including the geometric configuration, constructed auxiliary points, and all currently proven propositions, providing feedback to the agent after each action.

#### 3.2. Geometric Proof Agent (InternGeometry)

The InternGeometry agent is an LLM (based on InternThinker-32B) designed for long-term interaction with the InternGeometry-DDAR engine.

- **Iterative Reasoning Loop:** At each step `t`, the agent performs natural-language reasoning (`P_t`), outputs a structured action in DSL (`A_t`), and receives execution results (`O_t`) from the engine. This feedback is appended to the interaction history to guide subsequent reasoning.
- **Dynamic Memory (W):** To manage the potentially hundreds of turns in an interaction history and overcome context window limitations, a dynamic memory mechanism `W` is employed. It compresses earlier exchanges by retaining only core actions and key environment feedback, while keeping the most recent turn's feedback intact. This aims to provide a concise overview of progress without losing critical information, enabling longer reasoning horizons.
- **Prior-Guided Rejection Sampling:** To mitigate "action collapse"—the tendency of LLMs to produce repetitive or highly similar outputs in long interactions—a rule-based `PassCheck` policy is used during inference. This policy rejects actions that are identical to previous ones, excessively long thoughts without an action, invalid actions, or repeated action types in consecutive turns, promoting diverse exploration.

#### 3.3. Complexity-Boosting Reinforcement Learning (CBRL)

CBRL is a multi-stage curriculum reinforcement learning pipeline designed to train InternGeometry efficiently.

- **Cold Start:** The process begins with a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) phase. InternThinker-32B is fine-tuned (as InternGeometry-Formalizer) on 7K formalized geometry problems and solution trajectories, enabling the agent to acquire basic reasoning patterns and tool invocation behaviors.
- **Iterative RL Phase:** Following SFT, an iterative interaction-training loop commences.
 - **Reward Function:** A binary reward system is used. A reward `r` is 1 if the overall proof is complete AND the current step's action is effective. For proposition-proposing steps, `r_s` is 1 if the proposition is successfully proven by the engine. For auxiliary-construction steps, `r_s` is 1 if the construction is added and used in the final proof. Otherwise, `r` is 0. This simple, rule-based reward calculation provides signals for both successful problem-solving and effective intermediate steps.
 - **GRPO Optimization:** The agent's policy is updated using a gradient ascent method (Generalized Proximal Policy Optimization, GRPO), which aims to maximize rewards while controlling policy divergence.
 - **Data Synthesis Pipeline (X):** This pipeline dynamically generates geometry problems with controllable difficulty. Problem difficulty (complexity `𝜅`) is measured by the number of DDAR proof steps required.
 - **Generate Data Algorithm:** It iteratively samples raw geometric structures, augments them with auxiliary constructions (both conditioned on `𝜅`), and then uses exhaustive search to identify non-trivial conclusions that become provable only after the auxiliary constructions are added. The most complex such conclusion (by proof length) forms a new problem. This process enriches a global cache of problems.
 - **Data Synthesis Pipeline Algorithm:** It manages the generation and selection of `K` problems from the cache that are within a specified range around the target complexity `𝜅`.
 - **Curriculum Algorithm:** In each CBRL iteration, a batch of data is sampled according to the current complexity `𝜅`. After RL training on this batch, the average reward is calculated. If the average reward is greater than 0.5, `𝜅` is increased; otherwise, it is decreased. This mechanism, based on theoretical findings (Theorem 2.2), aims to keep the task difficulty at a "moderate" level for the agent, maximizing the expected absolute advantage and accelerating learning (Theorem 2.1). The pipeline produces a total of 6K problems dynamically during CBRL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate the capabilities of InternGeometry in solving IMO-level geometry problems and validate the effectiveness of its design components.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance on IMO 50

InternGeometry solved 44 out of 50 geometry problems from the IMO 2000-2024 test set (IMO 50). This performance surpassed the average score of IMO gold medalists (40.9 points) and exceeded that of existing state-of-the-art expert models, AlphaGeometry 2 (42/50) and SeedGeometry (43/50). A notable aspect of InternGeometry's achievement is its data efficiency: it utilized approximately 13K training examples, which is 0.004% of the data used by AlphaGeometry 2 and 0.006% of SeedGeometry. Furthermore, its test-time scaling budget (Pass@256) was significantly lower than the ensemble of search trees employed by AlphaGeometry 2.

#### 4.2. Problem-by-Problem Comparison

When evaluated on individual problems from IMO 50 and additionally IMO 2025 P2, InternGeometry successfully solved 45 out of 51 problems. It solved all problems that AlphaGeometry 2 could solve, and additionally solved IMO 2018 P6 and IMO 2023 P6. Compared to SeedGeometry, InternGeometry additionally solved IMO 2001 P5 and IMO 2009 P4b, but missed IMO 2006 P1. The problems that InternGeometry failed to solve primarily involved computations or non-geometric analyses that extended beyond the expressive scope of its current geometric DDAR system.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Long-Horizon Agent Capabilities

Investigations into the agent's long-horizon interaction capabilities yielded the following:

- **Impact of Interaction Steps:** The success rate of proofs improved significantly as the maximum number of interaction steps allowed for the agent increased. This trend was consistent across different sampling times (Pass@K), indicating that longer interaction horizons enable the agent to explore and develop problem-specific heuristics more effectively.
- **Test-Time Scalability:** An increase in sampling times (K) also led to a rise in Pass@K, demonstrating the test-time scalability of InternGeometry.
- **Efficiency of Trajectory Length:** Extending the agent's trajectory length proved more effective for scaling performance than merely increasing the number of repeated samples, given a fixed total inference budget (sampling number K multiplied by agent steps). This supports the hypothesis that long-horizon interactions are crucial for enhancing geometric proof heuristics.
- **Ablation Studies:** Ablation experiments confirmed the critical roles of various long-horizon components:
 - Removing the ability to propose propositions significantly degraded performance (from 44/50 to 35/50).
 - Eliminating "slow thinking" (chain-of-thought reasoning) further reduced performance (to 23/50).
 - Disabling dynamic context compression severely impacted performance (to 20/50), highlighting its importance for managing long interaction histories.
 - Removing prior-guided rejection sampling reduced solved problems to 38/50.
 These results collectively underscore the necessity of iterative reasoning, reflection, memory management, and action diversification for achieving IMO-level performance.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Complexity Boosting Reinforcement Learning (CBRL)

The effectiveness of the CBRL method was analyzed:

- **Dynamic Difficulty Curriculum:** The distribution of proof lengths (as an indicator of complexity) in the synthetic data generated during CBRL training showed a consistent, uniform upward trend across training rounds. This indicated that the CBRL framework successfully provided a structured curriculum, progressively exposing the agent to more complex tasks.
- **Generalization during Training:** The agent's generalization performance on the IMO 50 test set steadily improved throughout the training process as the data complexity escalated. A notable performance jump was observed in the sixth training round, suggesting breakthrough progress linked to the incremental increase in task difficulty.
- **Ablation Studies on CBRL:**
 - The baseline performance after supervised fine-tuning (SFT Cold Start) was 22/50.
 - Training only with low-difficulty "Easy Data" resulted in 29/50 solved problems, indicating a limitation in generalizing to IMO-level problems.
 - Training only with high-difficulty "Challenging Data" led to 24/50 solved problems, demonstrating that directly training on hard data hinders convergence due to sparse learning signals.
 - Removing the dynamic curriculum ("Same Data without Schedule," i.e., uniform sampling from all synthesized data) degraded performance to 38/50. This confirms that the progressive difficulty curriculum is essential for efficient learning by providing accessible rewards and preventing the agent from struggling with overly sparse signals on hard problems.

#### 4.5. Case Study

In a case study of IMO 2018 P6, InternGeometry demonstrated creativity by proposing novel auxiliary constructions that differed from human solutions, which often rely on inversion, trigonometry, or complex numbers. The agent solved the problem using classical angle chasing and basic theorems, employing an elegant geometric construction involving an isogonal conjugate pair and symmetric points to exploit rotational symmetry. This indicates a capacity for discovering implicit geometric structures and generalizing concepts from simpler configurations (e.g., triangles to quadrilaterals).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents InternGeometry, an LLM agent that establishes a new benchmark for geometry problem-solving at the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) level. Its findings hold several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Pioneering LLM Agents in Expert-Level Geometry:** InternGeometry is presented as the first medalist-level LLM agent for IMO geometry, a domain previously dominated by expert models. This work demonstrates that LLM agents can move beyond other mathematical domains to tackle geometry's unique challenges, particularly auxiliary constructions.
- **Demonstrated Data Efficiency:** Achieving superior performance (44/50 on IMO 50) with only 13K training examples—a fraction (0.004%) of the data used by AlphaGeometry 2—highlights the substantial data efficiency of the LLM agent approach. This suggests a potential paradigm shift away from reliance on massive synthetic datasets, making expert-level AI systems more accessible and resource-efficient.
- **Validation of Long-Horizon Reasoning and Reflection:** The critical role of long-horizon interactions, dynamic memory, and reflective feedback in achieving high performance is substantiated. This reinforces the importance of agentic capabilities—iterative proposal, verification, and reflection—for solving complex problems where initial heuristics are weak, aligning with human expert problem-solving processes.
- **Advancement in Automated Curriculum Learning:** The Complexity-Boosting Reinforcement Learning (CBRL) framework provides an effective, automated method for curriculum generation. By dynamically adjusting task difficulty based on the agent's performance, CBRL optimizes learning efficiency and enables the gradual acquisition of complex skills, improving generalization to challenging tasks. This approach could be broadly applicable to other domains requiring skill development from simple to complex.
- **Potential for Creative Problem Solving:** The observed ability of InternGeometry to propose novel auxiliary constructions, distinct from human-generated solutions, indicates a capacity for geometric creativity. This suggests that LLM agents, when properly integrated with symbolic engines and reinforced through exploration, might not merely reproduce known solutions but could also discover alternative, potentially more elegant, problem-solving paths.
- **Future Directions for AI in Mathematics:** This work opens new avenues for research into LLM agents in complex mathematical fields, potentially fostering the development of AI systems that can reason more deeply, adapt more flexibly, and exhibit greater autonomy in problem discovery and solution generation. It could lead to hybrid AI systems where the symbolic rigor of formal provers is synergistically combined with the flexible reasoning and generation capabilities of LLMs.
