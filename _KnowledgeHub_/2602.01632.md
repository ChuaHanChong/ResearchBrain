# A Closed-Form Geometric Retargeting Solver for Upper Body Humanoid Robot Teleoperation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology developed SEW-Mimic, a closed-form geometric solver that retargets human upper body motion to 7-DoF robot arms by aligning limb orientations, achieving significantly faster inference times (median 0.587 ms) and superior pose similarity. This approach enhances real-time teleoperation and leads to robot policies with approximately three times higher success rates.

## Problem

- Existing teleoperation methods often suffer from high latency, leading to hesitant robot movements and poor-quality data for training autonomous policies.
- End-effector only retargeting for 7-DoF robot arms can result in uncontrolled null-space elbow motion, reducing pose similarity and increasing collision risks.
- Jacobian-based inverse kinematics (IK) solvers are prone to numerical instability and reduced speed near robot joint singularities.

## Method

- ==SEW-Mimic== formulates the retargeting problem as an orientation alignment task, minimizing error across human upper arm, lower arm, and wrist segments.
- It employs a closed-form geometric solver leveraging canonical subproblems to derive robot joint angles, ensuring a provably optimal and scale-independent kinematic solution.
- A real-time safety filter is integrated using capsule collision volumes and an XPBD-based approach to prevent self-collisions during bimanual teleoperation, utilizing SEW-Mimic's computational efficiency.

## Results

- SEW-Mimic achieved significantly faster inference (median **0.587** ms) and dramatically lower alignment error (median **1.57** × 10−13) compared to baseline methods.
- Robot policies trained on demonstration data collected with SEW-Mimic showed approximately 3× higher success rates and shorter task completion times than those trained on baseline data.
- The integrated safety filter reduced self-collision instances from nearly **50%** to approximately **1.3%** in bimanual tasks, albeit with a slight increase in alignment error and computation time (to ~**4.0** ms for both arms).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritizing orientational alignment of human limb segments enables a robust, scale-independent, and analytically solvable kinematic mapping to 7-DoF robot arms.
> - Closed-form geometric solutions can overcome the latency and numerical instability issues commonly found in iterative optimization or learning-based retargeting methods.
> - The high computational speed of the core retargeting algorithm provides sufficient overhead for implementing real-time safety measures like self-collision avoidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Retargeting human motion to robot configurations is a common strategy for teleoperating high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) robots and for collecting training data for autonomous robotic policies. However, the inherent physical differences between human and robot embodiments, such as varying sizes, DoFs, and motion constraints, present complexities in developing effective retargeting solutions.

Existing teleoperation systems generally fall into two categories: hardware puppeteering and software pose retargeting. Hardware-based approaches, such as those utilizing exoskeleton cockpits, can offer high computation speed and pose similarity but are often limited in accessibility and adaptability across different robot platforms. Software-based methods, which typically derive human motion from cameras or wearable devices, often employ inverse kinematics (IK) to retarget either end-effector motion or the entire arm.

End-effector retargeting, a common software approach, focuses on matching a human operator's 6D hand pose to the robot's end-effector. These methods often utilize Jacobian-based IK solvers. A limitation of this approach is its susceptibility to numerical instability and reduced computation speed near robot joint singularities, such as full extension. For 7-DoF humanoid arms, end-effector only retargeting can also lead to unwanted null-space motion, particularly around the elbow joint, increasing collision risks and reducing pose similarity.

To address the limitations of end-effector-only control, some contemporary learning-based humanoid retargeting approaches optimize over robot joint angles to match multiple human body keypoints (e.g., shoulder, elbow, wrist). While these methods can achieve higher pose similarity by incorporating elbow control, they often suffer from significant latency (e.g., up to 0.7 seconds), which can hinder real-time teleoperation and introduce hesitant behaviors into collected demonstration data, negatively impacting subsequent policy learning.

Analytical geometric IK solvers, a third category, avoid Jacobian singularities and can offer rapid computation speeds. While such methods have a long history and recent improvements, they traditionally focus on pure IK for 6-DoF manipulators rather than directly addressing pose similarity in human-to-robot retargeting for 7-DoF arms. Some recent work has explored parameterizing the redundant 7th DoF using elbow angles, leading to closed-form or low-dimensional search solutions depending on morphology.

This paper positions its work, SEW-Mimic, as an advancement within the analytical geometric IK domain, specifically tailored for retargeting. It aims to overcome the latency and suboptimal motion issues of optimization-based methods and the null-space problems of end-effector-only approaches by offering a closed-form, provably-optimal geometric solution that directly prioritizes orientation alignment across key human limb segments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a retargeting algorithm for upper body humanoid robot teleoperation that is both computationally efficient and accurate, thereby addressing limitations observed in existing methods. The method is specifically designed to enable intuitive and robust control of 7-DoF robot arms.

The key motivations driving this work stem from several challenges in the current robot teleoperation landscape:
- **Suboptimal Motion and Latency:** Many existing retargeting methods, particularly those based on iterative optimization, can be slow. Delays of hundreds of milliseconds can lead to hesitant robot movements during teleoperation, which reduces the quality of collected training data for autonomous policies. The authors aim to minimize this latency to enable smoother and more responsive teleoperation.
- **Limited Workspace and Singularity Issues:** Methods that focus solely on matching the robot's end-effector to the human hand position and orientation often constrain the robot's workspace to that of the human. These methods, frequently relying on robot Jacobian matrices, are also prone to numerical instability or degeneracy when the robot operates near joint singularities or at the limits of its range of motion. The authors seek a method that avoids these issues.
- **Lack of Explicit Elbow Control:** For 7-DoF robot arms, end-effector-only retargeting can result in uncontrolled or undesirable null-space motion of the elbow. This reduces pose similarity between the human and robot, potentially increasing collision risks and making dexterous tasks more challenging. A motivation for this work is to provide direct control over the robot's elbow pose to improve mimicry and safety.
- **Calibration Dependence and Embodiment Gap:** Retargeting methods that rely on Euclidean distance minimization between human and robot keypoints can be sensitive to differences in limb lengths and proportions between the human operator and the robot. The authors aim for a solution that is calibration-free concerning size differences.
- **Computational Overhead for Downstream Applications:** Slower retargeting algorithms consume significant computational resources, leaving little overhead for real-time safety measures or other complex downstream applications essential for safe and effective robot operation. The authors intend to develop a fast solution that frees up computational capacity for such applications, exemplified by a safety filter.

To address these motivations, the paper reframes the retargeting problem as an **orientation alignment task**. By focusing on aligning the orientations of the human's upper arm, lower arm, and wrist (identified from Shoulder, Elbow, and Wrist keypoints, hence "SEW-Mimic") with corresponding robot limb vectors, the authors aim to achieve a closed-form, geometric solution with an optimality guarantee. This approach is intended to be scale-independent, applicable to various 7-DoF robot arms and humanoids, and agnostic to the input keypoint source.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, termed SEW-Mimic, is a closed-form, geometric algorithm designed for retargeting human upper body keypoints to 7-DoF robot manipulator joint angles. The central insight is to prioritize limb and hand/tool orientation error as the metric for pose similarity, which enables an analytical, provably-optimal solution that is scale-independent.

**Problem Formulation:**
The retargeting problem is formulated as minimizing a cost function composed of three orientation error terms:
1. **Upper Arm Alignment:** Cosine similarity error between the human upper arm unit vector (from shoulder to elbow) and the robot's 3rd joint rotation axis (as a proxy for the robot's upper arm direction).
2. **Lower Arm Alignment:** Cosine similarity error between the human lower arm unit vector (from elbow to wrist) and the robot's 5th joint rotation axis (as a proxy for the robot's lower arm direction).
3. **Wrist Alignment:** Orientation error (Frobenius norm-based metric) between the human hand orientation and the robot's end-effector orientation.

The method assumes a 7-DoF serial kinematic chain for the robot arm with consecutive perpendicular joint axes. Input human data consists of 3D keypoints for shoulder (s), elbow (e), wrist (w), and a rotation matrix for hand orientation (H).

**SEW-Mimic Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
The algorithm operates iteratively down the arm, from shoulder to elbow to wrist:
1. **Frame Synchronization (Sec. B):** A calibration-free procedure is used to map human and robot keypoints into a shared body-centric reference frame (the robot's 0th frame). This involves defining a frame based on left/right shoulder keypoints and a torso anchor point for both human input and robot's default frame, then transforming all keypoints. Wrist frames are similarly established, correcting for different conventions or deriving orientation from finger/wrist keypoints if direct hand orientation is unavailable.
2. **Upper Arm Alignment (q[1:2]):** The first two robot joint angles (q1, q2) are determined by aligning the robot's 3rd joint rotation axis (h3) with the human upper arm vector (u = unit(e - s)). This is achieved by solving a Subproblem 2.
3. **Lower Arm Alignment (q[3:4]):** The next two robot joint angles (q3, q4) are found by aligning the robot's 5th joint rotation axis (h5) with the human lower arm vector (l = unit(w - e)). This also uses Subproblem 2.
4. **Wrist Alignment (q[5:7]):** The final three joint angles (q5, q6, q7) are solved to align the robot's end-effector orientation with the human hand orientation (H). This typically involves applying Subproblem 2 to align the robot's 7th joint axis with the human hand pointing direction, followed by Subproblem 1 for the 7th joint angle. For robots with perpendicular wrist designs (e.g., Rainbow RB-Y1), Euler angle decomposition is used instead of subproblems 1 and 2 for the last three joints.

**Canonical Geometric Subproblems (Sec. III-C, Appendix A):**
The method leverages closed-form solutions for geometric subproblems:
- **Subproblem 1 (SP1):** Rotates a vector p1 about a unit axis k to align it with another vector p2, finding the optimal angle. This is used for the final wrist joint.
- **Subproblem 2 (SP2):** Aligns two vectors, p1 and p2, by simultaneously rotating p1 around k1 and p2 around k2, finding a pair of optimal angles. This is crucial for upper and lower arm alignment, and partially for wrist alignment. It relies on Subproblem 4.
- **Subproblem 4 (SP4):** Rotates a vector p about an axis k such that the rotated vector is on (or closest to) a plane defined by a normal h and offset d. This subproblem is used within SP2.

**Optimality (Sec. IV-C, Appendix D):**
The paper presents a proof (Proposition 3) that, in the absence of joint angle limits or self-collisions, SEW-Mimic provides a global optimizer to the defined orientation alignment problem. This optimality arises from the fact that each geometric subproblem (SP1, SP2, SP4) is itself provably optimal.

**Safety Filter for Self-Collisions (Sec. V, Appendix E):**
To address self-collisions in bimanual teleoperation, a safety filter is implemented, capitalizing on SEW-Mimic's computational speed.
1. **Capsule Collision Volumes:** The robot's links (torso, upper arms, lower arms, wrists/hands) are approximated by capsules (swept spheres) for efficient collision checking.
2. **XPBD Iteration:** The filter uses an Extended Position-Based Dynamics (XPBD) approach to iteratively adjust the robot's keypoints to resolve collisions. It computes collision gradients based on contact normals, updates Lagrange multipliers, and moves keypoints, subsequently enforcing kinematic link length constraints.
3. **Continuous Time Collision Checking:** To prevent "jumping" through obstacles, a continuous collision check is performed by interpolating between the current and desired poses. If a collision is detected during interpolation, the desired pose is truncated to the first collision-free interpolated step.
4. **SEW-Mimic as Fast IK:** After XPBD adjusts the keypoints to a safe configuration, SEW-Mimic is used as a fast Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver to map these adjusted keypoints back to the robot's joint angles, thus generating a safe robot pose.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an evaluation of SEW-Mimic across several experimental setups:

**A. Retargeting Performance (Sec. VI-A):**
- **Speed and Accuracy:** SEW-Mimic was compared against existing retargeting methods (GMR and xr\_teleoperate) using the Ubisoft LAFAN1 dataset for upper body motion.
 - **Alignment Error:** SEW-Mimic achieved a significantly lower alignment error (Median = 1.57 × 10−13, IQR = [1.06 × 10−13, 1.66 × 10−5]), indicating superior pose similarity, comparable to floating-point numerical precision.
 - **Inference Time:** SEW-Mimic demonstrated substantially faster inference (Median = 0.587 ms, IQR = [0.573, 0.612] ms), outperforming baselines by 2-3 orders of magnitude.
 - **Statistical Significance:** Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn's post-hoc tests confirmed a statistically significant effect of SEW-Mimic on both alignment error (H = 215864.09, p < 0.001) and inference time (H = 277254.30, p < 0.001) compared to baselines.
- **GMR Loss Metric:** When evaluated against GMR's specific optimization loss, SEW-Mimic performed worse, which is expected as it optimizes for a different objective.

**B. Pilot User Study (Sec. VI-B):**
- **Task Success:** A pilot user study (N=8) compared SEW-Mimic with MINK-IK for teleoperating a dual Kinova Gen3 robot in three bimanual manipulation tasks (Cabinet, Glass Gap, Handover).
 - **Interaction Effect:** Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA indicated a statistically significant interaction between the teleoperation method and the number of successes (p < 0.01) and failures (p < 0.01).
 - **Overall Performance:** Qualitatively, SEW-Mimic was associated with 125 total successes across all tasks and users, compared to 68 for MINK-IK. However, post-hoc analysis did not show statistically significant differences for any single task due to the small sample size.
- **MINK-IK Limitations:** MINK-IK exhibited a failure mode where users with shorter arms could not reach target objects due to fixed scaling factors between human and robot end-effector displacement. This highlights the benefit of SEW-Mimic's scale-independence.

**C. Safety Filter Ablation Study (Sec. VI-C):**
- **Self-Collision Reduction:** The safety filter's impact was evaluated during a "rolling punch" motion designed to induce self-collisions on an RB-Y1 robot.
 - Without the safety filter, SEW-Mimic had zero alignment error, approximately 0.8 ms computation time per arm (1250 Hz), but self-collisions in nearly 50% of retargeted poses (316 out of 765 frames).
 - With the safety filter, alignment error increased to an average of 0.019, computation time increased to about 4.0 ms (250 Hz) for both arms, but self-collision instances significantly decreased to approximately 1.3% of retargeted poses (10 out of 765 frames).
- **Trade-off:** The filter reduced self-collisions with a compromise in pose similarity and computation speed, demonstrating an adjustable trade-off.

**D. Policy Learning (Sec. VI-D):**
- **Improved Success Rate:** Diffusion Policy models trained on demonstration data collected using SEW-Mimic for the Glass Gap task achieved a substantially higher success rate (approximately 3×) and shorter task completion times compared to policies trained on MINK-IK demonstrations.
- **Demonstration Quality:** Preliminary analysis suggests that SEW-Mimic data exhibits smoother joint angle trajectories and lower desired joint velocities, which is associated with lower action prediction error in the trained policies.

**E. Full-Body Retargeting (Sec. VI-E):**
- **Computational Efficiency:** SEW-Mimic was implemented as a drop-in replacement for GMR within the TWIST full-body teleoperation framework, treating leg segments as SEW chains.
 - SEW-Mimic processed data 1-3 orders of magnitude faster than GMR.
 - It achieved this speed-up without increasing kinematic-to-dynamic (kin2dyn) joint angle error or PD controller joint angle tracking error.
- **Compatibility:** This result indicates that SEW-Mimic can act as a satisfactory accelerator for full-body retargeting frameworks, despite optimizing a different metric and not being explicitly designed for full-body applications.

**F. Demonstrations (Sec. VII):**
- Hardware demonstrations on Rainbow Robotics RB-Y1 and Kinova Gen3 dual-arm systems qualitatively illustrated fast, smooth robot motion, effective self-collision avoidance, and robust operation near singularities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of SEW-Mimic represents a contribution to the field of humanoid robot teleoperation and robot learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Real-Time Teleoperation and Reduced Latency:** By providing a closed-form, analytical solution, SEW-Mimic achieves high computation speeds (approximately 3 kHz per arm on standard CPUs). This speed addresses a critical limitation of many existing methods, which often suffer from latency that can disrupt real-time human control and introduce unnatural hesitation in robot movements. The reduced latency can lead to more intuitive and responsive teleoperation experiences.

- **Improved Pose Similarity and Control:** The method's focus on orientational alignment of shoulder, elbow, and wrist (SEW) keypoints directly addresses the issue of suboptimal or uncontrolled null-space motion, particularly around the elbow, which is common in end-effector-only teleoperation. This explicit control over elbow orientation enhances the robot's ability to mimic human poses more accurately, which is beneficial for complex manipulation tasks requiring dexterous movements.

- **Enhanced Safety through Dedicated Filtering:** The computational efficiency of SEW-Mimic creates significant overhead that can be leveraged for downstream applications. This is demonstrated by the integration of a real-time safety filter that utilizes capsule collision volumes and an XPBD-based approach to avoid self-collisions during bimanual teleoperation. While not formally proven for correctness, the filter significantly reduces collision instances in practice, improving the safety of robot operation.

- **Improved Data Collection for Robot Learning:** The user study and policy learning experiments indicate that demonstrations collected using SEW-Mimic result in policies with higher success rates and shorter task completion times, compared to data collected with end-effector-only methods. This is attributed to the smoother joint angle trajectories and lower desired joint velocities inherent in SEW-Mimic's output. High-quality, smooth demonstration data is crucial for training robust and effective autonomous robot policies.

- **Versatility and Applicability:** SEW-Mimic is designed to be agnostic to the input keypoint source (e.g., MediaPipe, Meta Quest headset) and suitable for most 7-DoF robot arms and humanoids, regardless of their specific size or proportions. This versatility is due to its scale-independent orientation alignment approach and its calibration-free nature for human-robot embodiment differences. The demonstration of its utility as a drop-in accelerator for full-body humanoid retargeting frameworks like TWIST further highlights its broader applicability.

- **Fundamental Building Block:** The paper emphasizes SEW-Mimic's role as a fundamental building block for bimanual robot manipulation and humanoid robot teleoperation. Its speed, accuracy, and optimality in a kinematic sense can serve as a robust foundation upon which more complex systems, including dynamic control, advanced safety measures, and sophisticated learning algorithms, can be developed.

**Potential Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the kinematic nature of SEW-Mimic (which does not inherently consider dynamics), its constraint to humanoid morphologies, and the fact that its optimality proof ignores joint angle limits and self-collisions. The practical safety filter also lacks a formal correctness proof, and the method's accuracy depends on the precision of human keypoint detection. Future work could focus on integrating provably-correct collision avoidance methods, mitigating remaining latency from communication/tracking control, and conducting larger, more detailed user studies to further elucidate its impact on teleoperation performance. The preliminary findings on policy learning also warrant deeper investigation into the causal mechanisms behind improved policy performance.
