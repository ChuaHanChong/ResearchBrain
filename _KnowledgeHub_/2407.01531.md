# Sparse Diffusion Policy: A Sparse, Reusable, and Flexible Policy for Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley, Carnegie Mellon, and UNC-Chapel Hill developed the Sparse Diffusion Policy (SDP), an architecture integrating Mixture of Experts with transformer-based diffusion policies for robot learning. SDP achieved improved performance in multitask and continual learning while significantly reducing trainable parameters for new tasks, demonstrating enhanced efficiency and adaptability across simulations and real-world robot experiments.

## Problem

- Traditional monolithic policies for generalist robots become computationally expensive and inefficient as task complexity and diversity increase, activating all parameters for even simple actions.
- Catastrophic forgetting is a major hurdle in continual learning, where robots learning new tasks often degrade performance on previously mastered skills.
- Existing methods lack efficient knowledge transfer, preventing the reuse of common skills across diverse tasks and leading to redundant learning.
- Scalability of skill discovery is limited by the need for extensive human-derived knowledge or meticulous design, and network structures are often not optimized for sparsity.

## Method

- ==Integrates Mixture== of Experts (MoE) layers into a transformer-based diffusion policy, replacing standard Feed-Forward Networks with multiple specialized expert networks and a router.
- Employs task-specific routers for multitask learning, allowing each task to activate a distinct and sparse subset of experts, thereby reusing common skills across tasks while maintaining computational efficiency.
- Addresses continual learning by freezing previously learned experts and routers, adding a small number of new trainable experts, and training only the corresponding new task-specific router to prevent catastrophic forgetting.
- Incorporates a Mutual Information (MI) loss into the training objective, alongside the behavior cloning loss, to encourage expert specialization and prevent expert overload, leading to a more balanced skill set.

## Results

- In multitask learning, SDP achieved an average success rate of **0.76** in 2D simulation, outperforming baselines (best **0.73**) with comparable active parameters, and maintained superior performance in 3D simulations and real-world robot tasks.
- For continual learning, SDP prevented catastrophic forgetting, maintaining high success rates on previous tasks (e.g., **0.94**-**1.00**) while achieving **0.75** on new tasks, with only 9.2M active parameters, significantly outperforming fully fine-tuned baselines.
- SDP enabled efficient task transfer to complex, long-horizon tasks by fine-tuning only 0.1M parameters (less than **0.5%** of total), achieving a **0.80** success rate which surpassed models trained from scratch (**0.70** success rate).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mixture of Experts architectures can be effectively integrated into transformer-based diffusion policies for robot learning, offering inherent benefits in sparsity, reusability, and flexibility.
> - Conceptualizing experts as specialized 'skills' and the router as a 'skill planner' provides an interpretable framework for composing complex behaviors from fundamental actions.
> - A 'fixed-and-add' approach for new tasks, coupled with mutual information loss, is effective in mitigating catastrophic forgetting while maintaining a constant computational cost for active parameters during inference.
> - Fine-tuning only the lightweight router (and vision encoder) for novel or long-horizon tasks enables highly efficient knowledge transfer by leveraging a rich, pre-trained pool of expert skills.
