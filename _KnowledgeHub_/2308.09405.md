# Robust Quadrupedal Locomotion via Risk-Averse Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and Shanghai AI Lab developed Risk-Averse Legged Locomotion (RALL), a framework for quadrupedal robots that uses risk-sensitive policy learning to enhance robustness in uncertain environments. This approach explicitly models environmental uncertainty through distributional value functions and optimizes for worst-case outcomes, significantly improving stability and success rates, such as achieving a 92.7% success rate on a 0.4m high platform compared to 14.4% for baseline methods.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) methods for quadrupedal locomotion struggle with truly uncertain scenarios, like abrupt terrain changes or unexpected external forces.
- Conventional RL optimizes for expected returns, potentially leading to policies that tolerate rare but catastrophic failures if they contribute to higher average performance.
- Current robustness strategies, relying on privileged information, external sensors, or extensive domain randomization, often have limitations in explicitly accounting for future risk events.

## Method

- Introduces **Risk-Averse** Legged Locomotion (RALL), a framework that injects rare risk penalization terms into the reward function to distort value distributions, focusing on future risk events.
- Employs an Implicit Quantile Network (IQN) to learn the full distribution of returns and optimizes the policy using Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) to prioritize worst-case outcomes.
- Integrates a risk-aware meta controller that dynamically adapts risk preferences (risk-averse vs. risk-neutral) based on real-time environmental risk assessed by the Interquartile Range (IQR).

## Results

- RALL significantly improved "Time to Fall" (TTF) metrics in simulation across various payload scenarios and achieved a **92.7%** success rate when walking down a 0.4m high platform, substantially outperforming baselines.
- Qualitative real-world experiments on a Unitree Aliengo robot confirmed enhanced stability, rapid recovery from disturbances, and effective traversal of challenging terrains, without reliance on external sensors.
- The risk-aware meta controller, by adaptively switching policies based on IQR-derived risk, achieved the highest overall returns across all outcome quantiles (worst-case, middle-case, and best-case scenarios).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling the distribution of returns, rather than just the expected return, allows policies to proactively mitigate rare but catastrophic failures in uncertain environments.
> - Rare but impactful risk penalties, even with low probability, can effectively shape the value distribution to highlight and train against worst-case scenarios without significantly affecting average expected returns.
> - The Interquartile Range (IQR) derived from the learned quantile distribution provides a robust, proprioception-based, real-time measure of environmental uncertainty, enabling adaptive risk-aware control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadrupedal locomotion has seen rapid advancements, driven by the increasing demand for robots capable of traversing complex and uncertain environments. Traditionally, **Model Predictive Control (MPC)** methods were employed, offering high performance but requiring precise dynamic models and intricate domain-specific knowledge, often involving a trade-off between model accuracy and computational complexity.

More recently, **model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL)** has emerged as a powerful paradigm, enabling quadrupedal robots to learn agile and dynamic motor skills without explicit dynamics modeling. Frameworks like Isaac Gym have facilitated this by providing massively parallel simulation environments, allowing policies to be trained on diverse terrains such as rocks, stairs, and sand.

Despite these successes, a critical challenge remains: achieving **robustness and generalization** in the face of unexpected, uncertain, and abrupt events in real-world environments. Existing RL-based approaches primarily address this through several strategies:
1. **Privileged Distillation:** Utilizing a teacher-student architecture where a "teacher" policy with access to privileged information (e.g., terrain maps, randomized parameters) distills this knowledge into a "student" policy that only uses accessible on-robot states. Examples include RMA and techniques for inferring terrain properties.
2. **Scene Modeling:** Explicitly learning the geometry, traversability, or volumetric representations of the environment to inform locomotion.
3. **External Sensors:** Equipping robots with cameras or LiDAR to gather more environmental context for improved terrain traversal.
4. **Domain Randomization:** Randomizing physical parameters, sensor noise, and environmental conditions during simulation training to encourage robust policies that transfer better to the real world.

The authors highlight key limitations of these existing methods when confronted with truly *uncertain scenarios*, such as abrupt terrain changes or unexpected external forces. They argue that these methods, by inferring current state information from historical or privileged data, do not explicitly account for *future risk events*. External sensors, while useful, can be unreliable and necessitate additional perception models. Furthermore, excessive domain randomization can sometimes lead to overly conservative policies that compromise performance in routine situations.

This paper positions itself as addressing this critical gap by introducing a **risk-sensitive perspective** to policy learning. Unlike conventional RL that primarily optimizes for *expected returns*, which might inadvertently lead to policies that tolerate rare but catastrophic failures (e.g., falls) if they contribute to higher average rewards, RALL explicitly aims to mitigate these worst-case scenarios. By focusing on *modeling and actively avoiding risks*, RALL represents a significant shift from reactive robustness (recovering from disturbances) to proactive robustness (preventing hazardous situations). The paper claims to be the first to apply risk-sensitive policy learning with distributional value functions to real quadruped robots, carving out a novel direction in the pursuit of truly resilient robotic locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research stems from the critical need for quadrupedal robots to operate reliably and safely in **challenging, unpredictable, and uncertain real-world environments**. Such capabilities are indispensable for applications like industrial inspections, disaster response, and firefighting, where abrupt terrain changes, unexpected external forces, or dynamic loads are common and can lead to costly hardware damage or mission failure. The authors explicitly state a priority: "preventing the robot from falling which may cause hardware damage rather than precisely tracking the speed and directional commands." This highlights a fundamental shift from optimizing for average performance to guaranteeing safety and resilience.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out the following key objectives:

1. **Enhance Robustness in Challenging Scenarios:** The primary objective is to develop a legged locomotion controller that can efficiently handle various external disturbances, recover from significant pushes, traverse hazardous terrains, and withstand dynamic loads, exhibiting improved robustness in harsh and uncertain situations.
2. **Model Aleatoric Uncertainty through Distributional Value Functions:** To move beyond simple expected returns, the authors aim to employ a distributional value function, learned via quantile regression, to explicitly model the *aleatoric uncertainty* (inherent randomness or noise) of the environment. This allows the system to understand not just the average outcome of an action, but the entire spectrum of possible outcomes, including worst-case scenarios.
3. **Implement Risk-Averse Policy Learning:** A core objective is to leverage this distributional understanding to perform *risk-averse policy learning*. This involves optimizing the policy to perform well in these identified worst-case scenarios, specifically through the use of a risk distortion measure like Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR).
4. **Develop a Risk-Level Measurement for Adaptive Control:** Recognizing that a perpetually risk-averse policy might be overly conservative in benign environments, the authors aim to propose a proprioception-based measure for assessing the current risk level of the environment. Specifically, they introduce the Interquartile Range (IQR) of the quantile function as a real-time risk indicator, enabling the robot to dynamically switch between different risk preferences (e.g., risk-averse vs. risk-neutral policies).
5. **Validate on Real-World Hardware:** A crucial objective is to demonstrate the practical efficacy and robustness of the proposed method not only in high-fidelity simulations but also on a real quadrupedal robot (Unitree Aliengo), confirming its ability to withstand disturbances without relying on external sensors or extensive randomization.

In essence, the motivation is to create a more "intelligent" and "self-aware" quadrupedal robot that can perceive and react to potential future risks, thereby enhancing its reliability and safety in complex and unpredictable real-world deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Risk-Averse Legged Locomotion (RALL)**, introduces a novel risk-sensitive perspective to reinforcement learning for quadrupedal robots. It integrates a distributional value function with a risk-distortion measure within an actor-critic framework.

**A. Problem Definition and Risk Injection:**
The locomotion control problem is formulated as a standard Markov Decision Process (MDP). The robot's observation (`o_t`) consists solely of proprioceptive feedback (base linear and angular velocities, gravity vector, velocity command, joint angles, joint velocities, and last action). Critically, RALL does **not** rely on privileged information or external sensors during training, distinguishing it from many prior works.

To explicitly model environmental risks, the authors augment the standard reward function (`r_task`) with a set of **risk penalization terms**. These penalties are triggered when certain proprioceptive state components (e.g., base pitch/roll exceeding thresholds, joint velocity/acceleration/torque exceeding limits) occur. A key innovation is that these penalties are applied with a **very small Bernoulli probability `p` (e.g., 10^-4)**. This subtle but crucial detail ensures that the risk penalties have negligible impact on the *expected return* (which traditional RL optimizes) but significantly distort the *value distribution*, particularly its lower quantiles, thereby highlighting worst-case scenarios.

**B. Distributional Value Function:**
Instead of learning the expected cumulative return `Q_π(s, a)`, RALL's critic network learns the **entire distribution of returns `Z_π(s, a)`**. This is achieved using techniques from distributional RL, specifically the **Implicit Quantile Network (IQN)**. IQN parameterizes the quantile function `Z_τ(s, a; θ)` (the inverse Cumulative Distribution Function) continuously for `τ ∈`. The critic is trained to minimize the quantile Huber loss between the predicted quantile values and a target distribution derived from the distributional Bellman equation. To manage the complexity of continuous action spaces, the method approximates the next state value distribution `Z(S')` directly, rather than `Z(S', A')`, as in.

**C. Risk-Sensitive Policy Learning:**
The actor network learns the locomotion policy using a policy gradient method. Unlike traditional methods that maximize the *expected* return, RALL's policy gradient is adapted to maximize a *distorted expectation* of the return distribution. This distortion is applied using a **risk distortion measure**, specifically **Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)**.

CVaR at level `η` (`CVaR_η(Z(s))`) corresponds to the expected return of the worst `η` fraction of outcomes. By setting `η < 1` (e.g., `η = 0.5`), the policy is optimized to perform well in the *bottom percentile* of possible outcomes, effectively making it **risk-averse**. The advantage function `A_t` in the policy gradient update is thus modified to incorporate this CVaR objective: `A_CVaR_t = r_t + γCVaR_η(Z(s_t+1)) - CVaR_η(Z(s_t))`. This ensures the policy prioritizes actions that improve performance in unfavorable situations, enhancing robustness. The policy learning also incorporates advanced techniques like Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO-Clip) for stable and efficient training. Domain randomization is also employed to aid sim-to-real transfer.

**D. Risk-Aware Meta Controller:**
To address the potential drawback of an always-conservative policy, RALL proposes a **risk-aware meta controller**. This meta controller dynamically switches between different risk preferences (e.g., a risk-averse CVaR_0.5 policy for high-risk situations and a risk-neutral CVaR_1 policy for safe environments). The decision to switch is based on a real-time assessment of the environment's risk level. For this assessment, RALL uses the **Interquartile Range (IQR)** of the learned quantile distribution (`IQR = F_Z^-1(0.75) - F_Z^-1(0.25)`). IQR, being robust to outliers, serves as a reliable proprioception-based indicator of the environment's aleatoric uncertainty or "riskiness." A higher IQR suggests greater uncertainty and potential risk, prompting a switch to the more conservative policy.

**E. Implementation and Training:**
Policies are trained in the Isaac Gym simulation using a curriculum-based approach for terrains. The actor and critic networks use a architecture, with the critic outputting 64 quantiles. Standard PPO parameters (clipping range 0.2, GAE λ 0.95, discount factor γ 0.99, learning rate 1e-3) are used. Extensive domain randomization (friction, base mass, velocity perturbations, observation noise) is applied during training to facilitate sim-to-real transfer. Training involves 4096 parallel agents and takes approximately 7 hours on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. For real-world validation, a Unitree Aliengo robot is used, with the policy running at 50Hz and a low-level PD controller at 200Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experiments, conducted in both simulation and on a real quadrupedal robot, demonstrate the significant improvements in robustness achieved by RALL.

**A. Robustness in Simulation:**
The evaluation focused on scenarios designed to induce risks, including carrying various payloads, encountering external pushes, and traversing challenging terrains. The primary metric used was "Time to Fall (TTF)," representing the average duration a robot could maintain stability.

1. **Challenging Payloads:** RALL significantly outperformed baseline (PPO with curriculum terrain), expanded domain randomization (DR), and RMA (teacher-student framework) across all payload scenarios:
 - **Carrying a rolling ball:** RALL showed superior stability, with TTF values consistently higher than competitors. For instance, on flat terrain without pushes, RALL achieved a TTF of 0.963, almost twice that of the baseline (0.539).
 - **Carrying a fixed robot arm (Unitree Z1):** RALL maintained its lead, exhibiting remarkable robustness. On flat terrain without pushes, RALL's TTF was 0.987, vastly outperforming the baseline's 0.187.
 - **Carrying a dynamically moving arm:** Even with the added complexity of a changing center of mass, RALL sustained longer operational periods. On random terrain with pushes, RALL achieved a TTF of 0.285 compared to the baseline's 0.206.
 These results consistently showed that RALL enables the robot to survive for the longest duration under diverse and demanding load conditions, with and without external disturbances.

2. **Hazardous Terrains (Platform Walking):** Experiments involving walking down high platforms confirmed RALL's superior ability to handle abrupt terrain changes.
 - For a 0.4m high platform, RALL achieved a 92.7% success rate, dramatically higher than RMA (43.5%), DR (20.6%), and baseline (14.4%).
 - Even at a more challenging 0.45m height, RALL still achieved 30.5% success, while competitors struggled to surpass 21.4%.

**B. Real-World Experiments on Unitree Aliengo:**
Qualitative demonstrations on a physical Unitree Aliengo robot provided compelling evidence of RALL's practical robustness. The robot successfully:
- Regained stability rapidly after missing a step.
- Withstood significant external pushes and recovered balance.
- Traversed challenging terrains effectively.
These real-world validations confirm that the robustness learned in simulation translates effectively to physical hardware, crucially without relying on external sensors or *excessive* domain randomization compared to the baseline methods.

**C. Risk-Aware Meta Controller Validation:**
Experiments on the meta controller, which dynamically switches policies based on perceived risk, yielded important insights:
- **IQR as a Risk Measure:** A custom race track combining challenging and normal terrains, along with random external forces, showed a clear correlation: when the robot encountered risky situations (e.g., stepping off a platform, traversing obstacles), the Interquartile Range (IQR) of its value distribution was noticeably higher, validating IQR as an effective proprioception-based measure of environmental risk.
- **Improved Overall Performance:** The meta controller, which switched between CVaR_1 (risk-neutral) and CVaR_0.5 (risk-averse) policies based on the IQR value, achieved the highest returns across all quantiles (worst-case, middle-case, and best-case scenarios) compared to agents using fixed CVaR_1 or CVaR_0.5 policies. This demonstrates the benefit of dynamically adapting risk preferences, allowing the robot to be conservative when necessary and more performant otherwise.

Overall, the findings strongly support the central hypothesis: explicitly modeling and optimizing for risk-averse behavior via distributional RL significantly enhances the robustness of quadrupedal locomotion, enabling superior performance in highly uncertain and challenging real-world conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic locomotion and reinforcement learning, with several important implications:

1. **Pioneering Risk-Sensitive Locomotion:** RALL is presented as the first method to apply risk-sensitive policy learning via distributional value functions to real quadrupedal robots. This opens up a new, principled avenue for addressing the long-standing challenge of robustness in legged robots, moving beyond reactive control to proactive risk mitigation.
2. **Enhanced Real-World Robustness and Reliability:** The demonstrated ability to withstand dynamic loads, abrupt terrain changes, and significant external disturbances is crucial for deploying quadrupedal robots in practical, real-world applications such as industrial inspections, logistics, search and rescue, and hazardous environment exploration. By preventing falls and damage, RALL significantly increases the operational reliability and lifespan of robotic platforms.
3. **Reduced Dependency on Complex Sensors and Architectures:** A major strength of RALL is its reliance solely on proprioceptive feedback for risk identification and policy learning. This circumvents the need for complex and often unreliable external sensors (cameras, LiDAR) and intricate teacher-student privileged information distillation architectures. This simplicity leads to more robust, cost-effective, and computationally lighter systems.
4. **Actionable Risk Assessment:** The ability to derive a real-time risk level (via IQR) from the learned value distribution is highly impactful. It transforms the critic network from a mere value estimator into a "risk sensor," providing valuable insights into the robot's current environmental challenges. This internal risk awareness is a step towards more intelligent and adaptive robotic behavior.
5. **Adaptive and Intelligent Control:** The proposed meta-controller, which dynamically switches between risk-averse and risk-neutral policies based on perceived risk, represents a significant advancement towards more intelligent and context-aware robot control. This allows robots to be efficient in safe environments while becoming appropriately cautious in hazardous ones, optimizing for both performance and safety.
6. **Broader Implications for Safe RL:** The methodology of injecting rare but impactful penalties to shape value distributions and using risk distortion measures like CVaR extends the principles of safe reinforcement learning. This approach could be generalized to other domains where avoiding rare catastrophic events is paramount, even if it slightly compromises average reward.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work explicitly points to future directions, such as integrating external sensors to further enhance environmental risk estimation. This indicates a clear roadmap for building upon RALL's foundations, potentially leading to even more sophisticated and resilient locomotion controllers.

In conclusion, RALL offers a robust, principled, and practically validated approach to enhancing quadrupedal locomotion. Its novelty lies in its risk-sensitive perspective, providing a significant step towards creating autonomous robots that can operate reliably and safely in the complex and unpredictable environments of the real world.
