# CORE Planner: Contextual-memory Oriented Reinforcement-learning in Unknown Environments for Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> The CORE planner, developed at Xi'an Jiaotong University, introduces a deep reinforcement learning framework that integrates contextual memory and dynamic graph sparsification to enable robots to efficiently navigate unknown environments. This approach achieves higher success rates and reduced travel distances by up to 20.8% compared to baselines, demonstrating robust zero-shot simulation-to-real transfer on various robotic platforms.

## Problem

- Traditional navigation methods often rely on predefined rules, leading to inefficiencies, susceptibility to local optima, and high computational costs with dense map representations.
- Existing learning-based approaches for navigation commonly suffer from limited environmental memory, which restricts holistic understanding and causes issues like 'Navigation Deadlock Oscillation' (NDO) in complex scenarios.
- A significant challenge in learned navigation policies is achieving robust zero-shot simulation-to-real transfer, often requiring extensive real-world fine-tuning.

## Method

- **CORE** formulates navigation as an RL problem using a ==visibility graph-based state representation==, where nodes incorporate a 'trajectory indicator' as ==contextual action memory== to track visit history.
- Implements a ==dynamic graph sparsification== method that fuses visible points into representative nodes outside a robot's immediate vicinity, maintaining efficiency in large environments.
- Utilizes a ==Transformer-based encoder-decoder== architecture with a ==Pointer Network== for waypoint selection, trained using the ==Soft Actor Critic (SAC)== algorithm to learn adaptive policies.

## Results

- Achieved a **100%** success rate and reduced average travel distance by **20.8%** in image-based simulations compared to CADRL, along with superior performance against other learning-based baselines in Gazebo environments (e.g., **37.1%** shorter travel distance than YOPO in complex indoor scenes).
- Ablation studies confirmed that contextual memory eliminated NDO and reduced path length by **52%** in complex environments, while sparsification maintained efficiency with minimal impact on performance.
- Demonstrated successful zero-shot simulation-to-real transfer on both LiDAR-equipped UGVs (Scout Mini) and RGB-D-equipped quadrupeds (LYNX M20), enabling multi-room navigation and dynamic obstacle avoidance without real-world fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The contextual memory mechanism, by explicitly recording node visit counts, effectively prevents local optima and oscillatory behavior (NDO), enabling more informed long-term decision-making.
> - Dynamic graph sparsification significantly reduces computational complexity in large-scale environments without compromising essential geometric information for navigation.
> - Integrating sparse environmental representations with a Transformer-based RL framework facilitates a robust and generalizable navigation policy capable of zero-shot transfer from simulation to diverse real-world robot platforms and sensor modalities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous navigation in unknown environments is a critical area in robotics, with applications spanning post-disaster rescue and planetary exploration. This field traditionally relies on methods that incrementally build environmental maps and use heuristic functions to determine subsequent waypoints. While approaches like FAR Planner have introduced sparse representations such as visibility graphs to improve computational efficiency, traditional methods are often constrained by predefined rules, prone to local optima, and can be computationally intensive with dense grid maps, particularly in large-scale settings.

The emergence of deep learning and reinforcement learning (RL) has led to new paradigms for path planning. Learning-based methods, such as NavRL and YOPO, have demonstrated capabilities in simpler unknown environments, often relying on specific obstacle structures or ESDF maps. Hybrid approaches like GDAE use classical planners to generate sub-goals for RL-based local planners. However, many existing learning-based solutions struggle with global planning due to limited environmental memory and fixed-size neural network inputs, preventing them from developing a holistic understanding of the environment and often leading to local optima.

More recent developments involve large vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) for navigation, which leverage semantic reasoning or natural language instructions. These models, however, typically focus on semantic understanding rather than geometric reasoning, often require substantial computational resources for real-time deployment on edge platforms, and do not explicitly address issues such as structured environment representation or consistent belief updates under partial observability.

The CORE (Contextual-memory Oriented Reinforcement-learning) planner positions itself by integrating advantages from both traditional and learning-based methods. It combines the sparse environmental representation of visibility graphs with the complex rule-learning capabilities of reinforcement learning, while addressing the memory limitations and sim-to-real transfer challenges common in many learning-based approaches. By introducing a contextual memory mechanism and a graph sparsification method within a Transformer-based RL framework, CORE aims to overcome local optima, enhance computational performance in large-scale scenes, and achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robot navigation system that can efficiently and reliably reach predefined goals in unknown environments without prior maps. The authors identify several key limitations in existing methods that motivate the CORE planner:

- **Reliance on traditional planning methods with hand-crafted rules:** Traditional approaches often involve predefined rules and assumptions about the environment, which can lead to inefficiencies, susceptibility to local optima (such as dead ends or oscillatory behavior), and task failure, as illustrated by the "Navigation Deadlock Oscillation (NDO)" phenomenon.
- **Computational inefficiency of dense grid-based representations:** Many traditional planners use dense grid maps, which incur high computational overhead, especially in large-scale environments. While sparse representations like visibility graphs (e.g., FAR Planner) have improved efficiency, these systems can still lack a holistic environmental understanding.
- **Limited environmental memory in learning-based methods:** Most learning-based approaches for navigation process only current sensor readings or use fixed-size inputs. This restricts their ability to build a comprehensive, long-term understanding of the environment and the robot's past actions, making them prone to local optima in complex or large-scale scenarios. The continuous expansion of explored areas in unknown environments poses a challenge for fixed-input neural architectures.
- **Challenges in simulation-to-real (sim-to-real) transfer:** Learning-based methods often require extensive fine-tuning in real-world environments after simulation training, hindering their practical deployment. Achieving robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is a significant challenge.
- **Geometric reasoning vs. semantic understanding:** While VLM/LLM-based methods offer semantic reasoning, they typically do not explicitly address geometric reasoning, partial observability, or efficient structured environment representation, which are critical for robust low-level navigation.

To address these motivations, the CORE planner aims to:
- Integrate sparse environmental representation (visibility graphs) with the adaptive decision-making capabilities of reinforcement learning.
- Develop a mechanism for contextual memory to enable a holistic environmental understanding, thereby mitigating local optima and the NDO phenomenon.
- Introduce a dynamic graph sparsification method to maintain computational efficiency in large-scale environments.
- Achieve robust navigation performance that enables zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, eliminating the need for real-world fine-tuning.
- Create a geometry-driven and edge-efficient planner suitable for real-time deployment on resource-constrained platforms.

The proposed approach extends the objective function of navigation to explicitly incorporate complete historical states (`S(0), ..., S(t-1)`) by encoding them into node-wise contextual memory, rather than processing them as an ever-growing temporal sequence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CORE planner formulates robot navigation as a sequential decision-making problem within a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework, specifically a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

#### 3.1. RL Problem Formulation

- **Action Space (A):** The robot's action is defined as selecting one of the currently visible, collision-free neighboring nodes within its visibility graph as the next waypoint. This selection is handled by a Pointer Network, which allows for flexible input and output dimensions, adapting to the dynamic number of available waypoints.
- **Reward Function (R):** The reward function is a weighted sum of five components:
 - `r_goal`: Encourages movement towards the goal, inversely proportional to the distance to the goal.
 - `r_frontier`: Promotes exploration by rewarding the number of newly observed frontier points.
 - `r_ds`: Penalizes stagnation by considering the Euclidean distance between current and previous robot positions.
 - `r_stay`: Applies an additional penalty if the robot remains stagnant for more than five consecutive steps.
 - `r_f`: A positive reward upon successfully reaching the goal, terminating the episode.

#### 3.2. State Representation with Contextual Action Memory

The robot's state at time `t` is represented by a visibility graph `G_t = (N_t, E_t)`. This graph is incrementally constructed from sensor data (LiDAR or RGB-D point clouds).
- **Nodes (N_t):** Each node `n_i` in the graph is characterized by a feature vector `x_i`, which concatenates four components:
 - `p_i`: Spatial coordinates of the node.
 - `u_i`: Utility value, defined as the number of observable frontier points around the node, indicating unexplored areas.
 - `d_i`: Euclidean distance from the current node to the goal.
 - `v_i`: Trajectory indicator, representing the number of times the agent has visited the node `n_i` since the beginning of the task. This component serves as the **contextual action memory**, providing a quantitative history of visits rather than a simple binary "visited" flag. It helps the agent perceive its movement history and escape local optima.

#### 3.3. Visibility Graph-Based Graph Sparsification

To improve computational efficiency in large-scale environments, a dynamic graph sparsification method is introduced.
- **Non-sparsified region:** A region around the robot is designated as non-sparsified to preserve full environmental detail in the immediate vicinity.
- **Node fusion:** For areas outside this region, interconnected visible points within a predefined radius are clustered. These clusters are then merged into a single representative node located at their centroid. All original edges connected to the clustered nodes are inherited by the new representative node. This process reduces the total number of nodes while maintaining graph connectivity.
- **Computational complexity:** The sparsification process has a cost of `O(N log N)`, where `N` is the number of raw nodes. When combined with the Transformer encoding cost of `O(N'^2)` (where `N'` is the sparsified node count), the total complexity `O(N log N + N'^2)` is significantly lower than the `O(N^2)` of non-sparsified approaches, especially when the sparsification ratio `N'/N` is small.

#### 3.4. Contextual-Memory Network Architecture

The CORE planner employs a standard Transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture, adopted for its ability to process sequence-like or graph-structured data with dynamic input sizes.
- **Encoder:** The feature vectors `x_i` from the visibility graph (including the `v_i` contextual memory) are aggregated into a global feature matrix `X`. This matrix is linearly projected into a latent embedding space and then processed through multiple Transformer encoder layers. An encoder mask is applied to ensure that nodes only attend to adjacent nodes based on graph edges. The encoder's output, `h_e`, represents environment-aware features, capturing both spatial and historical action information.
- **Decoder:** The environment-aware feature of the current robot node `h_r` serves as the query, while `h_e` acts as both key and value in a standard Transformer decoder layer. The decoder output is then concatenated with `h_r` and projected into a new embedding `~h_r`.
- **Pointer Network:** Finally, `~h_r` and the encoded embeddings of neighbor nodes are fed into a Pointer Network (implemented as an attention mechanism). The resulting attention weights directly yield the action probability distribution over candidate next waypoints.

#### 3.5. Theoretical Analysis of Policy Gradient Convergence

The CORE planner is trained using the Soft Actor Critic (SAC) algorithm, which operates within a maximum entropy reinforcement learning framework. The authors analyze how CORE's structural components preserve the conditions necessary for SAC's convergence guarantees.
- **Boundedness of Representation:** Node features (`p_i, u_i, d_i, v_i`) in the visibility graph are inherently bounded (spatial coordinates within map, utility/distance normalized, visit count clipped), ensuring finite variance of the policy gradient estimator.
- **Preservation of Markov Property:** The contextual memory (`v_i`) is explicitly integrated into an augmented state space `S' = S × V`. This ensures that the system's transition dynamics remain fully Markovian, upholding the theoretical validity of the Bellman contraction and SAC's convergence properties.

#### 3.6. Training Details

- **Environment:** Training is conducted using simple and challenging 500x500 grid-based simulated environments (100m x 100m physical space), with a sensor range of 16m. Critically, no simulated physical engines (like Gazebo) are used during initial training.
- **Algorithm:** Soft Actor Critic (SAC) is employed. The Actor network comprises the encoder and decoder, while the Critic network shares the same architecture but replaces the final pointer layer with a single-output linear layer.
- **Parameters:** Learning rate of 2e-5 for Adam optimizer, replay buffer capacity of 20,000, batch size of 256. Episodes are capped at 250 steps.
- **Efficiency:** Training converges within approximately five hours on a workstation with an Intel Xeon E5-2660 v4 CPU and three NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPUs, demonstrating rapid convergence and the potential for strong sim-to-real zero-shot transfer without real-world fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The CORE planner's performance was evaluated across image-based simulations, Gazebo simulations, and real-world environments, using metrics such as average success rate (S), average travel distance (D), average inference time (T_e), average planning time (T), and the number of human interventions (I).

#### 4.1. Comparison in Image-based Environments

- **Performance:** CORE achieved a 100% success rate across 100 distinct, unseen image-based environments, compared to CADRL's 97%.
- **Travel Distance:** CORE reduced average travel distance by 20.8% (310.10m vs. 391.74m) relative to CADRL.
- **Planning Time:** CORE had a slightly longer average planning time (0.30s) than CADRL (0.21s), but remained acceptable for a global planner. The authors attribute CADRL's longer distances to its reliance on dense grid-based representations, leading to excessively large input dimensions for its model.

#### 4.2. Comparison in Gazebo Environments

Evaluations were performed in simple indoor, complex indoor, and outdoor forest scenarios against traditional (FAR Planner) and learning-based (CTSAC, NavRL, YOPO) baselines.
- **Traditional Baseline (FAR Planner):** CORE exhibited superior performance across all environments, reducing travel distance by up to 13% compared to FAR Planner (e.g., 681.1m vs. 785.6m in Complex Indoor; 272.0m vs. 285.1m in Forest). CORE also required zero human interventions, while FAR Planner required one in the Complex Indoor scenario. FAR Planner's computational cost significantly increased with environment complexity (0.002s in Simple to 0.120s in Forest).
- **Learning-based Baselines:** CORE consistently outperformed all learning-based baselines:
 - **Simple Indoor:** Reduced travel distance by 18.3% over CTSAC, 23.3% over NavRL, and 14.7% over YOPO.
 - **Complex Indoor:** While CTSAC and NavRL failed (exceeded intervention limits), CORE significantly reduced travel distance by 37.1% compared to YOPO (681.1m vs. 1082.5m). CORE achieved zero human interventions compared to YOPO's seven.
 - **Outdoor Forest:** CTSAC again failed. CORE reduced travel distance by 48% over NavRL (272.0m vs. 524.2m) and 30.1% over YOPO (272.0m vs. 389.0m). CORE required zero interventions compared to NavRL's two and YOPO's zero.
- **Contextual Memory Impact:** The paper notes that baselines like NavRL and YOPO, and the ablated CORE w/o Mem, suffered from "Navigation Deadlock Oscillation (NDO)"—oscillatory back-and-forth movement—due to a lack of contextual memory, leading to inefficient paths.
- **Computational Efficiency (Inference Time T_e):** Learning-based baselines processing RGB-D observations maintained relatively constant inference times. CORE Planner's inference time scaled with graph size but remained low (0.004s in Simple, 0.005s in Indoor, 0.009s in Forest), demonstrating efficiency due to its sparse graph representation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study in Gazebo environments compared CORE with CORE w/o Sparse (memory without sparsification) and CORE w/o Mem (sparsification without memory).
- **Graph Sparsification:** In the Simple scenario, sparsification had no significant effect on planning time or distance. In larger environments (Indoor, Forest), removing sparsification (CORE w/o Sparse) slightly increased planning time (e.g., 0.22s vs. 0.20s in Indoor; 0.545s vs. 0.309s in Forest) but sometimes yielded a small reduction in travel distance. The study concluded that sparsification effectively reduces graph complexity while preserving essential information.
- **Contextual Memory:** CORE w/o Mem exhibited severe oscillatory behavior and frequently fell into local optima (NDO). In the Indoor environment, it required five human interventions and incurred a 52% increase in path length (1036.1m vs. 681.1m) compared to the full CORE. In the Forest environment, travel distance increased by 42.2% (386.8m vs. 272.0m).
- **Combined Effect:** The full CORE planner consistently demonstrated the best overall performance, with zero human interventions, fast planning, and no oscillatory behavior, indicating the complementary roles of contextual memory and graph sparsification.

#### 4.4. Comparison in Real-world Environments (Zero-shot Sim-to-Real Transfer)

Experiments were conducted on two robotic platforms (LYNX M20 quadruped with RGB-D, Scout Mini UGV with LiDAR) to validate zero-shot transfer.
- **Multi-room local-optimum escape (Scout Mini):** CORE successfully navigated a multi-room environment, escaping a local optimum without human intervention. It achieved a shorter travel distance (109.07m vs. 132.21m) and lower planning time per decision (0.12s vs. 0.73s) compared to FAR Planner, which required three interventions.
- **Dynamic obstacle avoidance (LYNX M20):** Using RGB-D sensing (converted to point clouds), CORE enabled the quadruped robot to navigate a corridor with moving pedestrians. The robot maintained progress, responded to dynamic obstacles, and executed avoidance maneuvers without human intervention, confirming cross-sensor and cross-platform generalization.
- **Overall:** The real-world results confirm the robustness, generalizability across different sensing modalities and robot morphologies, and successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capability of the CORE planner.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CORE planner presents a novel approach to autonomous navigation in unknown environments by effectively combining established principles of traditional planning with advances in deep reinforcement learning. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Addressing Local Optima and Oscillatory Behavior:** The introduction of a **contextual memory mechanism** through the trajectory indicator `v_i` directly addresses a fundamental challenge in unknown environment navigation: entrapment in local optima and oscillatory behavior (Navigation Deadlock Oscillation). By providing the agent with a quantitative history of visited nodes, CORE enables more informed decision-making over longer horizons, leading to more robust and efficient pathfinding.
- **Efficient Environmental Representation and Scalability:** The use of a **sparse visibility graph** as an environmental representation, coupled with a **dynamic graph sparsification method**, allows CORE to maintain computational efficiency even in large-scale environments. This is a notable improvement over dense grid-based methods and enhances the practical applicability of the system for complex, expansive scenarios.
- **Demonstrated Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful **zero-shot transfer** from training solely in image-based simulations to deployment on heterogeneous real-world robotic platforms (LiDAR-equipped UGV and RGB-D-equipped quadruped) without fine-tuning is a substantial achievement. This capability significantly reduces the cost and complexity of real-world deployment, accelerating the adoption of learned navigation policies in practical applications.
- **Generalizability Across Sensors and Platforms:** The ability of CORE to function effectively with both LiDAR and RGB-D sensor inputs, and across wheeled and legged robot morphologies, indicates a high degree of **generalizability**. This broad applicability suggests that the CORE framework could be adapted for diverse robotic systems and operational contexts.
- **Enhanced Performance over State-of-the-Art:** Quantitative results consistently show CORE outperforming both traditional (e.g., FAR Planner) and learning-based (e.g., CADRL, CTSAC, NavRL, YOPO) baselines in terms of reduced travel distance, higher success rates, and fewer human interventions across various simulated and real-world environments. This indicates an advancement in the state of the art for autonomous navigation in unknown environments.
- **Edge-Efficient Design:** The architecture's design, which leverages a Transformer-based encoder-decoder with a sparse graph input, demonstrates **real-time performance** on edge computing units like NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin and Orin NX. This efficiency is crucial for autonomous systems that require on-board processing without reliance on off-board computational resources.

The CORE planner's integration of structural environmental understanding, historical awareness, and efficient processing offers a practical and robust solution for autonomous navigation, potentially impacting fields such as mobile robotics, exploration, and automated inspection where robots operate in previously unmapped or dynamic settings.
