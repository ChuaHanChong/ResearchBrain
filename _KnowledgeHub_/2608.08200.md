# Spatiotemporal Context-dependent Personalized Movement Compensation in Delayed Telemanipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A human-centered delay-compensation method for telemanipulation that fits per-operator sub-unity motion-scaling gains conditioned on communication delay, reach direction, and reach distance, stored as a gain tensor and smoothed by delay-conditioned additive models. Across twenty participants in simulation and on a da Vinci Research Kit, scaling cut overshoot and improved smoothness, economy of motion, and speed-accuracy by up to 20-25%.

## Problem

- Prior delay-mitigation deployments apply fixed or group-averaged motion-scaling gains, implicitly assuming every operator is affected by latency identically despite documented individual differences in sensorimotor adaptation.
- Earlier sub-unity scaling evidence came from single-degree-of-freedom needle insertion, leaving it unclear whether the compensation principle generalizes to higher-DOF, more complex telemanipulation scenarios.
- Multi-DOF scaling studies never systematically varied delay magnitude, nor examined how latency interacts with task geometry (direction, distance), nor whether simulation-tuned gains transfer to physical hardware.

## Method

- A ==gain tensor== G(delay, distance, direction) sets each user's scalar gain as the ratio of mean no-delay peak reach distance to mean delayed peak reach distance, per condition cell.
- Direction-specific ==generalized additive models== regress gain on delay with an order-four ==spline basis==, interpolating continuous gain curves across the discrete 100/250/400 ms conditions and damping trial-level noise.
- A two-session protocol (VR shape-matching gain-identification plus baseline blocks, then ==dVRK== peg transfer) compares personalized versus generic gains under ==mixed-effects models== with geometry-matched zero-delay covariate and per-user random intercept.

## Results

- In simulation, assistance cut initial reaching error progressively with delay (about **-0.035** at **100 ms**, **-0.068** at **250 ms**, **-0.122** at **400 ms**; all p < **0.001**).
- Path straightness and speed-accuracy improved with delay too: PSI gains **0.016**/**0.047**/**0.086** and nCET gains **0.129**/**0.184**/**0.224** at **100**/**250**/**400 ms**; NASA-TLX fell from **50.2** to **46.1**.
- Transfer to the physical dVRK weakened: smoothness, economy, nCET still improved (p < **0.001**), but endpoint error worsened at **250 ms** and personalization beat generic gain only inward or at **400 ms**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Delay is better compensated spatially than temporally: operators encode latency as a visuomotor gain, so sub-unity scaling attacks hypermetria directly, with benefits growing as delay grows.
> - Personalization pays off only where feedforward control dominates; when perceptual uncertainty pushes operators toward closed-loop visual feedback, population-average gains capture nearly all of the benefit.
> - Simulation-fitted human-in-the-loop parameters transfer to hardware only partially; looser task success criteria and restored depth uncertainty shift operator strategy and erode assistance benefits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Communication delay presents a significant challenge in telerobotics, particularly in teleoperated surgery, where it can disrupt visuomotor coordination and diminish task precision. Previous research has explored various strategies to mitigate delay-induced performance degradation. Early work, such as that by Marescaux et al., demonstrated the feasibility of telesurgery under substantial delays, yet highlighted safety concerns and performance impairments at higher latencies (e.g., beyond 330-400 ms).

Several studies have contributed to understanding how humans cope with delay. Avraham et al. proposed that delayed feedback is perceived as a state-based visuomotor gain, leading to hypermetria (overshoot) of intended targets, suggesting that spatial scaling adjustments might be more effective than temporal corrections. Nisky et al. further investigated sub-unity motion scaling, demonstrating its ability to mitigate overshoot in single-degree-of-freedom tasks without causing perceptual distortions. Richter et al. extended this to multi-degree-of-freedom telemanipulation, showing that specific motion scaling gains could improve accuracy and stability under high-delay conditions.

A recognized gap in the existing literature concerns the systematic investigation of contextual task factors that influence variations in personalized motion scaling gains. Prior delay-mitigation techniques often rely on fixed compensation strategies or generic parameters, implicitly assuming uniform effects across operators. However, human operators exhibit variability in sensorimotor adaptation, suggesting that fixed methods may not fully address individual differences. While the general benefits of motion scaling and personalization have been demonstrated, the specific contextual factors such as movement direction, workspace configuration, or the difference between virtual and physical environments that shape these variations remain understudied.

This work addresses this gap by proposing a human-centered method to derive personalized, delay-, direction-, and distance-specific scaling parameters. It systematically explores how these contextual factors influence optimal compensation and evaluates the transferability of simulation-derived gains to a physical robotic system. By focusing on context-dependent personalization, the research aims to refine delay compensation strategies beyond uniform or generic approaches, potentially leading to more adaptive and effective teleoperation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate whether performance degradation caused by communication delays in telemanipulation could be effectively compensated by deriving individualized motion scaling parameters that account for temporal delay, movement direction, and reach distance. The authors hypothesized that such a personalized approach would improve telemanipulation performance relative to unassisted or non-delayed baseline conditions.

This overarching objective was driven by several specific motivations:
- **Addressing the limitations of generic compensation:** Existing delay-mitigation techniques often employ fixed or generic parameters, neglecting the inherent variability among human operators and across different task contexts. The authors sought to move beyond these general approaches by exploring personalization.
- **Incorporating contextual variability:** Prior studies on motion scaling had not systematically varied factors such as delay times, movement directions, or reach distances in conjunction with personalization. The motivation was to determine how these contextual factors influence the optimal personalized scaling gain for a given individual.
- **Improving task precision and safety:** In applications like telesurgery, precise control is critical. Delay-induced hypermetria and reduced accuracy can compromise safety and efficacy. By developing a more refined compensation strategy, the authors aimed to enhance the precision of teleoperated procedures.
- **Evaluating simulation-to-real transfer:** Many compensation strategies are developed and validated in simulated environments. A crucial motivation was to assess the practical utility of personalized gains by determining if parameters identified in a virtual simulator could effectively transfer and provide benefits when controlling a physical robotic system, specifically the da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK).

The work aimed to make the following specific contributions:
1. Demonstrate that personalized motion scaling, conditioned on delay, distance, and direction, leads to improved telemanipulation performance.
2. Show that personalized scaling enhances accuracy without a consistent increase in movement time across most experimental conditions, addressing concerns about speed-accuracy tradeoffs.
3. Investigate the transferability of personalized gains derived in simulation to real-world tasks, and identify any changes in effectiveness during this transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employed a two-session, within-subjects experimental design to evaluate personalized motion compensation.

**A. Participants:**
Twenty right-handed participants (5 female, 15 male; age range: 18–45 years) without prior experience with the da Vinci Surgical System or dVRK (except for 3 expert dVRK operators) were recruited. All participants were screened for virtual reality or motion sickness. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board at Case Western Reserve University.

**B. Telemanipulation Hardware Setup:**
- **Virtual Environment (SimOnly):** A kinematic simulation of the dVRK Patient Side Manipulator (PSM) was implemented in CoppeliaSim. Participants viewed the virtual scene through a stereoscopic Goovis G3 Max headset, with the virtual Endoscopic Camera Manipulator (ECM) providing a stereoscopic viewpoint calibrated to match the physical setup.
- **Physical Robot (Sim2Real):** Participants controlled a physical dVRK PSM. An optical tracker (MicronTracker) measured the PSM end-effector position relative to task pegs.
- **Input Device:** A Haply Inverse3 haptic device provided 6-DOF motion tracking and 3-DOF force feedback. To focus on translational reaching, rotational degrees of freedom were locked in the virtual PSM.
- **Communication:** Robot Operating System 2 (ROS2) facilitated communication between all system components.

**C. Experimental Design:**
The study incorporated four within-subjects factors:
1. **Assistance:**
 - SimOnly: Personalized Assistance (calibrated from individual baseline) and Unassisted.
 - Sim2Real: Personalized Assistance, Generalized Assistance (derived from SimOnly aggregates across all participants), and Unassisted.
2. **Reaching Distance (robot-side):**
 - SimOnly: 5.0, 10.0, and 15.0 mm (corresponding to user movements of 12.5, 25.0, and 37.5 mm with a base motion scaling of 0.4).
 - Sim2Real: 10.0, 20.0, and 30.0 mm (corresponding to equivalent user movements with a 2:1 input scaling).
3. **Reaching Direction:** Lateral, longitudinal, and vertical (SimOnly); Lateral and longitudinal (Sim2Real, due to physical constraints and prior findings on minimal impact of short delays). Directions were later classified as 'inward' (proximal, toward body midline) or 'outward' (distal, away from midline) for statistical analysis.
4. **Communication Delay:** 100 ms, 250 ms, and 400 ms (based on literature defining acceptable tele-surgical delay bounds). In Sim2Real, 100 ms delay was omitted due to its limited perceived impact.

**D. Task Design:**
- **SimOnly:** A shape-matching task where participants aligned a virtual ring with a target ring using the haptic device, receiving real-time visual feedback.
- **Sim2Real:** A modified peg transfer task. Participants picked up a ring from a central peg and placed it onto a target peg using the physical dVRK system.
Participants were instructed to move naturally and deliberately, focusing on both accuracy and completion time.

**E. Procedure:**
- **Simulation-Only Session:**
 - **Phase 0 (Consent & Familiarization):** Consent, system overview, instructional video.
 - **Phase 1 (Calibration & Practice):** VR headset adjustment, posture standardization, 5 practice trials with random conditions. Trials initiated by double-pressing a button and ended upon perceived target reach.
 - **Phase 2 (Gain Identification Block):** Used to compute personalized gains. Consisted of sub-blocks of three delayed "real trials" interleaved with two no-delay "catch trials." Five repetitions per unique condition (delay, direction, distance). NASA Task Load Index (TLX) survey administered after completion.
 - **Phase 3 (Baseline Block):** 56 no-delay trials to establish a reference for scaling gain computations. Conducted *after* gain identification to ensure consistent relative overshoot/undershoot.
 - **Phase 4 (Evaluation Block):** Participants experienced personalized compensation gains for non-delayed conditions, with three repetitions per unique condition. TLX survey administered.
- **Sim-to-Real Session:**
 - **Phase 1 (System Re-familiarization):** Reacquaintance with interface, instructional video for real task.
 - **Phase 2 (Practice Block):** Twice as many practice trials as SimOnly due to increased physical task complexity.
 - **Phase 3 (Control Block):** Modeled after SimOnly gain ID, three repetitions per unique condition, no delay compensation. TLX administered.
 - **Phases 4 & 5 (Evaluation Blocks with Compensation):** Two blocks, one using generic gain and one using personalized gain (from SimOnly). Order counterbalanced. Five repetitions per condition. TLX administered.

**F. Derivation of Personalized Compensation Factor:**
A scalar gain metric, $G(\delta, d, \theta)$, was computed for each combination of temporal delay ($\delta$), spatial displacement magnitude ($d$), and perturbation direction ($\theta$). This gain was defined as the ratio of the arithmetic mean of peak reach distances in baseline (no-delay) trials ($P_0^{(i)}(d, \theta)$) to the mean peak reach distances in delayed gain identification trials ($P_\delta^{(j)}(d, \theta)$), under matched spatial conditions. This "gain tensor" served as a multiplicative modifier to the original task scaling factor, adjusting commanded motion. Generalized additive models (GAMs) were fitted to these estimated gains to provide continuous, direction-specific gain curves as a function of delay.

**G. Performance Metrics:**
1. **Initial Reaching Error:** Overshoot ($O$) quantified maximal displacement beyond the target, and undershoot ($U$) measured trajectory shortfall at the first stabilization point.
2. **Endpoint Error ($E_{final}$):** Euclidean distance between final end-effector position and target.
3. **Movement Smoothness:** Quantified by Spectral Arc Length (SAL) from the velocity profile's power spectrum, with lower values indicating smoother motion.
4. **Economy of Motion:** Assessed by the Path Straightness Index (PSI), the ratio of straight-line distance to actual path length (closer to 1 indicates straighter, more economical trajectories).
5. **Speed-Accuracy Tradeoff:** Measured by Normalized Combined Error-Time (nCET), calculated as Endpoint Error multiplied by task completion time normalized by Fitts' Law Index of Difficulty (ID). Lower nCET indicated better overall efficiency.
6. **Cognitive Load:** Assessed using the NASA-TLX questionnaire (six dimensions, 0-20 scale, equally weighted for total workload score).

**H. Data Processing:**
Synchronous recordings of operator hand motion and PSM end-effector trajectories were sampled at 200 Hz. Velocity profiles were derived by differentiating position samples and low-pass filtered with an 8Hz Butterworth filter.

**I. Statistical Analysis:**
Linear mixed-effects models were fitted for each primary metric, with fixed effects including assistance paradigm, temporal delay, movement distance, and movement direction. Four-, three-, and two-way interactions were modeled. A random intercept for the user and a geometry-matched 0 ms baseline covariate were included to account for individual ability. Type-III tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom were used for omnibus inference, and Tukey-adjusted estimated marginal means for post-hoc comparisons. Box-Cox transformations were applied to most metrics to improve distributional properties.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Personalized and Generic Gains:**
Analysis of the Gain ID block in the SimOnly task showed that most users exhibited a consistent inverse relationship between required gain and temporal delay, meaning gains generally decreased with longer delays. While some users displayed hypometria at 100 ms delay (gains above unity), the generalized gain (averaged across participants) indicated that unity gain could be maintained up to 100 ms on average.

**B. Simulation-Only Task:**
1. **Initial Reaching Error (Overshoot/Undershoot):** Assistance significantly reduced over/undershoot compared to unassisted trials across all conditions. This benefit increased proportionally with longer delays (e.g., reductions of approx. 0.035 mm at 100 ms, 0.068 mm at 250 ms, and 0.122 mm at 400 ms).
2. **Endpoint Error:** Assistance generally reduced endpoint error, but its benefit did not increase with delay. A significant interaction between assistance and reach direction indicated that assistance was more consistently beneficial for inward reaching directions.
3. **Movement Smoothness (SAL):** Assistance broadly increased movement smoothness (lower SAL values). Similar to initial reaching error, the smoothness benefit from assistance increased with more severe latency (e.g., reductions in SAL values of approx. 6 at 100 ms, 7.8 at 250 ms, and 13.9 at 400 ms).
4. **Movement Economy (PSI):** Assistance improved movement economy, resulting in straighter trajectories (higher PSI values). The benefit in economy of motion also increased with longer delays (e.g., PSI improvements of approx. 0.016 at 100 ms, 0.047 at 250 ms, and 0.086 at 400 ms).
5. **Combined Error-Time (nCET):** Personalized assistance significantly improved the nCET metric across all delays, with the magnitude of improvement systematically growing with delay (e.g., reductions of approx. 0.129 at 100 ms, 0.184 at 250 ms, and 0.224 at 400 ms). This indicated a favorable speed-accuracy tradeoff.
6. **Perceived Workload (NASA-TLX):** Personalized assistance resulted in significantly lower subjective workload scores (mean difference = -4.17, p < 0.0001) compared to unassisted control.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer Task:**
1. **Initial Reaching Error:** Assistance broadly reduced initial reach error. However, unlike in simulation, the increased benefit of assistance at higher delays was not consistent; it was observed only for 5 mm and 10 mm reach distances, with the benefit diminishing beyond these. Inward reaching showed greater benefit from assistance compared to outward reaching, with personalized assistance specifically showing benefits over generalized assistance for inward reaches only.
2. **Endpoint Error:** Assistance did not consistently reduce endpoint error in the real task. A two-way interaction between assistance and delay indicated a decrease in accuracy due to assistance at 250 ms delay (p < 0.001).
3. **Movement Smoothness (SAL):** Smoothness generally declined with higher delay. Assistance significantly improved smoothness compared to no assistance across all delay levels (p < 0.001 for both 250 ms and 400 ms).
4. **Movement Economy (PSI):** Assistance improved movement economy, with a more pronounced benefit under longer delays compared to no assistance (p < 0.001 for both 250 ms and 400 ms). However, the benefit of personalized versus generalized assistance was only significant at 400 ms delay.
5. **Combined Error-Time (nCET):** Assistance provided greater speed-accuracy benefits relative to no assistance at all delay levels (p < 0.001 for both 250 ms and 400 ms). Personalized assistance resulted in significantly lower nCET compared to generalized assistance only at the 400 ms delay level (p < 0.05).
6. **Perceived Workload (NASA-TLX):** Overall assistance did not significantly alter subjective workload compared to unassisted control. However, personalized assistance led to significantly lower TLX scores compared to the generic gain (mean difference = -3.50, p = 0.0013).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work provides evidence for the effectiveness of personalized, context-dependent motion scaling as a strategy for mitigating delay impairment in telemanipulation. The findings highlight several significant points:

- **Efficacy of Motion Scaling:** Sub-unity motion scaling consistently improved operator performance across multiple metrics (initial reaching error, smoothness, economy, speed-accuracy), particularly at longer delays and distances. This corroborates and extends previous findings on motion scaling as a practical delay mitigation strategy.
- **Context-Dependent Personalization:** The study demonstrates that optimal motion scaling gains are not static but exhibit dependence on temporal delay, movement direction, and reach distance. This emphasizes the need for compensation strategies that adapt to specific task contexts rather than relying on uniform parameters.
- **Directional Modulation:** The observed increase in accuracy benefits for inward (proximal) reaching directions, especially in simulation-only endpoint error and Sim2Real initial reaching error, suggests that arm proprioception accuracy may modulate the reliance on delayed visual feedback, influencing the effectiveness of assistance. This has implications for task design and compensation strategies in specific workspace regions.
- **Improved Speed-Accuracy Tradeoff:** Unlike some earlier motion scaling approaches that resulted in speed-accuracy tradeoffs, the empirically derived personalized gains in this study led to improved overall speed-accuracy, demonstrating the potential for enhanced task throughput without sacrificing precision.
- **Simulation-to-Real Transfer:** Parameters learned in simulation demonstrated specific transferability to the real-world dVRK task, improving initial reach error, movement smoothness, economy, and speed-accuracy. However, the benefits were reduced and less consistent, especially for endpoint error. This suggests a "simulation-to-real gap" likely due to differences in task complexity (shape-matching vs. peg transfer with grasping) and visual feedback (planar vs. perspective view, reintroducing depth uncertainty).
- **Role of Personalization:** While personalization did not consistently outperform generic gains when averaged across all conditions, it offered specific advantages in conditions of reduced perceptual uncertainty, such as inward movements at low visual delays, and in improving speed-accuracy at higher delays. This indicates that generic gains can provide a robust starting point, with personalization offering further optimization for specific applications or user needs.
- **Reduced Workload:** Personalized assistance significantly reduced subjective workload in both simulation and, notably, compared to generic gains in the physical task, suggesting improved human-system interaction.

The potential impact of this research is in advancing the design of more adaptive and user-centric teleoperation systems. By moving towards dynamic, context-aware compensation strategies, the findings could contribute to:
- **Enhanced Safety and Precision:** Particularly in critical applications like telesurgery, improved delay compensation can lead to safer and more precise execution of tasks.
- **More Resilient Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability of teleoperation systems to adapt to individual differences and changing task contexts can foster greater reliability and flexibility.
- **Foundations for Future Adaptive Systems:** The identification of context-dependent gain factors (delay, direction, distance) lays a groundwork for integrating online parameter adaptation and intent estimation, potentially enabling real-time refinement of compensation parameters without extensive calibration.

Future work outlined includes extending compensation to rotational degrees of freedom, investigating more complex manipulation tasks, and integrating online parameter adaptation with contextual cues to reduce the time-consuming calibration process. This research moves the field towards intelligent teleoperation systems that can dynamically optimize performance based on both the operator's state and the specific demands of the task environment.
