# A Reward-Free Viewpoint on Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University and MIT integrated Reward-Free Reinforcement Learning (RFRL) into Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) by adapting the Forward-Backward algorithm into a new framework called MORL-FB. This approach achieved superior performance and data efficiency across various continuous and discrete control tasks, showcasing robust generalization to unseen preferences and zero-shot transfer capabilities across tasks with different objective counts.

## Problem

- Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) struggles with efficiently learning policies that adapt to diverse, often unknown, user preferences for multiple conflicting objectives.
- Existing MORL methods often suffer from inefficient exploration or limited generalization when trained on sparse preference samples.
- Direct application of Reward-Free Reinforcement Learning (RFRL) to MORL yields suboptimal performance because vanilla RFRL exploration does not adequately prioritize states critical for MORL-relevant, preference-weighted rewards.

## Method

- The proposed **MORL-FB** method systematically adapts the ==Forward-Backward== (FB) RFRL algorithm, designed to learn universal policy representations, to the MORL setting.
- It introduces 'Preference-Guided Exploration' (PG-Explore), which uses mini-batch sampled preferences from the replay buffer to generate relevant latent vectors, directing exploration towards policies aligned with MORL objectives.
- An auxiliary Q loss is incorporated, utilizing observed scalarized multi-objective rewards to enhance representation learning efficiency and directly connect learned FB representations to task-specific value functions.

## Results

- MORL-FB consistently achieved superior Utility (UT), Hypervolume (HV), and Episodic Dominance (ED) scores across diverse continuous and discrete control tasks compared to state-of-the-art MORL methods.
- The method demonstrated high data efficiency, reaching performance levels comparable to or exceeding baselines within 3 million environment steps, significantly fewer than the **63** million steps required for **21** separate single-objective policies.
- It exhibited robust generalization, maintaining performance with limited preference training, and showed effective zero-shot cross-objective transfer, adapting to tasks with additional reward terms without retraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RFRL's objective to learn optimal policies for any reward function can serve as an effective auxiliary task for MORL, promoting broader knowledge sharing and generalization beyond predefined scalarizations.
> - Effective integration of RFRL into MORL requires specific algorithmic enhancements, such as guided exploration and direct utilization of observed multi-objective rewards, to overcome the limitations of naive RFRL application.
> - Disentangled representations of environmental dynamics, learned through the adapted FB framework, enable robust generalization to unseen preferences and facilitate zero-shot transfer across tasks with varying numbers of objectives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses challenges in sequential decision-making tasks that involve multiple, often conflicting, objectives. This domain is broadly known as Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL). A common approach in MORL is to train policies that can adapt to varying user preferences, often represented as a weighted sum (scalarization) of objectives. Since these preferences are typically unknown at test time, MORL methods aim to learn a set of policies or a single preference-conditioned policy capable of handling diverse preference vectors. Current methods include training separate policies for each preference (which can be inefficient) or training a single network conditioned on preferences, which allows for parameter sharing and generalization.

Independently, Reward-Free Reinforcement Learning (RFRL) has been developed to address the problem of learning optimal policies for any possible reward function without receiving reward signals during the training phase. The paper identifies a conceptual alignment between MORL and RFRL: both paradigms aim to derive optimal policies for a reward function that is not fully specified or known during the initial learning phase. Specifically, MORL can be considered a specialized instance of RFRL, as RFRL is designed to handle an arbitrary scalar reward function, a category into which linearly scalarized multi-objective rewards fall.

Despite this connection, prior research has not explicitly adapted RFRL methods to solve MORL problems. This paper proposes a novel algorithmic perspective by systematically adapting an RFRL framework to the MORL setting. The work positions RFRL not merely as an analogous problem, but as a source of auxiliary tasks that can enhance MORL, thereby facilitating knowledge sharing and potentially improving generalization and sample efficiency beyond the predefined multi-objective reward function used during training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether insights and methodologies from Reward-Free Reinforcement Learning (RFRL) can be leveraged to inform and improve Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL). This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and objectives:

1. **Addressing Unknown User Preferences:** In many real-world sequential decision-making tasks, users may have dynamic or initially unknown preferences regarding multiple conflicting objectives. Existing MORL approaches often tackle this by training policies conditioned on preference-weighted rewards. The authors observe that RFRL inherently addresses a similar challenge by learning policies for *any* possible reward function, making it a natural fit for scenarios with unknown preferences.

2. **Enhancing Knowledge Sharing and Generalization in MORL:** The paper hypothesizes that the broader objective of RFRL—to learn optimal policies for an extensive range of reward functions—could serve as a valuable auxiliary task for MORL. This auxiliary learning could foster more effective knowledge sharing within the agent's network, enabling it to generalize better across different preference vectors, even beyond those explicitly encountered during training through linear scalarization. This is particularly relevant given that standard MORL, under linear scalarization, might only require optimal policies for a limited subspace of reward functions.

3. **Overcoming Limitations of Direct RFRL Application:** A direct, naive application of existing RFRL algorithms to MORL tasks was observed to yield suboptimal performance compared to established MORL methods. This motivated the objective to develop specific algorithmic enhancements. The challenge was identified as the potential for purely reward-free exploration in vanilla RFRL to not adequately prioritize states that are critical for optimizing preference-weighted rewards relevant to the specific MORL task. Therefore, a key objective was to modify the exploration strategy to focus learning on pertinent parts of the environment.

4. **Developing Algorithmic Enhancements for RFRL in MORL:** To bridge the gap between RFRL and MORL effectively, the research aimed to introduce specific algorithmic improvements:
 - **Preference-Guided Exploration:** To direct learning towards relevant policies, the objective was to develop an exploration strategy that uses sampled preferences to guide the generation of latent vectors. This aims to ensure that the agent visits states that are important for maximizing preference-weighted rewards.
 - **Leveraging Mini-Batch Sampling for Auxiliary Tasks:** The goal was to train the policy network on a broader range of latent vectors computed from mini-batch transitions. This design intends to provide richer learning signals, effectively acting as auxiliary tasks that go beyond the exact preference-aligned policies.
 - **Integrating Auxiliary Q Loss:** To better integrate the RFRL framework with MORL's observable reward structure, an objective was to incorporate an auxiliary Q loss that facilitates the learning of representations directly from the observed reward vectors, rather than relying on pseudo-rewards as in some RFRL settings.

5. **Empirical Validation and Performance Demonstration:** A final objective was to empirically demonstrate that the proposed RFRL-informed MORL approach (MORL-FB) offers superior performance, data efficiency, and generalization capabilities compared to state-of-the-art MORL methods across a diverse set of multi-objective reinforcement learning benchmarks. This also included evaluating its potential for zero-shot cross-objective transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named MORL-FB (Forward-Backward MORL), systematically adapts the Forward-Backward (FB) RL algorithm to the Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) setting. The core approach involves leveraging the FB framework to learn universal policy representations while introducing specific enhancements to optimize its applicability and efficiency for MORL tasks.

#### 3.1. RFRL Implementation via Forward-Backward (FB) Algorithm

MORL-FB is built upon the state-of-the-art FB algorithm, which learns a set of policies optimized for diverse reward functions by decomposing the Q-value into two neural networks:
- **Forward Network (F_θ):** Maps a state-action pair (s, a) and a latent vector *z_R* to a *d_z*-dimensional output.
- **Backward Network (B_ω):** Maps a state-action pair (s, a) to a *d_z*-dimensional output.

The Q-function for a given reward function *R* is expressed as *Q(s, a, z_R) = F_θ(s, a, z_R)^T z_R*. The latent vector *z_R* is designed to encode the optimal policy for the reward function *R*. During training, *z_R* is implicitly learned. At test time, given a reward function *R*, *z_R* is computed as the expectation of *B_ω(s, a)R(s, a)* over a state-action distribution (empirically, from a replay buffer). The policy *π(s, z_R)* then greedily selects actions that maximize *F_θ(s, a, z_R)^T z_R*.

#### 3.2. Algorithmic Enhancements for MORL-FB

The direct application of vanilla FB to MORL showed limitations, primarily due to inefficient exploration. MORL-FB addresses this through three key enhancements:

1. **Preference-Guided Exploration (PG-Explore):**
 - **Motivation:** In traditional FB, *z* is typically sampled from a standard normal distribution (e.g., *N(0, I_dz)*). However, for MORL, the relevant *z* vectors correspond to preference-weighted reward functions, which form a specific, potentially low-dimensional, manifold in the *d_z*-dimensional latent space. Sampling *z* indiscriminately from a normal distribution can lead to inefficient exploration of policies relevant to MORL objectives.
 - **Approach:** PG-Explore generates a more relevant *z* distribution by incorporating the preference vector *λ*. Instead of a fixed mathematical expectation, it constructs *ẑ_λ* using mini-batch sampling from the replay buffer:
 - *ẑ_λ = (1/n_s) Σ_{(s,a,r,s')∈D} B_ω(s,a)r^T λ*
 where *D* is a batch of *n_s* transitions sampled from the replay buffer. This mini-batch approach introduces variability, encouraging exploration of *z* vectors that are "nearby" the true *z_λ* (derived from the full expectation) but still relevant to the MORL task. This process is conceptualized as providing auxiliary tasks, as the agent learns policies for a diverse set of *z* vectors, which are approximately aligned with preferences but vary due to mini-batch noise.
 - **Normalization:** The computed *z* vector is normalized as *z ← √(d_z) z / ||z||₂*.

2. **Auxiliary Q Loss (L_Q):**
 - **Motivation:** While the FB framework includes a "measure loss" to learn successor measures (which are reward-agnostic), explicitly guiding the learned representations with respect to the *observed* scalarized rewards in MORL can further enhance learning efficiency. The original FB, designed for RFRL, typically uses pseudo-rewards when true rewards are absent during training. In MORL, however, vector-valued rewards *R(s,a)* are observed.
 - **Approach:** MORL-FB introduces an auxiliary Q loss that uses the actual scalarized reward *λ^T r* to enforce Bellman consistency:
 - *L_Q(F_θ; z_λ) = E[(F_θ(s, a, z_λ)^T z_λ - (λ^T r + γF̄_θ(s', π(s', z_λ), z_λ)^T z_λ))²]*
 This loss directly connects the learned FB representations to the value function of the scalarized rewards, strengthening the link between the universal policy learning of FB and the specific objectives of MORL.

3. **Standard FB Loss Components:** MORL-FB also incorporates the standard loss functions from the FB framework:
 - **Measure Loss (L_M):** Minimizes the Bellman residual on the successor measure, enabling the F and B networks to learn task-agnostic environmental dynamics.
 - **Orthonormality Loss (L_n):** Regularizes the backward network *B_ω* to maintain a well-conditioned and diverse set of basis functions, preventing representational collapse.
 - **Policy Loss (L_π):** An actor-critic style loss that trains the policy network *π_η* to maximize the Q-values derived from *F_θ* and *z_λ*.

#### 3.3. Training Algorithm Overview (Algorithm 1 & 2 in paper)

The training process involves an iterative loop:
1. **Preference Sampling:** A preference vector *λ* is uniformly sampled from the preference set.
2. **Latent Vector Generation:** During a warm-up phase, *z* is sampled from *N(0, I_dz)*. Afterward, *z* is generated using the PG-Explore mechanism based on the sampled *λ*.
3. **Rollout Generation:** The agent executes actions guided by the policy conditioned on the generated *z*, collecting transitions (*s, a, r, λ, s'*) which are stored in a replay buffer.
4. **Network Updates:** In each gradient step, a mini-batch of transitions is sampled from the replay buffer. The F_θ, B_ω, and π_η networks are updated using a combination of the Measure Loss (L_M), Auxiliary Q Loss (L_Q), Orthonormality Loss (L_n), and Policy Loss (L_π). Target networks for F_θ, B_ω, and π_η are updated softly.

#### 3.4. Test Time

At test time, given a user-specified preference vector *λ*, the corresponding *z_R* is computed using the learned *B_ω* network and the available reward signals (typically, the average from the replay buffer or current environmental interactions). This *z_R* is then used with the learned *F_θ* network to derive the optimal policy *π(s, z_R)* for the given preference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of MORL-FB was conducted across a range of tasks in the MO-Gymnasium benchmark suite, encompassing both discrete and continuous control environments (Multi-objective MuJoCo tasks like Halfcheetah2d, Walker2d, Hopper3d, Ant3d, Humanoid2d, Humanoid5d, and classic control tasks like Deep Sea Treasure and Fruit Tree Navigation). The method was compared against various state-of-the-art MORL algorithms, including single preference-conditioned policy methods (PD-MORL, Q-Pensieve, CAPQL, EQL, PCN), multi-policy MORL methods (PG-MORL, SFOLS, MORL/D, GPI-LS, GPI-PD), and the original FB (RFRL baseline). Performance was assessed using Utility (UT), Hypervolume (HV), and Episodic Dominance (ED), with normalized scores and aggregated metrics (median, mean, IQM) for robustness.

#### 4.1. Performance and Data Efficiency

- **Continuous Control Tasks:** MORL-FB demonstrated consistent performance across continuous control tasks. It achieved UT and HV scores that were among the highest or the highest across all tested methods, irrespective of the number of objectives. In pairwise comparisons via ED, MORL-FB generally outperformed benchmark methods, with ED(ALG, MORL-FB) consistently below 0.5.
- **Discrete Control Tasks:** For discrete control tasks like Deep Sea Treasure and Fruit Tree Navigation, MORL-FB also achieved competitive or superior performance across UT, HV, and ED metrics compared to specialized discrete MORL algorithms.
- **Aggregated Performance:** Evaluation using aggregated metrics (median, mean, and Interquartile Mean (IQM)) indicated that MORL-FB reliably outperformed benchmark methods. MORL-FB recorded the highest IQM scores, suggesting robust performance across tasks.
- **Sample Efficiency:** The results indicate that MORL-FB exhibits high sample efficiency. It could achieve performance levels comparable to or exceeding baselines within 3 million environment steps, which is noted as comparable to expert policy training in single-objective settings. In some evaluations, MORL-FB trained for 1.5 million steps surpassed baselines trained for 3 million steps. When compared to training 21 separate single-objective SAC (SOSAC) models (requiring 63 million steps), MORL-FB achieved comparable or superior Pareto fronts with only 3 million total steps.

#### 4.2. Generalization Across Preferences

To assess generalization, algorithms were trained on a reduced set of preference vectors (standard basis preferences and the uniform preference).
- MORL-FB maintained consistent performance with minimal degradation in UT and HV values compared to full preference training.
- In contrast, PD-MORL and Q-Pensieve showed a notable decline in performance across all three metrics under this limited training setup. This suggests that MORL-FB generalizes more effectively over the entire preference set, even when trained on a sparse set of preference samples.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Preference-Guided Exploration (PG-Explore):** An ablation study confirmed the importance of PG-Explore. When *z* was sampled from a standard normal distribution (as in vanilla FB) instead of using PG-Explore, MORL-FB's performance, measured by UT and HV, decreased significantly. Visualizations using t-SNE showed that PG-Explore resulted in a multi-modal distribution of *z* vectors, indicative of a richer and more diverse set of latent representations, whereas normal sampling yielded a unimodal distribution.
- **Auxiliary Q Loss:** Removing the auxiliary Q loss also led to performance degradation in MORL-FB. Furthermore, using a "Pseudo Q Loss" (adapted from original FB's pseudo-reward mechanism) instead of the proposed auxiliary Q loss with observed scalarized rewards also resulted in lower performance, indicating the value of using actual reward signals.
- **Measure Loss:** Removing the measure loss also led to significant performance degradation.
- **Q-loss Coefficient:** The performance of MORL-FB was largely insensitive to the coefficient of the Q-loss, suggesting the robustness of the default direct addition of the measure and Q losses.

#### 4.4. Zero-Shot Cross-Objective Transfer

MORL-FB was evaluated for its ability to transfer knowledge across tasks with different numbers of objectives (e.g., training on Hopper2d and testing on Hopper3d and Hopper4d).
- MORL-FB demonstrated effective zero-shot transfer, maintaining performance on tasks with additional reward terms, suggesting that the F and B networks learn a disentangled representation of environment dynamics from task-specific rewards.
- The vanilla FB algorithm did not achieve effective cross-objective transfer.

#### 4.5. Robustness Under Worst-Case Preferences

Evaluation using Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR@0.1) indicated that MORL-FB achieved the highest CVaR scores in tested environments (HalfCheetah2d and Walker2d). This suggests superior robustness to adverse or unfavorable preference settings compared to other baselines.

#### 4.6. Computational Cost

MORL-FB maintained a reasonable wall-clock training time compared to other strong baselines, balancing performance with computational efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces a novel perspective on Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) by leveraging concepts from Reward-Free Reinforcement Learning (RFRL). This integration represents the first systematic adaptation of an RFRL framework to MORL, bridging two previously distinct lines of research that, while addressing similar underlying problems of unknown preferences, had evolved independently.

The significance of MORL-FB stems from several key aspects:

1. **New Paradigm for MORL:** By treating the RFRL objective as an auxiliary task, the paper proposes a new paradigm for MORL. This approach potentially moves beyond solely optimizing over linearly scalarized objectives and opens avenues for more comprehensive knowledge sharing within the learning agent. The ability of RFRL to learn policies for an extensive range of reward functions inherently equips MORL-FB with a robust mechanism to handle diverse and unforeseen user preferences.

2. **Algorithmic Innovation and Practical Enhancements:** The paper's core contribution includes critical algorithmic enhancements to the Forward-Backward (FB) RFRL framework. The introduction of **preference-guided exploration** with mini-batch sampling and an **auxiliary Q loss** based on observed reward vectors are crucial. These innovations specifically address the challenges of adapting a reward-free approach to an environment where multi-objective rewards are observable, directing exploration more effectively and grounding representation learning in actual task values. The empirical demonstration of these components' importance through ablation studies reinforces their contribution to the overall system.

3. **Empirical Effectiveness and Scalability:** MORL-FB demonstrates substantial empirical advantages over existing state-of-the-art MORL methods. Its superior performance across diverse discrete and continuous control tasks, including those with a higher number of objectives (e.g., Humanoid5d), suggests enhanced scalability. The method also exhibits higher data efficiency, achieving strong results with fewer environment interactions compared to baselines. This efficiency is critical for applying RL to complex, real-world problems where data collection can be costly.

4. **Enhanced Generalization and Zero-Shot Transfer:** The ability of MORL-FB to generalize effectively across preference sets, even when trained on a limited number of preference samples, is a significant practical benefit. Furthermore, its demonstrated capability for **zero-shot cross-objective transfer**—applying a learned policy to tasks with a different number of objectives without retraining—underscores the robustness and disentanglement achieved by its representations. This feature is particularly impactful for scenarios where new objectives might be introduced dynamically or where knowledge needs to be transferred across related but not identical multi-objective tasks.

5. **Robustness and Reliability:** The evaluation under worst-case preferences (CVaR@0.1) indicates that MORL-FB exhibits superior robustness compared to other methods. This suggests that the approach is not only high-performing on average but also more reliable in unfavorable conditions, which is crucial for deployment in sensitive applications.

The limitations, as noted by the authors, primarily revolve around the inherent challenge of advanced exploration in complex or sparse-reward environments, inherited from FB-based RFRL. Future work suggests exploring other RFRL methods beyond FB, such as representation learning of successor measures or distance-preserving state representations, to further uncover the advantages of an RFRL perspective in MORL. The successful integration presented in this paper paves the way for a more unified understanding of policy learning under diverse reward structures and offers a robust, efficient, and generalizable solution for complex multi-objective decision-making.
