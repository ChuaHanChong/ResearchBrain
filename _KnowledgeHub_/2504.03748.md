# TDBench: A Benchmark for Top-Down Image Understanding with Reliability Analysis of Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> TDBench is a benchmark of 2,000 curated multiple-choice and grounding questions that tests vision-language models on top-down drone and simulated aerial images across ten capability dimensions. It adds RotationalEval, which counts a question correct only if all four 90-degree rotations are answered correctly, plus a probabilistic reliability decomposition separating genuine knowledge from lucky guesses. Across 60 models, rotation-consistent accuracy falls 16-26 points below single-view accuracy.

## Problem

- VLM training and evaluation data are dominated by front-view images; fewer than 7% of VisDrone images are truly top-down, leaving aerial understanding largely untested.
- Remote-sensing benchmarks use low-resolution, fixed-nadir satellite imagery for land-cover classification, rarely probing near-surface drone-scale tasks like localization, attribute comparison, or temporal change detection.
- Single-pass accuracy and binary hallucination metrics cannot tell whether a correct answer comes from visual grounding or a lucky guess, inflating apparent model reliability.

## Method

- **TDBench**: 2,000 human-curated and ==CARLA-simulated== questions across ten dimensions grouped into ==image perception==, ==single-instance understanding==, and ==multi-instance reasoning==, standardized to 512x512 inputs and filtered for discriminative value.
- **RotationalEval**: each image is evaluated at 0, 90, 180, and 270 degrees, with ==placeholder tokens== auto-remapping directional phrases, and a question counts correct only if all four answers are right.
- ==Knowledge reliability decomposition==: a ==known-versus-guessed mixture model== assuming independent rotations solves for fraction known, accuracy when known, and guess accuracy, yielding ==Adjusted Accuracy== as known fraction times known accuracy.

## Results

- Across **60** VLMs, RotationalEval cuts accuracy by **16-26 points** versus single-view evaluation; Gemini 2.5 Pro leads at **0.611**, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B leads open models at **0.470**.
- Gemini 2.5 Pro knows the most questions (**0.822**) and reaches **0.754** Adjusted Accuracy, while GPT-5 and o3 show the highest guess accuracy at **0.265** and **0.279**.
- Case studies: GPT-4o detects objects at **0.8%** image occupancy versus **2-4%** for open models, and accuracy stays above **90%** until object visibility drops below roughly **60-70%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Exploiting a domain's natural symmetry, such as rotation for overhead imagery, gives a cheap consistency test that exposes ungrounded answers single-pass accuracy hides.
> - Higher single-view accuracy does not guarantee higher grounded accuracy; Gemini 1.5 Pro beats GPT-5 under rotation despite scoring lower on single-view evaluation.
> - Thinking variants and newer models trained on more general data can underperform on unusual viewpoints, suggesting chain-of-thought and data dilution can weaken visual grounding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a recognized gap in the evaluation and understanding of Vision Language Models (VLMs) when applied to top-down imagery. Historically, VLM development and benchmarking have predominantly focused on front-view images, as reflected in large-scale datasets such as COCO and ImageNet, and general-purpose VLM benchmarks like MMBench and MMMU. These datasets and benchmarks often contain a limited representation of top-down perspectives, leading to an incomplete understanding of how VLMs perform in domains critical for aerial systems, autonomous navigation, and surveillance.

Top-down images possess unique characteristics, including holistic spatial information, reduced object occlusion, consistent scale, and distinct object appearances, which differ significantly from front-view perspectives. Existing efforts to evaluate VLMs on remote sensing data typically involve low-resolution satellite images, which are not representative of human-scale or near-surface drone views required for tasks such as object localization or attribute comparison. Furthermore, the property of rotational invariance, where the physical meaning of a top-down scene is preserved under rotation, has been largely unaddressed in VLM evaluation.

A parallel challenge in VLM research is the issue of hallucination, where models generate plausible but ungrounded responses. While several benchmarks (e.g., POPE, HallusionBench) have been developed to detect object hallucination, they often treat correctness as a binary outcome without differentiating between genuine visual understanding and accidental "lucky guesses." This can lead to an inflated perception of model reliability.

This research contributes to the landscape by introducing a specialized benchmark, TDBench, designed for high-resolution, near-surface top-down image understanding. It also proposes a novel evaluation strategy, RotationalEval, that explicitly leverages the rotational invariance of these images to provide a more rigorous assessment of visual grounding. Finally, it introduces reliability-oriented metrics that aim to disentangle a model's true knowledge from chance successes, thereby offering a more nuanced perspective on VLM trustworthiness in top-down applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a comprehensive framework for evaluating and analyzing Vision Language Models (VLMs) in the context of top-down image understanding. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations:

First, the authors highlight the critical role of top-down images in safety-critical applications such as autonomous navigation, aerial surveillance, mapping, and disaster assessment. These images offer a unique "bird's-eye" view, providing comprehensive spatial overviews, clear geometric context, and reduced occlusion, which are capabilities that front-view images cannot fully capture. Despite their importance, top-down images are underrepresented in the datasets and benchmarks commonly used to train and evaluate current VLMs. This limited exposure leads to an insufficient understanding of VLM performance in top-down settings and potential generalization failures due to differences in object appearance, scale, and depth cues from aerial perspectives.

Second, existing VLM benchmarks are not specifically designed to address the distinct challenges posed by top-down images. These challenges include small and densely packed objects, unusual viewing angles, and weak perspective depth cues. Without a dedicated evaluation tool, systematically measuring and improving VLM performance on these unique conditions remains difficult.

Third, the research identifies a unique property of top-down images: their physical meaning is preserved under rotation. Unlike front-view images, where rotation can create physically implausible scenes, rotating a top-down image is analogous to a drone changing its heading, with the scene's semantics remaining consistent. This property presents an opportunity for a more rigorous and diagnostic evaluation of VLM visual reasoning, moving beyond single-orientation assessments.

Finally, the authors are motivated by the limitations of conventional accuracy metrics in assessing VLM reliability. Traditional accuracy scores can be artificially inflated by instances of hallucination, where models generate plausible but ungrounded answers, or by "lucky guesses." This obscures a model's true reliability and the extent to which its responses are genuinely grounded in visual evidence. The work seeks to develop metrics that can quantitatively distinguish visually-grounded knowledge from chance successes, providing a more trustworthy assessment of VLM capabilities.

In summary, the key objectives are to create a specialized benchmark (TDBench) for top-down image understanding, introduce a novel rotation-invariant evaluation methodology (RotationalEval), and develop reliability metrics that differentiate genuine knowledge from lucky guesses, all to guide the development of more robust and trustworthy AI systems for aerial perception.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development of TDBench, a benchmark for top-down image understanding, coupled with novel evaluation strategies and reliability analysis.

**A. TDBench Construction:**
The benchmark consists of 2,000 core question-answer pairs, complemented by an additional 2,100 questions used in specific case studies. The questions are categorized into ten abilities, grouped into three high-level types:
1. **Image Perception:** Evaluates holistic semantic understanding (e.g., Scene Understanding, Hallucination Detection).
2. **Single-Instance Understanding:** Focuses on detailed object-level recognition and localization (e.g., Object Presence, Attribute Recognition, Object Localization using a 3x3 grid, Visual Grounding with bounding box coordinates, Object Counting).
3. **Multi-Instance Reasoning:** Assesses compositional reasoning across multiple objects, including spatial, comparative, and temporal relationships (e.g., Spatial Relationship, Attribute Comparison, Dynamic Temporal analysis).

**Data Sources:** TDBench integrates images from two primary sources:
- **Curated Public Datasets:** Includes data from aerial traffic images, semantic drone datasets, Visdrone, Seadronesee, AFO, and ERA, covering diverse real-world environments.
- **Realistic Simulation:** Images generated using CARLA Simulator and Grand Theft Auto V (GTA V). Simulation enabled precise control over scene parameters (e.g., object instances, camera altitude) for specific challenges like object counting and altitude-dependent detection.

**Problem Formulation:** Most problems are framed as Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs) with 2 to 4 options. Visual Grounding (VG) tasks require models to output precise bounding box coordinates.

**Image Standardization:** To minimize evaluation biases from inconsistent image preprocessing across different VLMs, all images were standardized to a 512x512 pixel resolution. For tasks involving image pairs, sub-images were concatenated horizontally or vertically to fit this standard canvas.

**Quality Control:** A two-stage process ensured data quality:
1. **Human Review:** Six annotators independently reviewed questions for clarity, solvability, and correctness of ground truth.
2. **Model Filtering:** Open-source models (Deepseek VL2-small, InternVL2.5-MPO, Phi4, Qwen2.5-VL 7B) were used to identify and remove questions that were either too easy (consistently solved by all models) or problematic (consistently failed due to formulation issues), thereby increasing the discriminative value of the benchmark.

**B. RotationalEval (RE) Strategy:**
This novel evaluation method leverages the rotational invariance of top-down images. For each scene, four orientations are generated: the original, 90°, 180°, and 270° clockwise rotations. A question is considered correctly answered only if the model provides the correct response for *all four* rotated views of the same scene. This approach is designed to:
- Test the model's ability to consistently reason about semantics and object identities despite changes in orientation.
- Filter out "lucky guesses" or hallucinations that might produce a correct answer in one orientation but fail in others.
- **Rotation-Aware Question Design:** Questions were classified as rotation-invariant (e.g., object presence) or rotation-sensitive (e.g., spatial relationships). For rotation-sensitive questions, directional references in text (e.g., "top-left") are automatically transformed corresponding to the image's rotation using placeholder tokens, ensuring semantic consistency across orientations.

**C. Probability-based Knowledge Reliability Analysis:**
Beyond standard accuracy, the research proposes a probabilistic framework to decompose model performance into underlying components of knowledge and guessing. Three observable statistics are defined:
- **RE (RotationalEval Accuracy):** Probability of being correct across all four rotations.
- **VE (VanillaEval Accuracy):** Expected average accuracy across the four rotations.
- **MA (Mistake Accuracy):** Probability of being incorrect across all four rotations.

These observations are used to infer three hidden parameters:
- **`θ` (Proportion of Questions Known):** The percentage of questions a model genuinely understands.
- **`r` (P(Correct|Known)):** The accuracy of the model on questions it truly knows.
- **`g` (P(Correct|Guessed)):** The accuracy of the model on questions it does not know, effectively representing the rate of "lucky guesses."
These parameters are estimated by solving a system of equations, assuming conditional independence of answers across rotations. The framework also defines **Adjusted Accuracy (`A_adj = θ * r`)**, which represents the accuracy after excluding contributions from lucky guesses.

**D. Evaluation Setup:**
A total of 60 VLMs (17 proprietary, 43 open-source, ranging from 0.5 billion to 38 billion parameters) were evaluated in a zero-shot setting. VLMEvalKit, an open-source evaluation framework, was used, with model temperature set to 0 and GPT-4o serving as the answer extractor for model outputs.

**E. Case Studies:**
Four specific case studies were designed to investigate real-world challenges in top-down perception:
1. **Digital Magnification:** Examines the impact of increasing target object pixel coverage on small object detection.
2. **Altitude Effects:** Studies how varying camera altitude (physical zoom-in) affects object detection, using CARLA simulation to control height.
3. **Object Visibility and Partial Occlusion:** Investigates the effect of varying object "integrity" (amount visible) at fixed magnification levels.
4. **Z-Axis Perception and Depth Understanding:** Evaluates a model's ability to reason about intrinsic object height and contextual elevation from 2D top-down views.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation yielded several findings regarding VLM performance on top-down images, particularly concerning robustness and reliability.

**RotationalEval vs. VanillaEval:**
Comparison between RotationalEval (RE) and conventional VanillaEval (VE@0°) demonstrated a consistent performance drop across all evaluated VLMs when using RE. This decline is attributed to RE's stricter success criteria, which filters out correct answers resulting from random guessing rather than grounded visual understanding. The magnitude of this drop varied among models, with some high-performing VE models not achieving proportionally high RE, suggesting differing levels of robustness to rotation and potential susceptibility to hallucination. For instance, DeepSeek VL2 achieved the best VE among open models, while Qwen2.5-VL-7B had the highest RE, indicating better rotational consistency.

**Overall RE Performance:**
Across the 10 evaluation categories, proprietary models generally exhibited higher average RE scores than open-source models. Within model families, performance typically scaled with model size, although exceptions were observed. "Thinking" variants (e.g., chain-of-thought models) often underperformed their standard counterparts, particularly at smaller scales, which suggests that explicit reasoning prompts might sometimes reduce visual grounding. Notably, some newer models did not consistently outperform older versions (e.g., InternVL3.5 versus InternVL3), indicating that general-purpose training data additions might not always translate to improved top-down understanding. Gemini 2.5 Pro achieved the highest overall RE among all models.

**Reliability Analysis (θ, r, g, A_adj):**
The probabilistic analysis provided insights beyond raw accuracy:
- **`θ` (Proportion of Questions Known):** For InternVL3 models, `θ` increased with model size, implying that larger models genuinely understand a greater proportion of questions. Gemini 2.5 Pro demonstrated the highest `θ` (0.822), suggesting broad knowledge coverage.
- **`r` (Accuracy among Known Questions):** This metric remained high across models, often approaching 100%, and showed a slight increase with scale for InternVL3. GPT-5 and o3 achieved the highest `r` values (0.941 and 0.921, respectively).
- **`g` (Accuracy among Guessed Questions):** This varied independently of model size. GPT-5 and o3 also exhibited relatively high `g` values (0.265 and 0.279), indicating a higher propensity for "lucky guesses" compared to other models. Conversely, Claude Opus 4.1 showed a low `r` (0.849), suggesting lower accuracy even on questions it purportedly "knows."
- **`A_adj` (Adjusted Accuracy):** This metric, representing accuracy without lucky guesses (`θ * r`), generally improved with larger models for InternVL3, supporting its utility in assessing genuine knowledge. Gemini 2.5 Pro had the highest `A_adj` (0.754).

**Case Studies:**
1. **Digital Magnification for Small Object Detection:** Accuracy generally increased with digital magnification (area ratio) up to an optimal point, after which it declined due to context loss. GPT-4o peaked at a low 0.8% object occupancy, while open-source models required 2-4%.
2. **Altitude Effects on Object Detection:** Detection accuracy typically decreased with increasing camera altitude. However, optimal heights were observed for different object categories: 5m for bicycles/motorcycles, 10m for cars, and 15m for trucks/buses. This phenomenon was attributed to partial object visibility at very low altitudes for larger objects.
3. **Object Visibility and Partial Occlusion:** Model accuracy remained high until object integrity (the visible portion) dropped below a specific threshold, typically 60-70%, which was influenced by the initial area ratio. Below this threshold, accuracy sharply declined.
4. **Z-Axis Perception and Depth Understanding:** Models exhibited varying abilities to assess intrinsic object height and contextual elevation. DeepSeek performed well on identifying the tallest/highest objects but struggled with ranking tasks. GPT-4o demonstrated robust performance across both types of depth-related questions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Vision Language Models (VLMs) by addressing critical deficiencies in their evaluation and understanding within the domain of top-down imagery. The significance and potential impact of this work span several areas:

Firstly, the introduction of **TDBench** fills a notable gap by providing a comprehensive, application-driven benchmark specifically for high-resolution, near-surface top-down image understanding. This is crucial for advancing AI systems in safety-critical applications such as autonomous navigation, drone-based surveillance, and disaster response, where front-view-centric evaluations are insufficient. By focusing on diverse top-down tasks and scenes, TDBench offers a standardized platform to assess and compare VLM capabilities relevant to real-world aerial operations.

Secondly, the proposed **RotationalEval (RE)** strategy establishes a more rigorous and diagnostic method for evaluating VLM robustness. By leveraging the unique rotational invariance of top-down images, RE can differentiate superficial successes from genuine visual reasoning. This method provides a stricter performance measure that inherently reduces the influence of "lucky guesses" or hallucinations, thereby offering a more reliable indicator of a model's grounding in visual evidence. Its application could encourage the development of VLMs that are inherently more consistent and geometrically aware.

Thirdly, the development of **probability-based knowledge reliability analysis** offers a novel framework for assessing model trustworthiness beyond conventional accuracy. By decomposing performance into `θ` (proportion known), `r` (accuracy on known), and `g` (accuracy from guesses), researchers can gain a deeper understanding of a model's intrinsic knowledge and its propensity for ungrounded responses. This enhanced transparency is vital for deploying VLMs in high-stakes environments, where understanding not just *if* a model is correct, but *why* it is correct, is paramount. These metrics are presented as generalizable probes, potentially applicable beyond TDBench to other datasets, thereby offering a broader impact on VLM reliability assessment.

Finally, the **four targeted case studies** provide actionable insights for the design and deployment of VLM-based aerial systems. The findings on digital magnification, optimal altitude for object detection, performance under partial occlusion, and z-axis perception offer practical guidance for maximizing detection reliability and understanding depth from 2D aerial views. These studies highlight specific challenges and inform strategies for improving VLM performance in operational settings, potentially leading to more effective and reliable drone autonomy and remote sensing applications.

Overall, this work not only benchmarks current VLM capabilities in an underexplored but critical domain but also introduces innovative evaluation paradigms that can guide the future development of more robust, grounded, and trustworthy multimodal AI systems. The detailed analysis provided by TDBench and its associated metrics can pinpoint specific areas for improvement in VLM architectures and training methodologies, ultimately fostering progress toward more reliable AI for complex real-world scenarios.
