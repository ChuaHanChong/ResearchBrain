# Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Least-to-Most (L2M) prompting from Google Research's Brain Team enables large language models to generalize to problems significantly harder than demonstration examples by systematically decomposing them into sequential subproblems. This method achieved 99.7% accuracy on the SCAN compositional generalization benchmark and notable improvements on symbolic manipulation and multi-step math reasoning tasks over Chain-of-Thought prompting.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with "easy-to-hard generalization," meaning they perform poorly when test problems are significantly more complex or longer than their demonstration examples.
- Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, while effective for eliciting step-by-step reasoning, often fails when tasks require out-of-distribution generalization, especially with increased compositional complexity.
- Compositional generalization benchmarks, such as SCAN, traditionally required specialized neural-symbolic architectures and extensive training data to achieve high accuracy.

## Method

- Introduces **Least-to-Most (L2M)** prompting, a ==two-stage strategy== that guides LLMs to solve complex problems by breaking them down into simpler, ==sequential subproblems==.
- The ==Decomposition Stage== prompts the LLM to analyze the original complex problem and generate a list of ordered subproblems.
- The ==Subproblem Solving Stage== sequentially solves each generated subproblem, ==feeding previously obtained answers back into the prompt== as context for subsequent steps, all purely through ==few-shot prompting==.

## Results

- Achieved **74.0%** accuracy on the Last-Letter-Concatenation task (length 12) with L2M (2-shot) using `code-davinci-002`, significantly outperforming CoT's **31.8%**.
- Reached **99.7%** accuracy on the challenging SCAN length split using only **14** exemplars, vastly surpassing CoT (**16.2%**) and previous specialized models that required over **15,000** training examples.
- Demonstrated improved performance on multi-step math reasoning, including GSM8K (**62.39%** for L2M vs. **60.87%** for CoT) and significantly on the DROP benchmark (e.g., **82.45%** vs. **74.77%** on Non-football).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing complex problems into simpler, interdependent subproblems allows Large Language Models (LLMs) to generalize from easy examples to significantly harder ones.
> - Advanced prompting alone can unlock latent reasoning capabilities in LLMs, achieving results comparable to or better than specialized models without fine-tuning or extensive training data.
> - The systematic, multi-step nature of Least-to-Most (L2M) prompting mirrors human problem-solving strategies, leading to more structured and potentially interpretable LLM reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper directly addresses a critical challenge in the field of Large Language Models (LLMs): their ability to generalize to problems of increasing difficulty, often referred to as "easy-to-hard generalization" or "compositional generalization."

In recent years, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable emergent capabilities, particularly in "few-shot learning," where they can perform new tasks with only a handful of examples, reducing the need for massive labeled datasets. A significant breakthrough in enhancing LLM reasoning was the introduction of **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting** (Wei et al., 2022; Chowdhery et al., 2022). CoT prompting involves providing LLMs with demonstrations that include intermediate reasoning steps, allowing the models to generate a "thought process" before arriving at a final answer. This approach significantly improved performance on various complex reasoning tasks, such as arithmetic, common sense, and symbolic reasoning, often outperforming specially trained models by substantial margins.

However, a key limitation of CoT prompting identified by the authors is its tendency to perform poorly when tasks require solving problems *harder* than the exemplars provided in the prompts. This means that while CoT can effectively guide reasoning for tasks of similar complexity to its demonstrations, it struggles with out-of-distribution generalization, especially concerning compositional tasks where the structure or length of the problem increases.

"Least-to-Most (L2M) Prompting" is positioned as a direct advancement over CoT, specifically designed to overcome this "easy-to-hard generalization" hurdle. It draws inspiration from educational psychology, where a "least-to-most" approach uses progressive sequences of prompts to teach new skills, applying this metaphor to how humans "teach" language models.

The paper also situates its contribution in contrast to prior work on **compositional generalization** and **task decomposition**. Benchmarks like SCAN (Lake & Baroni, 2018), which test systematic compositional abilities, have traditionally required specialized neural-symbolic models or grammar induction techniques (e.g., Chen et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020; Nye et al., 2020) to achieve high accuracy, often relying on extensive training data. L2M demonstrates that LLMs, *without* any training or fine-tuning and using only a few examples, can achieve comparable or superior performance on such benchmarks. This fundamentally challenges the necessity of complex, specialized architectures and large datasets for certain types of complex reasoning, highlighting the power of advanced prompting strategies with existing large, pre-trained models.

Furthermore, L2M contributes to the broader area of **task decomposition** research, which explores how to break down multi-step problems into manageable subproblems. Unlike previous methods that often involve training separate models for decomposition and aggregation (Perez et al., 2020), or rule-based systems (Yang et al., 2022), L2M performs both stages purely through few-shot prompting, showcasing the inherent capabilities of LLMs to self-organize problem-solving. This work advances the understanding of how to elicit and guide complex, multi-step reasoning from LLMs in a flexible, zero-shot/few-shot manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable Large Language Models (LLMs) to perform complex reasoning and generalize effectively to problems that are significantly harder than those seen in their demonstration examples.** This capability, referred to as "easy-to-hard generalization," is a major outstanding challenge in the field of artificial intelligence.

The motivations driving this objective stem from a critical limitation observed in current state-of-the-art LLM reasoning, particularly with existing prompting techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT):
- **Bridging the Human-Machine Intelligence Gap:** The authors highlight three key differences between human and machine intelligence: (1) humans learn from few examples, while machines need large datasets; (2) humans can explain their reasoning, while machines are "black boxes"; and (3) humans can solve problems harder than what they've seen, while machines typically perform similarly on training and testing data. CoT prompting made strides in the first two areas, but the third – easy-to-hard generalization – remained largely unaddressed.
- **Addressing CoT's Generalization Shortcomings:** While CoT revolutionized LLM reasoning by eliciting step-by-step thinking, it often struggles when the test problems exhibit higher complexity, longer sequences, or deeper compositional structures than the exemplars provided in the prompt. For instance, on tasks like symbolic manipulation (e.g., last-letter-concatenation), CoT performs well on inputs of similar length but degrades significantly for longer inputs.
- **Improving Compositional Reasoning without Specialized Training:** For tasks requiring compositional generalization, such as the SCAN benchmark, existing high-performing models are typically specialized neural-symbolic architectures trained on thousands of examples. A key motivation was to determine if LLMs, using only prompting and a handful of examples, could match or exceed this performance without any task-specific training or fine-tuning, thereby showcasing the power of emergent capabilities and advanced prompting.
- **Developing a More Robust and Interpretable Reasoning Strategy:** By breaking down complex problems into subproblems and solving them sequentially, the proposed "least-to-most" approach aims to provide a more structured and transparent reasoning process, potentially leading to more reliable and interpretable outputs from LLMs. This mimics a human problem-solving strategy, where complex challenges are often tackled by first deconstructing them.

In essence, the research aims to unlock a new level of reasoning capability in LLMs, allowing them to tackle inherently more complex problems by systematically decomposing them, thereby moving closer to human-like problem-solving versatility and efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the introduction of **Least-to-Most (L2M) Prompting**, a novel strategy designed to enable Large Language Models (LLMs) to generalize from easy demonstration examples to harder, more complex problems. The fundamental idea is to mimic a human problem-solving approach: breaking down a complex problem into a series of simpler, interdependent subproblems and then solving these subproblems sequentially.

The methodology consists of two distinct, sequential stages, both implemented purely through **few-shot prompting**, meaning no model training or fine-tuning is required:

1. **Decomposition Stage:**
 - **Objective:** To instruct the LLM to analyze a given complex problem and decompose it into a list of smaller, more manageable subproblems.
 - **Implementation:** The prompt provided to the LLM in this stage contains a few demonstration examples that illustrate how to break down various complex problems into their constituent subproblems. Following these examples, the specific complex question to be decomposed is appended.
 - **Output:** The LLM generates a sequence of subproblems that, when solved, would collectively lead to the solution of the original problem. These subproblems are typically ordered such that later subproblems might depend on the answers of earlier ones.
 - **Example (Figure 1, Table 1):** For a math word problem like "Elsa has 5 apples. Anna has 2 more apples than Elsa. How many apples do they have together?", the decomposition stage would lead the LLM to identify subproblems like "1. How many apples does Anna have?" and "2. How many apples do they have together?". Similarly, for symbolic manipulation, a list like "think, machine, learning" is decomposed into "think, machine" and "think, machine, learning."

2. **Subproblem Solving Stage:**
 - **Objective:** To sequentially solve the subproblems generated in the decomposition stage, leveraging previously obtained answers as context for subsequent subproblems.
 - **Implementation:** For each subproblem, a new prompt is constructed. This prompt comprises three parts:
 - Constant examples demonstrating how subproblems are solved (e.g., explaining calculation steps).
 - A list of previously answered subquestions and their generated solutions, providing crucial context and building blocks for subsequent reasoning.
 - The current subproblem to be answered next.
 - **Sequential Processing:** The LLM processes these prompts one by one. The answer generated for one subproblem is then fed back into the prompt for the next subproblem, creating a chain of inter-dependent reasoning steps. The answer to the final subproblem (which is often the original problem itself, rephrased as a subproblem) is taken as the ultimate solution.
 - **Example (Figure 1, Table 2):** Following the math problem example, after decomposing, the model first solves "How many apples does Anna have?" (e.g., "2 + 5 = 7 apples."). This answer ("Anna has 7 apples") is then included in the prompt for the next subproblem, "How many apples do they have together?" (e.g., "Elsa and Anna have 5 + 7 = 12 apples together."). This recursive nature allows the model to build up complex solutions. For symbolic manipulation, demonstrations show a base case and a recursive step: output of "think, machine" is used to solve "think, machine, learning".

**Key Features and Variations:**
- **Few-Shot Nature:** Crucially, L2M relies entirely on few-shot prompting, meaning no gradient updates or fine-tuning of the large language model weights occur. The model's inherent reasoning capabilities are elicited solely through the design of the prompts.
- **Combinability:** L2M can be combined with other prompting techniques, such as Chain-of-Thought itself (within the subproblem solving stage) and self-consistency decoding (Wang et al., 2022b), although the paper notes it's not strictly necessary.
- **Single-Pass vs. Two-Stage:** For some tasks (e.g., GSM8K math problems), the two stages can be merged into a single-pass prompt where decomposition and solving happen within one continuous output, followed by a final answer extraction.
- **Intermediate Representations:** For tasks like SCAN, the authors used Python notation (e.g., `“LOOK” * 2`) as an intermediate representation for action sequences to keep prompts concise, with a post-processing script (or even another prompted LLM call) to expand them to final actions.

This structured, multi-step prompting approach aims to guide the LLM through complex logical paths, enabling it to break down and conquer problems far beyond the superficial complexity of the initial few-shot demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Least-to-Most (L2M) prompting across three diverse domains—symbolic manipulation, compositional generalization, and math reasoning—demonstrates its superior capability in handling problems harder than those presented in demonstrations, especially compared to Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and standard few-shot prompting.

#### 4.1. Symbolic Manipulation (Last-Letter-Concatenation Task)

- **Challenge:** Generalizing to longer input lists than seen in prompts. CoT performs well on same-length lists but poorly on longer ones.
- **L2M Performance:** L2M significantly outperforms CoT. For lists of length 12, L2M achieves **74.0% accuracy** (with 2 exemplars) compared to **31.8% for CoT**. The performance gap widens as list length increases (Table 4), with CoT's accuracy dropping much faster.
- **Data Efficiency:** L2M is more data-efficient. Even a 2-shot L2M (123 GPT-3 tokens) surpasses an 8-shot CoT (573 GPT-3 tokens) on longer sequences (e.g., 74.0% vs. 38.4% for L=12, Table 13), highlighting its ability to generalize from fewer, well-structured examples.
- **Model Dependence:** The `code-davinci-002` model consistently outperforms `text-davinci-002` and `code-davinci-001`, particularly on longer sequences, suggesting a strength in iterative and recursive tasks.
- **Error Analysis (Table 14):** For L2M, common errors are concatenation mistakes (dropping or adding a letter), rather than incorrect identification of last letters or wrong template application. This suggests the model understands the core task but struggles with perfect execution of long sequences.

#### 4.2. Compositional Generalization (SCAN Benchmark)

- **Challenge:** Mapping natural language commands to action sequences, especially under the challenging "length split" where test sequences are longer than training ones. Specialized neural-symbolic models previously achieved high accuracy on this task, trained on over 15,000 examples.
- **L2M Breakthrough:** L2M achieves **99.7% accuracy** on the SCAN length split (and all other splits/full dataset) using only **14 exemplars** with the `code-davinci-002` model (Table 8).
- **Comparison to Baselines:** This is a dramatic improvement over CoT (16.2%) and standard prompting (16.7%) on `code-davinci-002`, and vastly superior to previous neural-symbolic models that required extensive training.
- **Model Performance:** `code-davinci-002` again significantly outperforms `text-davinci-002` (76.0%) and `code-davinci-001` (60.7%).
- **Error Analysis (Table 15):** The few errors made by L2M (with `code-davinci-002`) on SCAN are primarily due to incorrect interpretation of "twice" and "thrice" following "around" (e.g., miscalculating repetitions) and occasionally interpreting "after" as "and" in command concatenation.
- **Intermediate Python Expressions:** The use of Python notation as an intermediate representation proved effective, and the paper shows LLMs can even expand these expressions with near-perfect accuracy (99.7%) via prompting, eliminating the need for external scripts.

#### 4.3. Math Reasoning (GSM8K and DROP Benchmarks)

- **Challenge:** Solving multi-step math word problems, with difficulty measured by the number of reasoning steps.
- **GSM8K Results (Table 11, Table 12):**
 - Overall, L2M shows a modest improvement over CoT: **62.39%** vs. 60.87%.
 - Crucially, L2M demonstrates stronger "easy-to-hard generalization" on problems requiring **5 or more steps**, increasing accuracy from 39.07% (CoT) to **45.23%**. This aligns with the core hypothesis that L2M shines when problems become significantly more complex than demonstrations (which were 2-step problems in the 1-shot setup).
 - The authors note that almost all GSM8K failures could be solved with manually crafted decompositions, suggesting the decomposition stage is the key bottleneck for harder math problems.
- **DROP Results (Table 11):**
 - L2M significantly outperforms CoT on both "Non-football" (**82.45%** vs. 74.77%) and "Football" (**73.42%** vs. 59.56%) subsets of the DROP benchmark. The paper attributes this larger margin to DROP problems generally being "trivially decomposable," making the decomposition stage less challenging for the LLM.

In summary, the results provide compelling evidence that Least-to-Most prompting is a powerful technique for enhancing LLMs' complex reasoning capabilities, particularly enabling them to generalize to problems that are harder or more compositionally complex than the provided examples. The `code-davinci-002` model, in particular, consistently showed superior performance with L2M.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Least-to-Most (L2M) prompting represents a significant advancement in the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) and has several profound implications for the field of artificial intelligence:

- **Breaching the Easy-to-Hard Generalization Barrier:** This is arguably the most critical contribution. By effectively addressing the challenge of "easy-to-hard generalization," L2M enables LLMs to tackle problems significantly more complex or longer than their demonstration examples. This capability is a hallmark of human intelligence and a crucial step towards more robust and versatile AI systems, moving beyond simple interpolation or pattern matching.
- **Enhanced Complex Reasoning without Fine-tuning:** L2M achieves impressive results on complex reasoning tasks (symbolic manipulation, compositional generalization, multi-step math problems) *without* any model fine-tuning or training. This highlights the immense, latent reasoning capabilities within existing large pre-trained models that can be unlocked purely through sophisticated prompting strategies. This reduces computational costs, data requirements for specific tasks, and the need for constant retraining.
- **Challenging the Need for Specialized Architectures:** The L2M's performance on the SCAN benchmark is particularly impactful. Achieving 99.7% accuracy with only 14 exemplars dramatically surpasses specialized neural-symbolic models that require extensive training data (over 15,000 examples) and complex architectural designs. This suggests that for certain types of compositional reasoning, a well-designed prompting approach with a general-purpose LLM can be more effective and efficient than developing highly specialized models.
- **Towards More Human-Like Problem Solving:** The methodology of decomposing complex problems into simpler, sequential subproblems mirrors a common human cognitive strategy for problem-solving. By teaching LLMs to adopt this strategy, L2M moves them closer to mimicking human-like reasoning processes, which could have long-term implications for developing more intelligent and intuitive AI systems.
- **Increased Interpretability and Controllability:** The multi-step, explicit nature of L2M, where problems are broken down and solved step-by-step, naturally lends itself to greater interpretability. Researchers and users can inspect the intermediate subproblems and their solutions, gaining insight into the model's reasoning path, unlike "black-box" end-to-end models. This transparency is crucial for debugging, auditing, and building trust in AI systems.
- **Catalyst for Future Research in Human-AI Interaction:** The paper concludes by suggesting that prompting, as a unidirectional form of communication, should evolve into "fully bidirectional conversations" with immediate feedback for more efficient learning. L2M, with its structured interaction (decomposition query, followed by sequential solving queries), represents a significant stride in this direction. This outlook could inspire new paradigms for human-AI collaboration where humans guide AI through complex tasks not just with initial instructions but through iterative refinement and problem structuring.

Despite the limitations (domain-specificity of decomposition prompts, inherent difficulty of decomposition for certain problems), Least-to-Most Prompting marks a pivotal moment in LLM research, demonstrating a powerful and generalizable method for complex reasoning that leverages the emergent capabilities of LLMs in a novel, interpretable, and highly effective way. Its impact is likely to be seen in the development of more capable AI assistants, advanced problem-solving agents, and continued exploration of how to bridge the gap between artificial and human intelligence through innovative interaction strategies.
