# MimicDroid: In-Context Learning for Humanoid Robot Manipulation from Human Play Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> MIMICDROID enables humanoid robots to acquire new manipulation skills from a few video demonstrations by utilizing in-context learning trained exclusively on unlabeled, continuous human play videos. The system achieves significantly higher success rates on a new benchmark for few-shot humanoid manipulation compared to previous methods, including a nearly twofold improvement in real-world performance on a GR1 humanoid against baselines.

## Problem

- Existing in-context learning (ICL) methods for robot manipulation rely on costly and time-consuming teleoperated robot data, limiting scalability and diversity.
- Bridging the significant kinematic and visual embodiment gaps between human demonstrations and robot execution remains a challenge for transferring skills.
- A lack of standardized benchmarks for evaluating few-shot learning in humanoid manipulation hinders systematic progress and comparison.

## Method

- A self-supervised method is introduced to construct context-target training pairs from raw human play videos by retrieving similar manipulation behaviors based on visual and action features.
- The human-robot embodiment gap is addressed through kinematic retargeting of human wrist poses to humanoid control and visual masking on input images to prevent overfitting to human appearances.
- A ==Meta-ICL== framework with a transformer backbone is trained using a behavior cloning objective on these constructed human video-based context-target pairs.

## Results

- MIMICDROID achieved **+14%** (abstract) and **+18%** (humanoid) higher success rates in simulation compared to task-conditioned baselines, and **+29%** (abstract) and **+26%** (humanoid) over parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
- In real-world tests on a GR1 humanoid, MIMICDROID demonstrated nearly a twofold higher success rate over Vid2Robot on novel tasks (e.g., **0.23** vs **0.08** for unseen objects in seen environments).
- Performance consistently improved as the amount of training human play video data increased, confirming the benefits of data scaling, while performance plateaued beyond 3 in-context examples at test time due to architectural context length limitations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training ICL policies solely on diverse, unlabeled human play videos is effective for humanoid manipulation, offering a scalable data source for few-shot learning.
> - Random patch visual masking during training is crucial for robust transfer from human demonstrations to robots, mitigating visual embodiment differences effectively.
> - The quality of context retrieval during meta-training significantly impacts performance, with an optimal balance needed between diversity and similarity of retrieved segments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing towards creating generalist robots capable of performing diverse tasks in unstructured environments. A major bottleneck in achieving this goal is the difficulty of programming robots for every conceivable scenario. **Few-shot learning** has emerged as a promising paradigm, allowing robots to acquire new skills from a minimal number of demonstrations. Within this paradigm, **In-Context Learning (ICL)**, inspired by the success of large language models, has gained significant traction. ICL enables models to adapt to novel tasks at test time by simply conditioning on a few input-output examples, without requiring costly parameter updates or retraining.

However, existing ICL methods in robot manipulation face a critical limitation: their reliance on **labor-intensive teleoperated robot data** for training. Collecting such data is expensive, time-consuming, and often lacks the diversity needed to build robust, general-purpose policies. This severely restricts the scalability and applicability of ICL for real-world robotic systems, particularly for complex platforms like humanoids.

Simultaneously, researchers have explored **learning from human videos** as a more scalable data source. Human videos are abundant, diverse, and relatively easy to collect. Prior work has used human videos for visual representation learning, reward function derivation, or motion prior extraction. However, many of these approaches still require additional robot-specific data for fine-tuning or rely on specialized hardware (e.g., VR, hand-tracking devices) or unnatural human motions that mimic robot morphology.

MIMICDROID strategically positions itself at the intersection of these two critical research areas: **leveraging the power of ICL for few-shot learning in humanoid manipulation, while overcoming the data scalability challenge by training exclusively on diverse and readily available human play videos.** The paper addresses the unique challenges posed by this integration:
1. **Constructing ICL training samples from raw human play videos**: Human play videos are unlabeled and continuous, unlike the structured context-target pairs typically required for Meta-ICL. MIMICDROID introduces a self-supervised method to extract meaningful training pairs by identifying "similar manipulation behaviors."
2. **Bridging the human-robot embodiment gap**: Significant kinematic and visual differences exist between humans and humanoid robots. MIMICDROID tackles this by retargeting human wrist poses to humanoid control (kinematic similarity) and employing visual masking during training to prevent overfitting to human appearances (visual robustness).

By pioneering the use of human play videos for training ICL policies for humanoids, MIMICDROID pushes the boundaries of data-efficient robot learning. It moves beyond the limitations of robot-centric data collection, paving the way for more generalist and adaptable humanoid robots that can learn new skills from passively observed human interactions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **enable humanoid robots to efficiently solve novel manipulation tasks using only a few video examples as demonstrations.** This capability is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic, unstructured environments like homes, where variability in objects, layouts, and tasks necessitates rapid adaptation.

The motivation behind pursuing this objective through In-Context Learning (ICL) and human play videos is multifaceted:

1. **Need for Rapid Adaptation**: Traditional robot programming and even many imitation learning approaches struggle with the sheer variability of real-world tasks. Humanoids need to quickly acquire new skills for objects and environments they haven't encountered before. Few-shot learning, particularly through ICL's test-time data efficiency and gradient-free adaptation, offers a promising solution.
2. **Scalability of Training Data**: The effectiveness of ICL is highly dependent on large and diverse training datasets. However, current methods rely on teleoperated robot demonstrations, which are prohibitively expensive and slow to collect at scale. This data bottleneck limits the generalization capabilities of learned policies.
3. **Leveraging Human Play Videos**: Human play videos, characterized by continuous, unlabeled recordings of people interacting freely with their environment, offer a significantly more scalable and diverse data source. They are faster to collect (approximately 18× faster than teleoperated data), inherently capture a broad range of task-agnostic interactions, object configurations, and manipulation behaviors, and avoid the need for explicit task labeling. The motivation is to tap into this rich, naturalistic data to train robust ICL policies.
4. **Addressing Embodiment Gaps**: Directly applying knowledge from human videos to robots is challenging due to inherent kinematic and visual differences. The project is motivated to develop specific mechanisms (kinematic retargeting and visual masking) to bridge these "embodiment gaps" effectively, ensuring that policies learned from human demonstrations can be successfully transferred and executed on humanoid robots.
5. **Lack of Standardized Benchmarks**: The absence of a standardized benchmark for few-shot learning in humanoid manipulation motivates the creation of a new, open-source simulation benchmark. This benchmark aims to provide a systematic way to evaluate generalization across increasing levels of difficulty (novel objects, novel environments), fostering future research in this area.

In essence, MIMICDROID aims to unlock the potential of ICL for humanoid manipulation by finding a scalable and effective training data source in human play videos, while systematically addressing the critical challenges of data construction and cross-embodiment transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MIMICDROID's methodology revolves around training an in-context learning policy for humanoid robots using only unlabeled, continuous human play videos. The approach addresses the core challenges of data construction, embodiment gaps, and meta-training for ICL.

**A. Data Construction from Human Play Videos:**
The raw training data consists of continuous RGB human play videos without explicit task labels or action annotations. MIMICDROID transforms this into structured (observation, action) pairs suitable for ICL training:
- **Action and Proprioceptive Information Extraction**: Instead of explicit action labels, MIMICDROID uses future human hand poses as the intended action. An off-the-shelf, pre-trained hand pose estimation model (f = WiLoR) extracts 6-DoF wrist pose and a grasp signal from each RGB frame. The hand pose at *t+k* timesteps ahead (where *k* is 1 for real-world and not explicitly stated for simulation but implicitly used) is treated as the action *a_t*. The current hand pose *h_t* is included in the observation *s'_t = {s_t, h_t}* to serve as proprioception.
- **Context-Target Pair Construction for Meta-ICL**: To instill ICL capabilities, the policy needs to learn from structured context-target pairs. Human play videos naturally exhibit recurring manipulation patterns (e.g., "moving bread from plate to oven" then "moving bagel from plate to oven"). MIMICDROID leverages this by:
 1. Randomly sampling a "target trajectory segment" (σ_tgt) from a processed human play video.
 2. Retrieving the *k* (e.g., k=10, optimized to k=50) most similar trajectory segments from the entire dataset to serve as "context trajectories" ({σ_ctx_i}).
 3. Similarity between segments is calculated as the cosine similarity of their feature embeddings. These features are generated by first extracting frame-wise visual features using a pre-trained vision model (g = DinoV2), applying temporal mean pooling, and then concatenating with the sequence of actions. This process allows for self-supervised generation of Meta-ICL training samples.

**B. Overcoming the Embodiment Gap:**
To transfer policies learned from human videos to humanoid robots (specifically GR1), MIMICDROID employs two strategies:
- **Kinematic Retargeting**: The policy operates in the task space (Cartesian wrist pose). Predicted human wrist poses are directly retargeted to humanoid wrist poses, and inverse kinematics are applied to compute joint angles. This exploits the kinematic similarities between humans and humanoids, preserving the task intent without requiring human demonstrators to mimic robot morphology.
- **Visual Masking**: To mitigate the visual gap (differences in body appearance, occlusion patterns) and prevent overfitting to human-specific visual cues, random patch masking is applied to input images during training (with probability *p=0.8* and 1-16 patches covering 1-4% of the image area). This forces the model to learn more generalizable visual representations.

**C. Meta-training for In-Context Learning:**
The policy is trained using a **Meta-ICL framework** (Eq. 1) with a behavior cloning objective.
- **Policy Architecture**: A long-context transformer backbone with modality-specific encoders is used. It processes a sequence of observation and action tokens.
 - **Observation Encoder**: Consists of a 2-layer MLP for proprioception and a pre-trained Cross-MAE for visual features, fused via a self-attention module.
 - **Action Encoder**: A 2-layer MLP encodes action vectors.
 - **Transformer Backbone**: A 4-layer LLaMA-style transformer with RMSNorm and rotary positional embeddings processes the combined tokens.
 - **Action Decoder**: An MLP head predicts a chunk of *l=32* future actions at each timestep (action chunking).
- **Training Objective**: The policy minimizes the L1 loss between predicted and ground-truth action chunks on the target segment, conditioned on the context trajectories (Eq. 3). This meta-training process exposes the policy to numerous context-target pairs, enabling it to learn a general strategy for adapting to new tasks.
- **Implicit Task Distribution**: Instead of explicitly defining tasks, MIMICDROID relies on the implicit task distribution induced by the diverse, continuous nature of human play videos, sampling target trajectories uniformly.

**D. Simulation Benchmark:**
To systematically evaluate few-shot learning for humanoids, MIMICDROID introduces an **open-source simulation benchmark** built on RoboCasa. It features 8 hours of play data (320k timesteps) collected using a free-floating 6-DoF hand ("Abstract" embodiment) across 30 objects and 8 kitchen environments. Evaluation is structured into three progressively difficult generalization levels on both the Abstract hand and a GR1 humanoid:
- **L1 (Seen Objects, Seen Environment)**: Generalization to new object positions within familiar environments.
- **L2 (Unseen Objects, Seen Environment)**: Adaptation to novel objects within familiar environments.
- **L3 (Unseen Objects, Unseen Environment)**: The most challenging, requiring adaptation to both novel objects and entirely new environments with different layouts.

This comprehensive methodology, spanning data curation, embodiment gap mitigation, and robust meta-training, allows MIMICDROID to effectively learn ICL capabilities from readily available human play videos.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MIMICDROID, conducted in both simulation and real-world settings across a new, systematic benchmark, yielded several significant findings:

- **Superior Few-Shot Generalization via ICL**:
 - MIMICDROID consistently outperformed task-conditioned baselines (H2R and Vid2Robot), which lack the observation-action pairs necessary for ICL. In simulation, MIMICDROID achieved significant performance gains of +14% (abstract) and +18% (humanoid) in success rates compared to these baselines.
 - Against parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), MIMICDROID demonstrated both instant, gradient-free adaptation and higher success rates (+29% in abstract, +26% in humanoid). Notably, PEFT struggled significantly at the most challenging L3 generalization level, likely due to catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning, whereas ICL preserved pre-trained knowledge.
 - In real-world evaluations on the GR1 humanoid, MIMICDROID achieved success rates of 0.53 (L1), 0.23 (L2), and 0.08 (L3). This was nearly a **twofold higher success rate** compared to Vid2Robot (0.28, 0.08, 0.00 respectively), strongly validating its ability to adapt to novel objects and scenes from few demonstrations.

- **Effectiveness of Visual Masking in Bridging the Embodiment Gap**:
 - Ablation studies showed that removing visual masking ("MIMICDROID w/o Visual Masking") caused a sharp drop in transfer performance to the humanoid (−17% averaged over all levels) compared to only −3% with visual masking enabled. This highlights the critical role of random patch masking in making the learned representations robust to visual differences between human and robot embodiments, preventing overfitting to human-specific visual cues.
 - Random patch masking achieved comparable real-world performance to a more complex masking strategy (EgoMimic) that relies on external segmentation modules like SAM, demonstrating its efficiency and effectiveness.

- **Scalability with In-Context Examples**:
 - Performance improved as the number of in-context examples at test time increased from 1 to 3. This confirmed the utility of providing more demonstrations for ICL.
 - However, gains plateaued beyond 3 examples and even dropped slightly with 4-6 examples, attributed to the limited context length supported by the transformer policy during training. This indicates a current architectural limitation for handling very long contexts efficiently.

- **Impact of Training Data Quality (Context Retrieval)**:
 - The quality of retrieved context segments during Meta-ICL training significantly impacted performance. Using too many (large *k*) dissimilar segments (noise) or too few (small *k*) diverse segments (limited diversity) led to reduced success rates.
 - The optimal number of retrieved context segments (*k*) for training was found to be 50, balancing diversity with the avoidance of excessive noise. This emphasizes the importance of a robust retrieval mechanism for effective Meta-ICL.

- **Scalability with Dataset Size**:
 - MIMICDROID demonstrated consistent performance improvements across all generalization levels (L1, L2, L3) as the amount of training human play video data increased.
 - For example, L1 success rates improved from 35% (128k frames) to 59% (320k frames), and L2 from 21% to 45%. This confirms the benefits of scaling diverse human play videos for learning ICL capabilities. L3 also improved, though less dramatically, indicating that harder generalization tasks may require even more data or more sophisticated learning mechanisms.

- **Failure Analysis**:
 - Analysis revealed that failures primarily stemmed from **task misidentification (26%)** and **grasping failures (16%)**.
 - MIMICDROID significantly reduced both misidentification (−15%) and grasping errors (−5%) compared to the Vid2Robot baseline, showcasing the benefits of ICL.
 - Limitations for L3 tasks included struggling with novel robot motions (e.g., different faucet mechanisms requiring entirely new motion sequences) and overfitting to specific hand sizes in real-world training, leading to collisions. These point to areas for future improvement.

In summary, MIMICDROID successfully demonstrates the viability and effectiveness of training ICL policies for humanoid manipulation solely from human play videos, showcasing superior generalization and real-world performance compared to existing methods, while also highlighting key areas for future research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MIMICDROID represents a significant step forward in the field of robot learning, particularly for humanoid manipulation, with substantial potential impact across several dimensions:

1. **Enabling Generalist Humanoid Robots**: By demonstrating few-shot learning capabilities via ICL from human play videos, MIMICDROID moves closer to the vision of generalist humanoid robots that can rapidly adapt to novel tasks, objects, and environments. This is crucial for deploying robots in diverse, unstructured settings like homes, where pre-programming every scenario is infeasible.
2. **Scalable Data Acquisition for Robot Learning**: The reliance on human play videos as the sole training data source addresses a critical bottleneck in robot learning: the prohibitive cost and limited diversity of teleoperated robot data. Human play videos are abundant, inexpensive to collect, and naturally diverse, offering a pathway to build much larger and richer datasets for training highly capable robotic policies. This paradigm shift could accelerate research and development in robot learning.
3. **Advancement in In-Context Learning for Robotics**: MIMICDROID extends the success of ICL, primarily seen in large language models, to the complex domain of visuomotor control for physical robots. It provides a novel methodology for self-supervised construction of ICL training samples from raw, unlabeled video data, a technique that could be transferable to other forms of passive human observation.
4. **Bridging the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap**: The effective strategies for kinematic retargeting and visual masking are crucial contributions for cross-embodiment transfer. They offer practical solutions for translating human-centric knowledge into robot-executable actions, an enduring challenge in imitation learning. The simplicity and effectiveness of random patch masking are particularly notable for real-world application.
5. **New Research Benchmark**: The introduction of a systematic, open-source simulation benchmark for few-shot humanoid manipulation is invaluable for the research community. It provides a standardized platform with clear generalization levels (L1, L2, L3) to evaluate and compare future methods, fostering reproducible and impactful research.
6. **Insights into Scaling Robot Learning**: The results demonstrating consistent performance improvements with increasing training data highlight the potential of data scaling from human videos. The analysis of context length limitations and optimal context retrieval strategies offers important insights for designing more effective ICL models in robotics.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Faster Robot Deployment**: Robots could be deployed and adapted to new household appliances or tasks much faster, reducing the need for expert programmers.
- **Wider Adoption of Humanoids**: As humanoids become more adaptable and easier to train, their commercial viability and widespread adoption in various service industries (e.g., logistics, elderly care) could significantly increase.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: MIMICDROID lays the groundwork for several exciting future directions, including leveraging web-scale human videos, integrating language for semantic understanding, and developing more sophisticated full-body motion estimation techniques. The insights into failure modes (e.g., novel motions for L3, overfitting to hand sizes) provide clear targets for subsequent innovations.

In conclusion, MIMICDROID offers a compelling vision for adaptive robot assistants in everyday environments, underpinned by a robust methodology that effectively harnesses the abundance of human interaction data. Its contributions promise to accelerate the development of truly intelligent and versatile humanoid robots.
