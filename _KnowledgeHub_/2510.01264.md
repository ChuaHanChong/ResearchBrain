# A Framework for Scalable Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Adversarial Reinforcement Learning in IsaacLab

> [!summary] Summary
> HARL-Adversarial (HARL-A) is presented as an extended IsaacLab framework for scalable heterogeneous multi-agent adversarial policy training in high-fidelity physics simulations. The framework successfully trains robust policies that achieve high win rates and demonstrate emergent specialized adversarial behaviors across diverse robotic morphologies.

## Problem

- Existing Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) frameworks lack robust support for heterogeneous agents in adversarial settings within high-fidelity physics simulations.
- Training instability and ineffective policy learning often arise in adversarial settings due to co-evolving agents and the collapse of value estimates with shared critics in zero-sum games.
- Real-world robotic applications demand agents capable of anticipating and counteracting opponent strategies, which current frameworks often fail to adequately address with heterogeneous agent complexities.

## Method

- Developed HARL-Adversarial (HARL-A) by extending the IsaacLab simulator and integrating competitive variants of HARL algorithms to support scalable multi-agent heterogeneous adversarial learning.
- Introduced team-specific critics within the framework to ensure agents receive non-trivial advantage signals aligned with their individual or team rewards, overcoming the critic collapse issue in zero-sum games.
- Implemented a suite of benchmark adversarial environments (e.g., Sumo, Soccer, 3D Galaga) featuring heterogeneous robots and multi-stage curriculum learning with a zero-buffer strategy for observations.

## Results

- Adversarial policies consistently achieved higher win rates against untrained opponents across various heterogeneous environments, demonstrating successful learning of effective competitive strategies.
- Emergent adversarial behaviors, such as targeting opponent vulnerabilities (e.g., Leatherback targeting Anymal legs) and strategic maneuvers (e.g., Anymal dragging rovers), were observed among heterogeneous agents.
- The zero-buffer strategy, while initially slowing early convergence, enabled seamless expansion of the state space in later curriculum stages, facilitating policy transfer and accelerating overall curriculum progression.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Team-specific critics are crucial for stabilizing adversarial training in heterogeneous multi-agent competitive environments, preventing value function collapse and ensuring effective policy updates.
> - High-fidelity adversarial multi-agent training can induce emergent specialized behaviors and dynamic role specialization among heterogeneous agents without explicit programming.
> - A curriculum learning approach, combined with a zero-buffer observation strategy, allows for seamless state space expansion and accelerates training efficiency across progressively complex adversarial tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has significantly advanced the training of robots for complex skills, including locomotion and advanced behaviors in humanoids and quadrupeds. Building on this, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has shown potential for robotic coordination and control, facilitated by GPU-parallelized simulators like IsaacLab and MuJoCo.

However, the existing MARL landscape presents several challenges. While cooperative settings for heterogeneous teams and physics-based training have been explored, a notable gap exists in the domain of *adversarial* multi-agent reinforcement learning, particularly concerning heterogeneous agents in high-fidelity physics simulations. Prior research in adversarial RL has progressed from early demonstrations of self-play in physics-based environments (e.g., MuJoCo) to large-scale competitive frameworks (e.g., OpenAI Five, AlphaStar) and environments demonstrating emergent tool use and strategy (e.g., MuJoCo hide-and-seek). Advances in multi-agent extensions include algorithms like multi-agent deep deterministic policy gradient (MADDPG) for coordination in mixed cooperative-competitive environments. More recent work has focused on realism and team play in continuous control, leveraging high-fidelity simulators for tasks like humanoid soccer and robot soccer in IsaacLab.

Despite these developments, a unified framework capable of supporting scalable, heterogeneous, adversarial MARL in high-fidelity simulators has been identified as lacking. Many prior works tend to focus on either pure cooperation or simplified adversarial tasks without incorporating heterogeneous morphologies. Additionally, existing implementations are often isolated rather than presented as extensible frameworks. This paper addresses this gap by introducing an extended IsaacLab framework designed to facilitate heterogeneous adversarial MARL training, provide benchmark environments, and support the development of robust algorithms under competitive dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to extend the IsaacLab framework to support scalable training of adversarial policies involving heterogeneous agents within high-fidelity physics simulations. This work aims to establish a unified platform for studying morphology-diverse multi-agent adversarial competition.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations regarding the limitations of existing MARL frameworks:

- **Real-World Application Relevance:** Many practical robotics applications involve competitive or adversarial interactions rather than solely cooperative ones. Scenarios such as pursuit-evasion, security, and competitive manipulation necessitate agents that can anticipate and counteract opponent strategies. Current frameworks often do not adequately address these adversarial dynamics, particularly in high-fidelity, contact-rich environments where accurate physics simulation is essential.
- **Heterogeneous Agent Complexity:** Real-world robotic teams frequently comprise agents with diverse morphologies, capabilities, observations, and actions (e.g., legged robots competing with wheeled platforms). This heterogeneity introduces significant complexity, as physical differences lead to specialized strategies and require more sophisticated training paradigms than homogeneous agent settings. The concept of "heterogeneous teaming" is defined as situations where teams can have different numbers of agents, with differences in morphologies, observations, and actions.
- **Challenges in Adversarial Training:** Adversarial learning settings introduce specific technical challenges:
 - **Training Instability:** The co-evolution of competing agents can lead to unstable learning dynamics.
 - **Critic Design for Heterogeneity:** Standard parameter-sharing approaches for critics are insufficient for heterogeneous teams in adversarial settings. A single shared critic in a zero-sum game can lead to its value estimate collapsing to zero, resulting in vanishing advantage estimates and ineffective gradient signals for policy updates. Team-specific critics are required to ensure agents receive meaningful advantage signals aligned with their individual or team rewards.
 - **Reward Design:** Crafting effective reward functions for physics-based adversarial tasks is complex, requiring a balance between dense shaping rewards for guiding behavior and sparse success signals to prevent unintended strategies.

To address these motivations and challenges, the paper outlines three main contributions:

1. **Framework Modification:** Modifying both the Heterogeneous Agent Reinforcement Learning (HARL) library and the IsaacLab simulator to enable scalable multi-agent heterogeneous adversarial learning.
2. **Environment and Policy Implementation:** Implementing functional benchmark environments and developing trained policies for heterogeneous adversarial learning, thereby facilitating accelerated research in this domain.
3. **New Benchmarks:** Introducing novel benchmarks for testing MARL algorithms in high-fidelity adversarial settings, which is intended to promote the development of more robust algorithms capable of handling competitive dynamics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops a framework called HARL-Adversarial (HARL-A) by extending the IsaacLab simulator and integrating competitive variants of HARL algorithms.

#### 3.1. A. Framework Design

The developed framework builds upon a prior HARL implementation in IsaacLab that enabled cooperative heterogeneous multi-agent policy training. A core limitation identified in the cooperative setting, which becomes critical in adversarial scenarios, was the use of a single critic shared across all agents. In zero-sum adversarial settings where team rewards are strictly coupled ($r^{(0)}_t = -r^{(1)}_t$), a single critic's value estimate (V(s)) would approximate the average outcome, effectively collapsing to zero. This leads to vanishing advantage estimates ($A_t = R_t - V(s_t)$) and a degenerate PPO surrogate loss, hindering effective policy learning.

To overcome this, the framework introduces **team-specific critics**. Each team's critic learns a value function $V^{(i)}(s)$ aligned with its own reward $r^{(i)}$, ensuring that heterogeneous agents in competitive environments receive non-trivial advantage signals. This approach is consistent with the Heterogeneous Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (HAPPO) formulation. This modification to IsaacLab enables the training of both single-agent and multi-agent adversarial policies, expanding the range of achievable training paradigms (Figure 2b and 2d).

#### 3.2. B. Environment Development

A suite of adversarial environments was developed within IsaacLab to evaluate the framework's capabilities, incorporating heterogeneous robots, competitive objectives, and curriculum learning.

- **Sumo Environment:** Inspired by prior adversarial self-play domains, a Sumo environment was implemented to feature both homogeneous and heterogeneous teams. The heterogeneous setup specifically uses the Anymal C quadruped and the Leatherback rover to highlight interactions between robots with distinct morphologies and control spaces. While experiments focused on two agents per team, the framework supports arbitrary team sizes.

 - **Curriculum Learning for Adversarial Training:** To stabilize learning in complex tasks, a three-stage curriculum learning approach was employed, progressively increasing task difficulty while maintaining a consistent observation space using a zero-buffer strategy. Placeholder zeros in initial observations are later replaced with meaningful features, allowing seamless policy transfer.
 1. **Sumo Stage 1: Walk To Point:** Agents learn basic locomotion by navigating to a goal, rewarded for reaching the goal and penalized by distance.
 2. **Sumo Stage 2: Block Pushing:** Each robot learns to push a block out of the arena. Rewards are based on the block's distance from the arena center, the robot-block distance, a step penalty, and sparse event terms for pushing the block out or leaving the ring.
 3. **Sumo Stage 3: Adversarial Sumo:** The block is removed, and robots directly compete. Each team $i \in \{0, 1\}$ receives a reward based on eliminating the opponent (forcing them out of the ring) while remaining in the arena. Ties and timeouts yield no reward, incentivizing strategies to destabilize opponents.

- **Other Benchmark Environments:**
 - **Soccer:** This environment involves 1 vs 1 heterogeneous scenarios (e.g., Anymal vs. Leatherback) with morphology-appropriate observations (robot-ball, ball-goal vectors, velocities, poses) and actions. It uses a two-stage curriculum: Stage 1 for single-robot ball approach/driving skills, and Stage 2 for a two-team adversarial match with scoring rewards and penalties. Training for this scenario specifically used an alternating "leapfrog" strategy, where one team's actor is frozen while the other is updated, then roles switch.
 - **3D Galaga:** An aerial-ground interception task involving a team of two MiniTanks and two Crazy Fly quadrotor drones in an arena. Drones aim for a goal, while tanks "laser-tag" drones using arm-aligned ray casts. This environment demonstrates the framework's capability with teams composed of homogeneous morphologies playing a heterogeneous adversarial task. It is a single-stage adversarial play environment, showcasing transfer and emergent competence without online opponent-aware fine-tuning.

#### 3.3. C. Training Regimes and Evaluation

Simulations were conducted on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 30- and 40-series GPUs. Two adversarial training regimes were explored:
1. **Alternating Training:** One team's actor is frozen for a fixed number of timesteps while the other is updated, then roles are switched. This typically stabilized learning curves.
2. **Simultaneous Training:** Both teams are trained concurrently. This also produced meaningful behaviors, indicating the framework's robustness to different optimization strategies.

Evaluation of policy improvement was primarily conducted by measuring the **win rate** of the trained policy against its initialization, a common methodology in adversarial training. Each trained policy was evaluated across 1000 environment instances per episode.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across the developed environments demonstrated the framework's ability to model and train robust policies for morphologically diverse multi-agent competition while maintaining high throughput and simulation realism.

#### 4.1. A. Policy Performance and Win Rate Improvement

Across various environments, adversarial policies consistently achieved higher win rates over time compared to their initial, untrained versions (Figure 8). This confirmed that the agents learned effective strategies. For instance, in the Sumo scenario, with the exception of trained Leatherbacks against untrained Anymals, the framework generally demonstrated increased win rates across different training runs. This indicates effective adversarial learning within the HARL-A framework, whether teams were trained simultaneously or using alternating updates.

#### 4.2. B. Emergent Adversarial Behaviors

Beyond quantitative performance improvements, the training process resulted in the emergence of diverse and specialized adversarial behaviors, particularly evident in the heterogeneous Sumo environment (Figure 6).
- **Targeting Vulnerabilities:** Leatherback rovers frequently learned to destabilize opposing Anymal robots by specifically targeting and pulling their legs.
- **Strategic Maneuvers:** Anymal robots developed dragging maneuvers to pull rovers out of the arena.
- **Role Specialization:** Heterogeneous teams exhibited natural role specialization, where each morphology leveraged its unique physical strengths (e.g., rovers acting as disruptors, Anymals as grapplers). This occurred without explicit role assignment, suggesting that adversarial learning can induce a division of labor in such settings, a phenomenon previously observed in large-scale self-play systems but now demonstrated in a high-fidelity robotic context.
- **Stochasticity:** The prevalence of these emergent behaviors was observed to depend on random initialization, highlighting the stochastic nature of adversarial learning processes.
- **Soccer Specifics:** In the 1 vs 1 soccer scenario, Leatherbacks learned effective strategies against Anymal robots, which were initially more dominant (Figure 7), indicating successful adaptation to heterogeneous opponents.

#### 4.3. C. Curriculum Learning and Zero-Buffer Analysis

The study analyzed the effect of curriculum learning and the zero-buffer mechanism on training efficiency.
- **Trade-off:** As shown in Figure 9, padding observations with unused zero-valued features (zero-buffer) initially slowed convergence during the early stages of curriculum learning.
- **Seamless Expansion:** However, the zero-buffer strategy enabled the seamless expansion of the state space in later curriculum stages without requiring policies to be retrained from scratch. This design choice allowed for incremental task expansion, accelerating the overall curriculum progression by facilitating policy transfer across stages. This validated the utility of modular observation spaces for scalable adversarial training.

#### 4.4. D. Summary of Findings

The HARL-A framework successfully implemented and trained adversarial heterogeneous teams, and several key components were identified as improving the training process:
- Both alternating and simultaneous training regimes produced effective adversarial strategies, albeit with differing stability profiles.
- Heterogeneous teams demonstrated emergent role specialization, capitalizing on their morphological differences.
- Curriculum learning, combined with a zero-buffer approach for observations, proved to be a practical mechanism for iterative adversarial training across various tasks.

These findings collectively indicate that adversarial training in high-fidelity heterogeneous environments not only enhances raw performance but also gives rise to complex emergent behaviors and coordination patterns, demonstrating the framework's utility and novelty.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces HARL-A, a unified framework for scalable heterogeneous adversarial reinforcement learning within IsaacLab. By extending existing cooperative heterogeneous pipelines with team-specific critics, the framework enables efficient training of both homogeneous and heterogeneous agents in competitive dynamics. This represents the first high-fidelity platform specifically designed for studying morphology-diverse multi-agent adversarial competition.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:

- **Bridging the Simulation-to-Reality Gap:** By operating within IsaacLab, a high-fidelity physics simulator, HARL-A provides a platform that is intended to bridge the gap between abstract MARL benchmarks and embodied multi-robot systems. This is crucial for developing policies that are robust and transferable to real-world robotic deployments where contact dynamics, asymmetry, and role differentiation are intrinsic.
- **Advancing Adversarial MARL Research:** The framework and its benchmark environments offer a tool for researchers to develop and test new MARL algorithms in complex, competitive scenarios. It highlights the importance of evaluating adversarial MARL beyond simplified domains, focusing on contexts that mirror real-world robotic interactions.
- **Understanding Emergent Behaviors:** The observed emergent adversarial strategies, such as targeting specific vulnerabilities and role specialization among heterogeneous agents, contribute to a deeper understanding of multi-agent intelligence in competitive settings. This phenomenon of induced division of labor without explicit programming is of interest to both robotics and general multi-agent learning research.
- **Enabling Robust Algorithm Development:** By providing a scalable framework for adversarial training, HARL-A aims to catalyze the development of more robust and generalizable algorithms. These algorithms could be less susceptible to exploitation and perform reliably in dynamic, uncertain environments.

**Potential Impact:**

The implications of HARL-A extend to various real-world applications:
- **Security and Defense:** Developing advanced robotic systems for tasks like pursuit-evasion, surveillance, and competitive manipulation, where autonomous agents must operate effectively against intelligent adversaries.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** Improving the adaptability and safety of multi-agent systems by ensuring they can anticipate and respond to diverse adversarial challenges.
- **Competitive Robotics:** Providing a testbed for competitive robotics, enabling the training of agents that can outperform opponents in diverse physical challenges.

**Future Directions highlighted by the authors:**
The authors suggest several avenues for future research, including integrating advanced MARL algorithms (e.g., value-decomposition methods, graph attention networks, off-policy methods), developing richer evaluation methodologies (e.g., exploitability, cross-play, robustness against novel opponents), exploring more sophisticated curriculum learning strategies (e.g., value disagreement, gradual domain adaptation), and extending HARL-A to new modalities (e.g., aerial, aquatic, swarm agents) and multi-scale interactions. These directions indicate a continuous effort to enhance the framework's capabilities and address increasingly complex adversarial scenarios.

By lowering the barrier to scalable adversarial training in high-fidelity simulation, HARL-A serves as both a research tool and a benchmark suite, aiming to advance the safety, adaptability, and real-world deployment of multi-agent learning.
