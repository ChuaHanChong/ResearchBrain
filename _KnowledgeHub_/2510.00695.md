# HAMLET: Switch your Vision-Language-Action Model into a History-Aware Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> The HAMLET framework integrates history-awareness into pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, employing compact moment tokens and a lightweight memory module to efficiently process past observations. This approach leads to an average 47.2% improvement in success rates on real-world long-horizon tasks, while introducing only minimal computational overhead (e.g., 1.02x latency, 1.96x peak memory for 4 history frames).

## Problem

- Most existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models rely on a single-frame assumption for action prediction, which fundamentally restricts their capabilities on history-dependent robotic manipulation tasks.
- Complex robotic tasks often involve object occlusion, multi-step reasoning, or conditional actions that necessitate access to historical context, making current VLA policies prone to failure or inefficiency.
- Naively incorporating history by concatenating multiple past observation frames into VLA inputs leads to substantial computational overhead, increasing forward pass time by approximately 35% and peak memory consumption by 3.6 times for just four additional frames.

## Method

- A framework called **HAMLET** (History-Aware Memory with LEarned Tokens) is proposed as a fine-tuning module that integrates into pre-trained VLAs to efficiently incorporate historical context.
- Context compression is achieved using "moment tokens" which are learnable tokens appended to the VLM input sequence, summarizing the current observation and task instruction into a compact, task-relevant representation.
- A lightweight, shallow ==Transformer==-based memory module aggregates these moment tokens across past timesteps, consolidating historical information into a "memory feature" which is then used to augment the VLA's action prediction.

## Results

- HAMLET achieved an average **47.2%** improvement in success rate on real-world history-dependent long-horizon tasks (e.g., **66.7%** vs **12.5%** on Pick-and-Place Twice) compared to the GR00T N1.5 baseline.
- It incurred minimal computational overhead, with approximately **1.02x** latency and **1.96x** peak memory increase for a history length of 4, significantly outperforming the naive multi-frame baseline (**1.35x** latency, **3.64x** memory).
- Performance consistently improved across various state-of-the-art VLA backbones (GR00T N1.5, CogACT, OpenVLA, π0-FAST) and simulation benchmarks (RoboCasa, LIBERO, SimplerEnv-Bridge), with gains up to an **8.8%** improvement over strong baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Time-Contrastive Learning (TCL) effectively initializes moment tokens to capture temporally discriminative cues, filtering out static elements and focusing attention on task-relevant, changing regions of the scene.
> - A dedicated memory module, rather than simple concatenation of past observations, is crucial for effectively integrating historical context, enabling selective attention to important past events.
> - The learned memory representations and the memory module itself exhibit generalizability, demonstrating transferability across different robot manipulation datasets and embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic policy learning has seen significant advancements with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models leverage large-scale pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and diverse robot-specific datasets to learn generalist robot policies, bridging semantic understanding with low-level action prediction. Early VLAs, such as those by Zitkovich et al. (2023), Kim et al. (2024), and Driess et al. (2023), often discretized the continuous action space and predicted actions as tokens. More recent approaches, including those by Black et al. (2025), Bjorck et al. (2025b), and Li et al. (2024a), utilize VLM representations to condition action experts, frequently employing diffusion or flow-matching techniques for more precise action prediction.

A pervasive limitation across most existing VLA models is their reliance on a single-frame assumption, where actions are predicted solely from the current observation. This approach fundamentally restricts their capabilities, as many robotic manipulation tasks are intrinsically history-dependent. For instance, tasks involving object occlusion, multi-step reasoning, or conditional actions based on previous states (e.g., whether an object has been grasped) necessitate access to historical context. Without this context, policies may struggle to determine appropriate next actions, leading to failures or inefficiencies.

While incorporating history-awareness is recognized as a desirable property, it traditionally entails substantial computational overhead if implemented through naive multi-frame input concatenation. The paper cites observations where appending only four additional past observation frames can increase forward pass time by approximately 35% and peak memory consumption by about 3.6 times. This inflated memory footprint further limits scalability by reducing feasible batch sizes.

The broader research landscape for memory architectures in artificial intelligence has explored various solutions for long-horizon or partially observable problems. In reinforcement learning, recurrent policies (Hausknecht & Stone, 2015) and Transformer-based variants (Parisotto et al., 2020) have introduced memory mechanisms. Similarly, natural language processing has extensively utilized explicit memory architectures, from end-to-end memory networks (Sukhbaatar et al., 2015) to retrieval-based systems (Khandelwal et al., 2019; Lewis et al., 2020), to enhance long-context reasoning. In robotics, prior works (Wen et al., 2020; Seo et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b; Huang et al., 2025) have incorporated past observations into policy learning. However, many of these approaches rely on simpler architectures (e.g., MLPs or RNNs) with limited generalizability or necessitate training from scratch, incurring significant computational costs. Concurrent work, such as that by Shi et al. (2025), investigates human memory-inspired architectures, also trained from scratch.

This work positions itself to address the gap between the recognized need for history-awareness in robotics and the high computational cost of integrating it into large-scale pre-trained VLAs. The key research question guiding this work is how to integrate history-awareness into pre-trained VLAs efficiently, without requiring costly pre-training from scratch. The proposed framework, HAMLET, seeks to provide a scalable, plug-in solution that leverages historical context while maintaining computational efficiency and generalizability across diverse VLA backbones.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a recognized limitation in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: their predominant reliance on single-frame observations for action prediction. This design choice fundamentally restricts their effectiveness in robotic manipulation tasks that are intrinsically history-dependent. Robotic tasks often exhibit non-Markovian properties, meaning that optimal actions cannot be determined solely from the instantaneous sensory input but require reasoning over a sequence of past events.

Specific scenarios highlighting this limitation include:
- **Object Occlusion:** A robot tasked with manipulating an object might lose sight of it temporarily. If the policy only considers the current frame, it may not recall the object's last known position or previous interactions, leading to task failure.
- **Multi-step Reasoning:** Complex tasks involve a sequence of operations where the success of a later step depends on the correct execution and state changes from earlier steps. For instance, in a "swap cubes" task, the robot needs to remember which cube was moved first and its new location to correctly execute the subsequent steps.
- **Conditional Actions:** An action might be contingent on a preceding event. The example of placing an object on a table requires knowing whether the object has already been grasped. Without historical context, the policy might attempt to grasp an already-grasped object or release a non-existent one.

The overarching problem identified is that despite the clear benefits of history-awareness, its incorporation into large-scale pre-trained VLAs is computationally challenging. Naively extending VLA inputs with multiple past frames leads to significant increases in computational latency and memory consumption, thereby hindering scalability and practical deployment. This observation forms a critical research question: How can history-awareness be effectively and efficiently integrated into *pre-trained* VLAs without incurring the prohibitive costs associated with training entirely new models from scratch?

The key objectives of this research, driven by this motivation, are as follows:
1. **Develop a Scalable Framework:** Propose a framework, HAMLET, that adapts existing pre-trained VLAs to attend to historical context during action prediction in a computationally efficient manner.
2. **Compact Context Encoding:** Introduce "moment tokens" as a mechanism to compactly encode perceptual information from each timestep. These tokens are designed to summarize instantaneous VLM representations, preserving task-relevant details while filtering out redundant or static information from raw observations.
3. **Temporally Informed Action Prediction:** Design a lightweight memory module that integrates these moment tokens across past timesteps. This module is intended to consolidate historical information into a "memory feature" that can then be leveraged to enhance action prediction by the VLA's action expert.
4. **Enhance Performance on History-Dependent Tasks:** Demonstrate that HAMLET can significantly improve the success rates of state-of-the-art VLAs, particularly on long-horizon real-world tasks that necessitate historical context and on generic robot manipulation benchmarks.
5. **Ensure Generality and Plug-in Compatibility:** Ensure the proposed framework is backbone-agnostic, allowing seamless and efficient integration into diverse pre-trained VLAs without requiring extensive additional large-scale pre-training or modifications to the underlying VLA architecture.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to enable VLAs to achieve more robust and reliable control in complex, non-Markovian robotic manipulation scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, HAMLET (History-Aware Memory with LEarned Tokens), is designed as a fine-tuning module that integrates into pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to incorporate historical context efficiently. The approach augments the standard VLA pipeline, which typically processes a current observation \(o_t\) and a task instruction \(c\) to generate a hidden representation \(h_t\), subsequently used by an action expert \(A_\psi\) to predict a chunk of future actions \([a_t, \dots, a_{t+k-1}]\). HAMLET's goal is to enrich \(h_t\) with informative representations from previous timesteps. This is achieved through two complementary components: moment tokens and a memory module.

#### 3.1. Context Compression via Moment Tokens

The first challenge in leveraging historical context is the inefficiency of storing and processing raw past observations. Raw visual data is high-dimensional, computationally expensive, and often contains redundant information (e.g., static backgrounds). HAMLET addresses this by compressing each timestep's observation into a concise, task-relevant representation called "moment tokens."

- **Moment Token Mechanism:** At each timestep \(t\), a set of learnable moment tokens \(m_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n_m \times d}\) (where \(n_m\) is the number of tokens and \(d\) is the embedding dimension) is appended to the input sequence of the VLM. The combined input, \([o_t, c; m_t]\), is fed into the VLM encoder \(F_\theta\). The VLM produces a hidden representation \([h_t; m'_t]\), where \(m'_t\) represents the context-aware summary learned by the moment tokens. Due to the causal attention mechanisms within the VLM, \(m'_t\) effectively attends to the current visual observation \(o_t\) and the task instruction \(c\), thereby serving as a compact representation of the scene at timestep \(t\).

- **Time-Contrastive Learning (TCL) Initialization:** To ensure that moment tokens effectively capture temporally discriminative cues and filter out static elements, they are initialized using a time-contrastive learning objective.
 - **Objective:** The goal is to make representations of augmented views of the same timestep similar (positive pairs) while making representations from different timesteps distinct (hard negative pairs).
 - **Process:** For a given trajectory, moment token representations \(m'_t\) are extracted using the VLM. An anchor is formed from the current observation \(o_t\). A positive sample \(z^+_t\) is generated from an augmented view of \(o_t\), applying photometric distortions (brightness, contrast, color jitter), Gaussian blur/noise, and small-patch occlusions. A hard negative sample \(z^-_t\) is derived from a different timestep \(t' \ne t\) within the same trajectory, specifically chosen to be more than 16 timesteps apart.
 - **Loss Function:** An InfoNCE-like contrastive loss, \(L_{TCL}\), is optimized on the projected moment-token representations \(z_t = g(m'_t)\).
 - **VLM Freezing:** During this TCL initialization phase, the VLM \(F_\theta\) is frozen to prevent the contrastive loss from distorting its pre-trained representations. This ensures that the VLM's general visual-language understanding remains intact while the moment tokens learn to extract relevant temporal features.

#### 3.2. Memory Consolidation via Memory Module

The second component, a lightweight memory module, is responsible for aggregating the compressed moment token representations (\(m'_t\)) across multiple past timesteps. Simple concatenation of these tokens has been shown to be ineffective, motivating a more selective aggregation mechanism.

- **Memory Module Design:** A shallow Transformer architecture \(M_\phi\) is employed for this purpose. The Transformer uses causal self-attention to selectively attend over past moment tokens.
 - **History Matrix:** A history matrix \(M'\) is constructed by stacking the most recent \(T\) moment tokens: \(M' = [m'_{t-k(T-1)}; \dots; m'_{t-k}; m'_t]\), where \(k\) is the action-chunk length and \(T\) is the history length. The total number of tokens in \(M'\) is \(L = T \cdot n_m\).
 - **Self-Attention:** The memory module applies standard self-attention to \(M'\), using learned query, key, and value projections (\(W_q, W_k, W_v\)) and incorporating a causal mask \(C\) to prevent future information leakage.
 - **Output:** The output of the Transformer, \(\tilde{M}' \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}\), is then processed to extract the history-augmented moment token representation for the current timestep \(t\). This is done by taking the last \(n_m\) rows of \(\tilde{M}'\), denoted \(\tilde{m}'_t\). This \(\tilde{m}'_t\) effectively summarizes the most relevant historical context as processed by the memory module.

#### 3.3. Integration into Action Prediction

Finally, the history-augmented feature \(\tilde{m}'_t\) is concatenated with the original VLM representation \(h_t\). This combined, enriched representation, \([h_t; \tilde{m}'_t]\), is then fed into the VLA's action expert \(A_\psi\) along with the robot's proprioceptive state \(s_t\) to predict the next chunk of actions: \([a_t, a_{t+1}, \dots, a_{t+k-1}] = A_\psi([h_t; \tilde{m}'_t], s_t)\).

The overall training procedure follows that of standard VLA models, with the entire pipeline (VLM, moment tokens, memory module, and action expert) trained end-to-end using the appropriate action prediction loss (e.g., as used in Bjorck et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2024a; Black et al., 2025). Default hyperparameters include a moment token length of 4, a 2-layer Transformer for the memory module, and a history length of 4.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HAMLET was conducted across real-world and simulation environments to assess its effectiveness, generality, and efficiency.

#### 4.1. Real-World Evaluation

- **Tasks:** Three history-dependent long-horizon tasks were designed: Pick-and-Place Twice, Cover-and-Stack, and Swap Cubes. These tasks explicitly require temporal reasoning and memory.
- **Performance:** HAMLET, when applied to GR00T N1.5, significantly improved performance. The base GR00T N1.5 achieved an average success rate of 29.2%, struggling with multi-step dependencies and ambiguous states. HAMLET boosted this to 76.4%, an average improvement of 47.2% across the tasks. For example, on "Pick-and-Place Twice," HAMLET achieved 66.7% success compared to 12.5% for the baseline.
- **Qualitative Observations:** The base GR00T N1.5 frequently exhibited confusion (e.g., moving in the wrong direction, failing to identify which object was under a cup), while HAMLET consistently predicted appropriate actions by leveraging historical context.

#### 4.2. Simulation Benchmarks

- **RoboCasa Kitchen and LIBERO (with GR00T N1.5):**
 - The naive multi-frame baseline, which concatenates past observation frames, degraded performance compared to the single-frame GR00T N1.5. Specifically, it saw a 3.3% drop on RoboCasa (100 demos) and an 8.8% drop on LIBERO. This was attributed to generalization difficulties in dynamic environments and potential causal confusion from spurious temporal correlations.
 - In contrast, HAMLET consistently improved performance: an average success rate of 66.4% on RoboCasa (100 demos) compared to 64.1% for the baseline, and 97.6% on LIBERO compared to 95.6% for the baseline. HAMLET pushed LIBERO's prior best score of 95.6% to near-saturation.
- **SimplerEnv-Bridge (with CogACT):**
 - HAMLET demonstrated clear improvements when applied to the CogACT VLA backbone. It achieved an average full success rate of 63.5% across four tasks, substantially outperforming the original CogACT's 52.1%. The multi-frame baseline again did not consistently improve performance (47.9%).
- **Autoregressive VLAs:** HAMLET's effectiveness extended to autoregressive VLAs like OpenVLA and π0-FAST. It showed a 9.0% gain on LIBERO-Long for OpenVLA and a 5.8% gain on Pick-and-Place tasks in RoboCasa for π0-FAST.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Analysis

- **Computational Overhead:** Compared to vanilla GR00T N1.5 inference, the multi-frame baseline introduced significant overhead: ~1.35x latency and ~3.64x peak memory for a history length of 4, escalating to ~2.40x latency and ~7.00x memory for a history length of 8.
- **HAMLET's Efficiency:** HAMLET incurred minimal overhead: ~1.02x latency and ~1.96x memory for a history length of 4, and ~1.07x latency and ~2.00x memory for a history length of 8. This demonstrates HAMLET's design for efficiency.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Component Analysis:** Removing the memory module resulted in the largest performance drop, confirming its critical role. The Time-Contrastive Learning (TCL) initialization also consistently contributed to performance gains.
- **Moment Token Length:** Performance improved as moment token length increased up to 8, then gradually decreased, suggesting an optimal balance between compression and information retention.
- **Memory Architecture:** A Transformer-based memory module achieved the highest average success rate (65.4%) compared to no memory (62.6%), naive moment concatenation (62.7%), RNN (64.5%), LSTM (65.0%), and GRU (64.3%). Simple moment concatenation yielded almost no gains.

#### 4.5. Generalization Capability of Memory Module

- A memory module trained on the LIBERO dataset, when frozen and transferred directly to RoboCasa, achieved a success rate (64.5%) comparable to a module trained and evaluated on RoboCasa (65.4%). This suggests that the learned memory representations can generalize across different robot manipulation datasets and embodiments.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Analysis (Memory Content)

- **Moment Tokens:** Visualization of self-attention maps showed that TCL-initialized moment tokens concentrated attention on task-relevant regions (e.g., gripper, objects) that undergo changes across timesteps, effectively suppressing attention to static background elements.
- **Memory Module:** The memory module demonstrated selective attention to past information. In tasks like "Cover-and-Stack," it assigned higher attention to the timestep where a cube was covered when deciding which cup to approach next. In "Swap Cubes," it attended to the past timestep when a specific cube was placed down before determining the next grasping action, indicating contextual recall. Attention levels were lower when historical information was less critical.
- **Longer Horizon Tasks:** On "Pick-and-Place Three Times," HAMLET showed significant improvements (37.5% vs 8.3%), even when the explicit history window was shorter than the task's temporal dependency, suggesting that the causal nature of the Transformer effectively integrates and propagates information across steps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented on HAMLET offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation and generalist robot policies:

1. **Addressing a Fundamental Limitation:** HAMLET directly confronts the single-frame dependency prevalent in most contemporary Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This limitation has historically hindered VLAs from effectively addressing intrinsically history-dependent robotic manipulation tasks, which are common in real-world scenarios. By enabling VLAs to leverage past context, the framework enhances their ability to reason through complex, non-Markovian problems.

2. **Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** A key challenge in integrating history-awareness is the prohibitive computational overhead. HAMLET provides a solution that is both efficient and scalable. The use of compact "moment tokens" and a lightweight memory module allows for history-aware action prediction with minimal increases in latency and memory consumption, especially compared to naive multi-frame baselines. This efficiency is critical for deploying advanced robotic policies in real-world applications where computational resources may be constrained.

3. **Enhanced Performance on Complex Tasks:** The empirical evaluations demonstrate substantial performance improvements, particularly on long-horizon, history-dependent tasks in real-world settings. A 47.2% average improvement on custom real-world tasks over the GR00T N1.5 baseline highlights HAMLET's capability to make VLAs more robust and reliable in practical applications involving object occlusion, multi-step reasoning, and conditional actions. Consistent gains on generic simulation benchmarks like RoboCasa and LIBERO further underscore its broad applicability.

4. **Generality and Plug-and-Play Integration:** HAMLET is designed as a backbone-agnostic framework, demonstrated by its successful application and performance enhancements across diverse pre-trained VLA models, including GR00T N1.5, CogACT, OpenVLA, and π0-FAST. This "plug-in" nature eliminates the need for costly re-training of the entire VLA from scratch, offering a practical and flexible way to upgrade existing policies.

5. **Generalizable Memory Representations:** The finding that the learned memory module can transfer effectively between different datasets (e.g., from LIBERO to RoboCasa) suggests that HAMLET learns generalizable knowledge about what historical information is important. This transferability can reduce the training burden for adapting VLA policies to new tasks or environments, enhancing the framework's practical utility.

6. **Qualitative Insights into Temporal Reasoning:** The qualitative analysis provides insights into how HAMLET operates. The moment tokens' ability to selectively attend to task-relevant, temporally changing regions and the memory module's capacity for contextual recall (e.g., remembering specific past events to inform future actions) demonstrate a more sophisticated form of temporal reasoning than observed in single-frame models.

In conclusion, HAMLET represents a step towards making generalist robot policies more capable and practical by endowing them with history-awareness without sacrificing efficiency. Its impact could extend to enabling robots to perform more complex, adaptable, and reliable manipulation tasks in unstructured and dynamic environments, thereby advancing the state of the art in embodied AI and robotic control. The framework's modularity and demonstrated performance gains suggest it could become a standard enhancement for next-generation VLA models.
