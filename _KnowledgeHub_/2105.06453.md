# Episodic Transformer for Vision-and-Language Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Inria, Google Research, and Brown University introduced the Episodic Transformer (E.T.) architecture, which processes full episode histories and leverages synthetic instructions to enhance Vision-and-Language Navigation. This approach improved task success rates on the ALFRED benchmark, establishing new state-of-the-art performance, particularly for generalization to unseen environments.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) models struggle with highly compositional tasks requiring agents to remember past events or locations over long action sequences.
- Complex human instructions for VLN tasks are often ambiguous, context-dependent, and highly variable, making it difficult for agents to generalize.

## Method

- The **Episodic Transformer (E.T.)** architecture was developed, a ==multimodal transformer== that processes the complete history of visual observations and actions alongside language instructions without explicit recurrence.
- Synthetic instructions, based on ==Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL)==-derived subgoal actions, were generated as an unambiguous intermediate representation.
- The E.T. agent's language encoder was pretrained on a ==sequence-to-sequence== task translating human instructions to synthetic instructions, and the agent was jointly trained with both natural and synthetic language demonstrations.

## Results

- The E.T. architecture achieved **33.8%** task success in seen environments and **3.2%** in unseen environments, representing relative improvements of **45.6%** and **33.3%** respectively over a recurrent LSTM baseline.
- Joint training with synthetic subgoal actions improved E.T.'s task success to **38.5%** (seen) and **5.4%** (unseen), demonstrating **13.9%** and **68.7%** relative gains over E.T. without synthetic data.
- The combined strategy of language encoder pretraining and joint synthetic training achieved a new state-of-the-art on the ALFRED benchmark, with **38.4%** task success on the seen test split and **8.5%** on the unseen test split.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A purely transformer-based architecture that observes the full episode history effectively addresses long-term memory challenges in compositional VLN tasks, outperforming recurrent models.
> - Synthetic instructions provide a structured, domain-specific representation that significantly improves human instruction understanding and generalization, especially to unseen environments.
> - Combining language encoder pretraining with joint training using synthetic data offers complementary benefits, leading to substantial performance gains in both seen and unseen environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN). VLN tasks require an autonomous agent to navigate and interact with a simulated or real environment based on natural language instructions. This domain is increasingly relevant for developing intelligent agents capable of performing complex household tasks or assisting humans in dynamic settings.

Prior work in VLN has typically employed recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to process sequential observations and actions, aiming to maintain an internal state that captures the history of the episode. However, RNNs have known limitations in capturing long-term dependencies, which can impede their performance on highly compositional tasks requiring agents to remember past events or locations over extended sequences of actions. Recent advancements have seen the emergence of transformer architectures, which leverage attention mechanisms to model dependencies across entire sequences, achieving success in natural language understanding and multimodal learning. Some concurrent work has also explored transformers for VLN, such as VLN-BERT, which focuses on trajectory-instruction compatibility, and Recurrent VLBERT, which uses a recurrent state alongside a pretrained VLBERT. This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a purely transformer-based architecture that processes the full episode history without explicit recurrence, addressing the long-term memory challenge directly.

Another significant challenge in VLN is the variability and complexity of human instructions, which often involve coreference resolution (e.g., "where you were standing previously") and implicit knowledge. Traditional instruction-following agents often rely on structured commands or logic programs. Efforts to improve understanding of natural language instructions include data augmentation techniques, auxiliary tasks (like progress estimation or cross-modal matching), and text-based pretraining. This paper introduces the use of synthetic instructions as an intermediate representation, a concept related to semantic parsing, to bridge the gap between human language variability and the agent's task execution. This approach aims to provide a more structured and less ambiguous representation of the task goals, thereby facilitating learning and improving generalization.

The research primarily evaluates its approach on the ALFRED benchmark, a dataset recognized for its challenging nature due to its longer episodes and requirement for object interaction, distinguishing it from earlier VLN datasets focused mainly on navigation in navigation graphs or continuous environments. The ALFRED environment provides a more realistic testbed for complex, multi-step tasks that necessitate robust long-term memory and instruction understanding.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objectives of this research were to address two significant challenges in Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN):
1. **Handling highly compositional tasks consisting of many subtasks and actions**: VLN tasks, particularly those in complex environments like ALFRED, often involve long sequences of interdependent actions. Agents must maintain memory of past observations, actions, and environmental states for extended periods to successfully complete such tasks. For instance, an agent might need to recall the location of an object observed much earlier in an episode or remember a previous state change (e.g., washing an item). Recurrent neural networks, a common approach in prior VLN models, have demonstrated inefficiencies in capturing these long-term dependencies, leading to potential failures in executing lengthy action sequences. The motivation was to develop an architecture capable of retaining and accessing a comprehensive history of the entire episode.
2. **Understanding complex human instructions**: Natural language instructions for VLN tasks can be ambiguous, context-dependent, and highly variable. They often involve complex linguistic phenomena such as object- and location-grounded coreference resolution, requiring the agent to precisely map linguistic elements to visual entities and spatial relationships in the environment. This inherent variability makes it difficult for agents to generalize from seen instructions to novel ones or to new environments. The motivation was to improve the agent's ability to interpret diverse human instructions accurately and consistently.

To address these challenges, the authors were motivated by several factors:
- **Success of Transformer Architectures**: The attention-based transformer architecture has demonstrated robust capabilities in modeling long-range dependencies in various domains, including language understanding and multimodal learning. This motivated its application to VLN, where the ability to attend to arbitrary past elements of an episode (observations, actions, and instructions) could overcome the limitations of recurrent models.
- **Need for Full Episode Observability**: Directly providing the agent with the entire history of visual observations and previous actions, rather than relying on a compressed recurrent state, was hypothesized to be critical for solving tasks with extended horizons and complex temporal dependencies.
- **Leveraging Synthetic Instructions**: The paper hypothesizes that domain-specific or synthetic languages, such as Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL)-based subgoal actions, can provide an unambiguous, task-oriented representation that is decoupled from the visual appearance of an environment and the linguistic variations of human instructions. This "intermediate interface" was expected to facilitate easier learning and better generalization by simplifying the instruction grounding problem.

The overarching goal was to develop a more robust and generalizable VLN agent that could perform complex, multi-step tasks by effectively leveraging long-term memory and improved instruction understanding.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Episodic Transformer (E.T.), is a multimodal transformer designed for vision-and-language navigation, capable of encoding the full episode history of visual observations and actions alongside natural language instructions.

#### 3.1. Episodic Transformer (E.T.) Model Architecture

The E.T. model operates without a hidden state and directly observes the complete history of visual observations and actions, in addition to the language instruction. Positional encoding for language tokens and temporal encoding for observations and actions are used to inject sequence order information. The architecture comprises four main encoders:

- **Language Encoder**: This encoder processes the natural language instruction (sequence of word tokens $x_{1:L}$). It consists of a word embedding look-up table followed by a multi-layer transformer encoder, producing contextualized language embeddings ($h^x_{1:L}$).
- **Visual Encoder**: A ResNet-50 backbone, pretrained for object detection in ALFRED, extracts features from each visual observation ($v_t$). These features are then passed through two convolutional layers and one fully-connected layer to project $v_t$ into a visual embedding ($h^v_t$). All episode visual observations ($v_{1:T}$) are processed independently by this shared encoder. The ResNet-50 backbone is frozen during agent training.
- **Action Encoder**: This is a look-up table that maps previous action types ($a_t$) into action embeddings ($h^a_{1:T}$).
- **Multimodal Encoder**: This is a multi-layer transformer encoder that takes the concatenated embeddings from the language, visual, and action encoders ($h^x_{1:L}$, $h^v_{1:T}$, $h^a_{1:T}$) as input. It employs causal attention to ensure that visual and action embeddings do not attend to future timesteps. The output embeddings ($z^v_{1:T}$) corresponding to the visual observations are then passed through a single fully-connected layer to predict the agent's actions ($\hat{a}_{1:T}$).

**Training and Inference:**
During training, the E.T. model receives the full natural language instruction ($x_{1:L}$) and all expert visual observations ($v_{1:T}$) and actions ($a_{1:T}$) for an entire episode. It predicts all actions $\hat{a}_{1:T}$ simultaneously. A cross-entropy loss is computed between the predicted and expert actions. During testing, at each timestep $t$, the model is provided with visual observations up to the current timestep ($v_{1:t}$) and the actions taken by the agent so far ($\hat{a}_{1:t-1}$). The action predicted for the last timestep ($\hat{a}_t$) is then applied to the environment. Auxiliary losses for overall and subgoal progress estimation are also used during training.

#### 3.2. Synthetic Language Integration

To enhance the understanding of human instructions and improve generalization, the research proposes using synthetic instructions in two ways:

- **Synthetic Language Definition**: Synthetic instructions ($y_{1:M}$) are generated for each expert demonstration using the expert path planner arguments, which are based on the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL). These instructions consist of subgoal actions (e.g., "Put Apple Table" or "Goto Bed"), specifying a class but not an instance of the target.

- **Language Encoder Pretraining**:
 - The E.T. agent's language encoder is pretrained on a sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) translation task.
 - The translation model maps human instructions ($x_{1:L}$) to their corresponding synthetic instructions ($y_{1:M}$).
 - The translation model's language encoder is identical to the E.T. agent's language encoder.
 - Once the seq2seq translation training converges, the weights of this pretrained language encoder are used to initialize the language encoder of the E.T. agent. This aims to implicitly transfer domain-specific language understanding.

- **Joint Training with Synthetic Language**:
 - An additional dataset of approximately 45,000 expert demonstrations, annotated with synthetic language, is generated using the AI2-THOR simulator. This synthetic dataset varies in object configurations and agent initial positions compared to the original ALFRED dataset.
 - The E.T. agent is then jointly trained using both the original natural language demonstrations and these newly generated synthetic language demonstrations.
 - The same language, vision, and action encoders are shared, but two distinct look-up tables are used for natural and synthetic language tokens.
 - Batches from both datasets are sampled and combined, and a single gradient descent step is performed based on their respective losses.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Dataset**: ALFRED dataset, consisting of 8,055 expert trajectories with 25,743 natural language instructions, split into train, validation, and test folds (seen and unseen environments).
- **Metrics**: Task success rates (average length 50 timesteps) and subgoal success rates (average length 7 timesteps). For state-of-the-art comparison, goal conditions and path-length-weighted scores are also reported.
- **Implementation**: Observations resized to 224x224. Target object selection uses predicted pixel masks from a pretrained Mask R-CNN model (frozen). Visual encoder ResNet-50 backbone is also frozen. E.T. transformer encoders use 2 blocks, 12 attention heads, and hidden size 768. AdamW optimizer with learning rate scheduling and auxiliary progress losses.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several key findings, demonstrating the advantages of the Episodic Transformer architecture and the utility of synthetic instructions.

#### 4.1. Comparison with Recurrent Models

- The E.T. architecture significantly outperformed a recurrent LSTM-based model on full task success rates. E.T. achieved 33.8% in seen environments and 3.2% in unseen environments, representing relative improvements of 45.6% and 33.3% respectively, compared to the LSTM baseline (23.2% seen, 2.4% unseen).
- For individual subgoals, the performance difference was smaller (2.3% and 1.5% relative improvement for E.T.), indicating that the primary advantage of E.T. lies in its ability to handle long-term dependencies crucial for completing multi-subgoal tasks.
- Training an LSTM-based model with E.T.'s transformer language encoder showed performance similar to E.T. in unseen environments, but 17.7% less successful in seen environments, suggesting the attention mechanism and full episode observability are critical over shared language encoding.
- E.T. required only a single forward pass per episode for gradient updates, making it more computationally efficient during training than recurrent models which require a pass per timestep.

#### 4.2. Accessible History Length

- Access to the full history of visual observations was found to be important for model performance. Performance consistently improved from no past frames to the entire episode history.
- Conversely, the agent did not benefit from accessing more than one past action. Providing access to more than the last action sometimes led to reduced performance, which the authors attribute to potential "causal misidentification" or overfitting to expert demonstrations.

#### 4.3. Model Capacity

- An ablation study on the number of transformer blocks revealed that 2 blocks provided the best performance (33.8% seen, 3.2% unseen). Models with 1 block were considered under-expressive, while models with 3 or 4 blocks showed signs of overfitting to the training data, leading to decreased generalization (e.g., 19.8% seen, 1.1% unseen for 4 blocks).

#### 4.4. Training with Synthetic Annotations

- **Joint Training**:
 - Joint training with synthetic instructions, particularly using subgoal actions, demonstrably improved performance. Subgoal action annotations yielded relative improvements of 13.9% (seen) and 68.7% (unseen) over the baseline without joint training (38.5% seen, 5.4% unseen vs. 33.8% seen, 3.2% unseen).
 - Other forms of synthetic annotations, such as expert frames or speaker model generated text, performed less effectively than subgoal actions when evaluated on human-annotated instructions. Expert frames, while effective for synthetic instruction evaluation, did not generalize well to human instructions.
 - E.T. benefited more significantly from additional synthetic data than the LSTM-based agent, with relative gains 2.3 to 4.4 times higher for E.T. in seen and unseen environments, respectively (e.g., 38.5% seen, 5.4% unseen for E.T. vs. 25.2% seen, 2.9% unseen for LSTM with synthetic data).
 - Increasing the synthetic dataset size up to 22,000 demonstrations showed notable improvements, with diminishing returns when doubling to 44,000 demonstrations.

- **Language Encoder Pretraining**:
 - Pretraining the language encoder to translate human instructions into synthetic instructions, and then transferring the learned encoder weights to the E.T. agent, was the most effective pretraining strategy. This approach yielded 37.6% success in seen environments and 3.8% in unseen environments, outperforming transferring explicitly translated text (35.2% seen, 3.6% unseen) and BERT pretraining (32.3% seen, 3.4% unseen).
 - BERT pretraining, based on generic text data, surprisingly led to a decrease in performance in seen environments and only a marginal improvement in unseen environments, suggesting the importance of domain-specific language pretraining for the ALFRED benchmark.

- **Combined Effect**:
 - Combining language encoder pretraining and joint training with synthetic data proved complementary. This combined strategy achieved a substantial relative improvement of 37.8% in seen environments (46.6% vs. 33.8% baseline) and 228.1% in unseen environments (7.3% vs. 3.2% baseline). This highlights the particular importance of synthetic data for generalization to novel environments.

#### 4.5. Comparison with State-of-the-Art

- The E.T. model, even without synthetic data, achieved a new state-of-the-art on seen environments.
- With language encoder pretraining and joint training using synthetic instructions, the E.T. agent established a new state-of-the-art across all metrics on the ALFRED benchmark, including validation and test splits (seen and unseen). For instance, it achieved 38.4% task success on the seen test split and 8.5% on the unseen test split.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Analysis

- Attention visualizations indicated that the multimodal encoder of E.T. successfully attends to relevant past visual frames (e.g., recalling the location of a microwave, remembering where a potato was sliced, tracking object state changes).
- Language attention heatmaps showed that the pretrained language encoder exhibited more focused attention on task-critical words, aligning better with human interpretation, compared to models trained from scratch.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes several contributions that have implications for the development of embodied AI and vision-and-language understanding.

Firstly, the introduction of the **Episodic Transformer (E.T.)** architecture provides a robust alternative to recurrent neural networks for Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN). By leveraging a transformer encoder to process the full episode history of visual observations and actions, E.T. directly addresses the challenge of long-term dependencies in compositional tasks. The demonstrated superiority of E.T. over LSTM-based models, particularly for full task success rates on long-horizon problems, indicates a step forward in designing agents capable of sustained, multi-step goal completion. This architectural choice also offers computational advantages during training due to the ability to process full episodes in a single forward pass.

Secondly, the systematic exploration and validation of **synthetic instructions** as an intermediate representation offer a methodological contribution to improving an agent's understanding of complex human language. By pretraining the language encoder on a human-to-synthetic instruction translation task and by jointly training the agent with synthetic language demonstrations, the paper demonstrates a method to decouple environmental visual appearance from linguistic variability. The significant improvements in performance, especially in unseen environments, underscore the value of domain-specific, structured linguistic inputs for generalization. This approach provides a blueprint for leveraging automatically generated, unambiguous task descriptions to enhance learning from diverse and noisy human instructions.

Thirdly, the work sets a **new state of the art on the challenging ALFRED benchmark**, a dataset characterized by its lengthy episodes and requirement for object interaction. This achievement indicates that the proposed E.T. architecture combined with synthetic instruction strategies represents a significant advancement in the capabilities of embodied agents to interpret grounded instructions for everyday tasks. The consistent performance gains across seen and unseen environments suggest improved robustness and generalization capabilities.

The potential impact of this research extends to various domains:
- **Embodied AI and Robotics**: The advancements in handling compositional tasks and understanding complex instructions are directly applicable to robotics, enabling more capable and versatile robots for household assistance, industrial automation, or exploration. Agents could perform longer, more intricate sequences of actions in dynamic environments.
- **Natural Language Interaction**: The use of synthetic instructions offers insights into how to build more robust natural language interfaces for machines, potentially by guiding the learning process with structured, machine-interpretable representations.
- **Generalization in AI**: The emphasis on improving generalization, especially to unseen environments, is a critical aspect for deploying AI systems in real-world scenarios where complete prior knowledge of all situations is impossible. The strategies explored here contribute to the ongoing effort to develop more adaptable AI.

Future work could build upon this foundation by exploring alternative forms of synthetic annotations and developing more sophisticated techniques for their automatic generation, potentially integrating with object detectors or symbolic planning systems. This could further enhance the scalability and applicability of such models across a wider range of tasks and environments.
