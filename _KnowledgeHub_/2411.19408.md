# SoGraB: A Visual Method for Soft Grasping Benchmarking and Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces SoGraB, a visual method for soft grasping evaluation that quantifies object deformation using 3D imaging alongside grasp success and holding time. It demonstrated that soft grippers reduce deformation effectively when object stiffness is within a comparable range, and established a 900-grasp baseline dataset for diverse objects and grippers.

## Problem

- A lack of standardized protocols exists for systematically evaluating the performance of diverse soft robotic grippers.
- Existing evaluation metrics, such as grasp success and retention force, do not quantify object deformation, internal stresses, or the forces applied to the object itself, which are crucial for delicate items.
- Current methods often require specialized instrumentation or are derived from rigid grasping principles, limiting their general applicability to various soft gripper-object pairs.

## Method

- Developed **SoGraB**, a ==visual evaluation protocol== that combines grasp success, holding time, and quantifiable object deformation into a single scalar ==grasp quality score==.
- Quantified object deformation non-contact by capturing ==3D point clouds== of the object in ungrasped and grasped states, comparing them using the ==Density-Aware Chamfer Distance (DCD)== algorithm.
- Implemented a generalized visual methodology using a robotic arm and 3D cameras to collect point clouds, enabling evaluation across various soft grippers and deformable objects without specialized instrumentation.

## Results

- All **900** experimental grasps achieved successful retention, but SoGraB scores ranged from **0.517** (high deformation) to **0.940** (low deformation), demonstrating its ability to differentiate grasp quality.
- The evaluation successfully categorized object interactions into **three** types based on stiffness, revealing optimal operating regimes where soft grippers reduce deformation effectively.
- A baseline dataset of **900 grasps** was generated using **15** object geometries, **3** object materials (Shore 40A, 60A, 85A), and **4** Fin-Ray gripper designs, providing a foundation for future comparisons.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The SoGraB metric successfully differentiates soft grasping performance based on object deformation, even when all grasps achieve successful retention, providing a more nuanced quality assessment.
> - Soft grippers provide a quantifiable advantage in minimizing object deformation primarily when the object's effective stiffness is comparable to or slightly softer than the gripper.
> - For objects that are either significantly stiffer or too soft relative to the gripper, the benefits of soft gripping diminish, with performance often converging with rigid grippers or leading to high deformation regardless of gripper compliance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of soft robotics has seen an increase in the development and application of soft robotic grippers, which are valued for their ability to handle soft and fragile objects robustly. However, the research landscape currently lacks a commonly available, standardized protocol for evaluating the performance of these diverse soft gripper designs. Existing evaluation methods, often developed from the perspective of rigid grasping, primarily focus on metrics such as grasp success rate (how often an object is successfully picked up and held) or retention force (the force required to dislodge an object from a gripper). While these metrics characterize a gripper's ability to secure an object, they typically do not account for the forces applied to the object itself, the resulting internal stresses, or the deformation an object undergoes during grasping. This omission is particularly critical for soft and deformable objects, where excessive force or deformation can lead to damage.

Standardization efforts, such as those by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology and IEEE, have proposed quality metrics and testing procedures for robotic hands and end-effectors, but these largely concentrate on the mechanical characteristics of the gripper rather than its direct impact on the grasped object. In soft robotics, the distinction between mechanical design and control policy is often blurred due to morphological computation, where the gripper's physical structure inherently influences its behavior. This further complicates the direct applicability of rigid grasping benchmarks.

Current performance-based metrics used in real-world evaluations, which provide benchmarks generalizable across different hardware configurations, also suffer from limitations. They often rely on standardized objects in lieu of standardized testing platforms and primarily consider aspects like stability or disturbance rejection. Critically, none of these methods explicitly consider the effect of grasping on the object itself, nor do they leverage soft object databases for evaluation. Furthermore, measuring grasp force as a proxy for internal stress often necessitates the use of force sensors integrated into the gripper or instrumented objects, which limits the ability to benchmark arbitrary gripper-object pairs.

This work addresses this identified gap by proposing a novel evaluation protocol, SoGraB, that directly quantifies object deformation as a non-contact proxy for stress. By incorporating object-centric deformation alongside grasp success, SoGraB aims to provide a more comprehensive and appropriate assessment for soft grippers interacting with deformable objects. This contribution is positioned to establish a much-needed standardized framework that can drive advancements in soft gripper design by offering a consistent method for evaluating grasp quality and safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the absence of a standardized and comprehensive evaluation protocol for soft robotic grippers. While soft grippers have demonstrated utility in handling delicate items, the lack of agreed-upon metrics makes it challenging to objectively assess their performance, compare different designs, and identify areas for improvement. Current evaluation approaches are considered insufficient because they often:
- **Prioritize grasp success rate or retention force:** These measures indicate if an object is held but do not quantify the forces applied to the object, its internal stresses, or any resulting deformation. For soft and fragile objects, minimizing deformation and potential damage is a critical aspect of grasp quality that is not captured by existing metrics.
- **Lack consideration for object deformation:** The impact of a gripper on a deformable object, especially in terms of induced strain or shape change, is largely overlooked. This limits the ability to evaluate "grasp safety" alongside "grasp quality."
- **Require specialized instrumentation:** Methods that attempt to quantify grasp forces often necessitate integrating sensors directly into the gripper or the object, which complicates benchmarking for arbitrary gripper-object combinations due to varying hardware and material properties.
- **Are derived from rigid grasping principles:** The unique characteristics of soft grippers and deformable objects require a different set of evaluation criteria that account for compliance and morphological computation.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of this work are articulated through the following contributions:

- **Development of a Novel Evaluation Protocol (SoGraB):** The central objective is to introduce the Soft Grasping Benchmarking and Evaluation (SoGraB) method. This protocol is designed to provide a more holistic assessment of soft grasping quality by integrating metrics beyond simple success or retention.
- **Quantification of Object Deformation:** A core objective is to quantify object deformation as a non-contact proxy for stress. This is achieved by using 3D imaging to capture point clouds of soft objects both before and during grasping, and subsequently comparing these point clouds using a specialized distance metric.
- **Introduction of a Generalized Visual Methodology:** The researchers aim to establish a visual methodology for soft grasp benchmarking that is broadly applicable. This means the method should not be restricted by specific object types or gripper designs, thus enabling its use with a wide range of soft grippers and various deformable objects, provided the object remains at least partially visible during the grasp.
- **Experimental Validation and Investigation:** A significant objective is to validate the proposed protocol through extensive experimental investigation. This includes evaluating the grasp quality of specific soft gripper designs, such as Fin-Ray soft fingers printed at different hardness levels, to demonstrate the method's ability to differentiate performance.
- **Creation of a Baseline Grasp Dataset:** The project aims to generate a baseline dataset comprising a substantial number of grasps (900 in this instance). This dataset features objects with diverse geometries and material properties, providing a foundation for future comparisons and further research in soft grasping.

By achieving these objectives, the researchers intend to provide the soft robotics community with a standardized, objective, and practical tool for assessing, comparing, and ultimately improving soft gripper designs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SoGraB method is structured around a three-pronged approach to grasp quality assessment, complemented by a standardized experimental implementation.

**A. Grasp Quality Assessment**
SoGraB evaluates soft grasping quality based on three features: grasp success, holding time, and crucially, object deformation. These features are combined into a novel object-centric scalar benchmark for soft grasping quality.

- **Object Deformation Quantification:** Object deformation is quantified by capturing 3D point clouds of the object both in its initial, ungrasped state ($S_1$) and its deformed state while being grasped ($S_2$). The comparison between these two point clouds is performed using the Density-Aware Chamfer Distance (DCD) algorithm. DCD was selected over other metrics like standard Chamfer Distance or Earth Mover’s Distance due to its insensitivity to variations in density distribution and outlying points. This characteristic makes DCD suitable for analyzing incomplete point clouds and providing reliable evaluations even in the presence of occlusions, which are common in grasping scenarios.
 - The DCD metric, $d_{DCD}(S_1, S_2)$, calculates the average bounded Euclidean distance between each point in one point cloud and its nearest neighbor in the other. It bounds these distances within the range using a first-order approximation of the Taylor Expansion. A scalar, $\alpha$, is utilized to adjust the algorithm's sensitivity. To ensure density independence, the algorithm tracks how often a point is referenced as a nearest neighbor, with subsequent references having a diminishing effect on the final distance. The distance is computed with each cloud taking turns as the "reference" cloud, and the average of these calculations yields the final $d_{DCD}$ score, also within.

- **Scoring Metric:** A comprehensive scoring metric is defined in Equation (1), which assigns scores based on grasp success and object deformation.
 - **Unsuccessful Grasps:** Attempts that fail to grasp the object or drop it before the grasped point cloud can be captured are assigned a score of 0.
 - **Partially Successful Grasps:** If the object is dropped after the grasped point cloud is captured but before it is placed, the score is calculated as $(1 - d_{DCD}) \frac{t_{dropped}}{2t_{cycle}}$. This formula incorporates both the deformation and the proportion of the drop time ($t_{dropped}$) relative to the total cycle time ($t_{cycle}$), resulting in scores in the range [0, 0.5].
 - **Successful Grasps:** For grasps where the object is held for a complete pick-and-place cycle, the score is $1 - \frac{d_{DCD}}{2}$. This ranges from [0.5, 1], where lower deformation (smaller $d_{DCD}$) yields a higher score.

**B. Point Cloud Alignment**
To accurately quantify deformation and account for in-hand slippage or rotation during grasping, precise alignment of the initial and grasped point clouds is necessary. The Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm is employed for this purpose. Given that the point clouds possess different feature sets and the object undergoes shape changes, an exact transformation is not possible. However, ICP minimizes the distance between the two clouds, providing a reliable approximation of the true transformation for moderate deformations, initialized by the robot's kinematics. For highly symmetrical objects experiencing significant deformations, the center of mass and principal axes of the two point clouds are aligned to provide a reliable transformation.

**C. Experimental Grasp Evaluation Implementation**
A custom experimental platform was established to validate the SoGraB method and generate the initial dataset.
- **Hardware Setup:** The platform comprises a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda manipulator and two Zivid One Plus Small depth cameras. These cameras, arranged on opposite sides of the object, utilize structured light to reconstruct 3D images.
- **Scene Configuration:** To facilitate object segmentation from the gripper and background, the scene was configured for high contrast. Test objects were 3D printed in white, placed on a black background, and test grippers were printed in black. This setup ensures clear differentiation for point cloud extraction.
- **Grasping Procedure:** The test grippers were configured for an antipodal 2-finger grasp. Prior to grasping, the robot arm was rotated 90 degrees about the vertical axis to position the fingers vertically and minimize occlusion of the object by the fingers. The gripper then slowly closed around the object (0.1 m/s) until a target force of 0.5 N was applied. The complete evaluation procedure for each sample involved:
 1. Placing the object and capturing an initial point cloud.
 2. Positioning the gripper and performing the grasp.
 3. Raising the object and capturing the grasped point clouds.
 4. Raising the object to the manipulator's home pose.
 5. Returning and releasing the object at the origin.
- **Experimentally Evaluated Grippers:** Fin-Ray soft fingers were chosen due to their widespread use, geometric reconfigurability, and ease of evaluation. Three Fin-Ray designs with 4, 6, and 8 internal ribs (varying stiffness) were evaluated, all 90mm long, 35mm wide, and 20mm thick, printed in NinjaTek Shore 90A Eel TPU. A fourth, rigid Fin-Ray finger printed in PLA served as a comparison.
- **Evaluation Object Selection:** A total of 15 objects were used. Twelve were selected from the EGAD evaluation dataset, scaled to a maximum width of 55mm, representing diverse geometries. Three custom objects were generated using random splines, mirrored for symmetry, and extruded, resulting in amorphous shapes designed to be highly deformable. These objects were printed in three hardness levels: Shore 40A (softest), 60A, and 85A (hardest), providing a range from much softer than the gripper material to approximately the same hardness.
- **Dataset Size:** The experimental evaluation comprised a total of 900 grasps, derived from 15 object geometries, 3 object materials, 4 grippers, with 5 repeated grasps for each combination.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation, encompassing 900 grasps, aimed to understand the conditions under which soft gripping is important, what constitutes an effective soft gripper for soft objects, and how well soft grippers generalize across different objects.

**A. Overall Grasp Performance**
All 900 experimental grasps were classified as successful, meaning every object was stably grasped and lifted without being dropped before placement. Consequently, all grasp scores fell within the range of 0.5 to 1. The lowest recorded score was 0.517 (Object O3 Shore 40A with Rigid Grippers), and the highest was 0.940 (Object D1 Shore 85A with Rigid Grippers). This indicates that while all grasps were successful in terms of retention, the SoGraB metric effectively differentiated performance based on the degree of object deformation.

**B. Object Categorization Based on Stiffness and Gripper Interaction**
The evaluation objects were observed to fall into three categories based on their effective stiffness relative to the grippers and the resulting deformation.

- **Relatively Stiff Objects (e.g., D1, B5):**
 These objects, characterized by a solid material core, exhibited high effective stiffness across all three Shore hardness levels. For such objects, the grasping scores were uniformly high, and there was minimal discernible performance difference between the soft Fin-Ray grippers and the rigid PLA gripper. This suggests that when an object is significantly stiffer than the gripper, the benefits of soft gripping (i.e., reduced deformation) are diminished, and the gripper performs similarly to a rigid counterpart.

- **Relatively Soft Objects (e.g., O2, O3):**
 The three custom-generated objects (O1, O2, O3) were designed for softness and symmetry. Objects O2 and O3, in particular, experienced substantial deformations during grasping. For these very soft objects, the choice of gripper (whether soft or rigid) had minimal impact on the deformation, leading to consistently low mean grasp scores with little variation across different gripper designs. Object O1, however, possessed a corrugated surface that increased its effective stiffness, preventing complete flattening observed in O2 and O3. This allowed for a larger range of scores and better differentiation between the rigid gripper and the three Fin-Ray designs for O1.

- **Intermediate/Optimal Stiffness Objects (e.g., B1 Shore 40A, C2 Shore 40A, O3 Shore 40A):**
 This category represents objects where the effective stiffness was comparable to the evaluated grippers, allowing for clear distinctions in gripper performance.
 - **Within-Object Trends:** For objects like B1 Shore 40A, C2 Shore 40A, and O3 Shore 40A, the grippers were ranked from softest to hardest based on their mean grasp scores. Softer grippers generally achieved higher scores (less deformation) on these softer objects.
 - **Across-Object Trends:** While the softer grippers often outperformed harder ones on these objects, this trend was not universal. For objects printed at Shore 85A hardness, the performance scores of different grippers tended to converge, indicating that the benefit of soft gripping decreased as the object's hardness approached or exceeded that of the gripper. This suggests a need for more compliant grippers for harder objects if soft gripping benefits are to be maintained.
 - **Material Contribution:** Objects B1, D3, O1, and O3 demonstrated that higher Shore values (harder materials) collectively resulted in better grasp scores across all grippers, confirming that the object's intrinsic stiffness is a material factor in the overall grasp quality.
 - **Variability in Thin Features:** Objects with thin features (e.g., C2, D5, F5) exhibited highly varied grasp qualities and large standard deviations in scores. This was attributed to the sensitivity of grasp behavior to small changes in the object's position relative to the gripper, indicating that such objects might benefit from a larger sample set for more robust evaluation.

In summary, the results indicate that soft gripping provides a quantifiable advantage in reducing object deformation primarily when the object's effective stiffness is in a range comparable to or slightly softer than the gripper. Outside this range, the benefits of soft grippers diminish, either due to the object being too stiff (making all grippers behave rigidly) or too soft (leading to large deformations regardless of gripper compliance). The SoGraB method successfully quantified these differences and provided insights into gripper and object interactions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SoGraB method presents a new benchmarking protocol for the evaluation of soft grasping performance, offering a direct measure of object deformation as a proxy for internal stress. This approach addresses a significant gap in the soft robotics field, which has historically lacked standardized and comprehensive metrics for assessing how grippers interact with and affect deformable objects.

The key significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Generalizability and Accessibility:** SoGraB is designed to be a generalized visual methodology, applicable to a wide array of objects and most soft gripper designs. Critically, it does not rely on specialized instrumentation of either the gripper or the object, making it practical for implementation in a typical robotics laboratory equipped with a robot arm, a 3D printer, and a 3D camera. This broad applicability increases its utility for the wider research community.
- **Comprehensive Grasp Quality Assessment:** By integrating grasp success, holding time, and quantitative object deformation, SoGraB provides a more holistic and object-centric assessment of grasp quality than traditional metrics. It allows researchers to move beyond simple success rates to understand the "gentleness" and safety of a grasp, which is paramount when handling fragile or sensitive items.
- **Establishment of a Baseline Dataset:** The creation of a 900-grasp baseline dataset across diverse object geometries, materials, and gripper designs is a foundational contribution. This dataset provides a valuable reference point for future research, enabling direct comparison of new gripper designs or materials against established benchmarks.
- **Identification of Optimal Operating Regimes:** The experimental results demonstrate that there is a defined range of stiffnesses where soft grippers offer a quantifiable advantage in minimizing object deformation. Outside this range, the benefits of soft grippers diminish, performing similarly to rigid grippers. This insight is crucial for understanding the optimal use cases for soft gripping technologies and for guiding the design of grippers tailored to specific object properties.

The potential impact of the SoGraB protocol is substantial:
- **Driving Improved Gripper Design:** By providing an objective and repeatable method for evaluating soft gripper performance, SoGraB can accelerate the iterative design process for new grippers. Engineers and researchers can use the protocol to test prototypes, compare design variations, and quantify improvements in terms of reduced object deformation and enhanced grasp quality.
- **Informing Application-Specific Gripper Selection:** The ability of SoGraB to identify the range of object stiffnesses where soft grippers are most effective will help roboticists select the most appropriate gripper for specific manipulation tasks. This could lead to more efficient and reliable robotic systems in fields such as manufacturing, healthcare, and agriculture, where delicate object handling is common.
- **Facilitating Community Collaboration and Data Expansion:** The open and accessible nature of the SoGraB protocol encourages other researchers to contribute to the dataset. By expanding the range of objects (new shapes, hardnesses) and benchmarking new soft grippers against the existing dataset, the community can collectively build a more comprehensive understanding of soft gripping performance. The recommendation for symmetric objects without thin external features also guides future data collection towards more repeatable and robust results.
- **Standardization in Soft Robotics:** SoGraB represents a step towards establishing a much-needed standardized benchmark in soft robotics. Such standardization is critical for comparing research outcomes across different institutions and ensuring the reproducibility and validity of findings, ultimately advancing the entire field.
