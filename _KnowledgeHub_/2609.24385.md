# Tactile-JEPA: Topology-Aware Self-Supervised Representation Learning for Distributed Tactile Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> Tactile-JEPA is a self-supervised pre-training method for distributed tactile skins. It predicts embeddings of masked taxels from visible ones, sampling masks over a taxel connectivity graph at both local and global scales to yield topology-aware, multi-scale per-taxel representations. Across three datasets, it cuts force-estimation error 6.3% and in-hand orientation error 20.8% versus prior state of the art, and trains stably where baselines collapse.

## Problem

- Tactile encoders are usually trained from scratch on raw noisy signals, unlike vision and language branches that inherit large pre-trained backbones.
- Prior self-supervised tactile work targets vision-based camera sensors, whose pixel grid does not exist for sparse, irregularly arranged taxels on electronic skins.
- Graph-based taxel methods train end-to-end on labels, and contrastive taxel pre-training needs privileged simulation contact geometry, so no reusable label-free representation exists.

## Method

- **Taxel connectivity graph** built from the known sensor layout, needing no taxel coordinates, guides mask sampling; two-part embodiments yield two connected components.
- **Dual-scale target masking**: local masks grown by ==Dijkstra expansion== from a seed taxel, global masks sampled uniformly across the graph, mixed two local plus two global.
- ==Joint-embedding predictive training==: context encoder embeds visible taxels, a four-block predictor infers target embeddings from target positional embeddings, matched by MSE to an ==EMA target encoder==.

## Results

- On Sparsh-skin, force RMSE drops to **14.05 cN** and orientation RMSE to **5.48 degrees**, **6.3%** and **20.8%** better than the strongest baseline respectively.
- On tactile socks, action classification reaches **96.91%** and full-body pose RMSE **13.42 rad**, while BYOL and MAE collapse entirely on that dataset.
- Ablation: the **2L+2G** mix beats graph-agnostic I-JEPA block masking on all three tasks; pre-training runs **65-75%** faster than DINO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sensor topology belongs in the pre-training task design, not merely as an input coordinate feature appended to each element's signal.
> - Mask scale controls representation locality: local masks favor fine contact detail, global masks favor whole-hand state, mixing transfers broadly.
> - Best downstream accuracy and training stability diverge: MAE won policy learning yet collapsed elsewhere, so per-dataset robustness deserves reporting alongside peak numbers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Contact-rich manipulation exposes a limit of cameras: vision can lose sight of an object when a hand occludes it. Electronic skins let robots sense contact across more of a hand or body than fingertip cameras can. However, they produce noisy time series from sparse, irregularly placed sensing elements called taxels. The authors learn from the skin's own signals without task labels. They hide selected sensor elements, predict the learned features of the hidden ones, and choose what to hide according to the sensor's layout. In the tested hand-skin tasks, the learned representation improves force and object-orientation estimates.

### 2. From irregular skin to graph masks

This layout makes a visual pretraining recipe based on image grids a poor fit. The method, Tactile-JEPA, starts from a taxel graph instead. In this fixed map, taxels are nodes, and edges connect taxels that are adjacent in the known sensor layout.

A short window of readings is projected to one embedding per taxel. An embedding is a learned feature vector. Three components handle these embeddings:

- A context encoder processes the visible taxels.
- An exponential-moving-average target encoder processes all taxels.
- A predictor uses the context embeddings and the target taxel positions to predict the hidden embeddings.

Training minimizes mean-squared error between the predicted embeddings and the target-encoder embeddings. It does not reconstruct the raw, noisy sensor signals. After pretraining, the target encoder is frozen, and a task-specific head is trained on its per-taxel embeddings.

Masks come in two scales. Local masks are connected subgraphs grown outward from a seed taxel to cover compact regions. Global masks select scattered taxels across the skin. Local targets encourage prediction of contact detail in a region. Global targets represent a more distributed contact state. The standard configuration uses one context and four targets, two local and two global.

The closest predecessor, [[2301.08243|I-JEPA]], also predicts target-region embeddings from a visible context. Its masks, however, are image blocks on a regular pixel grid.

### 3. What the tests show, and where they differ

The strongest numbers come from Sparsh-skin, a magnetic sensor skin on an Allegro hand. For total force-estimation RMSE, in centinewtons, Tactile-JEPA records 14.05 ± 0.73 cN. The best competing method, the end-to-end model trained from scratch, records 15.00 ± 0.92 cN. For in-hand object orientation, θ-RMSE falls from 6.92 ± 0.36° for BYOL to 5.48 ± 0.39°. The authors report these as reductions of 6.3% and 20.8%. Each reduction is relative to that metric's strongest baseline, and these are separate task results rather than one pooled score.

The mask ablation tests the explanation. It compares block-based I-JEPA masks with all-local, all-global, and mixed graph masks on three measures: force RMSE, in-hand x-accuracy, and DECO-50 policy error. The results depend on the task:

- In the default global-context setup, the mixed 2L+2G scheme is best on force RMSE (14.05 ± 0.73 cN) and x-accuracy (95.74 ± 1.05%).
- The all-global 4G scheme is best on policy error, at 0.4600 ± 0.0019 versus 0.4635 ± 0.0044 for the mixed scheme.

The authors say that comparing the local-context 4L† setting against I-JEPA confirms the benefit of graph-based sampling. That is their interpretation of the ablation. It does not prove that topology alone explains the gains.

The policy benchmark uses only the DECO-50 Assembly subset. There, Tactile-JEPA with vision scores 0.4634 ± 0.0029. That beats vision-only (0.5132 ± 0.0103) and end-to-end tactile plus vision (0.4922 ± 0.0058). MAE plus vision is best, at 0.4400 ± 0.0021. This is a learned policy benchmark result. It is not evidence of improved physical robot success.

### 4. Let the Skin's Layout Guide Learning

The paper's contribution is to fit embedding prediction to distributed skins through dual-scale masks sampled from the taxel graph. The approach encodes both localized tactile cues and the overall contact state of the hand. The experiments support transfer in the tested benchmarks. They do not support a general claim of improved real-world manipulation, and the mixed scheme does not win every task.
