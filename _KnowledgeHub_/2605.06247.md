# CKT-WAM: Parameter-Efficient Context Knowledge Transfer Between World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework named CKT-WAM enables parameter-efficient knowledge transfer from a large teacher World Action Model to a lightweight student, achieving strong zero-shot generalization and robust real-world robotic manipulation with only 1.17% trainable parameters. The system overcomes architectural heterogeneity by injecting a compact contextual representation through the student's native conditioning pathway.

## Problem

- World Action Models (WAMs) often incur high computational overhead and test-time latency due to explicit video synthesis.
- Efficiently transferring action-relevant world knowledge between WAMs with heterogeneous architectures is difficult, as traditional methods struggle with mismatched latent interfaces and high adaptation costs.
- Existing knowledge distillation or fine-tuning approaches are either computationally expensive, unstable with disparate latent parameterizations, or impose rigid architectural constraints.

## Method

- **CKT-WAM** constructs a compact ==contextual interface== by extracting hidden states from an intermediate layer of a powerful teacher WAM, repurposing it as a single-pass observation encoder.
- A parameter-efficient ==CKT module== compresses teacher states using ==learnable-query cross-attention== and adapts them via both a generalized adapter and ==sparsely-routed specialized adapters==.
- Context is injected into the student WAM's frozen backbone by appending to its textual embeddings, serving as keys and values for cross-attention without modifying the student's core architecture or query mechanism.

## Results

- CKT-WAM achieved **86.1% total success** on the LIBERO-Plus zero-shot generalization benchmark, outperforming all prior VLA and WAM baselines, while utilizing only **1.17% trainable parameters**.
- The framework nearly matched full fine-tuning (**86.1% vs. 86.3%**) with a drastically smaller parameter budget, outperforming other parameter-efficient adaptation methods.
- In real-world multi-step, long-horizon manipulation tasks, CKT-WAM achieved the best average success rate of **83.3%** across four diverse tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Efficient knowledge transfer between heterogeneous WAMs can be achieved by extracting and injecting compact, task-relevant contextual information rather than relying on full representation matching.
> - A combination of a generalized knowledge transfer branch and sparsely-routed, instance-dependent specialized adapters significantly improves robustness and generalization to diverse out-of-distribution shifts.
> - Freezing both teacher and student backbones, and only training a lightweight context transfer module, allows for high parameter efficiency and architectural flexibility while effectively leveraging teacher knowledge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

World action models (WAMs) represent a significant paradigm in embodied intelligence, distinguished by their explicit modeling of how visual observations evolve in response to actions. This contrasts with standard vision-language-action (VLA) models, which primarily establish a direct, reactive mapping from visual and linguistic inputs to robotic actions. WAMs, building on latent dynamics research, operate through two main architectural approaches:
1. **Imagine-then-execute models:** These causally generate future visual trajectories before predicting actions. Examples include work by Li et al. (2026) and Feng et al. (2025).
2. **Joint-modeling frameworks:** These denoise future video and action tokens simultaneously within a shared generative process, as seen in models like Motus (Bi et al., 2025) and Zhu et al. (2025).

A practical challenge for WAMs is the computational overhead associated with explicit video synthesis, which often leads to substantial test-time latency. This has spurred efforts to improve inference efficiency, such as VPP (Hu et al., 2024), UVA (Li et al., 2025), and Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026), which aim to decouple video co-training from test-time future imagination.

This paper addresses a distinct but related challenge: the efficient transfer of action-relevant world knowledge between different WAMs. While knowledge distillation has proven successful in vision and language models, applying it to heterogeneous WAM architectures is complex due to mismatched latent interfaces, high adaptation costs, and the rigidity of conventional distillation objectives. Direct fine-tuning is computationally expensive, and output-level imitation can be unstable with disparate latent parameterizations or asymmetric action heads. Deep structural alignment methods, such as full hidden-state or attention-map matching, impose architectural constraints and overhead. This research therefore seeks a transfer mechanism that is both architecture-compatible and parameter-efficient, allowing a powerful, computationally heavy "teacher" WAM to impart knowledge to a lightweight "student" WAM, thereby fitting into efforts to make advanced robotic control more deployable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a parameter-efficient framework for transferring knowledge between World Action Models (WAMs), specifically from a robust teacher WAM to a more lightweight student WAM. The motivation for this objective stems from several practical and technical challenges:

1. **Heterogeneity of WAM Architectures:** Different WAMs often employ diverse latent parameterizations, token interfaces, and denoising backbones. This architectural mismatch makes conventional knowledge transfer methods, which often rely on direct output imitation or dense hidden-state matching, impractical or ineffective. Output-level imitation (e.g., policy distillation) can be brittle, while deep structural alignment (e.g., matching full hidden states or attention maps) imposes rigid architectural constraints and significant computational overhead by requiring layer-by-layer mimicry.

2. **High Adaptation Cost:** Full fine-tuning of large WAMs is prohibitively expensive in terms of computational resources (GPUs, energy) and time. In real-world deployment scenarios, there is a need for methods that can adapt models efficiently without incurring the cost of retraining entire large models.

3. **Deployment Constraints:** Practitioners often have access to powerful teacher WAMs that are too computationally intensive for real-time or memory-constrained applications. Conversely, lightweight student WAMs are desirable for deployment but may lack the comprehensive world knowledge of their larger counterparts. The goal is to imbue these student models with the teacher's expertise without sacrificing efficiency.

4. **Improving Generalization:** Transferring rich, action-relevant world knowledge from a strong teacher is hypothesized to improve the student's zero-shot generalization capabilities, particularly in out-of-distribution scenarios common in real-world robotics. Such scenarios include variations in camera angle, robot type, language, lighting, background, noise, and layout.

To address these motivations, the proposed CKT-WAM framework aims to transfer knowledge through a compact contextual interface. This approach seeks to:
- Extract only the *task-relevant sufficient statistics* from the teacher's intermediate representations.
- Reduce the token count of these representations to minimize transfer cost.
- Transform these representations through parameter-efficient adaptive modules.
- Inject the resulting compact context into the student's native conditioning pathway with minimal architectural modifications, keeping both teacher and student backbones frozen.
This strategy is designed to offer a balance between effective knowledge transfer, computational efficiency, and architectural flexibility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CKT-WAM is a parameter-efficient context knowledge transfer framework that operates in two main stages: teacher-side context construction and student-side context injection.

#### 3.1. Teacher-Side Hidden States Selection for Efficient and Single-Pass Extraction

The process begins by extracting hidden states from an intermediate layer (denoted as $\ell^*$) of the teacher WAM, which typically has $L_{tea}$ transformer blocks. The selection of an intermediate layer is based on providing a balance between semantic structure and avoiding the potentially strong task-specific bias of deeper layers, while also being less computationally intensive than using the deepest layer. The teacher WAM, a powerful generative model, is repurposed for inference as a single-pass observation encoder. This means its input consists only of the observed image tokens and instruction. The hidden states $H_T$ are extracted at a fixed timestep $t^*=0$, which corresponds to a clean endpoint, to encode the current scene into a stable, world-aware representation, avoiding timestep-specific noise. The extracted context $C_A$ is computed once per observation and then reused across all denoising steps of the student WAM, eliminating the need for iterative teacher forwarding.

#### 3.2. Parameter-Efficient CKT Module

The extracted teacher hidden states $H_T$ are processed by the CKT module, which consists of several components designed for compression, adaptation, and routing:

1. **Initial Projection:** $H_T$ is first mapped into the student's feature space using a shared bottleneck adapter comprising down- and up-projection matrices ($W_{down}$, $W_{up}$) and a non-linear activation function. This step translates the teacher's feature dimension ($d_{tea}$) to the student's textual embedding dimension ($d_{stu}$) via a bottleneck dimension ($d_b$).

2. **Compressor: Learnable-Query Cross-Attention (LQCA) for Token Compression:** The core of the compressor is to reduce the sequence length of teacher tokens from $N$ to a much smaller, fixed length. This is achieved using two sets of learnable query banks, $Q_g$ for the generalized branch and $Q_s$ for the specialized branch. These queries interact with the transformed teacher states (keys $K_Z$, values $V_Z$) via multi-head cross-attention (MHCA). The output, $C_0^*$, represents a compressed summary of the teacher's intermediate representations. This bottleneck aims to extract only task-relevant information, significantly reducing downstream transfer cost.

3. **Always-on Generalized Adapter:** The compressed context from the generalized branch, $C_0^g$, is passed through an always-on generalized residual bottleneck adapter. This adapter is parameterized by low-rank matrices ($W_{down}^{(g)}$, $W_{up}^{(g)}$) and is designed to capture task-agnostic transferable structure. It is active for every input.

4. **Routed Specialized Adapters:** To handle input-dependent transfer patterns, the framework employs $M$ specialized adapters, $\{E_m\}_{m=1}^M$. Each specialized adapter is also a lightweight residual bottleneck, operating on the specialized compressed context $C_0^s$. A lightweight router, which takes the average-pooled teacher hidden states $h_r$ as input, computes routing probabilities $p$ for these $M$ adapters. For each instance, only the top-$k$ adapters are selected ($I_b$), and their routing weights are renormalized. The final specialized context $C_s$ is then computed as a weighted sparse aggregation of the selected adapters' outputs. This route-first sparse execution means only $k$ out of $M$ adapters are active per instance, enhancing parameter efficiency.

5. **Transferred Context Formation:** The final transferred context $C_A$ is formed by concatenating the outputs of the generalized and specialized branches ($C_g$ and $C_s$).

#### 3.3. Student-Side Textual Context Injection

The constructed context $C_A$ is injected into the student WAM by appending it to the student's conditioning textual embeddings $E_t$. A learnable separator token ($⟨SEP⟩$) is inserted between $E_t$ and $C_A$. The augmented sequence, $\tilde{E}_t$, is then fed into the *frozen* student backbone. Critically, this augmented sequence is used only as the key and value arguments for multi-head cross-attention in the student's transformer blocks, leaving the student's query mechanism and denoising backbone unchanged. This design preserves the student's 3D rotary position encoding (RoPE) geometry, as RoPE is applied only in visual self-attention and does not depend on the conditioning sequence length.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

During training, both the teacher and student WAMs remain frozen. Only the CKT module's parameters (shared trunk, generalized adapter, specialized adapters, and router) are optimized. The training objective combines the student's original diffusion-style latent objective with a load-balancing loss:
- **Student Diffusion Loss:** This includes an action loss ($L_{act}$) and a future-video loss ($L_{vid}$), both calculated as weighted mean squared errors between the student's predictions of clean future actions ($\hat{a}_0$) and future videos ($\hat{v}_0$) and their respective ground truths ($a_0$, $v_0$). Noise levels are shared across modalities but perturbations are modality-specific.
- **Load-Balancing Loss ($L_{bal}$):** This auxiliary MoE-style loss encourages balanced utilization of the specialized adapters by penalizing routing imbalance. It is calculated based on batch-averaged routing mass ($P_m$) and empirical top-k selection frequency ($f_m$) for each adapter.
The overall objective is $L = L_{act} + \lambda_{vid}L_{vid} + \lambda_{bal}L_{bal}$, where $\lambda_{vid}$ and $\lambda_{bal}$ are scaling factors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across simulation and real-world experiments:

#### 4.1. LIBERO-Plus Zero-Shot Generalization Performance

- CKT-WAM achieved the best overall zero-shot performance on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, reaching an 86.1% total success rate. This performance surpassed prior VLA and WAM baselines.
- The model demonstrated strong robustness across diverse out-of-distribution dimensions, achieving the best results in camera (77.4%), language (86.7%), lighting (98.2%), layout (88.5%), and total success rate. It remained competitive on robot, background, and noise perturbations.
- This suggests that the transferred teacher knowledge effectively improves instruction grounding and generalization to unseen visual conditions.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Parameter-Efficient Adaptation Methods

- CKT-WAM outperformed various representative parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) baselines, including LoRA, PiSSA, DoRA, MiLoRA, Mona, AdaMoLE, and GOAT, on LIBERO-Plus.
- It achieved a total success rate of 86.1% with only 1.17% trainable parameters, which is the smallest fraction among the compared PEFT methods. For instance, it improved upon LoRA's 82.8% while using less than half its trainable parameters (1.17% vs. 2.59%).
- CKT-WAM's performance nearly matched full fine-tuning (86.1% vs. 86.3% total success rate) while using a significantly smaller parameter budget. It even surpassed full fine-tuning on robot, light, and layout shifts.
- These results indicate CKT-WAM's superior trade-off between adaptation efficiency and zero-shot generalization.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study on LIBERO-Plus revealed the contribution of each CKT-WAM component:
- **Without Specialized Adapters:** Removing this component led to the largest degradation in total success rate (82.7% vs. 86.1%), indicating the importance of instance-dependent specialization for diverse distribution shifts.
- **Without Generalized Adapter:** This also caused a noticeable drop in total success rate (83.0% vs. 86.1%), suggesting that the shared transfer branch provides a stable foundation for knowledge injection.
- **Without Auxiliary Loss:** Removing the load-balancing loss resulted in the smallest degradation (84.1% vs. 86.1%), implying its role primarily as a regularizer for routing stability rather than a main driver of performance gains.
- **Teacher Intermediate Layer Selection:** Experiments showed that the success rate improved significantly when extracting context from deeper teacher layers, peaking around layer $\ell^*=20$ (out of 40 layers) before saturating. This layer also offered a favorable latency-success trade-off.

#### 4.4. Real-World Experiments

- CKT-WAM demonstrated strong real-world long-horizon manipulation capabilities, achieving the best average success rate of 83.3% across four multi-step tasks: clothes folding, fruit sorting, cube catching, and unmanned retail.
- It outperformed baselines like $\pi^{0.5}$, Cosmos Policy, and Fast-WAM. While $\pi^{0.5}$ slightly surpassed CKT-WAM on the challenging clothes folding task, CKT-WAM achieved the best success rates on fruit sorting (88.9%), cube catching (86.7%), and unmanned retail (84.4%).
- Analysis of specialized adapter selection patterns during the "cube catching" task revealed dynamic, stage-dependent activation, with different adapters specializing in specific sub-stages (e.g., drawer opening, cube grasping), supporting the effectiveness of sparse routing.

#### 4.5. LIBERO Benchmark Results

- On the standard LIBERO simulation benchmark, CKT-WAM achieved an average success rate of 98.8%, slightly improving over the previous strongest baseline, Cosmos Policy (98.5%). It showed consistent gains on Spatial, Goal, and Long task suites.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CKT-WAM framework introduces a novel and effective approach to knowledge transfer between World Action Models (WAMs), addressing critical challenges in the field of embodied intelligence and robotic control.

### 6. Significance

1. **Parameter Efficiency:** A primary contribution is the demonstration of highly efficient knowledge transfer. By updating only 1.17% of the total parameters (relative to combined teacher and student models), CKT-WAM achieves performance comparable to full fine-tuning, which uses 100% of parameters. This efficiency is crucial for the practical deployment of large WAMs in resource-constrained environments.
2. **Architectural Flexibility:** The context knowledge transfer mechanism, which injects compact teacher knowledge through the student's native conditioning pathway, circumvents the rigid architectural constraints and interface mismatches that hinder traditional distillation methods between heterogeneous models. This flexibility allows for broader applicability across diverse WAM architectures.
3. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The consistent improvements in zero-shot generalization across various out-of-distribution dimensions on LIBERO-Plus highlight CKT-WAM's ability to imbue student models with robust and transferable world knowledge from the teacher. This is particularly valuable for real-world robotics, where robots encounter varied and often novel conditions.
4. **Improved Long-Horizon Real-World Manipulation:** The strong performance in multi-step, long-horizon real-world tasks (e.g., clothes folding, fruit sorting) indicates that the transferred contextual knowledge facilitates more effective planning and sequential execution, which are complex capabilities for robotic systems.
5. **Modular and Interpretable Transfer:** The use of generalized and sparsely activated specialized adapters, coupled with a routing mechanism, suggests a more structured and potentially interpretable way of transferring knowledge. The ablation studies and analysis of adapter selection patterns provide insights into how different types of knowledge (task-agnostic vs. instance-dependent) contribute to overall performance.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Democratization of Advanced Robotics:** By making powerful WAM capabilities accessible to smaller, more efficient student models, CKT-WAM could lower the computational barrier to deploying sophisticated robotic control systems, potentially leading to wider adoption in various industries.
- **Accelerated Model Development:** The parameter-efficient transfer mechanism could significantly reduce the time and resources required to adapt or upgrade robotic policies, allowing for faster iteration and deployment cycles.
- **Foundation for Future Transfer Mechanisms:** The concept of compact contextual transfer, rather than full representation matching or output imitation, could serve as a foundational principle for knowledge transfer in other multimodal AI domains beyond embodied control, especially where large, heterogeneous foundation models are involved.
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** The ability to generalize to unseen conditions and perform complex, long-horizon tasks robustly contributes directly to the advancement of autonomous robotic systems, making them more reliable and capable in unstructured environments.

While the current work focuses on embodied control, the authors note that the core design of CKT-WAM is not inherently tied to robotics. Future work could investigate its generalization to broader multimodal settings such as text modeling, visual question answering, or other non-robotic generative tasks, further expanding its impact.
