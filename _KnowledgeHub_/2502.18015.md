# $\texttt{SPIN}$: distilling $\texttt{Skill-RRT}$ for long-horizon prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> SPIN, a framework from KAIST, combines planning and imitation learning to tackle long-horizon prehensile and non-prehensile robotic manipulation tasks. It achieved 93-98% task success rates in simulation and 80-90% on a real Franka robot across three complex domains by distilling robust plans from a Skill-RRT planner, aided by noise-based data filtering and learned skill-bridging "connectors".

## Problem

- Robotic systems struggle with long-horizon tasks that require complex sequences of prehensile and non-prehensile skills, often involving transient object repositioning and contact-rich interactions.
- Existing methods face challenges with "state gaps" where the termination state of one skill is not directly compatible with the initiation state of the next, risking object disturbance.
- Online planning algorithms are too computationally expensive for real-time deployment, while end-to-end learning methods suffer from reward sparsity and data inefficiency in complex, long-horizon scenarios.

## Method

- SPIN utilizes a two-stage framework: a simulator-based ==Skill-RRT== planner generates diverse skill plans, which are then distilled into a reactive policy using imitation learning with a Diffusion Policy.
- Goal-conditioned "connectors" are trained via reinforcement learning to robustly bridge state gaps between different pre-trained manipulation skills, minimizing object disturbance during transitions.
- A noise-based data filtering mechanism replays Skill-RRT plans with perturbations, selecting only high-quality, robust trajectories for the imitation learning dataset to enhance policy resilience.

## Results

- SPIN achieved simulation success rates of **95%** (Card Flip), **93%** (Bookshelf), and **98%** (Kitchen), outperforming various RL and PAMDP baselines which showed 0-**78%** success.
- The distilled SPIN policy reduced online computation time from 79-121 seconds (for Skill-RRT) to an average of **2.68**-**3.02** seconds, enabling real-time operation.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Franka Research 3 robot demonstrated high real-world success rates of **85%** (Card Flip), **90%** (Bookshelf), and **80%** (Kitchen).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly addressing skill transition "state gaps" with learned, contact-aware connectors is crucial for robustly chaining diverse prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation actions.
> - Combining the exploratory power of RRT-based planning with the efficiency of imitation learning enables solving long-horizon tasks while overcoming limitations of both pure planning and end-to-end learning.
> - Filtering planning solutions based on their robustness to noise during replay significantly improves the quality of demonstration data, leading to learned policies with higher success rates and better zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Current robotic manipulation systems encounter challenges in executing long-horizon tasks that necessitate sequences of both prehensile (P) and non-prehensile (NP) skills, involve complex contact-rich interactions, and demand long-term reasoning. Such tasks, common in human daily life (e.g., manipulating objects on a crowded shelf), often require transient object repositioning to enable subsequent actions, leading to "narrow passages" in the state space.

Existing approaches to these problems include:
- **Learning-based methods (Reinforcement Learning - RL, Imitation Learning - IL)**: While showing promise, RL struggles with long horizons, large action spaces, and the difficulty of designing rewards for narrow passages where objects must temporarily move away from the goal. IL, though circumventing exploration, scales poorly due to the expense of collecting diverse, long-horizon demonstrations.
- **Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL)**: Approaches like the options framework can sometimes collapse to primitive actions, failing to achieve temporal abstraction. Goal-conditioned HRL often suffers from non-stationarity during training and the problem of proposing unachievable subgoals for low-level policies.
- **Parameterized-Action Markov Decision Processes (PAMDPs)**: These methods leverage pre-trained skills but frequently assume that the terminal state of one skill directly aligns with the applicable state of the next, often overlooking "state gaps." Some PAMDP variants introduce low-level primitives to bridge these gaps, but training such fine-grained motion policies for large state discrepancies remains difficult.
- **Task and Motion Planning (TAMP)**: TAMP integrates symbolic task planning with continuous motion planning. While effective for long-horizon problems, it typically requires predefined symbolic representations of skill preconditions and effects, which are challenging to define for contact-rich NP skills with stochastic outcomes. RRT-based skill planners exist but often differ in how skills are learned, state gaps are handled, and online computation time is addressed.
- **Learning from Planning Solutions**: This area involves generating imitation learning data from planners. A core challenge is that not all planning solutions are robust; low-quality trajectories can degrade policy performance. Existing filtering methods may not be suitable for contact-rich environments, and multimodal solutions require specialized learning algorithms.

This work, SPIN, positions itself by addressing these limitations through a hybrid approach. It extends Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) into Skill-RRT for planning sequences of P and NP skills, explicitly tackles the "state gap" problem between skills using learned "connectors," and then distills these computationally intensive planning solutions into a fast, reactive policy using imitation learning. A key innovation is a noise-based replay mechanism for filtering high-quality planning data, which improves the robustness and real-world transferability of the learned policy, differentiating it from prior work on learning from planning solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient robotic policy capable of solving complex, long-horizon prehensile–non-prehensile (PNP) manipulation tasks. These tasks involve moving an object from an arbitrary initial state to a specified target pose using a sequence of P and NP skills.

The motivation for this work arises from several challenges inherent in such tasks:
- **Complexity of Long-Horizon Tasks**: PNP problems demand intricate reasoning about both the feasibility of individual skills and their optimal chaining. This includes scenarios where an object must be temporarily moved away from its ultimate goal to facilitate a grasp or other manipulation, posing significant difficulties for traditional RL approaches due to reward sparsity and exploration challenges in high-dimensional action spaces.
- **Bridging State Gaps between Skills**: Independently trained P and NP skills often conclude in robot configurations that are not directly compatible with the initiation conditions of the next skill. For instance, a sliding skill might end with the gripper closed and in contact with the object, while a subsequent grasping skill requires the gripper to be open and positioned for a pre-grasp. Naively moving the robot to bridge this "state gap" can disturb the object, leading to task failure. A robust method is required to intelligently break and make contacts while transitioning between skills.
- **Computational Cost of Online Planning**: While planning algorithms like RRT can find long sequences of robot configurations and navigate narrow passages, their significant online computation time renders them impractical for real-time deployment in dynamic, real-world environments.
- **Quality and Multimodality of Demonstrations for Imitation Learning**: When generating demonstration data from planners for imitation learning, not all generated trajectories are equally robust or effective. Low-quality or sensitive plans can degrade the performance of a learned policy. Furthermore, for a given initial and goal state, a planner might generate multiple valid, but distinct, solution paths, necessitating a learning algorithm capable of handling such multimodality.
- **Zero-shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The goal is for policies trained entirely in simulation to directly transfer to physical robots without additional real-world training, which requires addressing the inherent sim-to-real gap through techniques like domain randomization and robust data filtering.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of the SPIN framework are:
1. **Develop Skill-RRT**: A novel planning algorithm that extends RRT to incorporate skill applicability checks and intermediate object pose sampling, enabling it to find solutions for complex PNP problems.
2. **Introduce Connectors**: Create goal-conditioned policies capable of robustly bridging state gaps between diverse pre-trained skills by minimizing object disturbance during robot transitions.
3. **Generate High-Quality Demonstrations**: Utilize Skill-RRT and the learned connectors to automatically generate a large corpus of effective skill plans (demonstrations).
4. **Distill Plans into a Policy**: Employ imitation learning to distill these computationally expensive skill plans into a reactive, efficient policy suitable for online execution.
5. **Enhance Policy Robustness**: Implement a noise-based data filtering mechanism to select only robust skill plans for distillation, thereby improving the learned policy's resilience to real-world uncertainties.
6. **Enable Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Design the entire framework with domain randomization and a suitable policy architecture (Diffusion Policy) to facilitate direct transfer from simulation to real-world robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPIN (Skill Planning to INference) is a two-stage framework designed to solve long-horizon PNP manipulation problems. It comprises a data-generation stage utilizing a simulator-based planner and a distillation stage for learning a policy via imitation learning.

#### 3.1. Pre-trained Manipulation Skills

The framework assumes a library $K = \{K_P, K_{NP_1}, \ldots, K_{NP_n}\}$ of pre-trained, goal-conditioned manipulation skills. Each skill $K$ is defined by a tuple $(ϕ, π)$, where:
- $π: S \times Q_{obj} \rightarrow A$ is a goal-conditioned policy, trained using reinforcement learning (PPO), that maps a current state $s$ and a desired object pose $q_{obj}$ to a low-level action (sequence of joint torques). These policies include a pre-contact component ($K.π_{pre}$) for computing initial robot configurations and a post-contact component ($K.π_{post}$) for executing the manipulation.
- $ϕ: S \times Q_{obj} \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$ is an applicability checker that determines if a skill can be executed from state $s$ to achieve target object pose $q_{obj}$ (e.g., checking grasp feasibility or region constraints).
Specific NP skills (e.g., slide, topple, push, sink, cupboard) and P skills (pick-and-place) are defined for each experimental domain, with their respective state spaces, action spaces, and reward functions tailored for PPO training.

#### 3.2. Training Connectors for Bridging State Gaps

A core challenge is the "state gap" between the termination state of one skill and the applicable initiation state of the next. To address this, SPIN introduces **connectors**—goal-conditioned policies trained to break contact and move the robot to a desired end-effector pose with minimal disturbance to the object.
- **Data Collection for Connectors (Lazy Skill-RRT)**: To generate relevant training problems for connectors, a variant called Lazy Skill-RRT is employed. During its planning process, Lazy Skill-RRT temporarily assumes the existence of connectors by directly "teleporting" the robot to the next skill's applicable configuration. The states where these teleports occur are logged, indicating specific state gap problems that connectors need to solve.
- **Connector Training**: A separate connector policy $π_C$ is trained for each skill transition using PPO. Given the current robot state and the desired pre-skill robot configuration (extracted from the logged teleportation points), the connector learns to move the robot. The reward function for connectors includes dense rewards for achieving the target end-effector and gripper tip positions, a penalty for object movement, and a success reward. This training focuses solely on states relevant to the identified state gaps.

#### 3.3. Generating Skill Plans with Skill-RRT

With the trained connectors, Skill-RRT is used to generate complete solutions (skill plans) to PNP problems.
- **Algorithm Overview**: Skill-RRT extends the standard RRT algorithm by operating in a joint space of skills and object subgoals. It constructs a tree where nodes represent states and applied skills.
- **Node Expansion**: In each iteration, Skill-RRT randomly samples a skill $K$ and a desired object pose $q_{obj}$. It then identifies the nearest existing node $v_{near}$ in the tree from which $K$ is applicable (using $K.ϕ$).
- **Extend Function**:
 1. It first computes the target pre-skill robot configuration $q'_{r}$ for $K$ using $K.π_{pre}$ given $v_{near}.s.q_{obj}$ and $q_{obj}$.
 2. Instead of directly teleporting, a trained connector $π_C$ (corresponding to $K$) is executed from $v_{near}.s$ to move the robot to $q'_{r}$, resulting in an intermediate connecting state $v_{connect}.s$. This ensures a robust transition that minimizes object disturbance.
 3. From $v_{connect}.s$, the post-contact policy $K.π_{post}$ is simulated with $q_{obj}$ as the goal to produce a new state $s'$.
 4. If the skill execution is successful, $v_{connect}$ and a new node $v'$ (representing $s'$) are added to the tree.
- **Goal Checking**: Periodically, the algorithm checks if the ultimate goal object pose $q_{g\_obj}$ is sufficiently close to any node in the tree. If so, the path from the root to that node is retraced to form a complete skill plan.
- **Parallelization (Skill-RRT-Batch)**: A GPU-accelerated version of Skill-RRT is used to significantly speed up the data generation process.

#### 3.4. Distilling Skill-RRT into a Policy (SPIN)

To overcome the computational expense of Skill-RRT for online deployment, its generated skill plans are distilled into a reactive policy using imitation learning.
- **Demonstration Data Collection**: A dataset of (state, action) pairs is collected from successful skill plans generated by Skill-RRT.
- **Noise-Based Data Filtering**: To ensure robustness and high quality of the demonstrations, a filtering mechanism is applied:
 1. Each generated skill plan is replayed $N$ times in simulation.
 2. During replay, Gaussian or uniform random noise is injected into states, joint torques, and physical parameters (e.g., friction, mass).
 3. Only skill plans that achieve a replay success rate above a predefined threshold $m$ (e.g., $m=0.9$) are retained for the training dataset. This process filters out plans that are sensitive to small deviations, leading to a more robust distilled policy.
- **Imitation Learning Policy (Diffusion Policy)**: To address the multimodality inherent in planning solutions (different intermediate subgoals can lead to the same ultimate goal), a Diffusion Policy is employed. This generative model is suitable for learning complex, multimodal action distributions.
- **State and Action Representation**: The state and action representations for the distilled policy are adapted for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The state includes robot joint positions, end-effector/gripper keypoints (both absolute and relative to the object), object keypoint positions, previous actions, and gripper action executability. Actions consist of changes in robot joint positions (avoiding IK computation), proportional gains, damping values, and target gripper width.
- **Domain Randomization**: Extensive domain randomization is applied during skill training, Skill-RRT execution, and data collection. This includes perturbing object poses, robot joint positions, end-effector poses, environment friction, robot end-effector surface friction, object mass, and commanded torques, all aimed at enhancing sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The SPIN framework was evaluated across three challenging long-horizon manipulation domains: Card Flip, Bookshelf, and Kitchen.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

Comparison with several baselines (PPO, HLPS, MAPLE, Skill-RRT, SPIN-w/o-filtering) demonstrated the following:
- **RL Baselines (PPO, HLPS)**: Both PPO (flat RL) and HLPS (hierarchical RL) achieved 0% success rates across all domains. This highlights their inability to solve long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks involving narrow passages and complex skill chaining, even with extensive training data.
- **PAMDP Baseline (MAPLE)**: MAPLE, which leverages pre-trained skills, achieved 78% success in the Bookshelf domain. However, it failed (0% success) in Card Flip and Kitchen domains. This indicates that while PAMDPs benefit from abstracted skills, they struggle significantly when tasks involve very narrow regions of intermediate object poses critical for task success, as found in the Card Flip (card at table edge) and Kitchen (cup in sink for specific grasp) scenarios.
- **Skill-RRT (Planner Only)**: The Skill-RRT planner achieved 39% success in Card Flip, 66% in Bookshelf, and 64% in Kitchen. While demonstrating the ability to navigate these complex problems by leveraging skills and RRT's exploration, its average plan computation times were substantial (85.3 ± 48.7s for Card Flip, 79.2 ± 67.1s for Bookshelf, 121 ± 39.5s for Kitchen), rendering it unsuitable for real-time deployment.
- **SPIN-w/o-filtering (Distillation without Filtering)**: This variant achieved 82% success in Card Flip, 83% in Bookshelf, and 87% in Kitchen. Crucially, its computation time was significantly reduced to an average of 2.68-3.02 seconds, making it practical for online use.
- **SPIN (Distillation with Noise-Based Filtering)**: SPIN achieved the highest success rates across all domains: 95% in Card Flip, 93% in Bookshelf, and 98% in Kitchen. Its computation times remained comparable to SPIN-w/o-filtering (2.68-3.02 seconds).
- **Impact of Data Filtering**: The comparison between SPIN and SPIN-w/o-filtering demonstrated approximately a 10% improvement in success rates due to the noise-based replay filtering mechanism. This indicates that filtering out plans sensitive to small execution errors (e.g., placing a card too close to the table edge) leads to more robust and higher-performing policies.
- **Data Efficiency**: SPIN required significantly fewer state-action pairs for training (approximately 0.003 billion) compared to the RL-based baselines (ranging from 0.15 to 2.5 billion), demonstrating its data-efficient nature.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

The distilled SPIN policies, trained exclusively in simulation, were deployed zero-shot on a real Franka Research 3 robot across the three domains.
- **Success Rates**: The real-world success rates were 85% (17/20) for Card Flip, 90% (18/20) for Bookshelf, and 80% (16/20) for Kitchen tasks.
- **Failure Analysis**: Out of nine total failures:
 - 55.5% (5/9) were attributed to unexpected contacts between the robot and object during non-prehensile skill execution, pushing the object into out-of-distribution states (e.g., cup trapped in sink).
 - 22.2% (2/9) resulted from hardware torque limit violations due to strong collisions with the environment.
 - 22.2% (2/9) involved geometric collisions between the object and the environment (e.g., book hitting a shelf during placement).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Connector Effectiveness**: Learned RL-based connectors significantly outperformed a Bi-RRT motion planner for bridging state gaps. Connectors achieved 90-99% success for NP to P transitions and 78-94% for P to NP transitions, while Bi-RRT showed lower success (0-80% for NP to P, 61-86% for P to NP). Motion planner failures stemmed from difficulties in precisely following paths, issues with static scene assumptions leading to continued contact, and computational expense in cluttered environments, justifying the use of RL for robust contact handling.
- **Filtering Method Impact**: The noise-based replay filtering with a high threshold (m=0.9) yielded the highest policy success rates (94-98%), compared to no filtering (79-95%) or a lower threshold (m=0.1, 82-87%). Qualitative analysis of skill plans revealed that the filtering process selected more robust intermediate object poses (e.g., card positioned optimally for graspability and stability, appropriate book toppling angles, and favorable cup handle orientations). While higher filtering thresholds increased data collection time per skill plan, the strategy of generating multiple successful trajectories from each robust plan improved overall data collection efficiency.
- **Imitation Learning Policy Architectures**: A comparison of different IL architectures (ResNet, LSTM+GMM, cVAE, Transformer, Diffusion Policy) indicated that while Diffusion Policy achieved the highest overall performance, especially in the highly diverse Card Flip domain (95% vs 54-63% for others), other architectures like ResNet, LSTM+GMM, and Transformer also demonstrated strong performance in Bookshelf and Kitchen (85-99%). This suggests that the framework's effectiveness is not solely reliant on Diffusion Policy, but it represents an optimal choice among viable options. cVAE consistently showed poor performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by presenting a comprehensive and effective framework for addressing long-horizon prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation tasks.

- **Advancing Long-Horizon Manipulation**: SPIN provides a structured solution for problems that are beyond the current capabilities of many end-to-end learning methods, particularly those requiring complex sequences of diverse manipulation skills and careful handling of object-robot-environment contacts.
- **Effective Integration of Planning and Learning**: The framework successfully combines the strengths of planning algorithms (Skill-RRT's ability to explore and navigate complex state spaces, including narrow passages) with the efficiency and generalization capabilities of learned policies (through imitation learning). This hybrid approach mitigates the online computational burden of planning while overcoming the exploration and data challenges of end-to-end learning for complex tasks.
- **Robust Skill Chaining**: The introduction and robust training of "connectors" directly addresses a critical and often overlooked problem: bridging the "state gaps" between independently trained skills. This mechanism enables reliable sequencing of diverse prehensile and non-prehensile actions without requiring explicit symbolic definitions of skill preconditions and effects, which can be difficult to specify for contact-rich interactions.
- **Enhanced Real-World Applicability**: The noise-based data filtering strategy significantly improves the quality and robustness of the generated demonstration data. This, coupled with extensive domain randomization and the use of a Diffusion Policy, enables the learned policies to achieve high zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capabilities, a key requirement for practical robotic deployment.
- **Data Efficiency**: Compared to traditional reinforcement learning methods, SPIN demonstrates significantly greater data efficiency by leveraging a planner to generate high-quality, diverse demonstrations, thereby reducing the immense data requirements typically associated with learning complex robotic behaviors.

The potential impact of this work includes facilitating the development of robots capable of more autonomous and dexterous manipulation in unstructured environments, such as homes or warehouses. By enabling robots to perform complex, multi-stage tasks involving both grasping and pushing/sliding, SPIN could contribute to advancements in areas like assistive robotics, logistics, and manufacturing, where flexible and robust object manipulation is essential.
