## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly complex dynamic tasks such as throwing, is an area of ongoing research. Historically, much of the work in robotic throwing has focused on systems with limited degrees of freedom, primarily stationary arm-mounted manipulators. These approaches often employ analytical solutions with trajectory optimization techniques or learning-based methods to control the arm and release parameters, typically for targets on a 2D plane. Examples include studies that achieve high accuracy for fixed-distance throws by optimizing release velocities or parameterizing release speeds based on target position.

However, these arm-only or constrained solutions often restrict the agility and force generation potential available through the robot's entire body. While some prior research has explored integrating a mobile base, such as wheeled robots, or using trajectory optimization for armed quadrupeds to leverage body momentum, these often lack generalization to arbitrary 3D targets or face computational challenges with high-dimensional action spaces.

The broader field of "loco-manipulation" explores tasks requiring simultaneous locomotion and manipulation, often utilizing deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to manage the high degrees of freedom (DoF) and complex dynamics of legged robots with arms. Existing loco-manipulation research has demonstrated full-body policies for tasks like walking, reaching, or grasping, where the robot body assists the manipulator by extending its range. However, dynamic tasks like throwing, which involve rapid, high-speed motions and significant stability challenges, present specific difficulties related to local minima in training and the need to balance aggressive movements with maintaining balance.

This work contributes to the landscape by specifically addressing the gap in full-body, dynamic throwing with legged manipulators (loco-manipulators). It moves beyond arm-only systems and 2D target constraints, offering a DRL-based solution that leverages the full-body dynamics of high-DoF robots to achieve throwing towards arbitrary 3D targets while actively managing stability. The paper integrates and refines techniques from DRL, loco-manipulation, and robotic throwing to enable more agile and capable robotic interactions in complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the practical utility and inherent challenges of robotic throwing, particularly in advanced real-world scenarios. Throwing an object offers several advantages over carrying it, such as faster transport and the ability to deliver objects to otherwise inaccessible locations. These capabilities are relevant for various applications, including emergency response, medical supply delivery, or logistical operations.

However, implementing complex dynamic manipulation tasks like throwing in robots presents difficulties:
- **Nonlinearity and Uncertainty:** High-speed movements are characterized by complex non-linear dynamics and uncertainties, requiring precise real-time control and actuators capable of high torque and accuracy.
- **Limited Capabilities of Traditional Setups:** Most existing robotic throwing systems rely on fixed or arm-mounted manipulators. While effective for certain tasks, these setups inherently limit the achievable throwing range, force, and overall agility that could be gained from full-body motion.
- **High Degrees of Freedom (DoF):** Legged manipulators possess many actuators (e.g., 13-19 DoF in the robots tested), resulting in high-dimensional action spaces. This complexity makes non-learning approaches, such as trajectory optimization or purely analytical methods, computationally and methodologically challenging to generalize across varied target locations and dynamic trajectories.
- **Stability-Performance Trade-off:** For legged robots, high-performance dynamic maneuvers, particularly throwing, introduce a significant risk of instability or falling. Balancing the objective of maximizing throwing capability with maintaining robot stability is a critical challenge.
- **Sparse Reward in DRL:** In the context of deep reinforcement learning (DRL), throwing tasks pose a sparse reward problem. The primary reward (accuracy and distance) is only evaluated once the projectile is released, occurring only a single time per episode. This delayed feedback can hinder efficient learning for RL algorithms.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:
- **Leveraging Full-Body Dynamics:** To investigate the advantages of utilizing all available motors and full-body dynamics for throwing with legged manipulators, in contrast to arm-only approaches.
- **Generalization to 3D Targets:** To develop a methodology that allows legged manipulators to throw objects accurately to any user-commanded target destination in 3D space, moving beyond the limitations of prior work focused on 2D planes.
- **Optimizing Accuracy and Stability via DRL:** To frame the throwing task as a deep reinforcement learning objective that simultaneously optimizes throwing accuracy and robot stability.
- **Efficient Learning with Adaptive Curriculum:** To introduce an optimized adaptive curriculum and a tailored RL environment setup designed to address the challenges of sparse rewards and local optima, thereby balancing throwing accuracy and stability during the learning process.
- **Comprehensive Evaluation and Real-world Transfer:** To conduct an extensive evaluation across different robot morphologies (humanoid and armed quadruped) in simulation, comparing full-body and arm-only throwing performance across various distances and angles, and to validate the learned controllers on a real humanoid platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework to train full-body joint-level controllers for dynamic throwing tasks. The methodology incorporates a custom environment setup and curriculum design to address the task's inherent complexities.

**A. Robot Platforms and Simulation Environment:**
Experiments were conducted using two distinct robot morphologies:
- **Humanoid Robot ("Chuck"):** An in-house designed humanoid with 12 DoF, equipped with two soft, three-finger FinRay grippers, each actuated by a single servo. Its DoFs are located in the ankles, knees, hips, shoulders (two axes each), and elbows.
- **Armed Quadruped ("ANYmal C"):** An ANYbotics ANYmal C quadruped integrated with a Kinova Gen3 six-degree-of-freedom arm and a three-finger gripper. This configuration results in 19 DoF.

All DRL training was performed in the Isaac Lab simulation environment, which leverages massively parallel GPU-based physics simulation. The chosen DRL algorithm was a massively parallel PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) implementation, following established practices in previous work.

**B. Throwing Task Definition:**
The task is defined as throwing a projectile to a target position.
- **Error Metric:** Throwing displacement error (E) is calculated as the minimum Euclidean distance between the projectile's trajectory (after leaving the end-effector) and the desired target position (Equation 1). This minimum distance criterion considers diverse trajectories equally desirable if they achieve the same proximity to the target.
- **Target Parameterization:** Target positions (x_A, y_A, z_A) are parameterized using spherical coordinates: a normalized polar angle (˜θ), an azimuthal angle (ϕ), and a radius (r). These are converted to Euclidean coordinates for evaluation.
- **Task Types:**
 - **Distance Throw:** ˜θ and ϕ are fixed to 0, meaning targets are directly in front of the robot on the ground plane, varying only by radius r.
 - **Generalised Throw:** ˜θ ∈ and ϕ ∈ [0, 2π], allowing targets anywhere above the ground plane at a given distance r. For both tasks, r is sampled within a specified range which linearly increases during training.

**C. Reinforcement Learning Formulation:**
- **Objective:** The policy (π) is optimized to maximize the discounted cumulative return.
- **Reward Function:** A composite reward function was used: r = λ1 * r_throwing + λ2 * r_stability + λ3 * r_roll.
 - **r_throwing:** This is a sparse reward, calculated as 1 - min(E/r, 1), based on the normalized throw displacement. Crucially, this reward is computed using projectile motion equations (with or without a drag model, but standard equations were used for simplicity) immediately after the ball's release, rather than waiting for its full trajectory completion in simulation. This reduces the delay between action and feedback.
 - **r_stability:** This is a sparse binary reward (1 for stable, 0 for unstable) awarded at the end of an episode. Stability is defined by meeting criteria such as preventing non-foot collisions, maintaining base height, and successfully releasing the ball.
 - **r_roll (Humanoid-Specific):** A dense, continuous penalty function designed for the humanoid to counteract significant roll rotation, a particular stability challenge due to its under-actuated legs. This dense feedback aids in maximizing the overall stability reward.
- **State Representation:** The policy receives observations including linear velocity from the IMU, target command (˜θ, ϕ, r), motor state (relative joint position and velocity), and the previous action. Additionally, it receives a binary 'ball released' flag and an estimated throwing displacement (a privileged observation in simulation, gradually replaced with noise during a curriculum to improve sim2real transfer).
- **Action Representation:** Actions are position commands for the robot's motors, implemented through a simulation of DC motor dynamics provided by Isaac Lab for improved sim2real transfer. An additional action controls the timing of ball release.
- **Neural Network Configuration:** A recurrent symmetric Actor-Critic network architecture was utilized with DPPO. The network comprised layers of sizes with ELU activation. Specific hyperparameters for clipping, entropy, optimizer (AdamW), and mini-batches were defined.

**D. Curriculum Design:**
An adaptive curriculum was introduced to balance throwing accuracy and stability, accelerate convergence, and mitigate local optima.
- **Adaptive Thresholds:** Unlike manually tuned curricula, the performance (throwing accuracy and stability) thresholds for advancing the curriculum difficulty were learned via an optimization process (using asynchronous TPE optimization, a black-box approach).
- **Difficulty Progression:** When criteria are met, the difficulty advances by 1% of the distance range.
- **Quadruped Curriculum (Distance Throw):** The target position was fixed to the maximum distance from the start to encourage arm momentum. Body actions were gradually introduced (0% to 100% in 2% increments) for non-arm motors to promote arm-centric momentum generation initially. The wait time for the stability reward after ball release was incrementally increased (0 to 2 seconds) to allow initial rapid policy convergence.
- **Humanoid Curriculum (Distance Throw):** The target distance was incrementally increased from 4 meters to a specified maximum distance, allowing the policy to learn stability at shorter ranges before tackling more challenging long-range throws.
- **General Throw Curriculum:** Identical to the distance throw curriculum but with a linearly widening range of uniformly sampled target distances from [1m, 3m] to [1m, max dist].

**E. Experimental Setup Details:**
- **Simulation Parameters:** 4092 parallel environments were used. Ball position was artificially fixed to the hand until a release command, followed by a 100ms delay to simulate real-world gripper opening. Policy actions were clipped to joint operating ranges.
- **Control Gains:** PD gains were specified for base and arm of both robots (e.g., Humanoid base 40/1, arm 10/1; Quadruped base 80/1, arm 20/1).
- **Initialization:** Robots were initialized in stable postures.
- **Domain Randomization:** Applied to assist sim2real transfer and policy exploration, including variations in joint initial positions, linear velocity noise, joint position/velocity noise, and body mass.
- **Stability Enhancement:** Policy actions were zeroed 250ms after ball release, allowing the robot to return to a home state for simplified stability management.

**F. Ablation Study:**
An ablation study was conducted to assess the importance of various system components and parameters using black-box optimization (Optuna with TPE). The objective function for optimization combined throwing accuracy and a clipped stability rate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the developed methods encompassed simulation experiments on both robot platforms and real-world validation on the humanoid robot.

**A. Simulation Experiments Results:**
- **General Throwing Task Accuracy:** For targets sampled from U(1, 5) meters (humanoid) and U(3, 8) meters (quadruped), full-body policies consistently achieved greater accuracy than arm-only policies across all tested distances.
 - For the general policies, the average throwing error for the Humanoid Full Body (Stable) was 0.914 ± 0.143 m. For the Quadruped Full Body, it was 0.550 ± 0.013 m, which compares to an Arm Only error of 0.740 ± 0.014 m.
- **Behavioral Observations:** Full-body controllers demonstrated complex dynamic behaviors to improve performance. These included utilizing stored elastic energy, counterbalancing with the non-throwing arm (on the humanoid), and employing foot steps for stability. Arm-only policies also generated momentum through "winding-up" motions.
- **Accuracy Across Throwing Angles:** When comparing full-body and arm-only policies across discretized throwing angles (ϕ and θ) at fixed distances (5m for humanoid, 8m for quadruped), full-body policies exhibited greater accuracy in over 99% of throwing angles. The humanoid's full-body policy showed notable improvements for throws directly to its left or right.
- **Stability:** Average stability rates (defined by maintaining base height, avoiding non-foot collisions, and successful ball release) were measured:
 - Humanoid (General Throw): 84.2 ± 5.89% stable.
 - Quadruped (General Throw): 81.8 ± 3.4% stable.
 - Stability varied with target distance, particularly for the humanoid, highlighting the challenge of dynamic balance during long-range throws. For example, humanoid stability at 4m was 98.1%, but dropped to 29.0% at 8m.
- **Ablation Study:** The black-box optimization identified the parameters of the adaptive curriculum schedule and the reward coefficients as particularly important for policy performance. The projectile motion reward prediction, which calculates reward immediately upon release, contributed to a 55.2% improvement in throwing accuracy and an 84.2% improvement in stability for the general humanoid policies.

**B. Real-world Experiments Results:**
- **Humanoid Policy Transfer:** A learned full-body policy was successfully transferred to the real "Chuck" humanoid robot.
- **Accuracy and Stability:** For a 4-meter target, the real-world policy achieved an average throwing error of 80 cm over 5 trials, with a best run of 6 cm. All 5 trials were stable, meaning the robot did not fall.
- **Payload Handling:** The robot maintained stability and comparable accuracy when throwing a 1.25 kg payload.
- **Extended Range:** A humanoid policy trained to throw 12 meters in simulation was deployed in an open-loop setting on the real hardware, achieving a throw distance of 13.8 meters. This exceeded the maximum stable distance achieved by an arm-only policy by 4.95 meters.
- **Limitations:** Sensor misalignment was identified as a difficulty, especially for policies exhibiting rapid movements on the real hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of robotics, particularly in dynamic manipulation and reinforcement learning, with implications for various applications.

**Advancements in Robotic Capabilities:**
- **Enhanced Performance:** The work demonstrates that leveraging full-body dynamics in legged manipulators significantly enhances throwing range, accuracy, and stability compared to traditional arm-only methods. This expansion of capabilities allows robots to perform more complex and robust object manipulation tasks.
- **Generalization to 3D Space:** By enabling robots to throw to arbitrary targets in three-dimensional space, the research moves beyond the limitations of prior work that often confined throwing targets to 2D planes or predefined trajectories. This capability is crucial for robots operating in dynamic and unstructured real-world environments.

**Methodological Contributions:**
- **Novel DRL Formulation:** The paper introduces a deep reinforcement learning formulation for full-body robotic throwing that effectively coordinates manipulation and locomotion. This approach allows for the discovery of complex, dynamic movements that are difficult to achieve with conventional analytical or trajectory optimization methods, especially with high-DoF systems.
- **Adaptive Curriculum Learning:** The development of an optimized adaptive curriculum, with learned rather than manually tuned thresholds, provides a robust strategy for balancing the trade-off between aggressive, high-performance movements and maintaining robot stability. This method helps the DRL agent navigate sparse reward landscapes and avoid local minima, improving both convergence time and final policy performance.
- **Efficient Learning in Sparse-Reward Conditions:** The tailored RL environment setup, particularly the method of calculating throwing rewards using projectile motion equations immediately upon release, addresses the sparse reward challenge. This accelerates learning by providing more immediate feedback to the agent, which can be beneficial for other complex, dynamic tasks.
- **Sim2Real Transfer:** The successful transfer of learned policies from simulation to a real humanoid robot validates the robustness of the DRL approach, domain randomization, and the careful consideration of real-world dynamics in the simulation environment.

**Potential Impact and Applications:**
- **Logistics and Material Handling:** The ability to throw objects accurately and stably could enhance efficiency in warehouses, distribution centers, and construction sites, allowing for faster movement of goods over distances or into specific locations.
- **Disaster Response and Emergency Services:** Robots capable of dynamic throwing could deliver critical supplies (e.g., medical kits, safety lines, sensors) to inaccessible areas or across hazardous terrain where direct human access is risky or impossible.
- **Exploration and Remote Operations:** For robots in remote or hazardous environments, throwing objects could facilitate sampling, deployment of sensors, or interaction with distant elements.
- **Sports and Entertainment:** The developed techniques could be applied to robotic sports, human-robot interaction in games, or for developing more lifelike and agile robotic performers.
- **Foundation for Future Loco-Manipulation:** The insights gained regarding full-body dynamics, curriculum design for complex tasks, and stability management in dynamic scenarios lay a foundation for developing more advanced loco-manipulation capabilities in legged robots, extending beyond throwing to other dynamic interactions with the environment.

While challenges such as sensor misalignment in rapid real-world movements were noted, the research presents a step toward more versatile and dynamically capable robotic systems.
