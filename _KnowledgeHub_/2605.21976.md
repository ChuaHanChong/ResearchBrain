# TacO: Benchmarking Tactile Sensors for Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TacO is a real-world benchmark that evaluates six tactile sensors spanning resistive, magnetic, vision-based, and acoustic modalities by training Action Chunking Transformer imitation policies with modality-specific tactile encoders on three manipulation tasks. Vision-only and visuotactile policies share identical data, isolating tactile contribution. Tactile helps most on insertion and reorientation, while sensor material friction and signal relevance matter more than spatial resolution.

## Problem

- Broad agreement exists that touch aids manipulation, but no empirical guidance says which tactile sensor modality suits which manipulation task or policy.
- Prior tactile benchmarks evaluate hardware in isolation via calibration or perception tasks, mostly with a single high-resolution vision-based sensor, not policy learning.
- Differing embodiments, tasks, and learning pipelines across prior systems make it impossible to attribute manipulation gains to the sensing modality itself.

## Method

- Modular ACT imitation pipeline: CVAE transformer policy taking wrist and third-person RGB, proprioception, and tactile tokens, predicting 64-step action chunks executed receding-horizon.
- Sensor-specific tactile encoders: linear or MLP projection for scalar and array resistive/magnetic readings, ResNet18 or PCA for tactile images, mel-spectrogram plus MLP for contact-microphone audio.
- Two controlled analyses plus an open-source motorized indenter repeatability kit: per-sensor visuotactile-versus-vision-only comparison, and cross-sensor vision-only comparison isolating embodiment and material friction.

## Results

- Plug insertion gains were largest: eFlesh 0.3 to 0.7, contact microphone 0.2 to 0.7, FlexiTac 0.1 to 0.3 over vision-only baselines.
- Reorientation improved for all sensors (Daimon 0.2 to 0.7, eFlesh 0.5 to 0.8, FSR 0.6 to 0.8), converging near 80% regardless of resolution.
- Vision-only high-friction sensors beat low-friction ones on pick-and-place (0.81 versus 0.625) and insertion (0.25 versus 0.1), but lost on reorientation (0.35 versus 0.6).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task-relevant physical signal beats dense spatial resolution: a $5 scalar sensor can match a $965 high-resolution camera-based sensor on coarse manipulation.
> - Sensor embodiment is a confound: surface friction and compliance shift vision-only success by tens of points before any tactile signal is used.
> - Encoder must match signal type; image encoders on sparse taxel arrays, or PCA on tactile images, can collapse policy performance outright.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation has seen significant advancements through vision-based learning from demonstrations, enabling robots to perform complex tasks and engage in high-level semantic reasoning. However, these vision-centric approaches often prove insufficient for contact-rich manipulation scenarios, where physical interaction signals are critical for success. There is a general consensus within the robotics community that tactile sensing can significantly enhance manipulation capabilities.

Despite the widespread availability of diverse tactile sensors, a notable gap exists in empirical guidance regarding which tactile sensors are best suited for specific manipulation tasks and how different sensing modalities impact learned manipulation policies. Prior tactile benchmarks primarily focus on evaluating sensor hardware in isolation through calibration, characterization, or perceptual tasks such such as object reconstruction or pattern recognition. These benchmarks often use high-resolution visual tactile sensors and make it difficult to disentangle whether observed performance gains are due to the sensing modality, task design, or the learning pipeline itself. Consequently, researchers face challenges in selecting appropriate tactile sensors, particularly when balancing performance with practical considerations such as cost, accessibility, and integration complexity.

This work addresses this deficiency by providing a systematic, task-driven evaluation of tactile sensors within the context of robot manipulation policies. It moves beyond isolated sensor characterization to assess tactile sensors based on their contribution to policy performance in real-world, contact-rich tasks. By comparing multiple tactile modalities and isolating factors such as sensor material and spatial resolution, the research aims to offer empirical guidance on the practical utility of various tactile sensing approaches, thereby informing the design of more capable robot manipulation systems. It also explicitly investigates the conditions under which tactile sensing is beneficial and which specific tactile signals are most effective for policy learning, using a consistent teleoperation and policy training framework across diverse sensors and tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:

1. **Provide a Systematic, Task-Driven Evaluation:** Conduct a systematic assessment of tactile sensors specifically for robot manipulation, evaluating their performance based on the success of learned manipulation policies.
2. **Propose a Sensor Selection Framework:** Establish a framework for selecting and evaluating tactile sensors based on their ability to improve manipulation policy performance in various tasks. This involves analyzing how sensor properties (e.g., spatial resolution, shear sensing, tactile representation) and inherent material friction affect task outcomes.
3. **Empirically Guide Sensor Choice:** Offer empirical guidance on which tactile sensors are most appropriate for different manipulation tasks, addressing the current lack of clear recommendations in the field.
4. **Isolate Tactile Contribution and Embodiment Effects:** Develop analytical methods to differentiate the performance gains attributable to the tactile sensing signal itself from those caused by the physical properties (material, appearance, form factor) of the sensor's embodiment.
5. **Evaluate Reproducibility and Accessibility:** Assess the hardware repeatability of the benchmarked sensors and demonstrate the effectiveness of accessible, open-source tactile hardware, aiming to lower the barrier of entry for researchers integrating tactile sensing into their pipelines.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the recognized importance of tactile sensing for contact-rich manipulation and the current absence of empirical data to guide sensor selection. Existing benchmarks often focus on hardware characterization or perceptual tasks, making it difficult to directly apply their findings to policy learning for complex manipulation. This work is motivated by the need to understand when, and with what characteristics, tactile information provides measurable gains to visuomotor policies, thereby enabling the development of more robust and adaptable robotic manipulation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a comprehensive methodology involving an imitation learning pipeline, a diverse set of tactile sensors, multiple manipulation tasks, and specific analytical approaches.

**A. Overall Framework:**
The core framework involves training visuomotor policies using the Action Chunking Transformers (ACT) architecture. ACT is selected for its ability to capture multimodal demonstration distributions and its transformer-based encoder, which accommodates diverse input modalities as tokens. The pipeline is designed to be modular, supporting various tactile encoders.

**B. Tactile Sensors Evaluated:**
Six tactile sensors spanning four distinct modalities were evaluated:
- **Resistive:** Force Sensing Resistor (FSR), FlexiTac, eGain.
- **Magnetic:** eFlesh.
- **Visual:** Daimon.
- **Acoustic:** Contact Microphone.
These sensors represent a range of data dimensionalities, costs, and material properties.

**C. Manipulation Tasks:**
Three representative real-world manipulation tasks were selected to cover different challenges in contact-rich interaction:
1. **Pick-and-Place with Unknown Object Mass:** Involves grasping a visually identical can with either unknown light or heavy internal mass. Success requires grasping without slipping and accurate placement.
2. **Plug Insertion:** Requires inserting a 3D-printed plug into a socket with partial visual occlusion. This task emphasizes tactile cues beyond normal force, such as shear force or vibration, for successful alignment and insertion.
3. **Object Reorientation:** Tasks the robot with rotating a container on a table around a fixed point, necessitating continuous force modulation to maintain contact and prevent lifting or sliding.

**D. Policy Training:**
- **Data Collection:** Demonstrations were collected via teleoperation using either the GELLO or Factr systems. Each demonstration consisted of time-indexed sequences of observations and actions.
- **Observations:** Included RGB images from a wrist-mounted camera and a third-person camera, robot proprioception (gripper width, joint states), and tactile measurements from the specific sensor modality.
- **Policies:** For each task and sensor configuration, two independent policies were trained: a visuotactile policy (using all modalities) and a vision-only policy (where tactile data was removed). Both policies were trained on the same collected demonstration data.
- **Tactile Encoders:** Given the diverse nature of tactile signals, modality-specific encoders were employed:
 - **Raw Tactile Data (FSR, FlexiTac, eGain, eFlesh):** FSR's single scalar value was linearly projected. Higher-dimensional signals from FlexiTac, eGain, and eFlesh were encoded using Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs).
 - **Tactile Images (Daimon, FlexiTac):** Daimon (vision-based) produced RGB images and processed depth/deformation/shear images, which were encoded using PCA or ResNet18. FlexiTac's raw array was converted to a tactile image and encoded similarly.
 - **Acoustic Tactile (Contact Microphone):** High-frequency audio (44.1 kHz) was converted into a mel-spectrogram (128x87 dimensions) using Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), then flattened and encoded with an MLP.
- **Overall Training Pipeline:** RGB images were encoded using a ResNet18 backbone (ImageNet pre-trained), and proprioception was linearly projected. All encoded features (latent token, proprioception, tactile, image) were concatenated into tokens and processed by a transformer-based policy (4 encoder layers, 7 decoder layers). The policy predicted fixed-horizon action chunks (H=64) using a CVAE objective with reconstruction and regularization losses. AdamW optimizer was used, along with image augmentations, Gaussian normalization, and conditional masking for proprioception. Actions were executed in a receding-horizon manner (32 steps executed from a 64-step prediction).

**E. Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted across two institutions, both utilizing Franka Panda 7-DOF robot arms.
- **Institution 1:** Tested FSR, FlexiTac, eGain, Daimon, and eFlesh with the Gello teleoperation system, a custom motorized gripper, and finray fingers adapted for each sensor.
- **Institution 2:** Tested the contact microphone using the Factr teleoperation system with a standard Franka gripper and finray fingers.
Both setups included a wrist-mounted RGB camera and a top-down RGB camera, with end-effector position control via the franky library.

**F. Analyses Performed:**
1. **Per-Sensor Analysis (Intra-Task):** Compared visuotactile policies against vision-only policies for each sensor on each task to isolate the contribution of the tactile signal.
2. **Cross-Sensor Vision-Only Analysis:** Compared the performance of vision-only policies across different sensors to isolate the effects of sensor material, appearance, and form factor (embodiment effects) on task success.
3. **Hardware Repeatability Analysis:** Developed a low-cost, open-source test kit (Dynamixel motor, 3D-printed parts) to evaluate sensor consistency. This involved applying constant force to sensors over multiple episodes and measuring response time to 90% of maximum reading and standard deviation to characterize hysteresis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the utility of different tactile sensors in robotic manipulation.

**A. Intra-Task Performance:**

- **Pick-and-Place with Unknown Object Mass:**
 - Most visuotactile policies showed improved performance over vision-only baselines, though the improvement varied.
 - FSR visuotactile policies performed similarly to vision-only, with both exhibiting specific failure modes (lifting heavy objects for vision-only, dropping due to slip for tactile).
 - The Daimon tactile policy unexpectedly underperformed its vision-only counterpart, showing failure modes involving collisions.
 - Tactile sensing was primarily beneficial for disambiguating object mass, enabling policies to learn distinct gripper widths for light versus heavy objects, leading to larger gains for heavier objects.
- **Plug Insertion:**
 - All tactile policies demonstrated substantial improvements over vision-only baselines.
 - eFlesh and the contact microphone achieved significantly larger gains.
 - Vision and proprioception facilitated grasping and coarse alignment, while tactile sensing was crucial during the final, occluded insertion phase.
 - Vision-only policies often failed by incorrect plug placement and premature release.
 - Tactile policies, when failing, typically slid the plug along the socket or partially inserted it before terminating, indicating their use of contact state cues.
- **Object Reorientation:**
 - All tactile policies outperformed their vision-only counterparts.
 - Daimon exhibited the largest relative improvement, followed by eFlesh and FSR.
 - The FSR vision-only policy had a higher baseline, attributed to its low-friction surface, which was advantageous for this task where controlled slipping is desirable.
 - High-friction sensors (eFlesh, Daimon) in vision-only mode showed failure modes like lifting the object or preventing rotation due to excessive force.
 - Despite Daimon's higher spatial resolution and richer shear information, all tactile policies achieved comparable success rates around 80%, suggesting that the presence of appropriate tactile feedback is more critical than high spatial resolution or complex representations for continuous force-regulation tasks.

**B. Cross-Task Analysis:**

- **Sensor Material and Form Factor:**
 - Vision-only policies performed better with high-friction, compliant sensors (eFlesh, Daimon, contact microphone) across most tasks.
 - An exception was the object reorientation task, where low-friction sensors (FSR, FlexiTac) performed better under vision-only control because controlled slipping was advantageous.
 - When tactile feedback was introduced, performance gaps between high- and low-friction sensors narrowed, indicating that tactile sensing enabled policies to mitigate the downsides of increased friction by precisely regulating applied forces. This suggests that high-friction, compliant materials are generally beneficial for manipulation.
- **Spatial Resolution:**
 - Across the evaluated tasks, no significant performance difference was observed between high-spatial-resolution tactile sensors (FlexiTac, Daimon) and lower-resolution sensors (FSR, eFlesh, contact microphone) in terms of manipulation success.
 - This finding suggests that while high-resolution visual tactile sensors excel in perceptual tasks like contact localization or object recognition, these advantages may not directly translate to improved policy performance in the coarse object manipulation tasks studied. Access to task-relevant physical signals appeared more important than dense spatial representations.

**C. Sensor Repeatability:**

- **Consistency:** The FSR demonstrated the highest consistency across episodes. FlexiTac showed consistency when activated but its resting reading drifted. eGain and Daimon exhibited a "training phase" behavior, likely due to viscoelastic material properties. eFlesh showed consistent drift, potentially addressable through structural modifications. The contact microphone's signal was inherently noisy due to its high-frequency nature.
- **Correlation with Policy Success:** Daimon had the second lowest variance after FSR, indicating good reliability. However, this hardware reliability did not consistently correlate with higher policy success, further supporting the idea that task-relevant signal utility outweighs raw sensor repeatability in many manipulation contexts.
- **Cost-Effectiveness:** Open-source and low-cost sensors achieved performance comparable to more expensive or custom alternatives across tasks, suggesting that accessible tactile hardware can be effective for learning-based manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of robotic manipulation by offering empirical, task-driven guidance for the selection and integration of tactile sensors. Its significance and potential impact include:

- **Empirical Foundation for Sensor Selection:** The study provides concrete, real-world evidence of how different tactile sensing modalities affect robot manipulation policy performance. This fills a critical gap in the literature, moving beyond theoretical discussions or hardware characterization to offer practical recommendations for researchers and developers.
- **Promotion of Task-Driven Evaluation:** By proposing and executing a framework that evaluates sensors based on policy success in specific manipulation tasks, TacO encourages a more holistic and application-focused approach to tactile sensor benchmarking. This shifts the focus from isolated sensor capabilities to their actual utility in complex robotic systems.
- **Clarification of Tactile Benefits:** The findings clarify that the usefulness of tactile information is not universal but highly dependent on the sensor modality, the material properties of the sensor's embodiment, and the specific requirements of the manipulation task. This nuanced understanding can prevent misallocation of resources towards sensors that may be high-fidelity but not task-optimal.
- **Challenge to Assumptions on Resolution:** The observation that high spatial resolution does not always translate to superior policy performance in coarse manipulation tasks challenges a common assumption. This could lead to a re-evaluation of design priorities for tactile sensors, emphasizing relevant physical signals over raw data density for certain applications.
- **Validation of Accessible Hardware:** Demonstrating that accessible, open-source, and low-cost tactile sensors can achieve performance comparable to more expensive commercial systems is a significant finding. This has the potential to lower the barrier to entry for researchers and educational institutions, fostering broader adoption and innovation in tactile-enabled robotics.
- **Promotion of Reproducibility and Open Science:** The commitment to publicly releasing all sensors, code, data, and the hardware setup for repeatability testing fosters transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative research in the robotics community. This enables other researchers to build upon and validate these findings.
- **Insights into Embodiment-Sensing Coupling:** The explicit analysis of how sensor material and form factor (embodiment) impact performance, even for vision-only policies, highlights the fundamental coupling between robot embodiment and sensing. This emphasizes the importance of co-designing hardware and sensing capabilities for optimal manipulation.

In conclusion, TacO provides a benchmark that addresses a fundamental question in robotics: how to effectively choose and integrate tactile sensors for improved manipulation. Its findings offer actionable insights for designing more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems, particularly those operating in contact-rich environments.
