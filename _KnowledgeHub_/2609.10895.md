# ReactHuman: A Physics-Grounded Benchmark for Human-Like Reactive Decision-Making in Embodied Multimodal LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> ReactHuman is a physics-grounded benchmark where a multimodal LLM acts as the brain of a simulated humanoid reacting to sudden household hazards. A freeze-and-predict protocol pauses a 240 Hz rigid-body simulation, the model commits a walking command plus hand keyframes, and the plan is physically executed. Across 17 event families and seven models, reactive safety averages 80.8% and accuracy 54.0%, with no gains from scale.

## Problem

- Physical-reasoning benchmarks probe intuitive physics passively via plausibility judgments or video question answering, never testing conversion of understanding into immediate action.
- Embodied-AI benchmarks target deliberate long-horizon navigation and rearrangement, so none measure safety-critical reactive decisions on sub-second timescales.
- Answer-only protocols cannot separate misreading a hazard from choosing an unsafe action from mispredicting the interception point by a meter.

## Method

- ==Freeze-and-predict protocol==: a 0.6 s multi-view observation window, then time pauses and the model emits intent, confidence, walking command, and hand keyframes classified by fixed rule into Catch, Dodge, or No-Action.
- ==Brain-spine-body execution== couples the frozen model to a pre-trained RL walking policy on a simulated Unitree G1, plus a hybrid pipeline where an LLM sets semantics and a seeded randomizer owns all physics.
- ==Five-metric suite== along three axes: semantic action accuracy and action-intent alignment, binary safety validity under four rules, and endpoint distance plus hand-distance evolution.

## Results

- Across **2,138** decisions, mean accuracy is **54.0%** versus **51.0%** majority-class guessing; on dodge scenes **35.9%** of decisions break a safety rule, with **253** of **392** mistakes being freezing in place.
- Scale does not help: 27B Gemma ranks first on safety at **88.2%** and second on accuracy at **62.4%**, while flagship Claude Opus 4.8 is least safe at **64.7%**.
- Correct actions still miss physically, hands landing a median **0.48 m** from impact with **89%** falling short; across **280** adversarial decisions no model ever questions an object's material.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Executing committed plans in physics exposes failures multiple-choice scoring marks correct, since right action labels coexist with hands out of reach.
> - Current models assign physics by visual category and never revise from observed motion, so appearance-contradicting objects defeat them completely.
> - Ensembling cannot repair shared blind spots: majority voting over seven models matches the best single model, and slow hazards are read as no event.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

ReactHuman addresses a gap between two established areas: visual and intuitive-physics reasoning benchmarks, and embodied-AI evaluations involving robots or simulated agents. Existing physics benchmarks such as Physion, Physion++, CLEVRER, IntPhys, ContPhy, and PhysBench generally ask models to predict outcomes, judge physical plausibility, or answer questions about videos. These evaluations measure whether a model can understand physical events, but they do not require the model to commit to and execute a safety-critical action.

Conversely, embodied-AI benchmarks such as Habitat, AI2-THOR, ThreeDWorld, ManiSkill, BEHAVIOR-1K, and HumanoidBench evaluate navigation, manipulation, rearrangement, or whole-body control. These tasks are typically deliberate and extended over longer time horizons. They do not specifically isolate the ability to react immediately to a sudden hazard, such as a falling object or a swinging mass.

The paper places ReactHuman between passive physical reasoning and low-level robotic control. It evaluates a frozen multimodal large language model (MLLM) as the “brain” of a simulated humanoid. A separate controller handles balance and joint-level execution, allowing the benchmark to test decision quality while still requiring the decision to have physical consequences. The approach is also distinct from vision-language-action models, which are trained to emit robot actions directly. ReactHuman instead evaluates general-purpose MLLMs in a zero-shot setting and provides a common environment into which future VLA systems could also be integrated.

A further contribution is the use of procedurally generated, simulator-derived ground truth. Unlike fully human-annotated datasets, the benchmark can generate large numbers of reproducible scenes and obtain exact labels for action type, impact location, and timing directly from the physics engine.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to determine whether an MLLM can transform visual and semantic understanding of a physical event into an immediate, safe, and executable reaction. The authors argue that human-like reactive behavior requires three related but distinguishable capabilities.

First, the reaction must be **reasonable**. The agent should select an action appropriate to the object and event. Catching a light, harmless object may be sensible, whereas attempting to catch a knife, hot object, or heavy object is not.

Second, it must be **safe**. A model should move away from objects that threaten the humanoid and should not remain stationary when a hazard is on course to make contact.

Third, the reaction must be **physically grounded**. Choosing “catch” is insufficient if the humanoid’s hand never reaches the object’s actual trajectory. Similarly, a dodge command may fail if the model moves into rather than away from the impact zone.

The benchmark is designed to diagnose these failure modes separately. A model may misunderstand the object, select an unsafe action despite recognizing the hazard, or choose the correct action but miscalculate the timing and spatial location needed for interception. Standard visual question answering cannot distinguish these cases because it evaluates an answer rather than an embodied outcome.

The work also examines whether current MLLMs adapt decisions to observed motion. Specific questions include whether models account for object speed, whether they revise visual assumptions when appearance conflicts with physical behavior, and whether larger models are more reliable in safety-critical reactive settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Freeze-and-Predict Evaluation Protocol

Each episode places a simulated humanoid in an indoor environment where a sudden event begins at time \(t_0\). Objects may fall, slide, roll, swing, topple, bounce, or be propelled toward the observer. The model receives approximately 0.6 seconds of video following the onset of the event. The video can contain up to three synchronized views: the observer’s perspective, a close-up view of the event origin, and an overhead view.

After this observation window, simulation time is paused. The model must produce a structured response containing:

1. A free-form intent statement.
2. A confidence value between zero and one.
3. A walking or base-velocity command.
4. A sequence of three-dimensional hand-position keyframes.

Freezing the simulation removes differences in API or inference latency from the comparison. Every model therefore makes its decision at the same physical point in the event.

#### 3.2. Action Execution and Classification

The plan is then executed in the same physics scene by a simulated Unitree G1 humanoid. The MLLM acts as the decision-making component, while a pretrained reinforcement-learning walking controller and a scripted upper-body controller translate the walking command and hand keyframes into movement and joint torques.

The model does not explicitly select one of three action labels. Instead, the benchmark deterministically infers whether its motor plan constitutes:

- **Execute_Catch**, involving movement toward the object and a reaching trajectory.
- **Trigger_Dodge**, involving movement away from the object’s path.
- **No_Action**, involving remaining stationary with hands at rest.

This design prevents a model from receiving credit merely for writing the correct word. The plan must imply the corresponding physical behavior.

The action ground truth is based on object properties and event kinematics rather than visual appearance. Light, graspable, benign objects are assigned Catch; sharp, hot, shattering, heavy, or fast objects are assigned Dodge. No_Action is correct only when the event cannot reach the humanoid. In the evaluated set, the action distribution was 141 Catch, 156 Dodge, and 9 No_Action scenes, meaning that always choosing Dodge would obtain approximately 51% accuracy.

#### 3.3. Event Taxonomy and Scene Generation

ReactHuman includes 17 event families covering six broad motion types: falling, sliding and rolling, toppling, articulated motion, ballistic motion, and causal chains. Examples include object drops, shelf slides, ceiling drops, furniture tipping, ladder slips, pendulum swings, thrown objects, bouncing objects, stair tumbles, chain reactions, and multi-object momentum transfer.

The families are intended to isolate different reasoning demands. Tabletop drops emphasize semantic judgments because the trajectory is relatively simple. Pendulums and bouncing objects emphasize interception-point prediction. Chain reactions require causal reasoning before the threatening object begins moving. The benchmark therefore supports analysis of whether errors arise from object recognition, hazard assessment, causal inference, or spatial prediction.

Scene creation uses three stages. A frozen LLM first selects semantic elements such as the event family, object, room, and event-specific relationships from a natural-language description. A deterministic randomizer, controlled by an integer seed, then selects continuous physical and visual parameters, including object placement, friction, restitution, initial velocities, geometry, lighting, and camera poses. Finally, scenes are simulated in Genesis using a rigid-body solver operating at 240 Hz and rendered at 60 frames per second at 1280 × 720 resolution.

Ground truth is extracted directly from the simulator. This includes the required action, the three-dimensional point where the object crosses the humanoid’s reach plane, and time-to-floor or first floor contact. The authors report that scene specifications can be reproduced bit-for-bit and extended beyond 10,000 scenes without manual annotation.

#### 3.4. Metrics

The benchmark reports five metrics:

- **Semantic Action Accuracy (SAA):** Whether the inferred action matches the ground-truth action.
- **Safety Validity:** Whether the plan violates rules such as catching a dangerous object, remaining stationary during an active hazard, or failing to dodge when Dodge is required.
- **Physical Endpoint Distance:** The Euclidean distance between the final hand position and the true impact point.
- **Action–Intent Alignment (AIA):** Whether the stated intent corresponds to the required action, scored from zero to one. This separates an appropriate idea from an incorrectly implemented plan.
- **Hand-Distance Evolution (HDE):** Whether the hand moves toward the impact point during the trajectory, including the minimum distance achieved and the amount by which the hand closes toward the target.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Seven MLLMs were evaluated through a common API: Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Kimi K2.6, Qwen3-VL-235B, Qwen3-VL-30B, and Gemma-3-27B. All received the same prompt format, video observation window, and JSON output schema.

The experimental subset contained 306 scenes, with 18 scenes per event family. Across seven models, this produced 2,138 model-scene decisions, with four lost to API errors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The average semantic action accuracy was **54.0%**, while average safety validity was **80.8%**. The average physical endpoint distance was **1.23 meters**, and average action–intent alignment was **0.47**. These results indicate that selecting an action and executing it in a physically useful manner are separate challenges.

Safety failures were concentrated in Dodge scenarios. On scenes requiring evasion, models violated a safety rule in **35.9%** of decisions, with individual model rates ranging from 23% to 66%. The most frequent failure was freezing in place. The paper reports 253 such mistakes among 392 recorded action mistakes. Other failures involved failing to move when danger was present or attempting to catch objects that should have been avoided.

The models also exhibited stable behavioral dispositions rather than scene-dependent decisions. Six of the seven models generally preferred retreating, resulting in better Dodge performance than Catch performance. Claude Opus 4.8 showed the opposite pattern: it caught 67% of Catch scenes but engaged hazards in 34% of Dodge scenes. No model performed strongly on both types of decision. The mean results also did not show a consistent relationship between model scale and safety. Gemma-3-27B achieved 62.4% SAA and 88.2% safety validity, whereas Claude Opus 4.8 achieved 50.7% SAA and 64.7% safety validity.

Performance varied with event structure. A single bouncing ball was caught in 66.7% of relevant decisions, compared with 38.9% for collapsing stacks of cans. For Dodge events, slowly falling fixtures were avoided in 91.3% of cases, while thrown objects were avoided in only 54.8%. Approximately 44% of thrown-object decisions violated a safety rule.

Physical execution revealed errors hidden by action labels. Even when models selected Catch correctly, their hands ended a median **0.48 meters** from the impact point, and 89% of misses fell short. In 49% of these cases, the humanoid was more than 0.8 meters from the target, beyond the effective reach of its arm. The results suggest that models often command an arm movement without adequately coordinating whole-body movement.

Models also treated speed in a largely binary manner. Across 826 decisions involving controlled speed variations, the freeze rate was approximately 42% for the slowest hazards, compared with about 21% for faster hazards. SAA fell to 38.1% in the slowest band, compared with 47–54% at higher speeds. Models produced the same action across all four speed variants in only 27% of scene groups, while the slowest and fastest variants agreed in 54% of cases. However, the action distribution changed little across speed bands, suggesting weak sensitivity to quantitative speed.

Adversarial appearance–physics tests produced a similar result. Across 280 decisions involving objects such as foam anvils, steel apples, foam panels, and lead-core balls, no model questioned the mismatch between visual appearance and physical behavior. Models treated disguised objects like their ordinary visual counterparts, indicating that observed motion did not reliably override appearance-based assumptions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ReactHuman contributes a framework for evaluating embodied MLLMs under immediate physical and safety constraints. Its primary significance is methodological: it turns physical reasoning into an action problem with observable consequences, rather than treating it as a question-answering task. The separation of reasonability, safety, and physical grounding can help researchers identify whether a system needs better perception, hazard classification, motion prediction, planning, or control.

The benchmark may support training and evaluation of household robots, humanoid assistants, and other embodied agents that must respond to unexpected events. Its deterministic generation pipeline can provide large-scale training data, while adversarial objects and controlled speed variations can expose reliance on visual shortcuts. The same scenes and metrics could also be used to compare MLLM planners with native VLA systems.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. The benchmark uses rigid-body simulation and does not model deformation, shattering, cloth, or fluids in detail. Execution is open-loop: the agent observes once, commits to a plan, and does not re-plan during movement. Results may therefore differ on real hardware or in closed-loop settings. The action classifier and intent metric also rely on fixed rules and keyword matching, respectively. Finally, model APIs and snapshots change over time. The findings should consequently be understood as measurements of the evaluated systems and benchmark configuration rather than permanent rankings.
