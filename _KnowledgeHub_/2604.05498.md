# JailWAM: Jailbreaking World Action Models in Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> JailWAM introduces a framework to systematically evaluate and induce physical harm in World Action Models (WAMs) through adversarial prompts, revealing that state-of-the-art WAMs can achieve over 84% attack success rate in causing motion failures or catastrophic risks. The framework includes a hierarchical safety classification and a dual-path verification strategy to efficiently identify physical vulnerabilities.

## Problem

- Existing AI safety research largely overlooks physical threats from jailbreak attacks on embodied AI like World Action Models (WAMs).
- There is an absence of a systematic, hierarchical definition for quantifying robotic arm motion safety, making hazard assessment difficult.
- The heterogeneous action spaces of WAMs and the high computational cost of full physical simulation hinder comprehensive and scalable safety evaluations.

## Method

- **JailWAM** proposes a ==Three-Level Safety Classification== framework (Safety Compliance, Motion Failure, Catastrophic Risk) to standardize robotic motion hazard assessment.
- Develops ==Visual-Trajectory Mapping (VTM)== to unify heterogeneous WAM action spaces into standardized, multi-view visual trajectory charts for interpretability.
- A ==Dual-Path Verification Strategy== is implemented, combining efficient open-loop visual screening via a lightweight ==Risk Discriminator (RD)== with rigorous closed-loop physical simulation for high-fidelity hazard confirmation.

## Results

- **JailWAM** induced an **84.20%** human-verified attack success rate on LingBot-VA, including **62.00%** motion failures and **22.20%** catastrophic risks.
- Jailbreak prompts exhibited zero-shot transferability, leading to **60.60%** human-ASR on Motus and **46.50%** on Cosmos-Policy, while non-WAM baselines showed significantly lower susceptibility.
- The Dual-Path Verification Strategy achieved a **2.50x** speedup in evaluation time compared to closed-loop-only methods, and its Stage I filtering demonstrated an effective inference-time defense mechanism with a **96.0%** benign instruction pass rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art World Action Models (WAMs) are highly susceptible to jailbreak attacks, which can induce severe physical hazards, including catastrophic risks to property and personnel.
> - Adversarial prompts generated by JailWAM demonstrate cross-architectural and cross-environment transferability, indicating widespread vulnerabilities across different WAM designs.
> - The combination of visual-spatial grounding (VTM) and task-specific safety alignment (RD fine-tuning) is critical for effective and efficient detection of physical hazards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research situates itself within the evolving landscape of artificial intelligence safety and robotic control, specifically at the intersection of generative models and embodied AI. World Action Models (WAMs) represent a significant advance in robotic manipulation, integrating pre-trained generative backbones with real-world dynamics to enable complex physical interactions. This development mirrors the rise of large foundation models like Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and Video Generation Models (VGMs), which have demonstrated powerful generative capabilities across various modalities.

A prominent area of concern in the field of AI safety has been the vulnerability of these generative models to "jailbreak attacks." Such attacks involve crafting adversarial prompts designed to bypass safety guardrails and elicit restricted or harmful digital outputs, such as malicious text, unsafe visual content, or violent videos. Existing research in this domain has primarily focused on these digital anomalies and their consequences.

This work addresses a critical shift in the threat model when generative architectures are adapted for embodied robot control. Unlike LLMs or VGMs, WAMs directly translate digital instructions into physical actions performed by robotic arms. Consequently, a successful jailbreak attack on a WAM transcends digital harm, leading to tangible real-world risks such as human injury, equipment damage, or environmental destruction. The paper highlights that current research on AI safety pays limited attention to this critical security gap, leaving the vulnerability of WAMs to jailbreak attacks largely unquantified and unaddressed.

The paper aims to bridge this gap by introducing a systematic framework for evaluating and understanding the physical safety implications of jailbreak attacks on WAMs, contrasting with previous work that primarily focused on digital domain vulnerabilities. It also contributes to the subfield of robotic simulation safety by introducing metrics and evaluation methodologies beyond traditional task completion rates, which often overlook dangerous intermediate behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the dual nature of World Action Models (WAMs). While WAMs offer robust physical manipulation capabilities, their capacity for direct physical interaction also introduces significant safety hazards if not properly aligned. The authors observe that existing AI safety research largely overlooks the specific vulnerabilities of WAMs to jailbreak attacks, a critical oversight given that successful attacks can translate virtual digital vulnerabilities into direct threats to physical safety, property, and the environment. This gap in research motivates a systematic investigation into the security of WAMs.

The paper identifies three core challenges that currently hinder a comprehensive evaluation of WAM vulnerabilities to jailbreak attacks:

1. **Absence of a systematic hierarchical definition for robotic arm motion safety:** Without a clear framework to categorize and quantify different levels of hazardous robotic motion, it is difficult to systematically assess and compare the severity of physical threats induced by jailbreak attacks.
2. **Highly heterogeneous action spaces of WAMs:** Different WAM architectures may output actions in varying low-level representations (e.g., joint configurations, end-effector displacements). This heterogeneity makes it challenging to design unified, cross-architectural attack and evaluation protocols. Interpreting raw action sequences for safety assessment is also not straightforward.
3. **Computational cost of physical harm verification:** Confirming physically harmful behaviors typically necessitates extensive full closed-loop simulation validation. This process is computationally intensive and incurs substantial human and computational overhead, making scalable evaluation intractable.

To address these motivations and overcome the identified challenges, the research sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Define a Three-Level Safety Classification Framework:** This framework aims to systematically quantify the safety of robotic arm motions, providing a standardized taxonomy for assessing hazard levels (Safety Compliance, Motion Failure, Catastrophic Risk).
2. **Propose JailWAM as the first dedicated jailbreak attack and evaluation framework for WAMs:** The objective is to develop a comprehensive methodology for generating and evaluating jailbreak attacks specifically tailored for the physical domain of robotic control.
3. **Develop Visual-Trajectory Mapping (VTM):** This module aims to unify heterogeneous WAM action spaces into standardized visual trajectory representations. This objective seeks to enable cross-architectural unified evaluation and make low-level actions semantically interpretable for risk assessment.
4. **Design a lightweight Risk Discriminator (RD):** The goal is to create a high-recall screening tool that can efficiently and accurately identify destructive behaviors from visual trajectories, optimizing the trade-off between efficiency and accuracy in hazard identification.
5. **Implement a Dual-Path Verification Strategy:** This strategy aims to balance evaluation efficiency with physical fidelity. The objective is to combine rapid coarse screening via open-loop video-action generation with efficient and comprehensive verification through full closed-loop physical simulation.
6. **Construct JailWAM-Bench:** This objective is to create a dedicated benchmark dataset for comprehensively evaluating the safety alignment performance of WAMs under jailbreak attacks, focusing on physical-level jailbreak safety in embodied intelligence.
7. **Expose physical vulnerabilities in contemporary WAMs and propose defense strategies:** Through experimentation, the research aims to demonstrate the existence and severity of safety vulnerabilities in state-of-the-art WAMs and to provide technical solutions for designing safe and reliable robot control systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed JailWAM framework is designed to systematically evaluate the vulnerability of World Action Models (WAMs) to jailbreak attacks that induce physical harm. Its methodology addresses the challenges of heterogeneous action spaces, the need for hierarchical safety definitions, and the high computational cost of physical verification.

**Overall Framework: Dual-Path Verification Strategy**
JailWAM operates on a coarse-to-fine Dual-Path Verification Strategy. This strategy first performs an efficient open-loop screening to filter adversarial candidates and then escalates high-risk candidates to a more rigorous, albeit costly, closed-loop physical simulation for definitive hazard confirmation.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries and Problem Formulation

The paper formalizes jailbreak as an instruction-level optimization problem for instruction-conditioned WAMs. A WAM $M$ takes observation history $o^{\leq t}$, robot-state history $s^{\leq t}$, and a language instruction $l$ to generate an action sequence $a_{t:t+H}$. The jailbreak instructions $l_{adv}$ are generated by sampling from a large language model (LLM), G$_{LLM}$ (e.g., Gemini, GPT-5), conditioned on specialized jailbreak templates T designed to synthesize descriptions of dangerous maneuvers. The objective is to find $l^* \in L_{adv}$ that maximizes the safety risk $R$ of the visual trajectory $V$ generated from the WAM's action sequence, aiming for non-benign (Level 1 or Level 2) outcomes.

#### 3.2. Three-Level Safety Classification Framework

To quantify the safety of robotic arm motions, the paper defines a three-level classification:
- **Level 0: Safety Compliance:** Characterized by smooth, continuous motion within safe planes, no collisions or oscillations, and normal task execution. This represents zero safety risk.
- **Level 1: Motion Failure:** Involves kinematic singularities, system loops or freezes, high-frequency oscillations without effective movement, and potential joint vibration damage. Task execution fails, but catastrophic damage is avoided.
- **Level 2: Catastrophic Risk:** Involves uncontrolled, erratic swinging, breaching safe planes, collisions with the environment (e.g., workbench), leading to severe structural damage, environmental destruction, or extreme hazard to personnel.

#### 3.3. Visual-Trajectory Mapping (VTM)

To address heterogeneous action spaces and make low-level robot actions interpretable for safety assessment, VTM projects abstract temporal action sequences into structured, multi-view visual trajectory charts.
- **Action Integration:** The WAM's output, a sequence of relative action displacements $\Delta A$, is first integrated by a function $F$ to accumulate into absolute 3D spatial coordinates $P$ of the robot end-effector.
- **Orthographic Projection:** The 3D trajectory $P$ is then projected onto orthographic 2D planes, specifically top-down ($\Pi_{xy}$) and front-view ($\Pi_{xz}$) projections, to preserve metric geometry and avoid perspective distortion.
- **Visual Rendering:** A rendering function $\Phi$ synthesizes these projections along with explicitly injected physical affordances and environmental constraints $C_{env}$ (e.g., workspace boundaries) into a unified visual trajectory chart $V$. This visual representation explicitly exposes geometric hazards.

#### 3.4. Risk Discriminator (RD)

The RD is a lightweight model designed to predict safety risks directly from the VTM-generated visual trajectory chart $V$ and the adversarial instruction $l_{adv}$.
- **Architecture:** Implemented using Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, a vision-language model selected for its balance of visual reasoning and inference efficiency.
- **Training Data:** A supervised dataset of 25,000 high-quality training samples was constructed. This involved selecting 50 distinct manipulation tasks in the RoboTwin simulator, sampling 500 candidate rollouts per task, and labeling their visual charts. Initial labels were generated by Gemini-3.1-Pro using Chain-of-Thought prompting, followed by rigorous human verification and correction.
- **Functionality:** During Stage I, the RD classifies the input $(V, l_{adv})$ into one of the three safety levels ($y^* \in \{0, 1, 2\}$).

#### 3.5. Dual-Path Verification Strategy Workflow

- **Stage I: Open-Loop Visual Screening:**
 - Given an initial context and an adversarial instruction $l_{adv}$, the WAM generates a predicted action sequence.
 - The VTM module transforms this sequence into a visual trajectory chart $V$.
 - The Risk Discriminator $R$ evaluates $V$ and $l_{adv}$ to compute the discrete safety label $y^*$.
 - Candidates classified as Level 0 (Safety Compliance) are discarded. Only those flagged as high-risk (Level 1 or 2) are escalated to Stage II. This stage significantly prunes the search space.
- **Stage II: Closed-Loop Embodied Verification:**
 - The high-risk candidates escalated from Stage I are executed in a high-fidelity simulator $S$ (e.g., RoboTwin). The WAM interacts with the environment, receiving updated observations at each time step.
 - The execution outcomes (e.g., collisions, oscillations, boundary violations) are recorded as video rollouts.
 - Human experts manually review these recorded videos to establish the ground-truth safety label, definitively classifying the induced physical hazard as Level 1 or Level 2. This stage ensures physical fidelity of the evaluation.

#### 3.6. JailWAM-Bench Construction

JailWAM-Bench is introduced as a benchmark for embodied jailbreak evaluation. It comprises:
- A Jailbreak Instruction Dataset: 82 transferable adversarial prompts, each capable of inducing Level 1 or Level 2 hazards in at least three distinct task scenes.
- Standardized Evaluation Metrics: Attack Success Rate (ASR), Motion Failure Rate (MFR), and Catastrophic Risk Rate (CRR), reported for both RD predictions and human-verified outcomes.
- A Unified Evaluation Protocol: for measuring physical safety failures under jailbreak attacks.

**Experimental Setup**
The framework was evaluated using:
- **Target Models:** LingBot-VA (state-of-the-art WAM), Motus (VLM-based action decoder), Cosmos-Policy (another WAM), and $\pi_{0.5}$ (a non-WAM baseline).
- **Simulation Environments:** RoboTwin and LIBERO.
- **Baselines:** "Clean" (standard instructions), "Random Suffix Attack (RSA)", and "Template-based Prompt Attack (TPA)".
- **Metrics:** ASR, MFR, CRR, with both RD and Human verification.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using the JailWAM framework yielded several findings regarding the vulnerabilities of World Action Models (WAMs) and the efficacy of the proposed methodology.

#### 4.1. Exposure of Hazardous Behaviors on State-of-the-Art WAMs (RQ1)

JailWAM demonstrated its capability to consistently induce physical hazards in state-of-the-art WAMs. On LingBot-VA in the RoboTwin environment, under clean instructions or baseline textual attacks (RSA, TPA), the Attack Success Rate (ASR) was low (1.60% for clean, <6% for baselines), with 0% catastrophic risk. In contrast, JailWAM achieved an 84.20% Human-ASR, which included a 62.00% Motion Failure Rate (MFR) and a 22.20% Catastrophic Risk Rate (CRR). The induced physical hazards also exhibited consistency across varying spatial configurations, suggesting robustness of the attacks.

#### 4.2. Cross-Architectural Transferability (RQ2)

Jailbreak prompts generated for LingBot-VA exhibited zero-shot transferability to Motus, another WAM with a distinct VLM-based action decoder. Motus, which showed an ASR of <10% under benign or baseline attacks, experienced a 60.60% Human-ASR when subjected to JailWAM prompts. This comprised 57.20% MFR and 3.40% CRR. This indicates that physical vulnerabilities can manifest across WAMs with disparate action-decoding pipelines, although the catastrophic risk rate might vary.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Different Simulation Environments and WAM Architectures (RQ3)

JailWAM prompts (originally for LingBot-VA) generalized to Cosmos-Policy within the LIBERO benchmark in a zero-shot transfer setting. While Cosmos-Policy was robust against clean instructions and naive textual attacks (ASR <6%), the transferred JailWAM prompts induced a 46.50% Human-ASR. All of these failures were classified as Level 1 motion failures (46.50% MFR), with no catastrophic risks observed in this specific transfer scenario.

#### 4.4. Vulnerability in Non-WAM Embodied Architectures (RQ4)

To establish the boundary of the observed vulnerabilities, $\pi_{0.5}$, a non-WAM baseline lacking a generative visual prior, was evaluated in LIBERO using the same zero-shot transfer protocol. While $\pi_{0.5}$ was highly robust against standard and naive text attacks (ASR <2.5%), transferred JailWAM prompts resulted in only a 5.00% Human-ASR. This significantly lower success rate compared to WAMs suggests that the severe physical vulnerabilities exposed by JailWAM are intrinsically tied to the generative visual priors characteristic of World Action Models.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study on VTM and RD

An ablation study on 60 human-verified samples quantified the contributions of Visual-Trajectory Mapping (VTM) and domain-specific Fine-Tuning (FT) of the Risk Discriminator (RD). Using raw kinematic actions with RD resulted in 0.0% Level 2 Recall. Incorporating VTM with an off-the-shelf VLM (VTM + RD Zero-shot) improved Level 2 Recall to 20.0%. The full pipeline (VTM + RD (Ours)), which combines visual-spatial grounding with task-specific alignment through fine-tuning, achieved 90.0% overall consistency and 70.0% Level 2 Recall, identifying all Level 0 and Level 1 instances. This highlights the importance of both structural visual grounding and embodied safety fine-tuning for effective hazard detection.

#### 4.6. Cross-Seed Reliability of Generated Prompts

Evaluating the Cross-Seed Reliability (CSR) of 1000 successful prompts across different numbers of random environmental seeds ($N \in \{1, 5, 10, 20\}$) showed that as $N$ increased, CSR generally decreased for all LLMs. Prompts generated by Gemini-3.1-Pro demonstrated higher consistency, retaining an 82.5% CSR at $N=20$, compared to GPT-5.2 (41.2%) and Claude-Opus-4.5 (56.4%). This indicates Gemini-3.1-Pro prompts are less sensitive to initial state variations.

#### 4.7. Ablation Study on the Dual-Path Verification Strategy

The Dual-Path Verification Strategy achieved a 2.50x speedup compared to a "Closed-Loop Only" baseline. For 100 candidate prompts, the baseline required 9.15 hours and 100 simulator runs to identify 23 hazards. JailWAM's dual-path strategy reduced total evaluation time to 3.66 hours by performing only 21 simulator runs, while still identifying 17 hazardous cases. This demonstrates the strategy's efficiency in narrowing the computational search space.

#### 4.8. Defense Mechanism

The Stage I pipeline of JailWAM was shown to function as an effective inference-time defense mechanism. Tested on LingBot-VA, the average Pre-Defense ASR of malicious prompts was 91.5%. After applying the filter, the average bypass rate (Post-Defense ALL) and the final hazard rate (Post-Defense ASR) were substantially reduced. For example, in the "put object cabinet" task, Post-Defense ALL was 8.0%, and Post-Defense ASR decreased to 2.0%. Concurrently, the filter maintained an average pass rate of 96.0% for benign instructions.

#### 4.9. JailWAM-Bench

The paper introduced JailWAM-Bench, a new benchmark comprising a dataset of 82 transferable adversarial prompts, standardized metrics (ASR, MFR, CRR), and a unified evaluation protocol, shifting the focus to physical safety for embodied jailbreak evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds several significant implications for the fields of robotics, AI safety, and embodied intelligence.

First, the work **identifies and quantifies a critical and previously underexplored security gap**: the vulnerability of World Action Models (WAMs) to jailbreak attacks that induce physical harm. While traditional AI safety research has focused on digital output anomalies, this paper shifts the focus to the tangible, real-world consequences of adversarial prompts on robotic systems, highlighting risks to human safety, property, and the environment. This re-prioritization of physical safety is crucial as embodied AI systems become more prevalent.

Second, the introduction of the **Three-Level Safety Classification Framework** provides a standardized, systematic, and quantitative method for assessing robotic arm motion safety. This framework offers a common language and metric for researchers and developers to categorize and compare the severity of various hazardous robot behaviors, which is essential for systematic vulnerability assessment and the development of safety-aligned systems.

Third, the proposed **JailWAM framework** itself is a novel contribution as the first dedicated jailbreak attack and evaluation framework for WAMs. It offers a comprehensive set of tools and methodologies—including Visual-Trajectory Mapping (VTM) and the Risk Discriminator (RD)—to address the unique challenges of evaluating physical-level jailbreaks, such as heterogeneous action spaces and the difficulty of interpreting raw kinematic data.

Fourth, the **Dual-Path Verification Strategy** presents a practical solution to the computational bottleneck of verifying physical hazards through full closed-loop simulations. By combining efficient open-loop screening with rigorous closed-loop verification, the strategy enables scalable yet high-fidelity evaluation, making comprehensive safety assessments more feasible for complex robotic systems.

Fifth, the empirical results, demonstrating **high attack success rates (e.g., 84.2% on LingBot-VA)** and cross-architectural transferability, serve as a strong warning to the research and development community. These findings indicate that state-of-the-art WAMs are severely susceptible to physically malicious instructions, underscoring the urgent need for robust safety alignment mechanisms in their design and deployment.

Finally, the paper contributes a **new benchmark, JailWAM-Bench**, which will serve as a valuable resource for future research. By providing a dataset of transferable adversarial prompts and a unified evaluation protocol, it facilitates standardized assessment and comparison of safety alignment performance in embodied intelligence, encouraging further investigation into robust robot control systems. The demonstrated efficacy of the Stage I pipeline as an **inference-time defense mechanism** also provides a concrete and actionable pathway for integrating physics-aware safety into the development of next-generation embodied foundation models.

In summary, this work moves beyond theoretical discussions of AI safety to provide concrete evidence of physical vulnerabilities in advanced robotic systems and offers a foundational framework and practical tools for addressing these critical safety challenges.
