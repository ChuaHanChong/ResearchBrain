# DISEIL: Demonstration Distillation for Sample-Efficient Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> DISEIL is an interactive imitation-learning loop that decides what demonstration to request, not just when. It marks each failed episode where the policy first becomes unreliable, encodes that moment in a geometric descriptor, clusters failures into recurring modes, and lets a VLM plus language model write a constraint-checked request. Across 5 simulated tasks and 2 observation types it gives the highest mean held-out success in all 10 settings.

## Problem

- DAgger-family methods only decide when to call the expert; which failed episode to correct and where the demonstration starts default to the triggering episode and its state.
- An uncertainty score attached to a single state cannot tell whether two failures share an underlying cause, so a small budget repeatedly corrects one common weakness.
- Active learning, core-set selection and dataset distillation all choose among data that already exists, offering no way to specify supervision that has not yet been collected.

## Method

- **Perceive** marks each failure at the first sustained loss crossing, reusing the Diff-DAgger query rule, then writes a ==six-dimensional geometric descriptor==: object position, sin/cos yaw, episode fraction, end-effector-to-object distance.
- **Partition** clusters standardized descriptors ==agglomeratively== with ==silhouette-chosen k==; **Prioritize** targets near-largest modes by ==memory-adjusted peak-loss severity==, discouraging correction of the same mode twice in a row.
- **Prescribe** has a vision-language model plus prescription LLM emit a targeted correction or bridging start configuration, gated by a ==task knowledge base== for workspace, reachability and valid-path feasibility.

## Results

- Highest mean held-out success in all **10** settings at **20** demonstrations, mean margin **2.80 points** (range **0.0-5.6**); Push-T state **96.1%** versus Diff-DAgger **94.1%**, GridWorld state **92.4%** versus **86.8%**.
- Budget sweep over **3** settings: margin over the strongest baseline is **9.07 points** at budget **10**, narrowing to **2.87** at budget **20** and **2.83** at budget **40**.
- Per-demonstration goodness, the pre-retraining per-step loss, exceeds Diff-DAgger in all **8** robot settings; removing mode-based allocation costs **4.37 points**, more than any other knockout.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spending a fixed budget across distinct recurring failure modes is the load-bearing idea; descriptor, clustering algorithm and language models look interchangeable.
> - Split the loop by competence: models read and describe failures, while geometry and feasibility stay in explicit symbolic checks, and rejected proposals cost no expert time.
> - Allocation gains shrink as budget grows, and counting demonstrations hides expert effort, since earlier anchor points make the teacher drive the robot longer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses interactive imitation learning, a setting in which a robot learns from expert demonstrations while periodically performing its own rollouts. Conventional behavior cloning trains a policy on expert state-action pairs, but it is vulnerable to covariate shift: small policy errors can lead the robot into states absent from the training data, where subsequent errors compound.

Interactive methods such as DAgger reduce this problem by allowing an expert to intervene when the learner becomes uncertain or begins to deviate. Existing DAgger-style methods primarily focus on the **query decision**: determining when the expert should be called. Their usual operating assumption is that the episode that triggered the query, and the state reached at that time, automatically determine the content and starting point of the next demonstration.

DISEIL argues that this leaves two important acquisition decisions unresolved. First, the system should determine **which recurring failure mode** deserves the next demonstration, rather than correcting whichever failure happened to trigger an intervention. Second, it should choose **where the demonstration should begin**, potentially at a strategically constructed configuration rather than at the exact state reached by the learner.

The work also relates to active learning, core-set selection, sub-trajectory retrieval, and dataset distillation. Those methods generally select or compress data that already exists. DISEIL instead uses observed failures to specify a demonstration that has not yet been collected. The paper therefore frames demonstration selection as a design problem involving failure analysis, allocation of a limited supervision budget, and generation of a feasible expert request.

A further contribution is the proposed division between neural and symbolic components. Vision-language and language models describe failures and formulate requests, while explicit geometric descriptors and task constraints handle clustering and feasibility. The language models do not generate robot actions and are not used during deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Improving the allocation of scarce demonstrations

The central motivation is that the number and composition of demonstrations both affect imitation-learning performance. When expert time or demonstrations are limited, repeatedly correcting similar failures may produce less improvement than distributing demonstrations across distinct weaknesses.

The authors identify three decisions in an acquisition round:

1. **When** to query the expert.
2. **Which failure mode** to supervise.
3. **Where the demonstration should start.**

DISEIL keeps the first decision fixed through the existing Diff-DAgger uncertainty rule and focuses on making the second and third decisions explicit.

#### 2.2. Addressing failures at their onset

A demonstration beginning at the point where a policy has already deviated substantially may teach recovery rather than prevention. DISEIL therefore identifies the first step at which the policy becomes persistently unreliable. This is intended to locate the onset of failure, before its effects accumulate.

#### 2.3. Using multiple failures to infer a request

A single failure may not reveal whether a problem is isolated or representative of a broader weakness. DISEIL aggregates evidence from several failed episodes, groups them geometrically, and selects a recurring mode for correction. This is intended to improve coverage of the failure distribution rather than simply maximize the loss associated with one episode.

#### 2.4. Constraining language-model proposals

Language models can describe plausible corrections but may produce physically impossible configurations. The paper therefore incorporates a task-specific knowledge base containing workspace, reachability, spawn, success, and path-validity constraints. Proposed demonstration starts are checked before expert time is used.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Acquisition loop

The policy is initially trained by behavior cloning on an initial demonstration set \(D_0\). In each acquisition round, it is rolled out from fresh starting configurations. Failed episodes and their per-step policy losses form the failure set.

For each failure, DISEIL identifies an anchor step \(t^\star\). This is the first step at which the loss exceeds a threshold for \(K\) consecutive steps. The threshold is derived from the policy’s training-loss distribution and recalibrated after retraining. If no sustained threshold crossing is found, the maximum-loss step is used.

#### 3.2. Failure representation

At the anchor step, each failure is represented by a six-dimensional geometric descriptor. Depending on the task, the descriptor includes object or agent position, orientation encoded through sine and cosine, normalized episode progress, and distances between the end effector and the manipulated object. Task-specific variants also include quantities such as height, grasp state, hinge angle, or remaining dirt.

The descriptor is intentionally low-dimensional and is computed from simulator state. It is used only for demonstration selection and is not provided to the policy being trained.

In parallel, a vision-language model receives three frames from each cited failure: the beginning of the episode, the anchor step, and the final step. It produces a spatial description of the failure. A language model then assigns a root-cause label and trajectory phase from a predefined task vocabulary.

#### 3.3. Failure-mode clustering

When at least four failures are available, the geometric descriptors are standardized and grouped using agglomerative clustering with Ward linkage. The number of clusters is selected by the highest silhouette score over a candidate range of two to six clusters. When fewer than four failures remain, each failure is treated as its own mode.

Each cluster stores its size, centroid, representative failure, and mean peak loss. The largest cluster establishes the dominant region of the failure distribution. DISEIL considers modes whose size is within one failure of that dominant cluster and selects among them using memory-adjusted severity. This avoids allowing a small, isolated high-loss outlier to consume the entire round.

A cluster memory records recently corrected modes. It reduces the priority of modes similar to those addressed in preceding rounds, encouraging the budget to rotate among different weaknesses.

#### 3.4. Demonstration prescription

The selected mode is summarized using a context set of up to three failures:

- The failure closest to the cluster centroid.
- The member with the highest peak loss.
- Additional geometrically diverse members selected by farthest-point sampling.

The prescription model can request either a targeted correction or a bridging placement. A targeted correction starts the demonstration from the anchor state of a cited failure. A bridging placement constructs a new configuration between related failures, allowing the expert to demonstrate a behavior that covers an intermediate region not directly observed in the rollouts.

Every proposed start configuration is passed through symbolic checks. It must satisfy workspace and reachability constraints, have a valid path where relevant, and be a configuration the current policy does not already solve. The language model may revise a rejected proposal up to five times. If all attempts fail, DISEIL falls back to the nearest untried recorded failure.

The expert then provides one demonstration, after which the policy is retrained from scratch on the augmented dataset. The main experiments use one demonstration per round and a total additional budget of 20 demonstrations.

#### 3.5. Experimental design

The evaluation covers five simulated tasks under state and image observations:

- GridWorld navigation.
- Push-T planar manipulation.
- Lift.
- Wipe.
- Door opening.

This produces ten task-observation settings. GridWorld uses an MLP for state input and a CNN for image input. The robot tasks use diffusion policies, with R3M visual features for image observations.

The comparisons include SafeDAgger, DropoutDAgger, EnsembleDAgger, ThriftyDAgger, Diff-DAgger, and, for GridWorld, STAGGER. Each method uses the same policy class, initial demonstrations, expert, retraining schedule, demonstration budget, and held-out evaluation set within a setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Held-out performance

DISEIL achieved the highest mean held-out success rate in all ten settings, with a tie on Lift using state observations. After 20 added demonstrations, the results were:

- **GridWorld, state:** 92.4%, compared with a best baseline of 86.8%.
- **GridWorld, image:** 91.3%, compared with 89.1%.
- **Push-T, state:** 96.1%, compared with 94.1%.
- **Push-T, image:** 92.6%, compared with 89.0%.
- **Lift, state:** 100.0%, tied with the strongest baseline.
- **Lift, image:** 100.0%, compared with 99.6%.
- **Wipe, state:** 93.1%, compared with 90.4%.
- **Wipe, image:** 92.3%, compared with 88.6%.
- **Door, state:** 96.6%, compared with 93.2%.
- **Door, image:** 88.6%, compared with 84.2%.

The mean advantage over the strongest comparison was 2.80 percentage points, ranging from zero to 5.6 points. The benefit was most pronounced with smaller budgets. In additional experiments at budgets of 10, 20, and 40 demonstrations, DISEIL’s average margin over the strongest baseline across three settings was 9.07, 2.87, and 2.83 points, respectively.

#### 4.2. Demonstration informativeness

DISEIL acquired demonstrations with higher pre-retraining policy loss than Diff-DAgger in all eight applicable robot settings. This indicates that the selected demonstrations were generally located in regions where the current policy was performing poorly.

However, the ablation results show that individual demonstration informativeness was not sufficient to explain final performance. Removing clustering and selecting only the highest-loss failure changed per-demonstration goodness by approximately 0.02 points on average, but reduced final success by 4.37 points. This suggests that the main advantage came from distributing demonstrations across recurring modes rather than repeatedly selecting individually severe failures.

#### 4.3. Ablation findings

The largest performance reduction occurred when clustering and mode-based allocation were removed. Other components also contributed:

- Removing the environmental constraint store reduced success by 2.37 points on average.
- Promoting the fallback rule to the entire method reduced success by 3.27 points.
- Removing either the prescription model or the vision-language model reduced success by approximately 1.33 points.
- Disabling bridging reduced success by 1.27 points.
- Removing cluster memory reduced success by 0.73 points.

The results indicate that geometric failure-mode allocation was the primary contributor, while language-based analysis, feasibility checking, bridging, and memory provided additional improvements.

#### 4.4. Computational and operational costs

The language-model pipeline added between 63 and 1,232 seconds per acquisition round, with approximately 9,560 to 12,116 tokens in the measured settings. This cost was incurred only during demonstration selection and not during policy execution. The authors note that its practical value depends on the relative cost of computation and expert supervision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DISEIL’s principal significance is conceptual: it treats the content of an expert demonstration as an acquisition decision rather than as an automatic consequence of an intervention trigger. The distinction between query timing, failure-mode allocation, and demonstration placement could be useful for interactive imitation-learning systems operating under strict supervision budgets.

The approach may be particularly relevant to human teaching, where avoiding redundant demonstrations could reduce teacher effort. Its constraint-checking mechanism also offers a way to use language models in robot-learning workflows without assigning them direct control authority. The separation between geometric selection, language-based interpretation, and symbolic validation is compatible with systems requiring explicit safety and feasibility checks.

The evidence remains limited. All experiments were conducted in simulation, and the geometric descriptors use privileged simulator state. Most experts were scripted, motion-planned, or learned policies rather than human teachers. The budget counted demonstrations rather than expert-controlled time, even though demonstrations starting earlier in an episode may require more effort. The failure-mode labels were evaluated for internal consistency with the model’s own labels rather than independent human annotations. In addition, DISEIL remembers previously corrected regions but does not explicitly model what behaviors the accumulated dataset already covers.

Consequently, the results support the value of structured failure allocation in the tested simulated settings, but they do not yet establish performance on physical robots or under realistic human-teaching costs. Future work would need to address noisy perception, sim-to-real transfer, cost-aware supervision, broader human-expert studies, and representations that track both observed failures and the behavioral coverage of the demonstration set.
