# EpiTransfer: Sparse, Training-Free Long-Range Depth Estimation from Temporal Monocular Aerial Frames

> [!summary] Summary
> EpiTransfer is a training-free method that estimates sparse metric depth from two monocular frames and their known camera poses. It matches features with XFeat, moves each match into a synthetic third view by intersecting epipolar lines, and triangulates against a virtual baseline the user can choose freely. It reaches about 90 m range outdoors, with indoor AbsRel 0.092, far ahead of off-the-shelf ZoeDepth and Depth Anything V2.

## Problem

- Physical stereo rigs have a fixed baseline, so compact aerial platforms must trade sensor footprint and calibration burden against depth accuracy at long range.
- Learned monocular metric-depth models generalize poorly to new altitudes and oblique viewpoints, and under domain shift they produce scale errors that are unbounded and hard to interpret.
- Existing temporal or virtual stereo methods either triangulate directly from the two real views or rely on dense learned rectification, and none characterize the geometric degeneracies these methods suffer from.

## Method

- Epipolar transfer builds a virtual stereo frame: ==fundamental matrices== from known pose map each correspondence to the intersection of two epipolar lines in a synthetic view.
- The virtual baseline is a free parameter, independent of the real inter-frame motion, and depth comes from the standard stereo relation, focal length times baseline over disparity.
- A hybrid pipeline pairs learned ==XFeat== sparse matching with closed-form geometric ==triangulation==, and a derived ==trifocal-plane degeneracy== analysis masks ill-conditioned points without empirical thresholds.

## Results

- Outdoors on a drone against USGS LiDAR, AbsRel is **0.138** versus **0.719** for ZoeDepth and **0.744** for Depth Anything V2, with RMSE cut **3.2x** (**10.3** m vs about **32** m).
- Indoors with OptiTrack pose, AbsRel **0.092** and delta<**1.25** of **0.940** are comparable to direct DLT triangulation (**0.073**, **0.943**); in low-texture outdoor scenes EpiTransfer keeps **4**-5% more valid points.
- The pipeline runs at about **10** Hz on an i7 CPU and on an NVIDIA AGX Orin. Pose-noise sensitivity stays bounded except in takes where the injected perturbations push the epipole into the image.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When pose is accurate, pure geometry beats pretrained metric-depth foundation models out of domain. Their learned scale priors can fail completely, scoring delta<1.25 of 0 outdoors.
> - Sensitivity to pose error depends mainly on how close the geometry is to a known degeneracy, not on a uniform error bound, so reliability can be checked directly from the candidate pose.
> - Oblique, downward-pitched cameras keep the epipole away from the content that matters, whereas forward-translating cameras like those in KITTI fall into the degenerate configuration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

EpiTransfer addresses a practical aerial-robotics problem. A small, inexpensive monocular camera is attractive when weight and power make LiDAR or a stereo rig undesirable. However, a single image cannot supply metric range, and a fixed stereo baseline trades compactness against long-range accuracy. The authors use camera motion between two images to create a *virtual* stereo view with a selectable baseline, then triangulate sparse depth from matched features. Two moments from one camera become a stereo pair, without adding a second camera or training a depth predictor. They report useful depth in an outdoor drone-image scene out to about 90 m, from selected, oblique outdoor imagery.

Outdoor qualitative results for five of the eight evaluated images. Columns: input image, EpiTransfer, Triangulation (DLT), ZoeDepth, Depth Anything. Black in the geometric outputs can mean beyond the 100 m ground-truth scoring range, no sparse correspondence, or masking for ill-conditioning.

### 2. How two views become virtual stereo

The pipeline starts with two calibrated monocular images and their known relative camera pose, and finds sparse feature matches with XFeat. An epipolar line is the set of possible image locations of a 3D point, constrained by camera geometry. For each match, each real image yields one epipolar line in a chosen virtual view. Intersecting those lines transfers the feature into the virtual image. Depth is then triangulated between the current image and the virtual view using their disparity and the chosen baseline.

The virtual baseline is a design parameter. It is not a new physical camera separation and not necessarily equal to the camera's real motion. This synthetic third view and controllable baseline distinguish the method from direct temporal triangulation.

Geometry illustration of virtual stereo construction (paper Fig. 3). It shows real-camera positions and rotations with translation vectors and a virtual-baseline distance. The flow runs from a video stream through drone trajectory estimation and virtual frame and baseline computation to depth via virtual stereo transfer.

The deployed pipeline uses sparse XFeat matches. The quantitative evaluation instead uses dense RAFT correspondences at LiDAR-projected pixels so that it can score those locations. The reported accuracy therefore tests the geometry with dense correspondences. It does not directly establish the accuracy or coverage of the deployed sparse XFeat output.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

The key baseline is direct two-view triangulation (DLT). It holds pose and correspondence inputs constant, which helps isolate the virtual-view formulation from the effect of accurate pose. The metric is AbsRel, the average absolute depth error normalized by ground-truth depth, where lower is better. Metrics are computed on matched valid-pixel masks.

**Indoors**, the evaluation covers seven frames against LiDAR ground truth. EpiTransfer's AbsRel is 0.092, versus 0.073 for DLT. The two pretrained monocular baselines score 0.225 (ZoeDepth) and 0.570 (Depth Anything V2), and neither was fine-tuned for aerial or oblique imagery. EpiTransfer is close to DLT but not better. In this evaluation, it is markedly better than these particular unadapted models.

**Outdoors**, on the common retained pixels, DLT's AbsRel is 0.124 and EpiTransfer's is 0.138. In two low-texture scenes, EpiTransfer retains 753 versus 715 valid points and 1,147 versus 1,103 for DLT, reported increases of 5.3% and 4.0%. These are only two scenes. The authors suggest the advantage may matter particularly with imperfect outdoor GPS/IMU pose. Indoors, with more accurate OptiTrack pose, the masks and maps are nearly indistinguishable.

### 4. Where the geometry breaks down

The trifocal plane is the plane containing the three camera centers. Near it, the two epipolar lines intersect at a poorly conditioned angle, and small correspondence errors can cause large transfer errors. The method can identify and mask this region, but it does not eliminate the degeneracy. The outdoor setup's 20° downward camera pitch keeps the epipole near the sky and horizon, away from ground-level areas of interest. The evaluation sequences also avoid near-degenerate forward motion. Performance with primarily forward motion and the epipole in the image is left to future work.

EpiTransfer shows that a sparse, training-free geometric method can produce metric depth in these selected evaluations. That result holds for oblique or downward-looking views, and the outdoor results are offline. The authors report 10 Hz operation on an NVIDIA AGX Orin using live camera input on the ground, but hexacopter flight testing is still in progress.
