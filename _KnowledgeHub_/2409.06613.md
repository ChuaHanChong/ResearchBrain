# DemoStart: Demonstration-led auto-curriculum applied to sim-to-real with multi-fingered robots

> [!summary] Summary
> DemoStart is an auto-curriculum reinforcement learning method for dexterous manipulation with a three-fingered hand on a 7-DoF arm. It converts a handful of simulated demonstrations into episode start states, filters them by zero-variance success signal, and biases toward earlier states, then distills the feature-based teacher into a pixel-based policy that transfers zero-shot to real robots at 97% plug-lift success.

## Problem

- High-quality teleoperation of dexterous multi-fingered hands remains unsolved, so real-robot imitation needs thousands of episodes and tens of hours of uncomfortable glove-based collection.
- Vanilla RL from sparse rewards fails outright on high-DoF insertion, while auxiliary-reward methods demand extensive hand-engineering of dense shaping terms and domain expertise.
- Prior curriculum methods keep auto-curriculum and demonstration resets as separate consecutive stages, and replay demonstration experience, so poor-quality or wrong-action-space demonstrations degrade learning.

## Method

- **DemoStart**'s ==Mechanism 1== turns recorded simulation demonstration states into task parameters providing start states of graded difficulty; later states are easier, earlier ones harder, and demonstration actions are never used.
- ==Zero-Variance Filtering== trains only on task parameters where the current policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails, plus a bias mechanism steering training toward earlier, less demonstration-corrupted states.
- ==Distillation== trains a ==Perceiver-Actor-Critic== student by behavior cloning on offline teacher rollouts from four RGB cameras plus proprioception, with physics, perturbation, visual randomization and photorealistic Filament rendering.

## Results

- DemoStart reaches **99.6%** simulated plug insertion versus **0%** for vanilla RL and **99.2%** for SAC-X, and above **98%** on lifting, cube reorientation, nut-bolt threading and screwdriver-in-cup.
- Real-robot zero-shot transfer gives **97%** plug lift, **64%** insertion, **97%** cube reorientation, against **20%/1%** for distilled SAC-X and **64%/2%** for behavior cloning on **2753** real teleoperated episodes.
- Ablations show Mechanism 1 alone yields **0%** and adding zero-variance filtering jumps to **97.2%**; dropping photorealistic data cuts real insertion to **29%**, and using one camera to **17%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Demonstrations are more valuable as a start-state distribution than as cloned actions; decoupling them from replay tolerates sub-optimal, partial, or different-action-space data.
> - Success-rate variance is a cheap, decentralized proxy for learning signal, but it misleads under environment stochasticity where randomness rather than policy quality drives outcome spread.
> - Distillability depends on teacher smoothness: jittery or human-diverse action distributions collapse under behavior cloning even when the source policy itself performs near-perfectly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing towards more intricate dexterous tasks, moving beyond basic pick-and-place operations. This shift involves increasingly complex robot embodiments, such as bimanual robots and multi-fingered hands, which possess higher degrees of freedom (DoFs) and necessitate sophisticated control strategies. Two primary methodologies currently dominate this domain: training policies in simulation using reinforcement learning (RL) and imitating human demonstrations on physical robots.

Simulation-based learning offers advantages in scalability and safety, significantly reducing the development cycle for behavior generation and mitigating risks associated with real-robot data collection. Domain randomization techniques are frequently employed to bridge the "sim-to-real" gap, enabling zero-shot transfer of policies from simulated to real environments. However, challenges persist, including learning from sparse rewards, managing high-dimensional action spaces, and dealing with limited, potentially suboptimal demonstrations. Existing approaches to sim-to-real transfer often rely on hand-crafted perception mechanisms (e.g., markers, point clouds, tactile sensing) or explicit state estimation from images. An alternative involves vision-based distillation, which directly converts feature-based policies into visuomotor policies, bypassing fixed state representations.

The integration of demonstrations with RL is a recognized strategy to enhance learning efficiency, especially in tasks with sparse rewards. This approach can "kick-start" RL, reducing the need for extensive exploration. Concurrently, auto-curriculum methods have emerged as a technique to facilitate RL in challenging environments by automatically adjusting the learning difficulty. Prior work has explored using state resets from demonstrations to induce curricula and integrating auto-curriculum methods with demonstration-derived states.

This work contributes to the landscape by proposing an integrated auto-curriculum method that specifically addresses the combination of minimal, suboptimal demonstrations, sparse rewards, and the complexities of high-DoF multi-fingered manipulation for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. It distinguishes itself from prior auto-curriculum methods by combining the curriculum generation and demonstration-based state resets into a single data generation procedure and by not feeding demonstration experience directly into the replay buffer, allowing for the use of lower-quality demonstrations. The approach leverages vision-based distillation for sim-to-real transfer, operating directly on RGB images and proprioception without requiring external perception mechanisms or explicit state estimation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generalizable approach for learning complex manipulation behaviors on robots equipped with multi-fingered hands, particularly under conditions of a large action space, very limited and suboptimal demonstrations, and access only to binary sparse rewards for task success. The motivation behind this objective stems from the challenges associated with current methods for synthesizing advanced robot skills:

1. **Complexity of Embodiments and Tasks:** Synthesizing functional, performant, and safe skills for complex robotic morphologies, such as dexterous multi-fingered hands and intricate manipulation tasks, remains a difficult problem. These systems typically have many degrees of freedom, making direct control and learning arduous.
2. **Limitations of Real-Robot Teleoperation:** While learning from human teleoperated robots has yielded results for simpler embodiments, achieving high-quality teleoperation for sophisticated dexterous hands is an ongoing research challenge. This limits the scalability and practicality of real-world demonstration collection.
3. **Scalability and Safety Concerns:** Learning directly on real robots is often time-consuming, expensive, and poses safety risks, especially during early exploration phases. Simulation-based learning with subsequent sim-to-real transfer is a compelling alternative that can alleviate these concerns.
4. **Reward Design Difficulty:** Designing dense and informative reward functions for complex tasks is a labor-intensive and expert-driven process. The goal is to reduce this burden by enabling learning from simple, sparse rewards (i.e., a binary success/failure signal).
5. **Leveraging Limited Demonstrations:** In many real-world scenarios, obtaining a large quantity of high-quality expert demonstrations is impractical. The motivation is to develop a method that can effectively utilize a small number of potentially suboptimal or incomplete demonstrations to initiate and guide the learning process.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives are:
- To propose DemoStart, a novel auto-curriculum reinforcement learning method that combines a minimal set of simulated demonstrations with sparse-reward RL.
- To enable the learning of manipulation behaviors that surpass the quality of the initial demonstrations.
- To facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of learned policies, utilizing raw pixel data from multiple cameras and robot proprioception, achieved through domain randomization and policy distillation.
- To significantly reduce the number of demonstrations required compared to traditional imitation learning or RL from real-world demonstrations.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to expand the range of tasks solvable by dexterous robots by lowering the development effort and increasing the efficiency of skill acquisition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, DemoStart, is structured into two main stages: training a teacher policy in simulation and distilling it into a vision-based student policy for real-world deployment.

**A. Teacher Policy Training in Simulation with DemoStart**

The first stage involves learning a feature-based "teacher" policy in a simulated environment using a novel auto-curriculum RL method called DemoStart, combined with physics domain randomization. The learning process operates within a Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework, where the agent selects actions based on its state, receives sparse binary rewards for task success, and transitions to new states. Episodes terminate upon the first success. Task parameters ($\psi$) define the MDP, including initial states ($s_0$), environment settings (physics, visual), and goal specifications.

DemoStart integrates three conceptual mechanisms within a single data generation procedure:

1. **Demonstrations into Task Parameters (TPs) of Varying Difficulty:** A small set of demonstrations, solving the task in simulation, is recorded. Each state along these demonstrations can serve as a starting state ($s_0$) for a new TP. TPs derived from later stages of demonstrations are generally easier, requiring fewer steps to goal, while those from earlier stages are harder.
2. **Zero-Variance Filtering (ZVF) for High Training Signal:** During learning, most TPs are either too easy (agent always succeeds) or too hard (agent always fails) to provide effective learning signals. DemoStart employs ZVF, a heuristic that identifies TPs where the current policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. The intuition is that only in such states is there variance in outcomes, indicating a learning opportunity. Experience generated from TPs with no outcome variance is discarded.
3. **Focusing on TPs with Minimal Bias from Demonstrations:** Demonstrations can contain unnatural or unstable states (e.g., an unstable grasp). Training from such states might lead the agent to focus on rectifying poor states rather than learning optimal behaviors. To counteract this, DemoStart biases the selection of TPs towards states occurring earlier in demonstrations, which encourages learning foundational skills (e.g., grasping) rather than recovering from suboptimal mid-demonstration states.

**Implementation of DemoStart:**
DemoStart operates within a distributed actor-learner setup. Actors generate experience by executing the policy in the environment, which is then sent to a learner via an experience replay buffer. The learner updates the policy using the Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization (MPO) algorithm. Each actor executes the following loop:
- **Step 1: Sample a Sequence of TPs:** The actor samples a sequence of $K+1$ TPs. The first TP ($\psi_0$) is from the target environment's initial state distribution ($T_{target}$). The subsequent $K$ TPs are generated by sampling one demonstration, dividing it into $K$ temporal chunks, and uniformly sampling one environment state from each chunk to serve as $s_0$. This creates a sequence of TPs generally ordered from harder to easier.
- **Step 2: Find a TP to Train On:** The actor iterates through the sampled TP sequence, starting from the hardest ($\psi_0$). For each TP, it generates $T$ episodes by executing the current policy. None of this data is sent for training. The ZVF criterion is applied: if there is variance in sparse reward outcomes across the $T$ episodes (some successes, some failures), the TP is deemed suitable for training. If the agent always fails (TP too hard), the actor moves to the next, easier TP in the sequence. If the agent always succeeds (TP too easy) or the sequence is exhausted, a new sequence of TPs is sampled (return to Step 1).
- **Step 3: Generate Training Data:** Once a suitable TP is found, the actor generates $M$ episodes using the policy on this TP and sends the collected data to the replay buffer for policy updates. After this, the process returns to Step 1.
In the experiments, typical values were $K=8$, $T=4$, and $M=50$.

**B. Distillation and Transfer**

After training the feature-based teacher policy with DemoStart, it is distilled into a "student" policy that relies on visual observations (RGB camera images) and proprioception. This is achieved via behavior cloning from an offline dataset.
- **Data Generation for Distillation:** The offline dataset consists of trajectories generated by the trained teacher policy in simulation. This data explicitly includes different types of domain randomization.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Three types of DR are applied during both teacher (partially) and student training to improve sim-to-real transfer:
 1. **Perturbations:** External force disturbances are applied to unconstrained objects.
 2. **Physics Randomization:** Friction, mass, and inertia of joints and bodies are randomized.
 3. **Visual Randomization:** Camera poses, lighting, and object colors are varied.
 Notably, perturbations and physics DR are not applied to episodes initialized from demonstration states when training the teacher policy.
- **Photorealistic Rendering:** For certain tasks (e.g., plug insertion), photorealistic images rendered with Filament, along with randomized lighting and exposure, are included in the training data to further reduce the visual sim-to-real gap.
- **Policy Distillation Model:** The Perceiver-Actor-Critic (PAC) model, a neural architecture designed for continuous control, is used for the student policy. It handles multimodal inputs (vision and proprioception) by encoding them into embeddings and using cross-attention with latent vectors to reduce computational complexity. The PAC model is trained solely via behavior cloning on the teacher-generated dataset.

**C. Experimental Setup and Tasks**

Experiments utilize a Kuka LBR iiwa 14 robot arm with a three-fingered DEX-EE Hand (12 DoFs) in a square basket cell, equipped with four cameras (two on the basket corners, two on the wrist). The simulated setup uses MuJoCo. The action space is 18-dimensional (6D Cartesian velocity for the arm, 12D joint positions for the hand).
Five challenging tasks, inspired by the NIST dexterous manipulation benchmark, are evaluated:
- **Plug Lifting:** Lift a yellow plug 5cm above the basket surface (5 demonstrations).
- **Plug Insertion:** Insert a plug into a socket. Demonstrations included 12 for insertion (upright plug) and 8 for flipping an oriented plug upright, totaling 20 demonstrations.
- **Cube Reorientation:** Reorient a cube to have a specific side facing upwards (2 demonstrations of unstructured interaction).
- **Nut and Bolt Threading:** Thread an oversized nut onto a fixed bolt (60 demonstrations: 20 full task, 20 lifts, 20 partial threading).
- **Screwdriver in Cup:** Ensure cup is upright, then insert a screwdriver handle-first into the cup (20 demonstrations).
Simulated demonstrations were collected using a 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse with a simpler action space, which DemoStart can accommodate due to not using demonstration actions for policy updates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental results from both simulation and real-world deployments, comparing DemoStart against various baselines and ablations.

**A. Simulation Experiments**

- **DemoStart Performance:** DemoStart achieved high success rates across all evaluated simulation tasks: 99.6% for Plug Insertion, 99.7% for Plug Lift, 99.9% for Cube Reorientation, 99.8% for Nut and Bolt Threading, and 98.6% for Screwdriver in Cup.
- **Efficiency:** DemoStart policies exhibited significantly greater efficiency compared to the demonstrations they were trained on. For example, in the Screwdriver in Cup task, agents solved the task in a median time of 3.5 seconds, while demonstrations had a median time of 93.2 seconds.
- **Baselines Comparison (Plug Insertion):**
 - **Vanilla RL:** An agent using the same sparse reward and MPO architecture as DemoStart, but without DemoStart's curriculum mechanisms, failed to solve any plug tasks (0% success).
 - **Scheduled Auxiliary Control (SAC-X):** While SAC-X achieved high performance (99.2% plug insertion, 100% plug lift) with manually designed auxiliary rewards, this required substantial domain expertise and engineering effort.
- **Distillation in Simulation:**
 - **DemoStart + Behavior Cloning (BC):** The vision-based policy distilled from DemoStart data retained its high performance (99.0% for Plug Insertion, 99.3% for Plug Lift).
 - **SAC-X + BC:** Distilling the SAC-X policy for Plug Insertion resulted in a significant performance drop (20.4%). This was attributed to the "jerky, non-smooth behaviors" of the SAC-X policy, which made it difficult for the student policy to learn.
- **Ablations on DemoStart Mechanisms (Plug Insertion):**
 - **Vanilla RL + Mechanism 1 (Demonstrations as TPs):** 0% success.
 - **Vanilla RL + Mechanism 1 + Success Filter:** 0% success.
 - **Vanilla RL + Mechanisms 1 & 2 (ZVF):** Achieved 97.2% success, indicating that Zero-Variance Filtering (ZVF) is a critical component for effective learning.
- **Emergent Curricula:** Qualitative analysis showed that DemoStart fostered semantically meaningful curricula. For instance, in the Screwdriver in Cup task, early training episodes started with the screwdriver already above an upright cup, while later episodes involved grasping the screwdriver or orienting an upside-down cup, demonstrating a progression from easier to harder sub-tasks.

**B. Real-World Performance**

Policies distilled from DemoStart data were evaluated for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a real robot.
- **Performance:**
 - Plug Lift: 97% success rate.
 - Cube Reorientation: 97% success rate.
 - Plug Insertion: 64% success rate.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap:** A performance drop was observed in real-world settings compared to simulation, particularly for Plug Insertion, which also required longer episode durations to achieve success.
- **DemoStart vs. Baselines on Real Robot:**
 - **SAC-X Distillation:** Showed significantly lower real-world performance (20% Plug Lift, 1% Plug Insertion).
 - **Behavior Cloning from Real Teleoperation:** Achieved 64% for Plug Lift but only 2% for Plug Insertion. The paper highlights that 20 demonstrations in simulation for DemoStart were equivalent to 0.5 hours of collection, whereas 2753 demonstrations on the real robot for baselines were equivalent to 27 hours of non-stop collection.
- **Behavioral Observations:** Policies distilled from DemoStart data produced more consistent and smoother behaviors, which were easier to learn and transfer. In contrast, policies from SAC-X and real teleoperation often exhibited "fidgety" or jerky motions, potentially due to diverse action distributions or suboptimal human teleoperation styles, hindering effective distillation and transfer. DemoStart policies also exhibited emergent behaviors not present in demonstrations, such as reorienting the plug in-air.
- **Failure Modes:** Common failures in real-world transfer included the cube ending in basket corners for reorientation, unstable grasps for plug lifting, and precision issues during the final insertion phase for plug insertion. Baselines also struggled with correctly understanding object orientation and recognizing incomplete insertions.
- **Generalization:** Preliminary observations indicated that the plug lifting policy could generalize to lifting objects of different shapes and colors (e.g., a cube or a lemon) with reasonable success.

**C. Distillation Ablations (Plug Insertion Real-World)**

Ablations on the distillation pipeline revealed key factors for real-world performance:
- **Photorealistic Data:** Without photorealistic rendering in training data, real-world performance dropped from 64% to 29%.
- **Observation Type:** Using Cartesian fingertip poses instead of finger joint angles resulted in a mild decrease to 50% success.
- **Number of Cameras:** Reducing the number of camera views significantly impacted performance, especially without wrist cameras. Success rates were 51% with 3 cameras, 42% with 2 cameras, and 17% with only 1 camera (no wrist camera). This indicates the importance of rich visual input.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents DemoStart, an auto-curriculum reinforcement learning method that contributes to the advancement of robotic manipulation, particularly for complex, multi-fingered systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Simplification of Task Design:** DemoStart reduces the burden of task design by requiring only a handful of low-quality or incomplete demonstrations and a simple, sparse binary reward function. This contrasts with traditional RL methods that often demand dense reward engineering or extensive, high-quality human demonstrations, making it easier to define and solve new manipulation tasks.
- **Enhanced Learning from Limited Data:** The method effectively leverages minimal, even suboptimal, demonstrations collected in simulation to initiate and guide the learning process. By using demonstrations to shape the initial state distribution for RL rather than for direct behavior cloning, DemoStart enables the emergence of more efficient and performant behaviors that can surpass the quality of the original demonstrations.
- **Effective Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The integration of comprehensive domain randomization techniques (physics, visual, perturbations) with policy distillation (using the PAC model) facilitates successful zero-shot transfer of policies from simulation to real multi-fingered robots. This is achieved while operating directly on raw RGB images and proprioceptive data, eliminating the need for complex external sensing or explicit state estimation.
- **Scalability and Development Cycle Reduction:** By enabling effective learning in simulation and reliable transfer to real hardware, DemoStart can significantly reduce the development cycle for new robotic skills. It addresses a key bottleneck in robotics by mitigating the costs, risks, and time associated with real-robot data collection and experimentation.
- **Emergent and Robust Behaviors:** The combination of an auto-curriculum and RL allows the agent to discover novel, more efficient, and robust manipulation strategies not explicitly present in the initial demonstrations (e.g., in-air reorientation of objects). This capability indicates that the learned policies are not merely mimicking but are synthesizing improved solutions.
- **Modularity and Future Extensions:** The pipeline's modular design suggests potential for future enhancements, such as incorporating real-robot data into the distillation phase to enable a continuous self-improvement loop during deployment. This could further close the sim-to-real gap and enhance long-term adaptation.

**Potential Impact:**
DemoStart's approach has the potential to broaden the applicability of dexterous robotic manipulation in various domains, including manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics, where tasks are often complex, require fine motor skills, and where obtaining abundant expert demonstrations or designing dense rewards is challenging. By lowering the barrier to entry for developing new robotic skills, it could accelerate research and deployment of more capable and autonomous robotic systems.

**Limitations and Trade-offs:**
The paper acknowledges several trade-offs and limitations:
- **Compute Intensity:** The reliance on sparse rewards and Zero-Variance Filtering (ZVF), which discards much of the generated data, makes the method compute-intensive compared to dense-reward approaches.
- **Behavioral Fidelity to Demonstrations:** While moving beyond demonstrations can yield superior behaviors, it also means there are fewer guarantees that the final solution will closely adhere to the original demonstrated actions, which might be a concern in contexts requiring specific interaction styles.
- **Stochasticity Impact on ZVF:** In highly stochastic environments, ZVF's variance-based signal might be misleading, potentially selecting TPs that offer little genuine learning signal due to environmental randomness.
- **Transfer Gap Persistence:** Despite advanced randomization, a performance gap between simulation and reality persists, particularly for highly precise tasks like plug insertion.

Future work directions include improving computational efficiency (e.g., GPU-based simulators, better state selection for ZVF) and exploring more advanced randomization techniques or integrating more informative reward signals when available.
