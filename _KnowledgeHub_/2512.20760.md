# Generalization of RLVR Using Causal Reasoning as a Testbed

> [!summary] Summary
> This research empirically investigates the generalization of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) in formal causal inference tasks using a new RLCausal dataset. It demonstrates that RLVR enhances LLM performance and cross-level generalization on associational and interventional queries for sufficiently capable models by refining reasoning strategies and reducing specific error types.

## Problem

- The robustness and boundaries of RLVR generalization in complex reasoning tasks, especially beyond training data, were not fully understood.
- Existing causal reasoning benchmarks often conflate natural language understanding with formal reasoning, obscuring LLMs' pure derivation and calculation capabilities.
- LLMs showed limitations in performing numerically challenging formal counterfactual reasoning problems, even with explicit instructions.

## Method

- Developed **RLCausal**, a custom dataset of formal ==causal inference== problems based on abstract causal graphs, to isolate derivation and calculation aspects of reasoning.
- Conducted an empirical study comparing RLVR and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) across various LLM scales (3B, 7B, 32B parameters) on associational, interventional, and counterfactual query types.
- Analyzed reasoning traces using an LLM judge to categorize marginalization strategies and types of errors, such as abstract probability derivation and calculation errors.

## Results

- RLVR generally outperformed SFT on both within-level and across-level generalization for associational and interventional queries with **7B** and **32B** parameter models.
- RLVR fine-tuning led to a notable reduction in abstract probability and causal derivation errors, shifting problem-solving towards more systematic strategies for complex queries.
- Both RLVR and SFT showed limited success on counterfactual queries in the RLCausal dataset, with no consistent performance gains even when explicit 'twin-network' hints were provided.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RLVR's generalization effectiveness is contingent on the LLM's initial reasoning competence, showing limited benefits for models with poor baseline abilities.
> - RLVR training encourages LLMs to adopt more systematic 'incremental marginalization' strategies and significantly reduces abstract probability and causal derivation errors.
> - Current LLMs, even with RLVR and explicit prompting, exhibit fundamental difficulties with complex formal counterfactual reasoning involving abduction and marginalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a method for post-training large language models (LLMs) on complex reasoning tasks. This paradigm leverages automatic correctness signals from domains where ground truth can be reliably verified, leading to advances in areas such as mathematical problem solving, formal theorem proving, code generation, and applications in biomedical and chemistry fields. Despite its rapid development and broad applicability, the conditions under which RLVR training yields robust generalization beyond the training data remain an area of active investigation.

Recent research has begun to compare the generalization behaviors of reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning (RL) against supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or hybrid approaches. Some studies suggest that SFT can be beneficial for initial model warm-up, while RL may enhance generalization. Other work explores optimal ordering or combination strategies for SFT and RL, as well as the underlying mechanisms of RLVR's effectiveness.

This paper contributes to this evolving landscape by specifically examining RLVR generalization within the domain of causal inference. This domain is chosen due to its structured nature, which facilitates systematic study of generalization across distinct reasoning levels. Unlike some prior works that evaluate generalization on novel variants of existing tasks or common-sense reasoning, this research focuses on formal causal reasoning with fully specified graphical models. By constructing a custom dataset, RLCausal, based on abstract causal graphs rather than natural language scenarios, the authors aim to isolate and investigate the derivation and calculation aspects of causal reasoning, allowing for a more controlled probe into LLM performance as the number of required reasoning steps increases and graph complexity varies. This approach differentiates it from benchmarks like CLadder, which emphasize natural language understanding in addition to formal reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct an empirical study of RLVR generalization, specifically in the context of probabilistic inference over causal graphical models. The study aims to delineate the conditions under which RLVR effectively improves LLM performance on such tasks and to understand the mechanisms by which these improvements manifest.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Underexplored Generalization of RLVR:** Despite the promising results of RLVR in various complex reasoning domains, the robustness and boundaries of its generalization capabilities are not fully understood. There is a need for systematic investigation into when and how RLVR enables LLMs to generalize beyond their training data, particularly for novel reasoning patterns or increased complexity.
- **Causal Reasoning as a Structured Testbed:** Causal inference offers a naturally structured domain for studying generalization. It features a hierarchical "causal ladder" with three distinct levels of inference: associational, interventional, and counterfactual. These levels inherently support both within-level generalization (e.g., handling more complex associational queries) and across-level generalization (e.g., applying knowledge from associational training to interventional tasks). This inherent hierarchy provides clear axes for evaluating generalization behavior.
- **Disentangling Reasoning Skills:** Traditional causal reasoning benchmarks, such as CLadder, often involve interpreting natural language scenarios, identifying query types, and formalizing causal expressions, in addition to derivation and calculation. This paper's motivation includes isolating the derivation and calculation aspects of causal reasoning by providing fully specified causal graphs and queries. This design allows for a focused examination of how RLVR fine-tunes LLMs' ability to perform multi-step, formal reasoning in a computationally demanding setting, particularly as graph size and interconnectedness increase.
- **Understanding Mechanisms of Improvement:** Beyond observing performance gains, the researchers are motivated to investigate *why* RLVR is effective. This involves analyzing the fine-tuned LLMs' reasoning traces to identify changes in problem-solving strategies (e.g., marginalization approaches) and the reduction of specific error types (e.g., abstract probability derivation errors, calculation errors). This mechanistic understanding contributes to broader knowledge about how RLVR enhances LLMs' reasoning capabilities.
- **Addressing Limitations in LLM Causal Reasoning:** Previous work has shown varying success for LLMs in causal reasoning, particularly on common-sense or simplified formal settings. This paper aims to probe the limitations on more numerically challenging formal counterfactual reasoning problems, where models might struggle even with explicit instructions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on an empirical study comparing RLVR and SFT across various LLM scales and query types on a custom dataset of causal inference problems.

- **Task Definition:**
 - **Input:** A description of a causal graphical model (including variables, their possible values (binary), the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) structure, and Conditional Probability Tables (CPTs)) and a probabilistic inference query.
 - **Output:** For RLVR, the LLM generates a reasoning chain followed by a probability distribution. For SFT, it directly outputs the probability distribution.
 - **Base Model:** Qwen-2.5-Instruct models (3B, 7B, 32B parameters) are used.
 - **Ground Truth:** For each task instance, a reference answer (p⋆) is computed using variable elimination (Zhang & Poole, 1994) on the (potentially modified) Structural Causal Model (SCM).

- **Training Objectives:**
 - **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR):** The objective is to maximize `E_x~T [E_y~pθ(x) [r(y)]]`, where `r(y)` is a composite reward function:
 - `r(y) = 0.8 * r_ans(ˆp_y, p⋆_x) + 0.2 * r_format(y)`.
 - `r_ans`: Binary reward (1 if total variation distance `D(ˆp_y, p⋆_x) < 0.01`, else 0). Probabilities are rounded to two decimal points.
 - `r_format`: Composite binary reward (0.5 if `ˆp_y` is extractable, 0.5 if `ˆp_y` length is correct).
 - Algorithms: Variants of GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025b) are used.
 - **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT):** The baseline maximizes the conditional likelihood of the reference answer `y⋆_x` for input `x`, `E_x~D [log pθ(y⋆_x | x)]`.

- **Study Design Factors:**
 - **Model Scale:** Experiments are conducted with Qwen-2.5-Instruct models of 3B, 7B, and 32B parameters.
 - **Training Query Level:** Models are trained on individual query levels (associational, interventional, or counterfactual) to assess both within-level and across-level generalization.

- **Data Generation (RLCausal Dataset):**
 - **Structural Causal Models (SCMs):** The dataset uses SCMs with 10 binary variables, no cycles (DAGs), and independent noise variables.
 - **Graph Sampler:** DAGs are generated using a workflow that iteratively adds nodes with one or two parents, followed by random permutation of node names. This provides structural diversity beyond manually curated topologies.
 - **Mechanism Sampler:** For each variable and its parent assignments, a binary distribution is sampled from the simplex, defining the conditional probability tables.
 - **Query Sampler:** Queries are generated for each of the three levels:
 - **Associational:** `p(v_i | v_j = v_j)` (statistical dependence).
 - **Interventional:** `p(v_i(v_j=c))` (causal effects, equivalent to `p(v_i | do(v_j=c))`).
 - **Counterfactual:** `p(v_i(v_j=c) | v_k = v_k)` (hypothetical alternatives).
 - Observations/interventions are on single variables.
 - **Solver:** Queries are reduced to exact inference in a (possibly modified) SCM, then solved using variable elimination. Graph modifications for intervention involve removing incoming edges to the intervened variable and setting its mechanism to a constant. Counterfactual queries involve constructing a "twin network" graph.
 - **Dataset Size:** 8000 training, 2000 development, and 8000 test examples per query level, using disjoint SCMs.
 - **Difficulty Metric:** Queries are stratified by:
 - **Query Level:** Associational, Interventional, Counterfactual. In this setup, with full SCM parametrization, the difficulty ordering is found to be associational > interventional, with counterfactual being the most complex due to requiring abduction followed by deduction.
 - **Query Complexity:** Measured by `|V_rel|`, the size (number of nodes) of the subgraph relevant to the query after graph modifications. Queries are grouped into "small," "medium," and "large" complexity bins.

- **Metrics:**
 - **Correctness:** `CORRECT_t(x, y)` is used, returning 1 if the extracted answer `ˆp_y` is formatted correctly and its total variation distance `D(ˆp_y, p⋆_x)` is less than or equal to `t = 0.01`, else 0.
 - **Filtering:** Insensitive examples (where intervention/observation does not measurably change the marginal distribution of the query variable) are filtered out for main analysis but included in supplementary results.

- **Fine-tuning and Inference Setup:**
 - **RLVR:** GRPO (with token-level normalization, specific PPO clip ratios, no KL term) and DAPO (without overlong buffer, stricter accuracy filtering) are used. Trained for 2.5k to 7.5k steps.
 - **SFT:** Trained for 5k steps using maximum likelihood.
 - **Optimizer:** Adam with default parameters, weight decay of 0.1, no warmup steps.
 - **Decoding:** Temperature 0 for inference.

- **Analysis Methods:**
 - **LLM-aided Qualitative Analysis:** Reasoning traces are analyzed using o4-mini (an LLM judge) to categorize:
 - **Marginalization Strategy:** Incremental, brute-force, neighbors, or no marginalization.
 - **Probability Derivation Errors:** Incorrect probability identity usage (e.g., chain/Bayes rule), false assumptions (e.g., ignoring dependencies, confusing intervention/observation).
 - **Copy Errors:** Incorrectly copying CPT values from the problem statement.
 - **Arithmetic Errors:** Numerical mistakes in calculations.
 - **Precision Analysis:** Accuracy is plotted against relaxed correctness thresholds (total variation distance up to 0.2) to assess how precise models are.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding RLVR's generalization on causal reasoning tasks:

- **Within-level and Across-level Generalization:**
 - **Within-level:** When trained and evaluated on the same query level, RLVR generally outperformed SFT on association and intervention queries for models of 7B parameters or larger. However, for the 3B model, RLVR underperformed SFT across all levels. On counterfactual queries, RLVR did not consistently outperform SFT across any model size, with both methods showing limited success.
 - **Across-level:** For models of 7B parameters or larger, RLVR demonstrated stronger generalization to different query levels than SFT. Both RLVR and SFT showed improved cross-level generalization as model scale increased, indicated by a decreasing performance gap between models trained on in-level versus out-of-level queries.
 - **Complexity-Dependent Performance:** RLVR models (7B and 32B) tended to perform better on more complex queries within each type, while SFT performed better on less complex ones.

- **Scaling and LLM’s Reasoning Prior:**
 - **Initial Competence is Crucial:** The effectiveness of RLVR was partly attributed to the initial reasoning competence of the LLM prior to fine-tuning. Models with poor initial reasoning capabilities (e.g., 3B models) showed limited benefit from RLVR.
 - **Scaling Benefits Reasoning:** Scaling up the LLM size (from 3B to 32B) significantly improved reasoning competence. The reasoning prompt (used for RLVR) benefited more from scaling than the direct prediction prompt (used for SFT). A 32B model prompted to reason zero-shot even outperformed a 32B model fine-tuned with SFT to predict answers directly on intervention and association queries. This highlights the substantial advantage provided by the reasoning prior, which RLVR aims to enhance.

- **RLVR Improves Specific Causal Reasoning Subskills:**
 - **Marginalization Strategy:** When LLMs possessed sufficient initial reasoning competence (7B and 32B models), RLVR training shifted their marginalization strategy towards "incremental marginalization," especially for more difficult queries involving larger relevant subgraphs. This is in contrast to "brute-force summation" or "no marginalization." The shift suggests that RLVR encourages more systematic and error-reducing approaches to complex calculations.
 - **Reduction of Abstract Reasoning Errors:** RLVR significantly reduced abstract probability and causal derivation errors. These include errors such as falsely assuming independence, dropping dependencies in formulas, or incorrectly treating interventions as observations. The presence of such errors was highly correlated with incorrect final answers.
 - **Reduction of Calculation and Copy Errors (Supplementary Analysis):** RLVR also led to a reduction in copy errors (incorrectly transcribing probability values) and arithmetic errors, though arithmetic errors sometimes still resulted in correct answers due to the rounding tolerance in the `CORRECT_t` metric.

- **Limitations on Counterfactual Reasoning:**
 - Models, both before and after RLVR, did not consistently attempt to construct "twin-networks" or perform inference over exogenous variables, which are standard methods for counterfactual reasoning. Even providing explicit hints in the system prompt about twin-network construction did not substantially improve performance on counterfactual queries, suggesting a fundamental difficulty in this specific formal reasoning task for current LLMs, even after RLVR.
 - However, on a simpler set of deterministic counterfactual problems derived from CLadder, RLVR models demonstrated high accuracy, indicating that the complexity of abduction and marginalization in the RLCausal dataset’s counterfactual queries is a key challenge.

- **Comparison with SFT on Rejection-Sampled Chains (Supplementary):** Fine-tuning on correct reasoning chains collected via rejection sampling improved SFT performance. However, this method still generally underperformed RLVR, particularly on more difficult problem splits, suggesting that the on-policy nature of RLVR contributes to its superior generalization, in addition to the benefit of training on explicit reasoning steps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides empirical insights into the generalization capabilities of RLVR for LLMs engaged in formal causal reasoning, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of this post-training paradigm.

- **Clarifying RLVR Generalization Conditions:** The findings demonstrate that RLVR's effectiveness in generalization is contingent on the LLM's initial reasoning competence. It is less effective when the base model's ability to perform relevant reasoning steps is too low (e.g., 3B models or complex counterfactuals). Conversely, with a sufficient foundation, RLVR significantly improves generalization, outperforming SFT. This informs future RLVR applications by suggesting the importance of a capable base model or effective warm-starting strategies.
- **Deepening Understanding of LLM Reasoning:** The detailed analysis of reasoning traces reveals *how* RLVR enhances reasoning. It promotes more systematic problem-solving strategies (e.g., incremental marginalization) and reduces domain-specific abstract reasoning errors. This sheds light on the types of cognitive "subskills" that RLVR can cultivate or refine within LLMs, which is critical for developing more reliable and interpretable AI systems.
- **Highlighting Challenges in Formal Causal Inference:** The study underscores the specific difficulties LLMs face with formal counterfactual reasoning problems, particularly those requiring complex abduction and marginalization over specified causal graphs. This contrasts with some more positive findings in common-sense causal reasoning, indicating a gap that warrants further investigation. This suggests areas where LLM architectures or training methodologies need to be advanced to handle the full spectrum of formal causal inference.
- **Development of Specialized LLMs:** The RLCausal dataset and its generation process can serve as a valuable resource for training and probing specific reasoning subskills essential for complex probabilistic and causal problems. Although the problems are abstract, the identified subskills (marginalization, probability identity application, arithmetic) are fundamental to practical applications in science and engineering (e.g., biomedical, chemistry), where domain-specific RLVR fine-tuning is increasingly being explored. The scalability of the dataset generation in terms of graph size and variable cardinality offers a flexible testbed for pushing the boundaries of LLM capabilities.
- **Contribution to AI Safety and Reliability:** Understanding the conditions and mechanisms of generalization for reasoning tasks is crucial for developing AI systems that can reliably operate in high-stakes domains. By dissecting RLVR's impact on causal reasoning, this work contributes to building more robust and trustworthy AI.
