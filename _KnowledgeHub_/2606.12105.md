# DAM-VLA: Decoupled Asynchronous Multimodal Vision Language Action model

> [!summary] Summary
> DAM-VLA introduces a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that processes robot sensor modalities asynchronously at their native rates, addressing the temporal mismatch of synchronous VLA architectures. This architecture achieved a 95.2% average success rate across seven contact-rich manipulation tasks, more than doubling the performance of synchronous baselines, while maintaining smooth and reactive 100 Hz control.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models use synchronous processing, which mismatches the heterogeneous and asynchronous nature of real-world robot sensor data.
- The synchronous paradigm leads to redundant computation, cross-modal rate mismatch (undersampling fast modalities like force, oversampling slow ones like vision), and increased action latency.
- Naive scaling of synchronous VLA models to higher control frequencies degrades performance due to contradictory training signals from repeated, identical visual frames paired with different actions.

## Method

- Developed **DAM-VLA**, a ==decoupled asynchronous== architecture that processes each modality (vision, force, proprioception, language) at its native sensor rate, maintaining per-modality latent buffers.
- Implemented dual-pathway modulation using Gated Cross-Attention (GCA) to integrate new modalities like visual memory and force without disrupting the pretrained VLM backbone.
- Utilized a short-term visual memory (Z_mem) with GRU and cross-attention, alongside independently updated force tokens (Z_ft), allowing continuous action generation at the control frequency.

## Results

- The full DAM-VLA model achieved a **95.2%** average success rate across seven diverse contact-rich real-world tasks, representing a greater than twofold improvement over the strongest synchronous baseline (**40.95%**).
- DAM-VLA demonstrated robust **100 Hz** control with the lowest SPARC (8.1) for command smoothness and lowest tracking lag (**0.118s**) compared to other **100 Hz** configurations.
- Gated Cross-Attention proved critical, as a concatenation-based baseline (X-VLA_AFM) with identical inputs achieved only **54.3%** success, highlighting the importance of the integration method.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling temporal processing for each sensor modality and allowing them to update at their native rates significantly improves representation quality and control robustness in contact-rich manipulation.
> - Integrating new modalities into pretrained VLM backbones requires careful mechanisms like Gated Cross-Attention to avoid corrupting existing representations, which concatenation-based methods fail to achieve.
> - Naive frequency scaling of synchronous VLA models leads to severe performance degradation due to the generation of contradictory training signals and increased latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot manipulation necessitates robust models capable of operating reliably across diverse tasks and environments. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a foundational approach, leveraging pretrained vision-language representations to generalize capabilities. These models typically operate within an imitation learning framework where representation quality directly influences behavioral robustness.

However, existing VLA architectures largely inherit a synchronous processing paradigm from their Vision-Language Model (VLM) pretraining origins. This design implies a single, uniform clock for all sensor modalities, processing every input (e.g., vision, force-torque, proprioception, tactile) simultaneously at a fixed timestep. This synchronous approach, while suitable for language-grounded perception tasks, presents fundamental mismatches with the real-world physics of robotic interaction. Robotic sensors inherently operate at heterogeneous rates: high-frequency modalities like force-torque can change at hundreds of hertz, vision typically updates more slowly (e.g., 3-10 Hz), and language instructions remain constant throughout an episode.

The synchronous paradigm leads to several issues:
1. **Redundant compute:** The VLM encoder re-processes semantically identical frames repeatedly, consuming computational resources that could be allocated to higher action rates.
2. **Cross-modal rate mismatch:** A uniform clock either undersamples fast-updating modalities, potentially missing critical transient events (e.g., contact forces), or oversamples slow-updating ones, leading to redundant processing.
3. **Action latency:** Policy execution is gated by the arrival of a complete, synchronized observation bundle, increasing latency and capping the effective control frequency at the rate of the slowest modality.

Previous work in asynchronous and efficient VLA inference has explored strategies such as overlapping chunk generation and execution, attaching lightweight correction heads, or splitting models into slow semantic VLM pathways and fast action pathways, often at fixed frequency ratios. Other efforts focus on efficiency through caching static visual tokens or system-level optimizations, or addressing temporal misalignment between prediction and execution. However, these generally assume a synchronized observation bundle at each inference cycle or address specific fixed-ratio splits. They do not broadly consider heterogeneous sensors streaming at diverse rates or how the modality integration mechanism affects representation quality.

Recent advancements in multimodal sensing for VLAs have integrated additional modalities like force and tactile sensing, primarily for contact-rich manipulation. These approaches typically fuse force signals synchronously into a shared token stream. Similarly, VLAs incorporating latent memory for temporally extended tasks often enforce a synchronous update regime across all sensor streams.

This work builds upon the foundation of VLA models and addresses the limitations of synchronous processing by proposing a fundamentally decoupled, asynchronous architecture. It differentiates itself by explicitly handling heterogeneous sensory streams updating at different rates, maintaining per-modality latent buffers, and introducing a novel gating mechanism for integrating new modalities without corrupting pretrained representations. This approach is motivated by prior findings that training with native asynchronous RGB and force/torque streams can outperform downsampled baselines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model capable of robustly performing contact-rich robot manipulation tasks by addressing the inherent temporal mismatch between synchronous VLA architectures and the heterogeneous, asynchronous nature of real-world sensor data.

The motivation stems from the observation that traditional VLA models, adapted from vision-language pretraining, operate on a single, shared synchronous clock. This design is misaligned with physical interaction, where sensor modalities generate information at significantly different frequencies and retain relevance over distinct temporal horizons. For instance, force-torque signals change rapidly (100-500 Hz), visual information evolves more slowly (3-10 Hz), and language instructions remain constant.

The authors hypothesize that decoupling temporal processing for each modality, allowing each to update and retain information at its native sensor rate, will yield stronger representations and more robust control. This decoupling principle extends across two dimensions:

1. **Asynchrony:** Each modality is processed at its natural sensor rate, avoiding downsampling or upsampling to a shared clock.
2. **Temporal Context:** Each modality maintains a buffer sized to its meaningful temporal horizon, preserving both short-lived and long-lived signals at their appropriate timescales.

The specific challenges that motivate this decoupled approach, which synchronous fixed-clock models fail to address, are:

- **Redundant compute:** The costly VLM encoder is repeatedly invoked to re-process visually identical frames, wasting computational resources.
- **Cross-modal rate mismatch:** A uniform clock simultaneously undersamples fast modalities (missing critical contact transients) and oversamples slow modalities (processing redundant visual data), preventing any modality from being processed at its optimal rate.
- **Action latency:** Policy execution is delayed, awaiting a fully synchronized observation bundle, which directly increases latency and limits the effective control frequency.

Based on these insights, the paper aims to contribute:

1. **An asynchronous multimodal architecture:** Introducing a decoupled processing design where each modality stream updates independently at its natural frequency, with per-modality temporal context windows tailored to the signal's meaningful horizon.
2. **Improved performance through asynchronous representations:** Demonstrating that by preserving the natural information structure of each sensor, the proposed architecture learns multimodal representations leading to higher task success rates compared to synchronous baselines.
3. **Reduced inference latency:** Showing that decoupling action generation from slow modality update cycles (e.g., periodic VLM re-encoding) enables the policy to act continuously at the control frequency, thereby reducing end-to-end latency and increasing the effective control frequency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, Decoupled Asynchronous Multimodal Vision Language Action (DAM-VLA), introduces an architecture designed to process heterogeneous sensor modalities at their native frequencies, maintaining per-modality latent buffers, and integrating new information via gated cross-attention.

**Problem Formulation:**
The policy receives observations from *M* heterogeneous sensing modalities, each producing information at distinct rates. In the experiments, these include a third-person scene camera (25Hz), a wrist-mounted camera (25Hz), force/torque (100Hz), proprioception (100Hz), and a static language instruction. The synchronous VLA approach, which queries all modalities at a single control frequency, is identified as suboptimal. DAM-VLA decouples each modality's update rate from the control rate, storing per-modality latent contexts (z_m) in a shared buffer. The action head continuously reads this buffer, ensuring action generation is not blocked by individual modality update rates.

**Asynchronous Data Collection:**
Instead of synchronizing all sensor streams, data is collected independently with per-modality timestamps. For training, observation context for each action label is formed by fetching a fixed history window from each modality at its natural temporal resolution: 16 visual frames at 25Hz (0.64 s context) and 96 samples each of proprioception and force/torque at 100Hz (0.96 s context). This provides semantic visual context over a longer horizon and high-resolution contact/state information over shorter periods.

**DAM-VLA Architecture:**
The core of DAM-VLA involves a multimodal asynchronous latent buffer and a dual-pathway modulation mechanism using gated cross-attention (GCA).

1. **Multimodal Asynchronous Latent Buffer:** A shared latent buffer *B* = {*Z*<sub>m</sub>} holds one token sequence per modality. Each *Z*<sub>m</sub> ∈ R<sup>N<sub>m</sub>×d</sup> is refreshed at a modality-specific rate. The action head reads the entire buffer at every inference step.
 - **Language tokens:** Encoded once at the beginning of an episode.
 - **Visual tokens:** From primary and wrist cameras, encoded by a VLM encoder. To avoid redundant re-encoding, the visual encoder is invoked every 4 inference steps. A short-term visual memory (Z<sub>mem</sub>) is maintained by appending new frame embeddings to a rolling buffer of K most recent frames, encoded by a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), and compressed to N<sub>mem</sub> tokens via learned-query cross-attention. This allows Z<sub>mem</sub> to remain valid across multiple inference updates.
 - **Proprioception tokens:** Joint states concatenated with action tokens, read at the full 100Hz control rate, then encoded and processed in the latent buffer.
 - **Force tokens:** Joint-torque readings (from Franka's internal sensor) arrive at 100Hz, smoothed via exponential moving average, accumulated in a rolling buffer, encoded by a GRU, and compressed to Z<sub>ft</sub> via cross-attention. Z<sub>ft</sub> is updated every control step independently of the visual schedule.

2. **Dual-pathway Modulation via Gated Cross-Attention (GCA):**
 Instead of concatenating heterogeneous streams into a single sequence (which can disrupt pretrained VLM weights), DAM-VLA augments the action expert's token sequence with two parallel GCA pathways:
 - **Visual Memory pathway:** Compressed visual memory tokens Z<sub>mem</sub> condition action tokens Z<sup>(ℓ)</sup> via a zero-initialized residual:
 Z<sup>(ℓ+1)</sup> = Z<sup>(ℓ)</sup> + tanh(α) CA(LN(Z<sup>(ℓ)</sup>), Z<sub>mem</sub>)
 Here, α is a learned scalar initialized to zero, allowing gradual contribution without disrupting pretrained representations. A global gate is used because visual context is broadly relevant.
 - **Additional Modality pathway (e.g., Force):** Other modalities use input-dependent gates. For force tokens Z<sub>ft</sub>:
 Z<sup>(ℓ+1)</sup> = Z<sup>(ℓ)</sup> + σ(W ¯z<sub>ft</sub>) CA(LN(Z<sup>(ℓ)</sup>), Z<sub>ft</sub>)
 ¯z<sub>ft</sub> is the mean-pooled force token. The sigmoid gate is initialized near-closed. This input-dependent gate allows the network to learn when force information is relevant (e.g., during contact), preventing noise during free motion. Crucially, force queries the pre-memory-update action tokens Z<sup>(ℓ)</sup> to compute a pure additive delta, maintaining orthogonality between conditioning pathways.

**Training and Inference-time Asynchrony:**
During training, all modalities are initially aligned to a common 100 Hz timeline for consistent action labeling. Visual observations are then sampled with stride *S* to recover a sparse history matching the camera's sensor rate, while force is sampled consecutively at 100 Hz, mirroring the inference-time buffer. The system uses asynchronous, delay-aware execution.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on a Franka Emika Panda arm with a fixed third-person camera and a wrist-mounted camera (25 Hz RGB) and Franka's internal force/torque and proprioception sensors (100 Hz). Evaluation is performed on seven contact-rich real-world manipulation tasks: Scarf folding, Whiteboard cleaning, Button pressing, Handwash top press, Socket insertion, Sweep beads into a dustpan, and Lego piece arranging. Metrics include task success rate (over 15 trials) and average episode length.

**Baselines and Ablations:**
Six configurations are evaluated, all built on the X-VLA backbone:
- **X-VLA<sub>25</sub>:** Standard synchronous VLA regime at 25 Hz.
- **X-VLA<sub>100</sub>:** Naive high-frequency synchronous VLA at 100 Hz (upsamples visual frames).
- **X-VLA<sub>AFM</sub>:** Concatenation baseline, using the same force and memory info as DAM-VLA but integrating via a flat token sequence.
- **DAM-VLA<sub>/F/M</sub>:** Asynchronous decoupling alone (vision sparse, proprioception at every step), without explicit force or memory pathways.
- **DAM-VLA<sub>/F</sub>:** DAM-VLA with memory contribution pathway enabled.
- **DAM-VLA<sub>/M</sub>:** DAM-VLA with force contribution pathway enabled.
- **DAM-VLA (Ours):** Full model with both force and memory GCA pathways.
All policies are run on a 100 Hz controller, with replanning frequencies varying based on the model. Ablations also included tests on a 200 Hz controller.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation addressed four research questions (RQ1-RQ4), demonstrating the effectiveness of the DAM-VLA architecture.

**RQ1: Synchronous processing limits manipulation performance, and naive frequency scaling does not resolve this.**
- **X-VLA<sub>25</sub>** achieved an average success rate of 40.95%. It performed well on visually-guided tasks (e.g., Whiteboard cleaning 86.7%, Sweep 100%) but struggled with contact-rich tasks (Handwash 0%, Lego 0%, Socket insertion 6.7%).
- Increasing the control frequency to 100 Hz for **X-VLA<sub>100</sub>** resulted in a further degradation of average success to 21.9%. Performance dropped across almost all tasks, with Sweep falling to 53.3% and Whiteboard to 13.3%. This degradation is attributed to the naive upsampling of visual observations, which paired identical frames with different action labels, creating contradictory training signals. This led to hesitant, jerky movements and stalled execution. These findings confirm that naive frequency scaling without architectural changes is not a viable strategy for high-frequency manipulation.

**RQ2: Asynchronous decoupling alone recovers performance lost by naive frequency scaling.**
- **DAM-VLA<sub>/F/M</sub>**, which implemented asynchronous decoupling without additional modalities (sparse vision, 100 Hz proprioception), achieved an average success rate of 40.0%. This nearly matched X-VLA<sub>25</sub>'s performance while operating at a higher control frequency (100 Hz vs. 25 Hz for X-VLA<sub>25</sub>) and significantly outperformed X-VLA<sub>100</sub>. The cached vision at inference prevented the execution collapse observed in X-VLA<sub>100</sub>. Decoupling also enabled initial success on tasks where synchronous baselines entirely failed, such as Button press (40.0% vs. 13.3%) and Handwash (20.0% vs. 0.0%). Some regression was noted on visually-guided tasks like Sweep (66.7% vs. 100.0%) due to the absence of a visual memory pathway to preserve context between sparse updates.

**RQ3: Additional modalities at their sensor rates further improve the decoupled foundation.**
- Building upon the DAM-VLA<sub>/F/M</sub> baseline (40.0%), every configuration that integrated additional modality information showed improvement: X-VLA<sub>AFM</sub> (54.3%), DAM-VLA<sub>/F</sub> (58.1%), DAM-VLA<sub>/M</sub> (66.7%), and the full DAM-VLA (95.2%). This confirmed that the decoupled architecture serves as a robust foundation for integrating new modalities, each contributing independently.
- **Memory alone (DAM-VLA<sub>/F</sub>)** provided gains, achieving 100% on Scarf and Sweep, and 86.7% on Button press. However, it struggled with contact-critical tasks, showing partial presses on Button, over-pressing on Handwash, and 0% on Lego.
- **Force alone (DAM-VLA<sub>/M</sub>)** also improved over the decoupled baseline, achieving 86.7% on Button and 80.0% on Handwash. However, without memory, it sometimes resulted in repeated actions (e.g., multipressing on Handwash in 46.67% of rollouts) or alignment failures (e.g., overshooting on Lego).
- The **full DAM-VLA model** successfully resolved the failure modes of the individual components by combining force-guided contact (for precise termination) with memory-stabilized sequencing (for long-horizon context and preventing repetitions). It achieved 93.3% on Lego and 80.0% on Socket, tasks where all synchronous baselines scored near zero. On Sweep, while DAM-VLA<sub>/F</sub> achieved 100% success, DAM-VLA completed episodes significantly faster (22.5s vs. 40s), demonstrating decisiveness and efficiency.

**RQ4: Gated cross-attention preserves pretrained representations under new modalities.**
- **X-VLA<sub>AFM</sub>**, despite having the same force and memory inputs as DAM-VLA, integrated them by concatenating tokens into a flat sequence. This resulted in a significant performance degradation across all tasks (54.3% vs. 95.2%). The authors attribute this to new, unseen modality tokens being pushed through pretrained self-attention weights, disrupting the backbone's existing visual-language features. This led to issues like under-pressing, repeated actions (e.g., Handwash multipressing), and stalling.
- The **Gated Cross-Attention (GCA)** mechanism in DAM-VLA avoided this by adding new tokens as zero-initialized residuals, leaving pretrained weights untouched. This finding emphasizes that the *method* of integrating new information into a pretrained backbone is as crucial as the information itself.

**Additional Results:**
- **Motion Smoothness:** On the Sweep task, **DAM-VLA** achieved the lowest SPARC (8.1), indicating the smoothest commands among 100 Hz configurations. It also recorded the lowest tracking lag (0.118s), meaning the robot's measured motion followed commands more promptly.
- **Execution Time:** DAM-VLA consistently produced shorter or near-shortest episode lengths on successful tasks, reflecting decisive execution without hesitation or unnecessary repetitions, as evidenced by its 24.4s average for Whiteboard compared to X-VLA<sub>100</sub>'s 83s.
- **Replanning Frequency Ablation (200 Hz controller):** DAM-VLA maintained 100% success on Handwash and Whiteboard at 200 Hz with different execution horizons (s=22 for 8 Hz replanning and s=6 for 17 Hz replanning). While s=22 yielded smooth, fluid motion, s=6 resulted in reactive but visibly less fluid motion. Further reducing horizons beyond s=6 led to task success degradation, indicating a bottleneck at very high replanning rates due to remaining VLM encoding latency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces DAM-VLA, a novel architectural principle for VLA models that significantly improves robotic manipulation capabilities by addressing the fundamental mismatch between synchronous model assumptions and the asynchronous nature of real-world sensor data.

The primary significance lies in demonstrating that **decoupling temporal processing per modality**, allowing each sensor to update and retain information at its natural rate, leads to substantially stronger representations and more robust control in robotic manipulation. This approach yielded a greater than twofold increase in average success rate (95.2% vs. 40.95%) compared to the strongest synchronous baseline across diverse, contact-rich real-world tasks, while maintaining smooth, reactive 100 Hz control.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** By enabling VLA models to process high-frequency contact information reactively and integrate long-term visual memory efficiently, DAM-VLA can improve robot performance on a wider range of complex, dexterous manipulation tasks, particularly those involving precise contact or sequential steps.
- **Improved Efficiency and Real-time Performance:** The asynchronous design reduces redundant computation and action latency, allowing for higher effective control frequencies without sacrificing performance. This is crucial for real-world robotic systems where timely and responsive actions are critical.
- **Generalizable Multimodal Integration:** The proposed Gated Cross-Attention mechanism provides a method for introducing new, heterogeneous modalities into pretrained VLM backbones without corrupting their existing representations. This architecture can be extended to integrate other fast or slow sensors, making it a flexible framework for future multimodal robot learning.
- **Foundational Shift in VLA Design:** This work challenges the inherited synchronous paradigm of VLA models and proposes a more biologically plausible and physically aligned architectural principle. It suggests that future VLA development should prioritize asynchronous, multi-rate processing.

The authors also acknowledge limitations and outline future work, such as using force to correct actions mid-chunk for even better performance on very contact-heavy tasks, implementing event-triggered vision updates (rather than fixed timers), and exploring external Force/Torque (F/T) sensors and torque-level control. Addressing these could further extend the model's capabilities and solidify the impact of the decoupled asynchronous approach. Overall, DAM-VLA represents a significant step towards more capable, robust, and responsive robot manipulation policies grounded in a better understanding of multimodal sensor integration.
