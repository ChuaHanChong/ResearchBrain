# GPQA: A Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q&A Benchmark

> [!summary] Summary
> The GPQA benchmark evaluates the ability of advanced AI systems to provide truthful information in complex scientific domains where human evaluators lack expertise. It reveals that current large language models perform similarly to skilled non-experts, remaining far below human expert performance on these challenging, 'Google-proof' questions.

## Problem

- Current AI alignment methods like RLHF rely on human evaluators, but this approach fails when AI systems develop narrow superhuman capabilities beyond human verification.
- Existing benchmarks like MMLU are not sufficiently challenging to evaluate AI models on complex, specialized knowledge requiring deep reasoning, making it difficult to assess truthful information extraction.
- There is a need for robust methods to ensure AI systems provide accurate and verifiable information in domains where human experts are scarce or unable to directly confirm outputs.

## Method

- The **GPQA** benchmark features graduate-level ==multiple-choice questions== in biology, chemistry, and physics, specifically designed to be ==Google-proof==.
- A rigorous ==multi-stage creation process== involving PhD-level domain experts for question writing and two rounds of ==expert validation== ensures accuracy and quality.
- ==Non-expert validation==, by highly-skilled individuals with PhDs in unrelated fields, verifies that questions are genuinely difficult to answer without specialized domain knowledge, even with internet access.

## Results

- Human domain experts achieved **65%** accuracy on the GPQA extended set (**74%** when accounting for expert mistakes), confirming the questions are solvable with expertise.
- Skilled non-experts, despite unlimited internet access and an average of **37 minutes** per question, attained only **34%** accuracy, validating the 'Google-proof' design.
- State-of-the-art LLMs like GPT-4, even with search capabilities and advanced prompting, reached a maximum of **39.4%** accuracy, performing only marginally better than non-experts and well below true experts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The benchmark effectively demonstrates a significant gap between the capabilities of current state-of-the-art LLMs and human domain experts in graduate-level scientific knowledge.
> - Designing 'Google-proof' questions, where even skilled non-experts with internet access struggle, is crucial for developing and testing scalable oversight methods for superhuman AI.
> - The creation process emphasizes rigorous multi-stage expert and non-expert validation to ensure question quality, objectivity, and targeted difficulty.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

GPQA (Graduate-level Google-Proof Q&A Benchmark) represents an important advancement in AI evaluation methods, particularly in light of increasingly capable large language models (LLMs). Developed by researchers from New York University, Cohere, and Anthropic, GPQA tackles a fundamental challenge in AI safety: how do we ensure AI systems provide truthful information in domains where human evaluators lack the expertise to verify answers?

The benchmark consists of challenging multiple-choice questions in biology, chemistry, and physics at the graduate level that are deliberately designed to be "Google-proof" - meaning that even skilled non-experts struggle to answer them correctly despite having unlimited internet access. This characteristic makes GPQA particularly valuable for researching scalable oversight methods, which aim to reliably extract truthful information from AI systems that may possess capabilities beyond human verifiability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

As LLMs continue to advance, we face the increasing possibility of AI systems that possess narrow superhuman capabilities in specific domains. Current approaches to aligning these models with human values, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), rely on human annotators being able to accurately evaluate model outputs. However, this approach breaks down when models begin answering questions in specialized domains beyond the knowledge of most human evaluators.

This creates a paradox: we want to use AI to help solve complex problems, but we need ways to verify their outputs are correct even when we lack the expertise to judge directly. This is the scalable oversight problem.

Previous benchmarks like MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020) test knowledge across various domains but aren't sufficiently challenging to evaluate potential solutions to this problem. GPQA addresses this gap by creating questions that:

1. Have objectively correct answers
2. Are so difficult that skilled non-experts cannot reliably solve them even with internet access
3. Can still be solved reliably by true domain experts

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Dataset Creation Methodology

The GPQA creation process involved a rigorous multi-stage pipeline designed to ensure question quality and difficulty:

1. **Question Writing**: Domain experts with PhD-level knowledge in biology, chemistry, or physics were recruited through Upwork to create challenging, objective multiple-choice questions in their areas of expertise. Each question writer provided:
 - A question with 4-5 answer choices
 - Detailed explanations for why each answer is correct or incorrect
 - References to supporting literature
 - Metadata about the question's subdomain and difficulty

2. **Expert Validation**: Each question underwent two rounds of expert validation:
 - First validation: Another expert in the same field attempted to answer the question and provided detailed feedback
 - Question revision: The original writer revised questions based on feedback
 - Second validation: A different expert attempted the revised question

3. **Non-Expert Validation**: To verify that questions were genuinely "Google-proof," three highly-skilled non-experts (individuals with PhDs in unrelated fields) attempted each question with unlimited internet access. They were given ample time (averaging 37 minutes per question) and incentivized to answer correctly.

4. **Dataset Curation**: Based on the validation results, the questions were organized into three sets:
 - GPQA Extended: 546 questions (the full set)
 - GPQA Main: 448 questions where at least half of experts agree on the answer and no more than two-thirds of non-experts answer correctly
 - GPQA Diamond: 198 higher-quality questions where experts show strong agreement and no more than one-third of non-experts answer correctly

#### 3.2. Question Standards and Quality Control

The researchers implemented several quality control mechanisms:

- **Expert Qualifications**: All question writers and expert validators were required to have completed or be pursuing a PhD in their domain, with preference given to those with high Upwork ratings.

- **Incentive Structure**: The payment system included bonuses for writing questions that met quality standards, properly balanced difficulty, and withstood expert scrutiny.

- **Objectivity Analysis**: The team manually analyzed cases where expert validators disagreed with question writers to determine whether disagreements stemmed from genuine ambiguity or simple mistakes. They conservatively estimated that 74% of questions had uncontroversially correct answers.

- **Domain Separation**: Experts were prevented from validating questions in closely related subdomains to their own research to ensure independence.

Example GPQA question format:

```
Question: Which of the following is NOT a feature of the mechanism of the Clemmensen reduction?
(A) It proceeds through a carbocation intermediate
(B) Amalgamated zinc is used as a reducing agent
(C) It requires acidic conditions
(D) It reduces ketones to alkanes

Answer: A
```

#### 3.3. Dataset Characteristics

The final GPQA dataset has several notable characteristics:

- **Subject Distribution**: Roughly equal distribution across biology, chemistry, and physics.

- **Expert Performance**: True experts achieved 65% accuracy on the extended set (rising to 74% when accounting for expert mistakes), demonstrating that the questions are challenging but solvable with appropriate expertise.

- **Non-Expert Performance**: Despite spending 37 minutes per question on average with unrestricted internet access, skilled non-experts achieved only 34% accuracy, confirming the "Google-proof" nature of the questions.

- **Difficulty Gradient**: Questions span various difficulty levels, with experts reasonably able to judge difficulty (correlation of 0.47 between predicted and actual expert accuracy).

- **Question Types**: The dataset includes various question types that test conceptual understanding, application of principles, analysis of complex scenarios, and knowledge of specialized terminology and methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Performance Analysis

To establish baseline performances, the researchers evaluated several state-of-the-art LLMs on GPQA:

- **Closed-book Models**: Llama-2-70B-chat, GPT-3.5-turbo-16k, and GPT-4 were tested without external knowledge access.

- **Open-book Model**: GPT-4 with search capability was tested to simulate internet access.

- **Prompting Approaches**: Zero-shot, few-shot, zero-shot chain-of-thought, and few-shot chain-of-thought prompting were compared.

The results showed that even the most advanced AI systems struggled with GPQA:

- GPT-4 with few-shot chain-of-thought prompting achieved only 39% accuracy on the extended set.
- GPT-4 with search performed similarly at 39.4% accuracy.
- These results are only marginally better than the non-expert human baseline (34%) and significantly below true expert performance (65-74%).

This performance gap demonstrates that GPQA effectively captures knowledge and reasoning capabilities that current AI systems haven't fully mastered, making it a valuable benchmark for tracking progress.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GPQA serves several important functions in AI research:

1. **Evaluating Scalable Oversight Methods**: The dataset provides a realistic testbed for techniques like AI safety via debate, market-making, and recursive reward modeling, which aim to extract truthful information from AI systems even when human evaluators can't directly verify correctness.

2. **Measuring Reasoning Capabilities**: The difficulty of questions requires deep conceptual understanding and complex reasoning rather than simple fact retrieval.

3. **Guiding Model Development**: By identifying specific gaps in model capabilities, GPQA can inform targeted improvements in knowledge representation and reasoning.

4. **Simulating Supervision Challenges**: The benchmark allows researchers to simulate scenarios where AI systems may exceed human capabilities in narrow domains, testing our ability to maintain effective oversight.

The dataset satisfies seven of the nine desiderata for scalable oversight benchmarks proposed by Irving & Askell (2019), making it particularly well-suited for advancing research in AI alignment and safety.

#### 5.1. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its strengths, GPQA has several limitations:

- **Limited Size**: With 546 questions, the dataset is relatively small compared to other benchmarks, limiting its statistical power for detecting small accuracy differences between models.

- **Specialized Non-Experts**: The non-expert validators used were highly skilled (PhD-level) individuals, which may overestimate the difficulty of questions for average users.

- **Coverage Gaps**: The questions cover only three scientific domains and may not represent all types of knowledge where AI systems could develop superhuman capabilities.

- **Potential Contamination**: As the benchmark becomes more widely known, there's a risk that future AI training data may include information about GPQA questions.

Future work could expand GPQA to include more domains, develop versions with different difficulty levels, or create dynamic question generation methods to avoid contamination concerns.

#### 5.2. Conclusion

GPQA represents a significant advancement in AI evaluation methodology, specifically targeting the crucial challenge of scalable oversight. By creating a benchmark of genuinely difficult questions that resist internet-aided solution but remain objectively answerable by true experts, the researchers have provided a valuable tool for developing and testing methods to ensure AI truthfulness.

As AI systems continue to advance in capability, benchmarks like GPQA will be essential for developing reliable oversight mechanisms that allow humans to safely leverage AI capabilities that may eventually exceed human expertise in specific domains. The gap between current AI performance and expert human performance on GPQA suggests there remains substantial room for improvement in both AI capabilities and oversight methods.
