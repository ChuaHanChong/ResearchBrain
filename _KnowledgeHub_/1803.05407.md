# Averaging Weights Leads to Wider Optima and Better Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) is a simple optimization technique that improves the generalization performance of deep neural networks by finding wider optima. It consistently outperforms conventional SGD across various architectures and datasets, achieving ensemble-like accuracy with a single model.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks trained with conventional SGD often converge to specific local minima, which may not be the optimal points for generalization.
- Ensembling multiple models is effective for boosting accuracy but incurs significant computational cost during inference, limiting practical deployment.
- The concept of "flat" versus "sharp" minima and their impact on generalization remained a subject of debate and lacked a practical method to consistently find flatter optima.

## Method

- ==Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA)== modifies the standard training procedure by averaging model weights during the final portion of training.
- During the SWA phase, training continues with SGD using either ==cyclical== or high constant learning rates, periodically capturing model weights.
- The final SWA model is the simple arithmetic average of the captured weights, with a post-averaging step to re-calculate ==Batch Normalization== statistics.

## Results

- SWA consistently improved test accuracy by **0.**5%** to 1.5%** on CIFAR datasets and **0.**6%** to 0.9%** on ImageNet, outperforming conventional SGD across various architectures.
- Empirical analysis shows that SWA solutions are significantly flatter and wider in the loss landscape compared to those found by standard SGD.
- SWA achieves generalization performance comparable to model ensembles like Fast Geometric Ensembling (FGE) with virtually no additional computational overhead during inference, using only a single model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - SGD with a high learning rate explores a region of high-performing networks, and SWA finds the center of this region, leading to more robust solutions.
> - Averaging weights in SWA effectively approximates the generalization benefits of ensembling (like FGE) with the efficiency of a single model.
> - SWA solutions are demonstrably wider and flatter in the loss landscape, providing empirical evidence that wider optima generalize better.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper significantly contributes to the ongoing discourse in deep learning regarding optimization, generalization, and the geometry of neural network loss surfaces. It addresses the fundamental challenge of why deep neural networks generalize well despite being highly over-parameterized and trained on non-convex loss landscapes.

Historically, deep neural networks have been trained using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) or its variants (e.g., Adam, RMSprop) with a decaying learning rate schedule. This conventional approach aims to converge to a single local minimum. However, recent theoretical and empirical work has begun to unravel the complex geometry of these loss surfaces. Studies by Garipov et al. (2018) and Draxler et al. (2018) demonstrated that local optima in deep neural networks are not isolated but can be connected by simple, low-loss curves. This "mode connectivity" insight provides a backdrop for understanding why exploring the weight space can be beneficial.

A major debate in deep learning theory revolves around the concept of "flat" versus "sharp" minima. Keskar et al. (2017) hypothesized that flatter minima generalize better than sharper ones, a notion supported by earlier work (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). Conversely, Dinh et al. (2017) challenged this by arguing that existing definitions of sharpness are insufficient. This paper robustly supports the "flat minima generalize better" hypothesis by showing that Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) specifically converges to wider, flatter regions of the loss landscape, which demonstrably leads to better generalization.

The concept of averaging model parameters is not entirely new; it dates back to convex optimization (Ruppert, 1988; Polyak and Juditsky, 1992). In deep learning, exponential moving averages (EMA) of weights have been used to smooth training trajectories. However, Izmailov et al. clearly differentiate SWA from EMA, demonstrating that a simple (non-exponential) average with specific learning rate schedules yields fundamentally different and superior results.

A critical piece of the immediate research context for SWA is Fast Geometric Ensembling (FGE) (Garipov et al., 2018). FGE showed that by using cyclical learning rates, one could collect multiple diverse models near the same low-loss region and ensemble their predictions to achieve high accuracy without significant additional training time. SWA emerges as a natural evolution of FGE, aiming to achieve similar performance benefits with a *single* model, thus eliminating the computational overhead of ensembling during inference. This positions SWA as a highly practical and scalable alternative to explicit ensembling.

Furthermore, the paper touches upon the interpretation of SGD with constant learning rates as approximate Bayesian inference, where the optimizer samples from a posterior distribution (Mandt et al., 2017). From this perspective, SWA can be seen as averaging these samples to estimate the mean of the distribution, thereby moving towards the "center" of a region of high-performing models rather than staying on its periphery. This connects SWA to the broader field of Bayesian deep learning, albeit in a highly practical, non-explicitly Bayesian manner.

Finally, SWA acts as a powerful regularizer, akin to techniques like Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014), both aiming to improve generalization by approximating an ensemble of models. The paper shows that SWA can even be combined with existing regularization techniques for further improvements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to significantly improve the generalization performance of deep neural networks. The authors are driven by several key motivations:

1. **Exploiting Loss Surface Geometry:** Recent insights from research into the geometry of deep learning loss surfaces (e.g., mode connectivity) suggest that conventional SGD training, which typically converges to a single point, might not be finding the *best* possible solution. The observation from FGE that SGD with cyclical learning rates explores regions of high-performing networks, yet these individual points lie on the "periphery" of optimal solutions, motivates the idea that averaging these points could lead to a more "centered" and robust solution.
2. **Bridging the Gap between Ensembles and Single Models:** While model ensembling (like FGE) has proven highly effective in boosting accuracy, it comes with a significant computational cost at inference time (predictions from multiple models). The authors sought a method that could approximate the generalization benefits of ensembling but with the efficiency, convenience, and interpretability of a single model.
3. **Finding Wider Optima:** The hypothesis that wider optima generalize better is a recurring theme in deep learning theory. The authors aim to empirically demonstrate that SWA leads to solutions that are indeed wider than those found by conventional SGD, thereby providing a practical method to achieve such desirable minima.
4. **Simplicity and Practicality:** A crucial motivation was to develop an optimization technique that is "extremely easy to implement" and has "almost no computational overhead." This focus on practical utility aims for broad adoption within the deep learning community.
5. **Challenging Conventional Training Paradigms:** The paper aims to show that the typical SGD training with a decaying learning rate until convergence might not be the optimal strategy, suggesting that maintaining a higher, more explorative learning rate combined with weight averaging can yield superior results.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology is the proposed **Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA)** algorithm.

#### 3.1. SWA Algorithm Details

SWA modifies the standard deep neural network training procedure. Instead of using a continuously decaying learning rate until convergence, SWA involves:
1. **Pre-training (Optional but Recommended):** The model is typically trained conventionally for a significant portion of its total training budget (e.g., 75% of epochs) to reach a good initial region of the loss landscape.
2. **SWA Phase:** From this pre-trained state, training continues with SGD using either:
 - **Cyclical Learning Rate Schedule:** The learning rate linearly decreases from a maximum ($\alpha_1$) to a minimum ($\alpha_2$) within each cycle. Critically, unlike some other cyclical schedules, it then "jumps" discontinuously back to $\alpha_1$ at the start of the next cycle. Model weights are captured and averaged at the end of each cycle (when the learning rate is at its minimum).
 - **Constant Learning Rate Schedule:** A fixed learning rate ($\alpha_1$) is used, and model weights are captured and averaged at regular intervals (e.g., every epoch).
3. **Weight Averaging:** The captured model weights ($w_i$) are simple arithmetically averaged to produce the final SWA model: $w_{SWA} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i$. This is a running average, updated iteratively.
4. **Batch Normalization Handling:** For networks using Batch Normalization, the running mean and variance statistics embedded within the BN layers are specific to the training dynamics and are not correctly captured by simple weight averaging. Therefore, after $w_{SWA}$ is finalized, an additional single forward pass over the training data is performed with $w_{SWA}$ to re-calculate these statistics for accurate inference.

#### 3.2. Analytical and Empirical Analysis

The paper employs a combination of theoretical intuition and extensive empirical validation:

- **Analysis of SGD Trajectories:** The authors visualize the train loss and test error surfaces in 2D planes defined by select points along SGD trajectories (with cyclical and constant learning rates). This visualization demonstrates how SGD explores the weight space and how SWA "centers" itself within these explored regions.
- **Solution Width Analysis:** To quantitatively and qualitatively compare the "flatness" of SWA and SGD solutions, the paper plots loss and error along random directions originating from $w_{SWA}$ and $w_{SGD}$, as well as along the direct line segment connecting $w_{SWA}$ and $w_{SGD}$. This analysis reveals that SWA indeed finds significantly wider optima.
- **Connection to Ensembling (FGE):** A theoretical linearization argument is presented to show that averaging weights (SWA) is a second-order approximation to averaging predictions (FGE), explaining why SWA can achieve ensemble-like performance with a single model. Empirical comparisons validate this claim.
- **Connection to Convex Minimization:** The paper draws parallels to established results in convex optimization (Polyak-Juditsky averaging) and the interpretation of SGD as approximate Bayesian inference, providing theoretical grounding for why averaging noisy SGD proposals converges to a more central, robust solution.

#### 3.3. Extensive Empirical Evaluation

The method is rigorously tested on a wide array of benchmark datasets and state-of-the-art architectures:
- **Datasets:** CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet ILSVRC-2012.
- **Architectures:** VGG-16, Preactivation ResNet-164, Wide ResNet-28-10, PyramidNet-272, Shake-Shake-2x64d, ResNet-50, ResNet-152, and DenseNet-161. This broad selection demonstrates SWA's architecture-agnostic nature.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against conventional SGD with decaying learning rates and, where applicable, FGE ensembles.
- **Metrics:** Test accuracy (Top-1 for ImageNet) is the primary metric, with means and standard deviations reported over multiple runs.
- **Training Budgets:** Experiments explore SWA's performance within the same training budget as conventional SGD, as well as extended budgets (1.25x, 1.5x) to show further improvements.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The paper also investigates the effect of different constant and cyclical learning rate parameters on SWA's performance, providing guidance on practical implementation.
- **Training from Scratch:** A surprising experiment demonstrates SWA's capability to train deep networks from random initialization with a fixed learning rate, although pre-training is recommended for stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings:

1. **Consistent Generalization Improvement:** SWA consistently and substantially outperforms conventional SGD training across all tested architectures and datasets.
 - On CIFAR-100, SWA achieved improvements of 0.75% to 1.5% in test accuracy.
 - On CIFAR-10, improvements were around 0.5%, except for Shake-Shake Networks which already achieve extremely high baseline accuracy.
 - On ImageNet, SWA provided 0.6% to 0.9% improvement in Top-1 accuracy for ResNet-50, ResNet-152, and DenseNet-161, even with just 10 additional SWA epochs.
2. **Wider Optima and Loss Surface Insights:** SWA solutions are demonstrably "much flatter" than those found by conventional SGD.
 - Visualizations of loss surfaces show that SWA centers itself within broad, low-loss regions, whereas SGD often converges to points near the *periphery* of these regions.
 - Despite occasionally having slightly worse training loss than conventional SGD, SWA models achieve significantly better test error, supporting the hypothesis that wider optima generalize better and that the training and test loss surfaces are shifted relative to each other.
 - The loss function is shown to be asymmetric in the direction connecting SWA and SGD, with SGD being near a steep ascent, highlighting SWA's ability to find flatter regions in critical directions.
3. **Efficient Approximation of Ensembles:** SWA effectively approximates the performance of Fast Geometric Ensembling (FGE) with a *single* model. This means SWA can achieve similar generalization benefits without the significant computational overhead of running multiple models at test time, offering superior practical convenience and interpretability.
4. **Negligible Computational Overhead:** SWA is exceptionally efficient. It adds virtually no computational time or memory overhead compared to conventional training, as it primarily involves maintaining and updating a running average of weights, an operation far less demanding than gradient computations.
5. **Robustness to Learning Rate Schedules:** While optimal performance varies, SWA proves effective with both constant and cyclical learning rate schedules. More aggressive constant learning rates often lead to faster SWA convergence.
6. **Capability for Training from Scratch:** The paper demonstrates that SWA can even be used to train deep networks from random initialization with a fixed learning rate, although starting from a partially pre-trained model is recommended for stability and faster convergence in practice. Individual models with a constant learning rate typically oscillate with poor accuracy, but their average (SWA) converges to a high-performing solution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) has significant implications for both the practice and theory of deep learning:

**Practical Impact:**
- **Easy Adoption:** SWA is remarkably simple to implement, requiring only minor modifications to existing training pipelines (maintaining a running average and a simple adjustment to the learning rate schedule). Its "drop-in replacement" nature makes it highly accessible for researchers and practitioners.
- **Near-Free Performance Boost:** The virtually zero computational overhead makes SWA an incredibly cost-effective way to improve model generalization. This is particularly valuable in resource-constrained environments or for large-scale deployments where every percentage point of accuracy and every computation matters.
- **Enhanced State-of-the-Art:** By consistently improving the performance of a wide range of state-of-the-art architectures on challenging benchmarks like ImageNet, SWA provides a new baseline for model accuracy.
- **Single Model Efficiency:** Achieving ensemble-like performance with a single model addresses a crucial practical limitation of traditional ensembling, making high-accuracy models more feasible for real-time applications and deployment.

**Theoretical and Conceptual Impact:**
- **Strong Support for Flat Minima Hypothesis:** The paper provides compelling empirical evidence that finding wider, flatter regions of the loss surface leads to better generalization, even for small-batch SGD. This strengthens a key theoretical link between optimization landscape and model performance.
- **Deeper Understanding of SGD Dynamics:** SWA offers new insights into how SGD explores the weight space. It suggests that SGD, especially with higher learning rates, effectively traverses the *periphery* of a region of good solutions, and that averaging these points allows one to find the *center* of this region, which is more robust.
- **Bridging Optimization and Generalization:** SWA effectively connects seemingly disparate areas: optimization algorithms, the geometry of loss surfaces, and the phenomenon of generalization. It shows how intelligent modification of the optimization trajectory can directly lead to better generalization.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The paper opens several exciting avenues for future work, including:
 - **Faster Convergence:** Exploring how SWA principles could lead to SGD variants that converge more rapidly to high-quality solutions.
 - **Large-Batch Training:** Potentially mitigating the "generalization gap" observed with large batch sizes by explicitly finding wider optima.
 - **Stochastic MCMC Connections:** Integrating SWA's exploration principles with advanced sampling techniques from Bayesian inference.
 - **Averaging Solution Regions:** Moving beyond point averaging to average entire "curves" or "regions" of good solutions, building on mode connectivity insights.

In essence, SWA is a simple yet powerful technique that offers immediate practical benefits for training deep neural networks while simultaneously contributing to a deeper theoretical understanding of why these complex models generalize. Its potential for widespread adoption and further research is substantial.
