# VLA-OS: Structuring and Dissecting Planning Representations and Paradigms in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at NUS and collaborating institutions developed VLA-OS, a unified framework to systematically evaluate planning representations and paradigms in Vision-Language-Action models. Their findings indicate that visually grounded planning consistently outperforms language-based planning, and that low-level policy learning, rather than high-level task planning, is the primary bottleneck in current robot manipulation systems.

## Problem

- The rapid proliferation of planning-integrated Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models has led to a fragmented research landscape with diverse architectures, planning paradigms, and representations.
- It is difficult to attribute performance gains in VLA models to specific components (e.g., planning, representation, architecture) or to identify bottlenecks (planning vs. policy execution).
- A lack of systematic comparisons hinders the guidance of future VLA development, relying more on intuition than empirical evidence.

## Method

- Introducing **VLA-OS**, a unified and composable Vision-Language-Action model series with an ==interchangeable VLM backbone== and plug-and-play planning/action heads, designed for systematic empirical comparison.
- Implementing three core VLA paradigms (ActionOnly, **Integrated-VLA**, **Hierarchical-VLA**) and meticulously annotating three distinct ==task planning representations== (language, visual, image foresight).
- Conducting a comprehensive experimental suite across diverse task domains (rigid, deformable, dexterous, dual-arm manipulation), modalities, and evaluation metrics, including novel metrics for planning correctness and policy following.

## Results

- Both Integrated-VLA and Hierarchical-VLA generally outperform ActionOnly-VLA across diverse tasks, with visually grounded planning demonstrating consistent superiority over language-based planning.
- Hierarchical-VLA exhibits better task planning correctness (Decomposition Score) and superior generalization capabilities under environmental perturbations.
- VLA paradigms with task planning show higher forward transfer but also faster forgetting in continual learning tasks compared to ActionOnly-VLA, though visually grounded planning mitigates forgetting better.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visually grounded planning representations (visual reasoning and image foresight) consistently outperform language-based planning in performance, generalization, and efficiency, and are easier for low-level policies to follow.
> - Policy learning is identified as a greater bottleneck than task planning itself, indicating that while high-level plans may be correct, the robot's ability to precisely execute them remains a primary challenge.
> - For Vision-Language-Action models trained from scratch with limited action-labeled data (around 5,000 demonstrations), smaller LLM backbones (0.5B parameters) are optimal, with larger models not providing benefits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical juncture in the rapidly evolving field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robot manipulation. Historically, robot control often relied on hand-engineered policies or reinforcement learning on constrained tasks. The advent of large-scale datasets and foundation models (like VLMs and LLMs) has spurred the development of VLAs, aiming for more generalizable and intelligent robotic agents.

The prevailing trend in VLA research has recently shifted from purely end-to-end action generation to a pipeline approach that explicitly incorporates "task planning" before action generation. This shift is motivated by the inherent complexity and long-horizon nature of many real-world manipulation tasks, which benefit from hierarchical reasoning. Approaches that involve planning have demonstrated improved performance, better success rates, and higher sample efficiency on complex tasks.

However, this rapid proliferation of planning-integrated VLA models has led to a fragmented research landscape. Different studies employ a wide variety of network architectures, planning paradigms (e.g., monolithic vs. hierarchical), planning representations (e.g., language, visual, latent), and training data sources. This heterogeneity makes it exceedingly difficult for researchers to:
1. **Attribute performance gains:** Is an improvement due to a novel planning paradigm, a better representation, a specific network architecture, or simply more data?
2. **Identify bottlenecks:** Which component (planning or policy execution) is the primary limitation for current systems?
3. **Guide future development:** Without systematic comparisons, it's unclear which directions are most promising.

This paper directly tackles these challenges by introducing **VLA-OS**, a unified and composable VLA architecture series. VLA-OS is designed to act as a standardized experimental platform, enabling controlled comparisons across different planning paradigms and representations while largely neutralizing the confounding factors of varying network architectures and training data sources. By doing so, the authors aim to provide much-needed empirical evidence and guidance for the VLA community, moving beyond intuitive designs to data-driven insights. It serves as a meta-analysis on the design choices within VLAs, critically evaluating the "how" and "what" of planning in robot foundation models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to bring clarity and empirical rigor to the design choices within task-planning enabled Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The authors explicitly state five core questions they aim to answer through systematic and controllable experiments:

1. **Representation:** What is the most effective representation for task planning and policy learning? Do multiple representations conflict or complement each other? (e.g., language vs. visual vs. image foresight).
2. **Paradigm:** Should task planning and policy learning be handled by a single, monolithic model (Integrated-VLA) or by separate, hierarchical models (Hierarchical-VLA)?
3. **Bottleneck:** For current robot manipulation tasks, which component poses a greater challenge: task planning or low-level policy learning?
4. **Scalability and Pretraining:** Do VLA models that incorporate task planning retain the advantageous properties of end-to-end foundation models, specifically in terms of model and data scalability, and the benefits derived from pretraining?
5. **Performance:** Do VLA models employing task planning exhibit superior generalization and continual learning abilities compared to end-to-end (ActionOnly) VLAs?

By addressing these questions, the authors seek to provide foundational empirical evidence and actionable insights to guide future development in VLA and the broader robotics community, moving away from fragmented, intuition-driven designs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology is the development of the **VLA-OS model series**, a unified and composable architecture designed for systematic comparison of different planning paradigms and representations.

#### 3.1. VLA-OS Model Family Design

- **Unified Backbone:** VLA-OS uses a unified, interchangeable Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone. For consistency in scalability experiments, they chose the Qwen2.5 LLM series (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B parameters) and fine-tuned it on LLaVa v1.5 data to become a VLM. This VLM processes visual (multi-view images) and language inputs.
- **Ensemble Vision Encoder:** To enhance robustness, they use an ensemble of DINOV2 and SigLIP vision encoders.
- **Composable Heads:** The VLA-OS architecture features plug-and-play planning heads and two different action heads (supporting 2D/3D tasks). The action head is a transformer decoder that processes robotics-specific tokens (action, proprioception states) and attends to the VLM's outputs.

#### 3.2. VLA Paradigms Implemented

- **VLA-OS-A (ActionOnly-VLA):** The baseline end-to-end model, directly mapping visual and language inputs to robot actions.
- **VLA-OS-I (Integrated-VLA):** A single model performing task planning and policy learning simultaneously.
 - **Implicit Planning:** Planning heads serve as auxiliary losses during training, but are not executed during inference. Action generation is independent of explicit planning output.
 - **Explicit Planning:** Action generation is conditioned on embeddings/results from the planning heads, requiring planning to be performed before action generation during inference.
- **VLA-OS-H (Hierarchical-VLA):** Uses two separate networks: one VLM with planning heads for high-level task planning, and a separate encoder-decoder transformer for low-level policy learning (action generation). The low-level policy takes planning representations as input but does not propagate gradients back to the high-level VLM.

#### 3.3. Task Planning Representations and Data Annotation

The authors meticulously annotate three types of task reasoning representations for controlled experiments:
- **Language Reasoning:** Contains 8 different keys (Task, Plan, Subtask, Subtask Reason, Move, Move Reason, Gripper Position, Object Bounding Boxes). The annotation process involves using Gemini1.5 to synthesize initial reasoning, followed by a crucial filtering and refinement step (using Gemini or human experts) to ensure consistency and alignment with trajectories, addressing issues of inconsistency and mismatch common with raw LLM outputs.
- **Visual Reasoning:** Uses "location tokens" (<loc i>) to represent spatial semantic information grounded in images. This includes object bounding boxes, end-effector flow (gripper trajectory in pixel space), and target object affordance (heatmap of target regions). It leverages instance semantic maps, object detection, and SAM2 for annotation.
- **Image Foresight Reasoning:** Aims to generate a third-person view image of a future goal state. The image generation head is based on the auto-regressive model from, known for its performance and inference speed advantages over diffusion models.

#### 3.4. Comprehensive Experimental Suite

To ensure robust evaluation and answer the core questions, experiments are conducted across diverse settings:
- **Task Domains:** Rigid body manipulation (LIBERO, FurnitureBench), visual generalization (The COLOSSEUM), real-world deformable manipulation (folding handkerchief, unfolding jeans, straightening rope), dexterous manipulation (DexArt), and dual-arm manipulation (PerAct2).
- **Modalities & Environments:** 2D and 3D visual inputs, simulation and real-world environments, different end-effectors (grippers and dexterous hands).
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate:** Standard task completion metric.
 - **Planning vs. Policy Learning:** Custom metrics: Decomposition Score (DCS) for planning correctness and Instruction Following Score (IFS) for policy execution (given ground truth planning).
 - **Scalability:** Performance trends with varying data amounts (10-100% of LIBERO-LONG) and model sizes (0.5B to 7B LLM backbone).
 - **Pretraining:** Evaluate benefits of pretraining planning components on a larger dataset (LIBERO-90) before fine-tuning.
 - **Generalization:** Test on perturbed environments (COLOSSEUM: No-Perturbation vs. ALL-Perturbation).
 - **Continual Learning:** Assess forward transfer (FWT), negative backward transfer (NBT), and area under the success rate curve (AUC) using sequential finetuning.
 - **Efficiency:** Training cost and inference speed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experiments yield significant insights into VLA design choices:

1. **VLA-OS Sanity Check (Finding 1):** VLA-OS-A-S (ActionOnly, Small 0.5B LLM, trained from scratch) performs competitively, even outperforming several fine-tuned existing VLAs and matching $\pi^0$-FAST. This suggests that excellent model architecture and algorithmic design can be more impactful than simply scaling up model size or relying on large-scale pretraining on downstream tasks with limited data.
2. **Planning Representation Comparison (Findings 2, 3, 4):**
 - **Implicit vs. Explicit Planning (Finding 2):** For Integrated-VLA, implicit planning (using planning as auxiliary loss) generally improves performance. Explicit planning, however, leads to significant performance degradation when trained from scratch. This is attributed to planning accumulation errors and the action head's lack of raw visual/language inputs for error correction in explicit settings.
 - **Visually Grounded vs. Language Planning (Finding 3):** Visually grounded planning representations (visual reasoning and image foresight) consistently outperform language-based planning in terms of task performance, generalization, training cost, and inference speed. They are also easier for low-level policies to follow.
 - **Multiple Representations (Finding 4):** Hierarchical-VLA benefits more from combining multiple planning representations compared to Integrated-VLA.
3. **Paradigm Performance, Generalization, and Pretraining (Findings 5, 6, 7):**
 - **VLA vs. ActionOnly (Finding 5):** Both Integrated-VLA and Hierarchical-VLA generally outperform ActionOnly-VLA across a wide range of tasks (2D/3D, sim/real-world, rigid/deformable, multi-arm, dexterous), with their performances often comparable to each other.
 - **Benefits from Planning Head Pretraining (Finding 6):** Both Integrated-VLA and Hierarchical-VLA show similar positive gains from pretraining their planning components on larger task-agnostic datasets.
 - **Generalization Ability (Finding 7):** Hierarchical-VLA demonstrates superior generalization capabilities, particularly when tested under significant environmental perturbations.
4. **Separate Investigation of Task Planning and Policy Learning (Findings 8, 9):**
 - **Hierarchical-VLA's Planning Superiority (Finding 8):** Hierarchical-VLA achieves better Decomposition Scores (DCS), indicating more correct task planning, compared to Integrated-VLA.
 - **Policy Learning as Bottleneck (Finding 9):** Across tasks, policy learning (Instruction Following Score, IFS) is consistently more challenging than task planning (DCS), regardless of the planning representation used. This implies that while high-level plans might be generated correctly, the robot's ability to precisely execute these plans remains a primary hurdle. Additionally, visually grounded planning representations are easier for low-level policies to follow, reinforcing Finding 3.
5. **Efficiency (Finding 10):** Language-planning representations incur higher training costs and slower inference speeds due to their autoregressive nature, further favoring visually grounded representations.
6. **Scalability (Findings 11, 12):**
 - **Data Scalability (Finding 11):** All VLA paradigms exhibit data scalability, meaning their performance improves with an increasing amount of action-labeled demonstration data.
 - **Model Scalability (Finding 12):** For training from scratch with approximately 5,000 demonstrations, the optimal LLM backbone size is around 0.5B parameters, or keeping the total model size under 1B parameters. Larger models do not necessarily yield benefits with limited data.
7. **Continual Learning (Findings 13, 14):**
 - **Planning vs. Non-Planning (Finding 13):** VLA paradigms with task planning (Integrated-VLA and Hierarchical-VLA) achieve higher forward transfer (learning new tasks better) but also exhibit faster forgetting of old tasks compared to ActionOnly-VLA.
 - **Visual vs. Language in CL (Finding 14):** Visually grounded planning representations lead to superior forward transfer and slower forgetting in continual learning tasks compared to language-based planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a much-needed systematic and empirical evaluation of design choices within Vision-Language-Action models, offering actionable insights for the burgeoning field of robot foundation models. Its significance and potential impact are substantial:

1. **Demythifying VLA Design:** By rigorously comparing different planning paradigms and representations under controlled conditions, the paper dispels assumptions and provides concrete evidence. It highlights that "more planning" isn't always better (e.g., explicit planning from scratch can degrade performance), and that the *type* of planning representation matters profoundly.
2. **Guiding Future Research:** The findings offer clear directions for researchers:
 - **Prioritize Visually Grounded Planning:** The consistent superiority of visual and image foresight planning suggests that future efforts should focus on these modalities over purely language-based planning. This could lead to more robust and efficient VLA systems.
 - **Focus on Policy Learning:** The identification of policy learning as a greater bottleneck than planning itself is crucial. This reorients attention towards improving the robot's low-level execution capabilities and the ability of policies to effectively utilize planning outputs.
 - **Rethink Model Scaling for Limited Data:** The finding on model scalability suggests that simply using larger LLM backbones is not a panacea, especially when training from scratch with modest datasets. Optimizing architectures and data efficiency for smaller models might be more beneficial.
 - **Address Forgetting in Continual Learning:** The observation of faster forgetting in planning-enabled VLAs necessitates research into novel continual learning strategies specifically tailored for these complex architectures.
3. **Providing a Unified Framework:** The VLA-OS model series itself is a valuable contribution. By open-sourcing the code, annotated datasets, and checkpoints, the authors provide a standardized and composable platform that other researchers can leverage to conduct fair comparisons and build upon, accelerating progress in the field.
4. **Advocating for Systematic Experimentation:** The paper serves as a strong example of how systematic empirical studies, isolating variables, are essential for true scientific understanding in a field often driven by rapid, less controlled advancements.

The authors also explicitly list several recommended future research directions directly derived from their findings, such as investigating the fundamental reasons for visual representation superiority, managing gradient conflicts, designing better information extraction mechanisms from VLMs, developing faster autoregressive planning heads, and constructing larger-scale, high-quality task planning datasets. These recommendations are direct consequences of their detailed analysis and offer a clear roadmap for advancing VLA capabilities.

While the paper acknowledges limitations (e.g., not covering all VLA variants, limited dataset scale for broad pretraining), its comprehensive scope and actionable conclusions make it a significant contribution to the field of robot learning and embodied AI.
