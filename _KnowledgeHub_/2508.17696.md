# Fair Cooperation in Mixed-Motive Games via Conflict-Aware Gradient Adjustment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed FCGrad, a method for multi-agent reinforcement learning that dynamically adjusts policy gradients to promote fair cooperation in mixed-motive games. The approach achieved superior fairness and competitive collective performance in sequential social dilemma environments, ensuring more equitable reward distribution among agents.

## Problem

- Multi-agent systems in mixed-motive settings often suffer from a deterioration of collective welfare when agents prioritize self-interest.
- Even when cooperation is achieved in mixed-motive scenarios, it frequently leads to unfair outcomes where some agents consistently receive lower task-specific rewards.
- Existing methods, like reward restructuring or gradient alignment, fail to simultaneously address both cooperative performance and fairness in task-specific reward distribution.

## Method

- The Fair and ==Conflict-aware Gradient== Adjustment (**FCGrad**) algorithm dynamically adjusts policy gradients for each agent based on individual and collective objectives.
- It detects conflicts between individual and collective gradients (negative inner product) and, in such cases, projects the gradient of the 'disadvantaged' objective onto the normal plane of the other, prioritizing the objective with lower current return.
- When no conflict is detected, FCGrad computes a weighted sum of individual and collective gradients to balance objectives, then updates policy parameters using this adjusted gradient.

## Results

- FCGrad demonstrated superior fairness across all evaluated environments (Unfair Coins, Cleanup, Harvest) as measured by geometric mean, minimum individual return (α-fairness), Gini coefficient, and Jain's index.
- In Unfair Coins, FCGrad achieved nearly identical returns for agents despite highly asymmetric reward probabilities, outperforming baselines which resulted in unfair distributions.
- The method maintained competitive collective performance while ensuring more equitable task-specific reward distribution in Cleanup and Harvest environments, even with spatial asymmetries, where baselines often led to specialized, unfair agent behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly addressing conflicts in gradient space through projection allows for targeted improvement of the less-performing objective without detriment to the other.
> - The method provides theoretical guarantees for monotonic non-decreasing improvement in both individual and collective objectives.
> - FCGrad ensures fairness by proving the convergence of the value gap between individual and collective objectives to zero over time, leading to equitable reward distribution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) broadly categorizes environments based on reward structures: cooperative (fully aligned rewards), adversarial (opposing rewards), and mixed-motive (partially aligned, partially opposing). This work specifically addresses the mixed-motive setting, which is prevalent in real-world scenarios such as traffic control or resource management, where agents must navigate the tension between individual gains and collective benefits.

Prior research in mixed-motive MARL has explored several avenues to foster cooperation. One significant stream involves **reward restructuring methods**, which introduce intrinsic rewards to guide agents toward prosocial behaviors. Examples include social influence mechanisms (Jaques et al., 2019), formal contracts (Haupt et al., 2024), gifting (Lupu & Precup, 2020; Kong et al., 2025), and inequity aversion (Hughes et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019). While these methods have demonstrated efficacy in promoting cooperation by managing the trade-off between individual and collective returns, the paper identifies a limitation: they primarily focus on maximizing collective return and may not explicitly address fairness with respect to the agents' *task-specific* rewards. For instance, gifting mechanisms might lead to improved collective performance, but some agents might consistently sacrifice their direct task-based rewards to benefit the group, leading to an unfair distribution of inherent task outcomes.

Another direction involves **direct optimization in the gradient space**. The Altruistic Gradient Adjustment (AgA) method (Li et al., 2023) modifies policy gradients to align individual and collective objectives by steering agents toward stable fixed points of the collective objective. However, this approach is noted to incur additional computational complexity, primarily focuses on stability rather than direct conflict resolution, and lacks guarantees regarding monotonic improvement or fairness.

Beyond MARL, the broader field of multi-task learning has also investigated **gradient adjustment techniques** to manage conflicting objectives. Methods such as CAGrad (Liu et al., 2021) and Nash-MTL (Navon et al., 2022) use quadratic programming or bargaining game formulations, respectively, to find conflict-averse gradient combinations. PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020) offers a simpler approach by projecting conflicting gradients onto each other's normal planes. These multi-task methods provide a foundation for gradient manipulation but typically do not directly address the dynamic interplay between individual and collective objectives in a mixed-motive multi-agent context, particularly concerning fairness among agents.

The concept of **fairness in MARL** has also been explored, both in cooperative settings (Zimmer et al., 2021; Grupen et al., 2022) and mixed-motive settings (Kong et al., 2025; Smit & Santos, 2024). These works consider how returns are distributed among agents, which can be complementary to, but often orthogonal to, cooperation and efficiency.

This work positions itself within this landscape by proposing a method that explicitly addresses the dual challenge of enhancing cooperation *and* ensuring fairness in mixed-motive MARL. It aims to overcome the limitations of reward restructuring methods that may disregard task-specific fairness and gradient-based methods that do not explicitly resolve conflicts or guarantee fairness. By introducing a conflict-aware gradient adjustment mechanism, the paper seeks to dynamically balance individual and collective objectives, providing theoretical guarantees for both performance improvement and equitable outcomes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central challenge in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) within mixed-motive settings is to enable agents to simultaneously optimize their individual interests while contributing to collective goals, given that these objectives are neither fully aligned nor entirely opposed. This inherent tension forms the primary motivation for the research.

The paper identifies two main problems arising from this mixed-motive dynamic:

1. **Deterioration of Collective Welfare due to Selfish Behavior:** If agents excessively prioritize their self-interest, it can lead to a degradation of overall collective performance. This, in turn, can negatively impact each agent's individual returns, creating a detrimental cycle for all participants. For example, in sequential social dilemmas (SSDs) like Cleanup or Harvest, individual agents may prioritize immediate resource collection, leading to depletion of shared resources and ultimately harming the group.
2. **Emergent Unfairness in Cooperative Scenarios:** Even when cooperation is achieved and collective welfare improves, it often comes at the cost of fairness. Certain agents may be compelled to sacrifice their individual rewards or specialize in less rewarding, but group-beneficial, tasks. This can lead to an uneven distribution of task-specific rewards, where some agents consistently receive lower returns. The paper highlights the Cleanup environment as an example: agents specializing in cleaning waste (a collective good) may never directly receive task rewards from apple collection, which other agents perform, even if the latter then "gift" some reward. This intrinsic gifting does not address the unfairness in *task-defined* reward acquisition.

Existing approaches, while partially addressing these issues, present their own limitations:

- **Reward Restructuring Methods (e.g., gifting, inequity aversion):** These approaches primarily focus on promoting cooperation by managing the trade-off between individual and collective returns. However, they typically do not explicitly guarantee fairness with respect to the *task-specific* rewards that agents are fundamentally trained to maximize. The gifted reward is often an intrinsic supplement, not a rebalancing of the base task rewards.
- **Gradient Alignment Methods (e.g., Altruistic Gradient Adjustment - AgA):** These methods aim to align individual and collective objectives by adjusting policy gradients. However, they often focus on stability of the collective return without explicitly addressing conflicts between individual and collective objectives or providing guarantees for fairness.

Given these challenges and the limitations of current methods, the key objectives of this research are to:

1. **Develop a method that promotes cooperation while explicitly ensuring fairness** in the distribution of individual rewards among agents in mixed-motive settings.
2. **Create a conflict-aware mechanism** that can dynamically detect and resolve situations where individual and collective objectives' policy gradients are misaligned or contradictory.
3. **Balance policy gradients** derived from both individual and collective objectives in a way that improves collective performance without compromising fairness across agents.
4. **Provide theoretical guarantees** for the proposed method, specifically demonstrating:
 - Monotonic non-decreasing improvement in both collective and individual objectives.
 - Convergence of the collective and individual objectives to the same value, which implies fairness across all agents' individual objectives.
5. **Empirically demonstrate the effectiveness** of the proposed method in various sequential social dilemma environments, evaluating it not only on aggregate performance but also on various fairness metrics.

In essence, the motivation is to enable MARL systems to achieve optimal collective outcomes that are both efficient and equitable, moving beyond solutions that prioritize one at the expense of the other.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Fair and Conflict-aware Gradient Adjustment (FCGrad), aims to reconcile individual and collective objectives by dynamically adjusting policy gradients. The approach operates within a decentralized training and execution paradigm where agents share rewards but maintain individual observation and policy generation.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Gradient Definition

The problem is framed within a Partially Observable Markov Game (POMG) in a mixed-motive setting. For each Agent *i*, two key objective functions are defined:
- **Individual Return (V<sub>ind</sub>(θ)):** The expected cumulative reward for Agent *i*, where θ represents the policy parameters for Agent *i*.
- **Collective Return (V<sub>col</sub>(θ)):** The average of individual returns across all *N* agents, i.e., $R_{col} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N R_j$. The gradient for the collective objective is computed with respect to Agent *i*'s policy parameters.

Correspondingly, two gradients are computed for Agent *i*:
- **Individual Gradient (g<sub>ind</sub>):** $ \nabla_\theta V_{ind}(\theta) $
- **Collective Gradient (g<sub>col</sub>):** $ \nabla_\theta V_{col}(\theta) $
These gradients represent ascent directions for their respective objectives.

#### 3.2. FCGrad Algorithm

The FCGrad algorithm operates by selectively combining or projecting these gradients based on whether a conflict exists and which objective is currently performing worse.

The steps are as follows:

1. **Conflict Detection:** The method first assesses whether the individual and collective objectives are in conflict by examining the inner product of their gradients: $ \langle g_{ind}, g_{col} \rangle $.
 - A negative inner product $ ( \langle g_{ind}, g_{col} \rangle < 0) $ indicates a conflict, meaning that moving in the direction of one gradient would decrease the other objective.
 - A non-negative inner product $ ( \langle g_{ind}, g_{col} \rangle \ge 0) $ indicates no conflict, implying that both objectives can be improved simultaneously.

2. **Non-Conflict Case $ ( \langle g_{ind}, g_{col} \rangle \ge 0) $:**
 - When no conflict is detected, FCGrad computes a weighted sum of the two gradients to determine the final update direction:
 $ g_{FCGrad} = (1 - \beta) g_{ind} + \beta g_{col} $
 - Here, $ \beta \in $ is a hyperparameter that controls the balance between individual and collective objectives. A higher $ \beta $ places more emphasis on the collective objective.

3. **Conflict Case $ ( \langle g_{ind}, g_{col} \rangle < 0) $:**
 - When a conflict is detected, FCGrad prioritizes the objective that is currently more "disadvantaged" (i.e., has a lower expected return) to ensure fairness. It achieves this by projecting the gradient of the lower objective onto the normal plane of the gradient of the higher objective.
 - **If V<sub>col</sub>(θ) $ \ge $ V<sub>ind</sub>(θ) (Individual is disadvantaged):** The individual objective is prioritized. The update direction is determined by projecting $ g_{ind} $ onto the normal plane of $ g_{col} $:
 $ g_{FCGrad} = g_{ind} - \frac{\langle g_{col}, g_{ind} \rangle}{\|g_{col}\|^2} g_{col} $
 This ensures that the update improves the individual objective while not interfering with (i.e., not decreasing) the collective objective.
 - **If V<sub>col</sub>(θ) $ < $ V<sub>ind</sub>(θ) (Collective is disadvantaged):** The collective objective is prioritized. The update direction is determined by projecting $ g_{col} $ onto the normal plane of $ g_{ind} $:
 $ g_{FCGrad} = g_{col} - \frac{\langle g_{ind}, g_{col} \rangle}{\|g_{ind}\|^2} g_{ind} $
 This ensures the update improves the collective objective without interfering with the individual objective.

4. **Policy Parameter Update:** The agent's policy parameters are updated using the computed $ g_{FCGrad} $ and a learning rate $ \eta $:
 $ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta g_{FCGrad} $

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides theoretical results for FCGrad under certain assumptions:

- **Theorem 3.1 (Monotonic Improvement):** Assuming V<sub>ind</sub>(θ) and V<sub>col</sub>(θ) are differentiable and L-smooth, FCGrad guarantees monotonic non-decreasing improvements in both V<sub>col</sub>(θ) and V<sub>ind</sub>(θ) for a sufficiently small step size $ \eta $. This implies that both collective performance and individual agent performance (averaged across agents) will improve or stay constant.
- **Theorem 3.2 (Fairness Convergence):** Assuming V<sub>ind</sub>(θ) and V<sub>col</sub>(θ) are L-smooth, the step size satisfies Robbins–Monro conditions ($ \sum \eta_t = \infty $ and $ \sum \eta_t^2 < \infty $), and a "conflict recurrence" condition (conflicts happen continuously when there is a significant value gap), the value gap $ |V_{ind}(\theta_t) - V_{col}(\theta_t)| $ converges to zero as $ t \rightarrow \infty $. This convergence to a zero gap implies that individual objectives across all agents will eventually converge to the same value, thereby ensuring fairness.

#### 3.4. Practical Algorithm Implementation

FCGrad is integrated into a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework with the following practical considerations:
- **Decentralized Training and Execution:** Each agent trains its policy and value functions based on its local observations and shared rewards.
- **Network Architecture:** Each agent employs two separate value networks (one for V<sub>ind</sub> and one for V<sub>col</sub>) that share a common encoder.
- **Value Function Training:** Both value functions are trained using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).
- **Policy Gradient Calculation:** Policy gradients (g<sub>ind</sub> and g<sub>col</sub>) are computed using the PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) algorithm. These gradients are then fed into the FCGrad mechanism for adjustment before the final policy update.

This methodology combines established MARL techniques with a novel gradient adjustment strategy, underpinned by theoretical guarantees, to address the intertwined challenges of cooperation and fairness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of FCGrad was evaluated across three distinct mixed-motive multi-agent environments: Unfair Coins, Cleanup, and Harvest, all based on the SocialJAX suite. The environments were modified to introduce specific fairness challenges. Evaluation primarily utilized **α-fairness**, which considers both aggregate performance and fairness, with specific instantiations: average return (Mean, α=0), geometric mean return (GeoMean, α=1), and minimum individual return (Min, α→∞). Lower Gini coefficient and higher Jain's index were also used as fairness metrics.

#### 4.1. Unfair Coins Environment

- **Environment Setup:** Two agents (Green and Red) collect coins, but green coins appear with a much higher probability (p<sub>green</sub> = 15/16 vs. p<sub>red</sub> = 1/16). Collecting an opposite-colored coin penalizes the other agent. Optimal collective performance involves collecting own-colored coins, but fairness requires the Green agent to yield coins to the Red agent, sacrificing some collective reward.
- **Findings:**
 - FCGrad demonstrated superior performance, particularly in terms of **Min performance**, indicating it effectively improved the outcome for the most disadvantaged agent (Red).
 - Baselines like Collective reward optimization (Col) and Individual reward optimization (Ind) resulted in highly unfair outcomes. Col favored the Green agent due to coin abundance, while Ind, surprisingly, sometimes favored the Red agent (attributed to Green agent being more frequently penalized by negative rewards from abundant green coins).
 - FCGrad led to **nearly identical returns** for both Green and Red agents, indicating a significant achievement in fairness by balancing rewards despite inherent asymmetry.
 - The performance of Col, Ind, and AgA decreased more significantly as α increased (higher fairness emphasis) compared to FCGrad and PCGrad.

#### 4.2. Cleanup Environment

- **Environment Setup:** Four agents collect apples (reward of 1), but apple growth depends on cleaning waste, a task that yields no immediate individual reward. Spatial asymmetry was introduced, with two agents spawning near apple orchards and two closer to waste areas, amplifying the fairness-efficiency conflict.
- **Findings:**
 - FCGrad **outperformed baselines in GeoMean and Min** α-fairness returns, demonstrating improved total return alongside better fairness.
 - FCGrad achieved **comparable Mean performance** to Col, which is designed solely for collective maximization.
 - Under Col, agents specialized unfairly: one agent consistently monopolized apple collection, while another was dedicated to waste cleaning (sacrificing individual reward).
 - FCGrad encouraged all four agents to achieve **reasonably similar returns**, illustrating fairer behavior and yielding the best Min performance.
 - Methods heavily reliant on individual rewards (Ind, IA) and AgA performed poorly, indicating the necessity of considering collective objectives in this environment.

#### 4.3. Harvest Environment

- **Environment Setup:** Four agents collect apples, with regrowth being stochastic and dependent on nearby apples. Over-harvesting depletes resources. Spatial asymmetry was introduced, with two agents spawning near apples and two farther away, making collection easier for the former.
- **Findings:**
 - FCGrad **outperformed all baselines across the considered α values** (Mean, GeoMean, Min).
 - Col optimization resulted in agents closer to apples (Agents 0 and 1) achieving higher returns than those farther away (Agents 2 and 3). While Col accounted for the intertemporal dilemma of resource depletion, it did not address the resulting unfairness.
 - FCGrad successfully led all four agents to achieve **similar returns**, implying that agents in advantageous positions (0 and 1) considered the outcomes of their less fortunate counterparts.
 - Similar to Cleanup, methods focused on individual rewards (Ind, IA) performed poorly, and AgA showed inferior performance compared to FCGrad.

#### 4.4. Additional Analysis

- **Weighting Factor (β) Ablation:** Experiments on the Harvest environment showed that setting β between 0.7 and 0.8 yielded the best GeoMean performance for FCGrad, indicating the importance of appropriately balancing collective and individual objectives when no conflict is present.
- **Other Fairness Metrics:** Evaluation using the Gini coefficient and Jain's index (Table 1) consistently demonstrated that FCGrad achieved superior fairness compared to most baselines across all environments, characterized by lower Gini coefficients and higher Jain's indices.

In summary, the results indicate that FCGrad effectively navigates the trade-off between cooperation and fairness in mixed-motive MARL. It consistently achieves strong collective performance while ensuring a more equitable distribution of rewards among agents, often outperforming methods designed purely for collective optimization or fairness through reward shaping.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) by addressing the fundamental challenge of simultaneously achieving cooperation and fairness in mixed-motive settings.

The **significance** of this work lies in several key aspects:

1. **Novel Conflict Resolution Mechanism:** FCGrad introduces a novel and explicit gradient adjustment method that dynamically detects and resolves conflicts between individual and collective objectives. Unlike prior approaches that primarily rely on reward restructuring or indirect gradient alignment, FCGrad directly manipulates gradients by projecting one onto the normal plane of the other, prioritizing the more disadvantaged objective. This direct approach offers a principled way to manage inherent tensions.
2. **Theoretical Guarantees for Performance and Fairness:** The paper provides theoretical proofs for FCGrad, guaranteeing monotonic non-decreasing improvement in both individual and collective objectives. Crucially, it also demonstrates that the gap between individual and collective objectives converges to zero under reasonable assumptions. This convergence implies that all agents' individual objectives will align, thereby ensuring a fair distribution of rewards. These theoretical underpinnings strengthen the robustness and predictability of the method.
3. **Empirical Validation in Complex Social Dilemmas:** FCGrad's effectiveness is empirically validated in challenging sequential social dilemma environments (Unfair Coins, Cleanup, Harvest) that explicitly model trade-offs between self-interest, collective good, and fairness. The results consistently show that FCGrad outperforms existing baselines in various α-fairness metrics, including the geometric mean and minimum individual return, while maintaining competitive aggregate performance. This demonstrates its practical utility in real-world-inspired scenarios.
4. **Addressing a Critical Gap in MARL:** Many existing MARL solutions for mixed-motive games tend to optimize for collective performance, potentially leading to unfair outcomes where some agents consistently sacrifice for the group. FCGrad directly confronts this by integrating fairness as an explicit objective into the learning process, ensuring that collective gains do not come at the expense of individual equity.

The **potential impact** of this research is substantial, particularly for the design and deployment of intelligent multi-agent systems:

1. **Fairer Autonomous Systems:** By promoting fairness in learned behaviors, FCGrad can contribute to developing autonomous systems that operate more equitably. This could prevent the emergence of inequalities in decentralized systems, where agents (e.g., in traffic management, resource allocation, or even robotic teams) must coordinate while pursuing local objectives. For instance, in future smart city applications, FCGrad could help ensure that traffic flow optimization algorithms do not disproportionately burden certain neighborhoods or individuals, or that resource allocation systems distribute resources equitably among various stakeholders.
2. **Enhanced Trust and Acceptance:** Systems that demonstrably act fairly are more likely to be trusted and accepted by human users and stakeholders. Research like FCGrad moves MARL closer to creating systems that are not only efficient but also socially responsible, which is increasingly important as AI systems become more integrated into daily life.
3. **Foundation for Future Research:** The conflict-aware gradient adjustment framework, with its theoretical guarantees, provides a new direction for research into multi-objective optimization in MARL. Future work could explore the impact of the weighting factor in non-conflict cases on the "conflict recurrence" assumption, or investigate how FCGrad could be extended to scenarios with a larger number of conflicting objectives or more complex fairness notions.

The authors acknowledge a practical limitation regarding the recurrence of gradient conflicts, which is crucial for theoretical guarantees and can be influenced by hyperparameters. Understanding this interplay remains a promising avenue for future investigation. Despite this, the work represents a step towards developing MARL agents that can navigate complex social dynamics, leading to both high-performing and equitable collective outcomes.
