# Teacher-Student Representational Alignment for Reinforcement Learning-Driven Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Örebro University developed a method that addresses the imitation gap in reinforcement learning-driven imitation learning by learning a shared latent representation between teacher and student observation spaces during teacher policy training. This approach ensures the teacher's behavior is inherently imitable by the student without altering the teacher's original reward function, achieving a 0.00 imitation gap in a TunnelVision environment and 0.01 in a CollectHealth environment.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained on low-dimensional 'privileged' states often exploit information unavailable to imitation learning (IL) student policies operating on high-dimensional sensory inputs, creating an 'imitation gap'.
- Existing solutions, such as post-hoc RL finetuning, reintroduce RL complexities, while modifying teacher rewards or actions can degrade teacher optimality or require extensive hyperparameter tuning.
- The inability to construct teacher policies that are inherently imitable from the outset necessitates reliance on complex post-hoc adjustments.

## Method

- A two-phase iterative training process is employed, alternating between learning a shared embedding space and training the teacher policy.
- A common latent embedding space is learned using self-supervised contrastive learning, augmented with explicit alignment and stability loss terms, from paired teacher and student observations.
- The teacher policy is trained using PPO on these shared latent embeddings, with policy gradients explicitly prevented from updating the encoder networks, thus forcing the teacher to rely solely on information shared with the student.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved a **0.00** imitation gap in the TunnelVision environment and **0.01** in the CollectHealth environment, with both teacher and student policies reaching near-perfect success rates (e.g., **1.00** in TunnelVision).
- It significantly outperformed standard Behavior Cloning (BC), which exhibited large imitation gaps (**0.81** in TunnelVision, **0.53** in CollectHealth), and the SITT baseline which showed smaller but still notable gaps (**0.19** and **0.43** respectively).
- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of the explicit alignment and stability loss terms, as their removal led to noticeable drops in student performance and increased imitation gaps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - By explicitly separating the training of observation encoders and the teacher policy, and carefully controlling gradient flow, the teacher can be compelled to learn policies based only on information accessible to the student.
> - A multi-component loss function for embedding learning (contrastive, alignment, stability) is crucial for robustly filtering private agent-specific information and ensuring stable, imitable representations.
> - Preventing the imitation gap from forming *during* teacher training through representational alignment is more effective than attempting to bridge it after the teacher has learned non-imitable behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), frequently confronts challenges associated with learning complex behaviors in high-dimensional observation spaces. Reinforcement learning methods, while capable of discovering optimal policies, are often characterized by high sample complexity, making them computationally expensive for intricate tasks, especially in robotics. Imitation learning offers an alternative by leveraging expert demonstrations, but the acquisition of such expert data can be costly or impractical.

A prominent paradigm, referred to as "learning by cheating," addresses these limitations by first training an RL-based teacher policy on simplified, low-dimensional analytical states. This teacher policy, often benefiting from privileged information and dense reward signals, can learn effectively and efficiently. Subsequently, imitation learning is employed to distill the teacher's learned behavior into a student policy that operates on more realistic, high-dimensional observation spaces, such as raw sensory inputs.

However, this two-stage approach introduces a fundamental challenge known as the "imitation gap." This gap arises when the teacher policy relies on "privileged" state information that is available to it but cannot be inferred by the student from its limited observations. When the teacher is trained in isolation, it can exploit this private information, leading to behaviors that are non-imitable by the student. Existing approaches to mitigate this imitation gap typically involve either post-hoc RL finetuning of the student policy or modifications to the teacher's training setup. RL finetuning often reintroduces the original difficulties of RL training, requiring a new and complex setup. Alternative methods attempt to constrain the teacher's action space or alter its reward function to encourage imitable behaviors. Such modifications, however, can disrupt the teacher's original task objective, lead to reward hacking, or necessitate extensive feature engineering and delicate hyperparameter tuning.

This research positions itself within this landscape by proposing a method that aims to prevent the imitation gap from forming during the teacher's initial training phase. Instead of addressing the gap after it emerges, the paper focuses on constructing teacher policies that are inherently imitable by the student. It frames this problem as one of representation learning, seeking to extract a common information subspace from diverse teacher and student observations. This approach distinguishes itself from prior work that leverages contrastive learning for robustness to visual shifts or domain transfer, by applying it specifically to filter agent-specific private information, making the teacher's behavior directly reproducible by the student without altering its reward structure.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel algorithm that addresses the irreducible imitation gap in reinforcement learning-driven imitation learning. The goal is to enable an RL-trained teacher policy to learn behaviors that are directly imitable by an IL-based student policy, even when the teacher initially has access to privileged information unavailable to the student.

This objective is motivated by several identified shortcomings in existing teacher-student imitation learning frameworks:

- **Irreducible Imitation Gap:** A significant challenge arises when a teacher policy, trained on low-dimensional analytical states, exploits information that is specific to its observation space and cannot be perceived by a student operating on high-dimensional, often sensory, observations. This leads to a performance disparity, or "imitation gap," where the student cannot replicate the teacher's behavior.
- **Limitations of Post-Hoc Finetuning:** A common strategy to improve student performance is to apply an additional RL finetuning phase to the distilled student policy. However, this often reintroduces the complexities and sample inefficiency associated with traditional RL training, frequently requiring a new and elaborate training setup.
- **Drawbacks of Teacher-Side Modifications:** Other approaches attempt to make teacher policies more imitable by constraining their action space or altering their reward signals. These methods can be problematic because modifying the teacher's sensitive reward function might inadvertently lead to reward hacking, degrade the teacher's optimality for the original task, or necessitate extensive, task-specific feature engineering and careful tuning of trade-off coefficients (as seen in methods like SITT). Such interventions often compromise the teacher's ability to maximize its primary task objective.
- **Desire for By-Construction Imitability:** The authors argue for a more desirable solution where the teacher policy is designed to be imitable from its inception, eliminating the need for complex post-hoc adjustments or compromises to the teacher's task performance. This implies a need for a mechanism that implicitly guides the teacher to rely only on information shared with the student.
- **Robust and Generalizable Representation Learning:** The motivation extends to leveraging representation learning to achieve this goal. By learning a low-dimensional, common embedding space that filters out agent-specific private information and retains only the shared components of observations, the teacher is naturally compelled to base its decisions on information accessible to the student. This approach aims for a task-agnostic solution that can be integrated into existing IL frameworks without extensive domain knowledge.
- **Maintaining Policy Refinement:** The choice of positive and negative samples in the contrastive learning objective is also motivated by the need to preserve subtle variations in consecutive states. This is crucial for enabling the agent to learn and execute more refined and nuanced policies, rather than coarse behaviors that might result from an overly simplified representation.

In essence, the research aims to fundamentally alter how the teacher perceives the environment, ensuring its "perception" is aligned with what the student can eventually infer, thereby building imitability directly into the teacher's learned behavior without compromising its original task-solving capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a combined reinforcement learning and imitation learning approach that operates by training a shared embedding space in parallel with the teacher policy. This design ensures that the teacher's policy is inherently imitable by the student. The overall system comprises two distinct modules with separate objectives, trained iteratively in alternating phases.

**Problem Setup: Multi-View Observations**
The problem is formalized using a multi-view perspective where teacher ($o_t^T \in O_T$) and student ($o_t^S \in O_S$) observations at a given timestep $t$ are considered different views of the same underlying environment state $s_t$. Each observation is conceptualized as a combination of a common component ($c_t$) and agent-specific private components ($p_t^T$, $p_t^S$). The core assumption is that these private components are conditionally independent given the shared variable $c_t$. The objective is to extract this common component $c_t$ from the raw observations.

**Two-Phase Iterative Training:**
1. **Phase 1: Embedding Space Learning:** The common embedding space is learned using self-supervised contrastive learning. This phase uses an aligned dataset of observations from both agents, collected by rolling out the teacher policy.
2. **Phase 2: Policy Network Training:** The teacher's policy network is trained using reinforcement learning (PPO in this work) on the latent embeddings generated by the learned common space encoders. The objective here is to maximize the task-specific reward.

A critical aspect of the methodology is **gradient flow control**. During Phase 2, the policy gradients from the RL objective are *only* allowed to update the policy network. They are explicitly prevented from updating the encoder networks that generate the embeddings. This restriction is central to preventing the teacher from implicitly extracting and relying on private information from its observations, thereby forcing it to operate solely on the shared, imitable representation.

**Components of the Embedding Loss Function ($L_{embedding}$):**

The encoder networks are trained to minimize a combined objective function that includes three distinct loss terms:

1. **Contrastive Learning Loss ($L_{contrastive}$):**
 - This loss is based on the InfoNCE objective, designed to bring representations of similar samples closer together in the embedding space while pushing dissimilar ones apart.
 - For a given teacher observation $o_i^T$, its corresponding student observation $o_i^S$ (at the same timestep) is considered a positive pair. All other student observations $o_j^S$ (where $j \neq i$) within the training batch are treated as negative samples. The same applies for a student observation $o_i^S$ with teacher observations.
 - The similarity between unit-norm embeddings ($z_i^T$, $z_i^S$) is measured by a dot product.
 - A key distinction from other time-contrastive methods is that only the *strictly aligned* observation is a positive, ensuring the policy can distinguish subtle variations between consecutive states.

2. **Alignment Loss ($L_{alignment}$):**
 - This term directly encourages similarity between the latent embeddings of paired teacher and student observations ($z_i^T$ and $z_i^S$).
 - While the contrastive loss implicitly encourages this, an explicit alignment loss is introduced because RL policies are known to be sensitive to small changes in the embedding space. Explicitly maximizing similarity helps stabilize the input to the policy network, promoting effective imitation.
 - It is calculated as the negative mean of the dot product similarity between paired embeddings across a batch.

3. **Stability Loss ($L_{stability}$):**
 - This loss aims to ensure that the encoder networks learn stable embeddings throughout their training. Since policy gradients do not update the encoders, large, unpredictable shifts in the embedding space could destabilize policy learning.
 - The loss is formulated to minimize the deviation of the current raw logits of the policy network (derived from the embeddings) from those produced before the current training phase commenced ($l_i^T, l_i^S$ vs. $l_i^{T,old}, l_i^{S,old}$).
 - Although the term involves the policy network, its gradients are specifically used *only* to update the encoder networks. This encourages encoder updates that maintain consistent actions under the current policy, while still allowing the embedding space to adapt for new observations.

**Teacher Policy Training:**
The teacher policy $\pi_T$ is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to maximize the expected discounted return of the task-specific reward function, operating on the learned common latent embeddings.

By separating the training of the embedding space and the policy, and by carefully controlling gradient flow, the method ensures that the teacher learns a policy that inherently relies on information shared with the student, thereby constructing an imitable teacher by design.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed method against state-of-the-art baselines in two distinct simulation environments designed to highlight the imitation gap problem.

**Environments:**
1. **CollectHealth:** A 3D environment where an agent collects health boxes. The teacher has access to exact box locations, allowing it to solve the task without reorienting. The student, observing via a limited Field-of-View (FOV) first-person perspective, must constantly reorient its camera to locate targets. This setup induces an imitation gap because the teacher's optimal strategy (not reorienting) is not feasible for the student.
2. **TunnelVision:** A grid world navigation task where an agent must reach a goal while avoiding randomly generated obstacles. The teacher observes all eight surrounding cells, while the student only sees three cells directly in front of it. The time-optimal teacher policy involves direct movement without reorientation, which would lead the student into unseen obstacles. The student requires frequent reorientation to avoid obstacles, highlighting the need for a deliberately imitable teacher behavior.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
1. **Behavior Cloning (BC):** A standard IL approach where a teacher policy is trained in isolation, and then a student policy is trained via supervised learning to mimic the teacher's actions on a collected trajectory dataset. This serves as a baseline to demonstrate the untreated imitation gap.
2. **SITT (Student-Informed Teacher Training):** A competitive baseline that jointly trains teacher and student. It penalizes the teacher for actions that the student cannot imitate, using a KL divergence term applied to both the reward signal and policy gradients. SITT introduces a hyperparameter ($\alpha$) to balance task performance and imitability.

**Key Results and Observations (Table I and Figure 5):**

- **Behavior Cloning (BC):** As anticipated, BC exhibits a significant imitation gap. The teacher policies achieve near-perfect success rates (1.00 in both environments) by exploiting private information. However, the student's performance is substantially lower (0.19 in TunnelVision, 0.47 in CollectHealth), resulting in large imitation gaps (0.81 and 0.53, respectively). This confirms that teachers trained in isolation do not produce imitable behaviors for observation-limited students.
- **SITT:** SITT successfully reduces the imitation gap compared to BC. For TunnelVision, the gap is reduced to 0.19 (student success 0.72), and for CollectHealth, to 0.43 (student success 0.51). This improvement comes at a cost to the teacher's original task performance, which slightly degrades (0.91 in TunnelVision, 0.94 in CollectHealth). This demonstrates the inherent trade-off introduced by modifying the teacher's reward function.
- **Proposed Method ("Ours"):** The proposed method achieves the highest student performance and the smallest imitation gap across both environments.
 - In TunnelVision, both teacher and student achieve a 1.00 success rate, resulting in a 0.00 imitation gap.
 - In CollectHealth, the teacher achieves 0.99 success, and the student 0.98, yielding a 0.01 imitation gap.
 - These results indicate that the method effectively compels the teacher to learn policies that are directly imitable by the student without compromising the teacher's task optimality.

**Ablation Studies:**
The paper includes an ablation study to evaluate the contribution of the individual loss terms within the proposed method.

- **"Ours w/o alignment":** Removing the explicit alignment loss leads to a noticeable drop in student performance. In CollectHealth, student success decreases from 0.98 to 0.73, and the gap increases from 0.01 to 0.21. This suggests that explicitly enforcing similarity between paired embeddings is important for effective and robust imitation, despite implicit encouragement from the contrastive loss.
- **"Ours w/o stability":** Eliminating the stability loss results in a substantial degradation, particularly in the TunnelVision environment, where student success falls from 1.00 to 0.78, and the teacher also sees a drop (to 0.79). In CollectHealth, the impact is less severe but still noticeable (student success from 0.98 to 0.95). This indicates that the stability objective is crucial for maintaining consistent embedding spaces, especially in environments where small shifts in representation can easily lead to task failure.

Overall, the full proposed model demonstrates the most consistent and high performance, successfully bridging the imitation gap while maintaining the teacher's ability to achieve its original task objective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a method designed to address a fundamental challenge in reinforcement learning-driven imitation learning: the irreducible imitation gap. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Bridging the Imitation Gap Fundamentally:** The work offers a novel, task-agnostic approach to bridge the imitation gap by learning a shared latent representation between teacher and student observation spaces. Unlike approaches that finetune the student or modify the teacher's behavior post-hoc, this method integrates imitability into the teacher's policy learning process by construction.
- **Preserving Teacher Optimality:** A notable impact is the ability to achieve high student performance and a minimal imitation gap without requiring modifications to the teacher's original reward function. This avoids the pitfalls of previous methods, such as reward hacking, degradation of teacher task performance, or complex hyperparameter tuning, allowing the teacher to optimize its true task objective using only shared information.
- **Plug-and-Play Adaptability:** The method is described as a "plug-and-play" adaptation, suggesting ease of integration into existing imitation learning frameworks with minimal hyperparameter tuning. This characteristic could facilitate its adoption by other researchers and practitioners.
- **Robust Representation Learning:** The explicit inclusion of alignment and stability losses in the embedding space learning objective contributes to the robustness and consistency of the learned representations. This is critical for stable policy learning, particularly when policy gradients do not propagate through the encoders. The ablation studies confirm the functional importance of these individual loss terms.
- **Potential for Enhanced Policy Transfer:** By ensuring that teacher policies operate on an inherently imitable representation, the method could facilitate more efficient and effective transfer of complex behaviors from powerful, state-aware RL systems to real-world agents with limited, high-dimensional observations (e.g., robots relying on camera feeds). This has implications for robotics, autonomous systems, and other domains where deploying RL policies requires bridging the gap between simulated privileged states and real-world sensory inputs.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The success of this representation-alignment approach opens avenues for future work. The authors suggest extending the method to more complex and high-dimensional continuous control tasks, indicating potential scalability. Further research could also explore theoretical guarantees of identifiability for the common component or investigate its applicability in dynamic environments with varied observation modalities.

In summary, this paper contributes a principled and effective solution to a long-standing problem in imitation learning, offering a pathway toward more robust, efficient, and broadly applicable policy transfer mechanisms.
