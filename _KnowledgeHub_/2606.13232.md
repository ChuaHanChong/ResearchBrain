# WT-UMI: Tactile-based Whole-Body Manipulation via Force-Supervised Contact-Aware Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel system enables humanoid robots to execute complex, contact-rich whole-body manipulation by integrating a wearable tactile interface (WT-UMI) with a learning framework that explicitly plans and regulates distributed contact forces. This approach achieves higher success rates and reduced contact-position errors for tasks involving deformable and bulky objects.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with reliable whole-body manipulation of complex objects due to insufficient distributed contact sensing and explicit force regulation.
- Current sensory modalities (vision, proprioception, limited tactile) are inadequate for comprehensive whole-body contact perception and force measurement.
- Translating natural, force-rich human demonstrations into robot-executable actions for imitation learning is challenging due to embodiment mismatch and data collection gaps.

## Method

- Developed **WT-UMI**, a ==wearable whole-body tactile== interface, to capture distributed contact information for both human demonstrations and robot teleoperation, minimizing domain gaps.
- Introduced a force-conditioned target-pose correction module that efficiently converts force-rich human demonstrations into robot-executable action labels by learning pose offsets.
- Designed a force-supervised planner and tactile-based admittance controller to jointly predict end-effector poses and contact-force trajectories, enabling explicit force regulation during manipulation.

## Results

- The force prediction head achieved an RMSE of **1.05 N** with a **68 ms** temporal lag on human demonstrations, demonstrating accurate and smooth force profile learning.
- Integrating force-conditioned target-pose correction with human demonstrations improved task success rates (e.g., **60%** to **80%** for Bucket) and reduced contact off-center drift by **11%** compared to teleoperation-only data.
- The tactile-based admittance controller further enhanced motion smoothness (**10.4%** reduction in translational acceleration) and contact quality by reducing off-center drift by an additional **10.9%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human demonstrations, when combined with a data-efficient target-pose correction mechanism, provide high-quality, smoother force supervision for robot learning.
> - Explicitly planning and regulating contact force trajectories, rather than relying on implicit contact handling, leads to more stable and robust whole-body manipulation.
> - A shared wearable tactile interface across human demonstrators and robots effectively reduces the domain gap, improving the transferability of learned skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards enabling humanoid robots to perform complex manipulation tasks involving diverse objects in human environments. Such tasks often necessitate whole-body contact, distributing interaction forces across various parts of the robot, including the torso, forearms, and hands, rather than relying solely on grasping. Current challenges in this area stem from the difficulty in achieving reliable whole-body manipulation, which requires distributed contact sensing and active regulation of both contact location and interaction force.

Existing learning-based manipulation systems predominantly rely on visual and proprioceptive feedback. However, vision can be obstructed during contact and does not directly measure interaction forces, while proprioception lacks the capability to localize body-surface contact. Prior tactile sensing research has largely concentrated on fingertip arrays, which are insufficient for capturing the distributed contact crucial for whole-body interactions. While some body-mounted tactile systems exist, they frequently depend on model-based controllers or task-specific reward shaping, which limits their generalization to diverse skills. Consequently, the challenge of translating distributed whole-body tactile sensing into an explicit contact force plan for humanoid whole-body manipulation remains largely unaddressed.

Another pertinent area is the collection of demonstration data for robot learning. Human demonstrations offer natural interaction forces but do not directly provide robot-executable actions. Conversely, teleoperation provides robot actions but often with less natural or precisely regulated force interactions due to operator challenges and robot dynamics. Bridging this gap to leverage the strengths of both data sources—natural force interactions from human data and robot-executable actions from teleoperation—is a key challenge in imitation learning for complex, contact-rich tasks.

This work contributes to addressing these limitations by introducing a system that integrates a novel wearable whole-body tactile interface with a force-aware learning framework. It aims to enable humanoids to perform complex, contact-rich tasks by explicitly planning and regulating distributed contact forces, thereby moving beyond implicit contact handling and localized sensing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the WT-UMI research are multi-faceted, aiming to overcome the limitations in current whole-body humanoid manipulation and data collection methods.

First, a central objective is to enable humanoid robots to robustly manipulate bulky, deformable, and shared-load objects. Such tasks inherently require coordinated and distributed contact across the robot's body, along with explicit force regulation to prevent failures like slips, collisions, or load loss. Current approaches often treat contact force implicitly, leading to suboptimal performance in these scenarios. The motivation here is to develop a system that explicitly plans and controls contact forces across the robot's body to achieve stable and reliable interaction.

Second, the researchers aim to address the limitations of existing sensory modalities in whole-body manipulation. Vision is prone to occlusion and does not directly measure forces, while proprioception cannot localize surface contacts. While fingertip tactile sensors exist, they are insufficient for whole-body interaction. The motivation is to integrate distributed whole-body tactile sensing to provide comprehensive contact information, including tactile images and calibrated contact forces, which is essential for detailed contact planning.

Third, the project seeks to bridge the gap between different demonstration sources for robot learning. Human demonstrations, while rich in natural force interactions, do not directly translate into robot-executable actions due to kinematic and embodiment differences. Teleoperation, conversely, provides robot-executable actions but may lack the natural force regulation observed in human interactions. The motivation is to develop a method that effectively combines these complementary data sources. Specifically, it aims to leverage the natural force supervision from human demonstrations while acquiring robot-executable action labels efficiently, without extensive robot-in-the-loop data collection.

Fourth, the work aims to overcome the "human-robot embodiment mismatch" in tactile sensing and action transfer. By designing a shared hardware interface (WT-UMI) that can be worn by humans or mounted on robots, the objective is to reduce the domain gap between human demonstrations and robot deployment, thereby facilitating more effective transfer of learned skills.

To achieve these objectives, the research proposes four key contributions:
1. **Develop WT-UMI:** A wearable whole-body tactile interface for collecting force-supervised demonstrations, usable for both human demonstrations and humanoid teleoperation, to reduce sensing mismatch.
2. **Design a Force-Conditioned Target-Pose Correction Module:** To convert human trajectories, which are rich in force information but lack direct robot actions, into contact-aware robot actions by learning pose corrections from a smaller set of teleoperation data.
3. **Propose a Force-Supervised Planner:** To explicitly predict both end-effector pose chunks and contact-force trajectories. This moves beyond implicit force handling by providing an explicit force reference for control.
4. **Integrate a Tactile-Based Admittance Controller:** To utilize the predicted contact force trajectory for online force regulation, modulating the predicted end-effector poses to achieve stable and force-regulated contact during deployment.

Ultimately, the motivation is to enable humanoids to perform complex, contact-rich whole-body manipulation tasks with improved success rates and reduced contact-position errors, leveraging the advantages of distributed tactile sensing and explicit force planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The WT-UMI system comprises a hardware interface for data collection and a learning framework for policy generation and deployment.

#### 3.1. Whole-Body Tactile Universal Manipulation Interface (WT-UMI) for Data Collection

WT-UMI is an extensible wearable system designed to capture operator motion and distributed tactile feedback. It integrates thin-film tactile sensors on hardware modules shared between human operators and humanoid robots. These modules include a chest plate (SensX 187), forearm covers (FlexiTac-style sensors), and handheld GripTacs (SensX 160) which are tactile-instrumented interfaces with interchangeable end-effectors. The use of identical sensing modules for human data collection, robot teleoperation, and robot execution aims to minimize the domain gap between demonstration and deployment.

Data collection occurs in two modes:
- **Teleoperation Mode:** WT-UMI is mounted directly on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. An operator streams bimanual pose commands via a PICO VR headset and handheld controllers. This mode records hand poses, tactile images, calibrated force measurements, robot proprioception, and VR commands, providing robot-executable actions.
- **Human Mode:** A human operator wears the WT-UMI components, with PICO controllers tracking bimanual poses. This mode records human hand poses, tactile images, and calibrated force measurements. It captures natural, force-rich human interactions without requiring robot-in-the-loop operation.

Force calibration for the palm SensX 160 sensors is performed under controlled quasi-static normal loading, using a compliant gel pad to distribute contact pressure and extend the usable force range. The calibration fits an inverse-exponential response model between the aggregate tactile activation score and applied normal force, followed by linear scaling and offset correction to provide a calibrated normal force estimate. Forearm and chest sensors are primarily used for contact localization and interaction-state inference. An online calibration procedure ensures consistent mapping of human motion to robot poses.

#### 3.2. Force-Conditioned Target-Pose Correction Module

This module is designed to convert force-rich human demonstrations into robot-executable action labels. It is pre-trained offline using paired robot teleoperation and human demonstration trajectories.
- **Data Alignment:** Trajectories from both sources are aligned by matching inferred contact modes (e.g., right-contact, both-contact) using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) over end-effector motion.
- **Learning Correction:** A lightweight CNN-GRU network learns to predict a hand-specific translational offset (∆p) from measured tactile images, poses, and forces. This offset represents the correction needed to transform a human hand pose into a robot target pose.
- **Supervision:** The target-pose offset from teleoperation data supervises the learning process. A force-gating mechanism ensures that a non-zero pose offset is only applied to hands that are inferred to be in contact.
- **Action Label Generation:** After pre-training, the learned correction module is applied to human demonstration data. The corrected target pose $\tilde{T}_s = T_{s,m} \oplus \mathbf{1}[f_{s,m} \geq f_{threshold}]\Delta p_s$ then serves as a robot-executable action label for training the subsequent planner.

#### 3.3. Force-Supervised Planner

This planner is designed to jointly predict end-effector pose chunks and contact-force trajectories, addressing the limitation of existing planners that implicitly handle contact forces.
- **Architecture:** The planner operates at 50 Hz with 400 ms observation and prediction horizons. It consumes an observation history (tactile images from left/right hands and chest, bimanual end-effector pose). A Vision Transformer (ViT) encodes tactile images, and an MLP encodes hand poses. Their outputs are concatenated into observation tokens.
- **Action Denoising Policy:** Conditioned on the observation history, a transformer-based action denoising policy (either flow matching or diffusion) generates a predicted action chunk (sequence of bimanual end-effector poses). This policy is supervised by the target-pose-corrected labels generated by the previous module.
- **Force Head:** A separate cross-attention decoder, sharing the same observation embeddings as keys and values, predicts a contact-force trajectory for both hands. This force head performs direct regression without a diffusion process, utilizing learnable positional queries. Supervision is provided by calibrated ground-truth forces, with a softplus projection ensuring non-negativity.
- **Joint Optimization:** The force head's gradients flow back through the shared encoder, allowing joint optimization by both the action denoising and force prediction objectives. A Huber loss is used for force prediction to mitigate gradient spikes from contact onset.

#### 3.4. Tactile-Based Admittance Controller

This low-level controller regulates the upper body, operating at 200 Hz, while a pre-trained RL locomotion policy handles the lower body.
- **Pose and Force Tracking:** At each timestep, the controller tracks the planner-predicted end-effector pose target ($\tilde{T}_s$) and a normal-force reference ($f_s$).
- **Admittance Regulation:** A proportional admittance controller refines the planner-generated pose using feedback from both normal-force and contact-centroid measurements. The corrective SE(3) increment ($\Delta T_s$) for each palm incorporates a rotation term to re-center the contact centroid and a translation term to track the reference normal force.
- **Inverse Kinematics and PD Control:** The commanded poses are passed to an optimization-based inverse kinematics solver to determine joint commands. A PD controller with gravity compensation then tracks these joint positions and velocities by applying appropriate torques.

#### 3.5. Experiment Setup

Five contact-rich tasks were used for evaluation: Yoga Ball Manipulation (deformable), Pillow Reorientation (deformable), Bucket Manipulation (bulky rigid), Beam Transport (human-humanoid collaboration), and Table Transport (human-humanoid collaboration). Policy baselines included ViT-FMT, ViT-DiT, $\pi_{0.5}$ (vision-language-action model), and $\Psi_0$ (humanoid foundation policy). Experiments were conducted on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, with tactile data streaming at 100 Hz and proprioception at 500 Hz, resampled to 50 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several key findings across various evaluations, demonstrating the effectiveness of the WT-UMI system and its components.

#### 4.1. Force Head Evaluation

The force head, when integrated into a ViT-FMT policy, demonstrated the ability to produce accurate, smooth, and temporally aligned force predictions.
- **Accuracy:** On held-out human demonstrations, the force head achieved an RMSE of 1.05 N, compared to a 5.56 N force RMS.
- **Temporal Alignment:** The temporal lag between predicted and ground-truth forces was 68 ms for human demonstrations.
- **Smoothness:** The force rate RMS was 3.74 N/s for human demonstrations, which was 2.2 times lower than for teleoperation data.
These results indicate that human demonstrations provide higher quality supervision for learning force profiles, leading to more accurate and smoother force predictions.

#### 4.2. Effect of Training Data Sources and Force-Conditioned Target-Pose Correction

Evaluation of the ViT-FMT policy on tasks T1 (Yoga Ball), T2 (Pillow), and T3 (Bucket) revealed the benefits of combining teleoperation data with target-pose-corrected human demonstrations (Comb. dataset) compared to using pure teleoperation data (Tel. dataset).
- **Success Rate:** The combined dataset (Comb.) improved the success rate for T3 from 60.0% to 80.0%, while maintaining 100% success for T1 and T2. Teleoperation alone provided robot-feasible actions, but human data enhanced robustness.
- **Contact Off-Center Drift:** The Comb. policy reduced contact off-center drift by approximately 11% on average across T1-T3 (e.g., from 20.41 mm to 18.12 mm for T1). This indicates better contact centering.
- **Mean Contact Force:** The Comb. policy approximately doubled the mean contact force on average (e.g., from 3.27 N to 4.77 N for T1), indicating firmer and more stable contact.
- **Motion Smoothness:** Translational accelerations were reduced by 46.3% on average, and rotational accelerations by 41.5% on average, demonstrating significantly smoother motion with the Comb. policy.
The target-pose correction module was shown to be data-efficient, enabling a much larger set of human demonstrations to be converted into robot-executable data with only a small amount of teleoperation data.

#### 4.3. Effect of Force-Conditioned Admittance Control

The tactile-based admittance controller was evaluated with the ViT-FMT policy, trained on the combined dataset.
- **Motion Smoothness:** Admittance control consistently improved motion smoothness, reducing translational acceleration by 10.4% and rotational acceleration by 9.0% on average across T1-T3.
- **Contact Quality:** Contact off-center drift decreased by 10.9% on average, and mean contact force increased by 2.7% on average, leading to more stable and better-centered contact when desired.
These results confirm that the admittance controller enhances motion smoothness while maintaining stable and firm contact.

#### 4.4. Effect of Policy Backbone on Whole-body Manipulation Tasks

Comparison of four policy backbones ($\pi_{0.5}$, $\Psi_0$, ViT-DiT, and ViT-FMT), all trained on the combined dataset with force prediction and deployed with the admittance controller, yielded the following:
- **Motion Smoothness:** ViT-FMT achieved the lowest translational (2.07 m/s$^2$) and rotational accelerations (14.18 rad/s$^2$), indicating the smoothest motion. ViT-DiT and $\Psi_0$ exhibited more frequent start-stop behaviors and larger accelerations, while $\pi_{0.5}$ showed jitterier motion.
- **Overall Performance:** ViT-FMT demonstrated the best overall performance, reproducing motion patterns most closely to the dataset. The study further notes (in Appendix 8.6) that ViT-FMT achieved the highest average success rate (98.7%) across tasks T1-T3 and both admittance settings. The admittance controller consistently improved contact centering and reduced contact drift across all backbones.

#### 4.5. Human-Humanoid Collaborative Manipulation

WT-UMI and the planning framework were evaluated on beam transport (T4) and table transport (T5) tasks, where the humanoid maintains distributed whole-body contact and adapts to human partner intent inferred from tactile feedback. The system successfully supported both forward and backward locomotion and transitions to an idle state based on contact distribution and force patterns.

#### 4.6. Ablation Study of Target-Pose Correction and Admittance Controller on Recorded Data

Direct replay of collected yoga-ball trajectories on robot hardware confirmed the independent contributions:
- **Raw Human Data:** Failed to complete the task, demonstrating the necessity of action labels.
- **Target-Pose Correction:** Improved the feasibility of human trajectories, raising the success rate from 0% to 89.29% compared to raw human data, and improving upon raw teleoperation's 85.35% success rate.
- **Correction + Admittance:** Further increased the success rate to 96.15% and reduced contact center drift by 35.9% compared to corrected human data alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The WT-UMI system and its associated learning framework represent an advance in whole-body humanoid manipulation, particularly for contact-rich tasks. The significance of this work stems from its integrated approach to addressing several long-standing challenges in robotics.

Firstly, the introduction of WT-UMI as a shared, wearable whole-body tactile interface offers a standardized and scalable method for collecting rich tactile data. By minimizing the domain gap between human demonstrations and robot deployment through shared hardware, it facilitates more effective human-to-robot skill transfer. This addresses a critical need for high-quality, distributed tactile feedback beyond fingertip sensors, enabling robots to perceive and interact with objects in a more comprehensive manner.

Secondly, the force-conditioned target-pose correction module provides a solution to the embodiment mismatch problem in imitation learning. It enables the conversion of natural, force-rich human demonstrations, which were previously difficult to leverage directly, into robot-executable actions. This process is shown to be data-efficient, requiring only a small amount of teleoperation data to unlock a larger pool of human demonstrations, thereby accelerating data collection and expanding the range of learnable skills.

Thirdly, the development of a force-supervised planner that explicitly predicts contact-force trajectories, alongside end-effector poses, marks a departure from systems that only implicitly manage contact forces. By providing an explicit force reference, the system allows for more precise and stable force regulation during manipulation. This is particularly impactful for handling deformable, bulky, and shared-load objects where controlled interaction forces are paramount.

Fourthly, the integration of a tactile-based admittance controller that leverages these predicted forces for online regulation significantly enhances the robustness and smoothness of robot movements. The controller's ability to maintain stable and centered contact while reducing motion jerkiness contributes to more reliable task execution and safer human-robot interaction in collaborative scenarios.

The validation of WT-UMI across diverse tasks—including manipulation of deformable objects (yoga ball, pillow), bulky rigid objects (bucket), and human-humanoid collaboration (beam, table transport)—demonstrates its versatility and potential for generalization. The consistent improvements in success rate, contact quality (reduced drift, firmer contact), and motion smoothness over various policy baselines underscore the practical benefits of the proposed framework.

The potential impact of this research includes:
- **Enabling New Robotic Applications:** Humanoid robots could more effectively perform tasks in unstructured human environments, such as assisting with household chores, logistics, or industrial assembly, which often require careful contact and force management.
- **Improving Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ability to infer human intent from tactile feedback and adapt robot motion accordingly could lead to more intuitive and safer collaborative robotic systems.
- **Advancing Robot Learning:** The framework contributes to the methodology of imitation learning by providing a pathway to combine the strengths of diverse demonstration sources (human intuition and robot feasibility) with explicit force supervision.
- **Enhancing Robot Embodiment:** By integrating comprehensive tactile sensing across the robot's body, this work moves towards robots that are more physically grounded and capable of rich physical interactions with their environment.

While the system has limitations, such as restricted tactile coverage and the absence of RGB vision input, the foundational contributions lay groundwork for future work in extending sensor modalities, refining force control, and expanding the scope of whole-body manipulation capabilities.
