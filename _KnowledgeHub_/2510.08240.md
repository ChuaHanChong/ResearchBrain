# The Alignment Waltz: Jointly Training Agents to Collaborate for Safety

> [!summary] Summary
> WaltzRL introduces a collaborative multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that jointly trains a conversation agent and a feedback agent to enhance LLM safety. This approach reduces both unsafe content generation and overrefusals, achieving an Attack Success Rate of 4.6% and an Over-Refuse Rate of 9.9% on respective benchmarks while preserving general capabilities.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) face a trade-off where aggressive safety measures to prevent harmful outputs often lead to an increase in overrefusals, declining to answer benign but sensitive prompts.
- Traditional standalone safeguard models (e.g., Llama Guard) act as external filters, often blocking entire responses for dual-use prompts without providing nuanced guidance or a path for improvement.
- Existing multi-agent AI safety approaches frequently adopt competitive dynamics or deploy only a single 'defender' model at inference, limiting collaborative and adaptive content moderation.

## Method

- **WaltzRL**, a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework, models safety alignment as a ==collaborative==, positive-sum game between a conversation agent and a feedback agent.
- An iterative feedback protocol enables the feedback agent to provide nuanced, constructive textual suggestions in JSON format, guiding the conversation agent to revise initial unsafe or overrefusing responses.
- A novel Dynamic Improvement Reward (DIR) incentivizes the feedback agent to generate feedback that genuinely improves the conversation agent's subsequent response, conditioned on the feedback agent's accurate identification of issues.

## Results

- WaltzRL reduced the Attack Success Rate (ASR) from **39.0%** to **4.6%** on the WildJailbreak dataset and the Over-Refuse Rate (ORR) from **45.3%** to **9.9%** on the OR-Bench dataset.
- The framework maintained the general capabilities of the conversation agent across benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0, IF-Eval, GPQA, MMLU, and TruthfulQA, demonstrating no degradation despite safety-focused RL training.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the Dynamic Improvement Reward (DIR) and the multi-agent co-evolution process in achieving superior safety and helpfulness compared to baselines and alternative training strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aligning LLMs for safety can be achieved through a collaborative, positive-sum multi-agent framework, simultaneously improving both harmlessness and helpfulness rather than presenting a trade-off.
> - Dynamic and specific textual feedback from a specialized agent is more effective at steering models away from overrefusals and towards helpful responses compared to binary classification or template-based guidance.
> - A two-stage training approach, initially focusing on the feedback agent's label accuracy and then enabling joint co-adaptation with a conditioned improvement reward, is crucial for stable and effective collaborative learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has introduced a tension between their potential for positive impact and the risks associated with harmful or unsafe content generation. This research addresses a core challenge in LLM safety alignment: balancing helpfulness and harmlessness. Current LLM alignment efforts face two primary issues: vulnerability to adversarial attacks that elicit unsafe content (e.g., jailbreaks) and a tendency for overrefusal, where models decline to answer benign but sensitive prompts. These two challenges often present a trade-off, where overly aggressive safety measures to prevent unsafe content can increase overrefusals.

A common paradigm for mitigating unsafe content involves deploying standalone safeguard models, such such as Llama Guard or Constitutional Classifiers. These models act as external filters, classifying prompts and responses and rejecting any content deemed unsafe. However, this approach can exacerbate overrefusals by blocking entire responses, even if only a minor portion is questionable or if the prompt has dual-use potential. This method can also fail to provide constructive guidance for refused queries.

This work situates itself within the evolving landscape of multi-agent reinforcement learning (RL) and AI safety research. Prior work in AI safety has explored debate-based systems, often framed as zero-sum games where agents compete. Other multi-agent RL approaches have been used to train LLMs for various roles, but frequently deploy only a single "defender" model at inference time. "The Alignment Waltz" distinguishes itself by proposing a collaborative, positive-sum multi-agent framework. Instead of a competitive dynamic, the agents are incentivized to cooperate, and crucially, both agents are designed to be deployed jointly during inference to enable adaptive and nuanced content moderation. This approach aims to move beyond simple refusal by enabling iterative improvement of responses, offering an alternative to the limitations of standalone safeguard models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a novel method for aligning large language models that simultaneously enhances safety by reducing unsafe content generation and improves helpfulness by mitigating overrefusal. The paper highlights a fundamental tension in LLM alignment: the more aggressively models are aligned to avoid harmful outputs, the more prone they become to refusing benign but sensitive prompts. Traditional safeguard models, which outright reject problematic content, are identified as a primary exacerbator of this overrefusal issue.

The motivation for this work stems from the limitations of existing safety alignment strategies:

- **Inadequate Handling of Dual-Use Prompts**: Current safeguards often block entire responses to prompts that, while sensitive, could lead to benign and helpful information. This prevents users from receiving any beneficial content.
- **Lack of Nuanced Guidance**: When a response is rejected by a safeguard, the user receives no explanation or suggestions on how to rephrase their query or what aspects of the response were problematic. This provides no pathway for improvement or learning.
- **Exacerbation of Overrefusal**: The binary nature of "accept or reject" employed by many safeguard models directly contributes to an increased rate of overrefusal, where models become overly cautious and refuse to answer even legitimate queries that might touch upon sensitive topics.
- **Trade-off in Performance**: The prevailing belief is that improving safety comes at the cost of helpfulness or general capabilities. This research aims to challenge this trade-off and advance the Pareto front between these two crucial aspects of LLM behavior.

To address these motivations, the authors propose "WaltzRL," a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework designed to foster collaboration between a conversation agent and a feedback agent. The key objectives are:

1. **Joint Optimization for Collaboration**: To jointly train a conversation agent and a feedback agent within a collaborative, positive-sum game framework, where both agents work towards generating safe and helpful responses.
2. **Adaptive and Constructive Feedback**: To enable the feedback agent to provide nuanced and actionable textual suggestions, rather than just binary classifications, thereby guiding the conversation agent to revise its responses for improved safety and helpfulness.
3. **Dynamic Improvement Reward (DIR)**: To introduce a novel reward mechanism that incentivizes the feedback agent to generate feedback that genuinely improves the conversation agent's performance in subsequent turns. This reward is designed to evolve as the conversation agent learns.
4. **Adaptive Inference-Time Deployment**: To ensure that the feedback agent intervenes adaptively at inference time, engaging only when a response is initially deemed unsafe or overrefusing. This design aims to preserve low latency and helpfulness for safe queries while enabling intervention when needed.
5. **Simultaneous Reduction of Unsafe Responses and Overrefusals**: To demonstrate that this collaborative framework can significantly reduce both the generation of unsafe content and the rate of overrefusals, thereby moving beyond the inherent trade-off observed in conventional methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

WaltzRL introduces a multi-agent reinforcement learning (RL) framework that models safety alignment as a collaborative, positive-sum game between two distinct agents: a conversation agent and a feedback agent.

#### 3.1. Collaboration Protocol

The framework involves an iterative feedback process:
1. A user provides a prompt `p`.
2. The **conversation agent (π_c)** generates an initial response `c_0`.
3. The **feedback agent (π_f)** analyzes `c_0` and outputs a JSON object containing:
 - `reasoning`: Its rationale for the assessment.
 - `unsafe` (true/false): A flag indicating if `c_0` is unsafe.
 - `overrefuse` (true/false): A flag indicating if `c_0` is an overrefusal.
 - `feedback`: A textual string containing constructive suggestions.
4. If `c_0` is deemed `unsafe` or `overrefuse`, the `feedback` string from `π_f` is provided to `π_c`.
5. `π_c` then produces a revised response `c_1` based on this feedback.
This process continues for a maximum of `T_max` rounds (set to 1 in experiments) or until the feedback agent deems the conversation agent's response "satisfactory" (i.e., `unsafe=False` and `overrefuse=False`). Only the feedback string is shared with the conversation agent, allowing the feedback agent to maintain private reasoning.

#### 3.2. Shaping Rewards to Encourage Collaboration

Rewards are designed to encourage both agents to fulfill their roles collaboratively:

- **Conversation Agent Reward (R_c)**:
 - For any revision `c_t`, `R_c` is `1` if the response is both *safe* (`¬unsafe`) and *not overrefusing* (`¬overrefuse`), and `0` otherwise.
 - Alignment Labels `J(p, c_t) = (unsafe, overrefuse)` are determined by an external LLM judge (WildGuard). An `overrefuse` label is applied if the original prompt `p` is not harmful but `c_t` is a refusal.

- **Feedback Agent Reward (R_f)**:
 - `R_f` is a weighted sum of three components: `R_f = α * R_DIR_f * R_label_f + λ * R_label_f + γ * R_format_f`.
 - **Dynamic Improvement Reward (R_DIR_f)**: This is the core collaborative reward. It is calculated as the difference in the conversation agent's reward *after* (`c_t+1`) and *before* (`c_t`) incorporating the feedback `f_t`: `R_DIR_f = R_c(c_t+1) - R_c(c_t)`. This incentivizes the feedback agent to produce suggestions that actually lead to a safer and more helpful revised response from `π_c`.
 - **Label Reward (R_label_f)**: Rewards the feedback agent if its predicted `(unsafe, overrefuse)` flags accurately match the ground-truth labels from the LLM judge. This ensures the feedback agent correctly identifies issues.
 - **Format Reward (R_format_f)**: Rewards the feedback agent for producing output in a parsable JSON format.
 - The term `α * R_DIR_f * R_label_f` is critical, as it conditions the improvement reward on label correctness, preventing the feedback agent from optimizing for improvement at the expense of accurate issue identification.

#### 3.3. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

The training process involves co-adaptation of both agents in each RL step, extending the REINFORCE++ algorithm:

1. **Collaborative Rollout**: For a batch of prompts, a multi-turn feedback-revision trajectory is generated through interaction between `π_c` and `π_f`.
2. **Gathering RL States and Actions**:
 - **Feedback Agent**: For each round, a state-action pair `((p, c_t), f_t)` is extracted, where `c_t` is the conversation agent's response and `f_t` is the feedback.
 - **Conversation Agent**: To ensure learning both initial good responses and feedback incorporation, two types of trajectories are blended:
 - Type A: `(p, c_0)` for learning to generate a satisfactory *initial* response.
 - Type B: `((p, H_T-1), c_T)` for learning to generate a satisfactory *final* response after incorporating feedback.
3. **Two-Agent Policy Gradient Step**: Rewards and advantages are computed for each agent separately based on their respective trajectories. Policy gradient updates are then applied to each agent's model in parallel.

#### 3.4. Learning to Give Feedback Adaptively (Two-Stage Approach)

To enable `π_f` to learn accurate issue identification and adaptive feedback, a two-stage training approach is used:

1. **Stage 1: Frozen Conversation Agent**:
 - `π_c` weights are frozen, and only `π_f` is trained.
 - All reward terms (`α`, `λ`, `γ`) are active.
 - Purpose: Allows `π_f` to learn correct output format and accurate `unsafe`/`overrefuse` labels without the `π_c` policy changing, ensuring stable learning of fundamental classification skills.
2. **Stage 2: Multi-Agent Collaborative Alignment**:
 - Both `π_c` and `π_f` are trained collaboratively.
 - The additive label reward (`λ`) for `π_f` is set to `0`. The multiplicative conditioning of `R_DIR_f` on `R_label_f` remains active.
 - Purpose: As `π_c` improves, fewer initial responses require revision. Disabling the additive label reward prevents `π_f` from overfitting to increasingly imbalanced data, while the conditioned DIR still maintains label accuracy by rewarding improvements *only if* the feedback agent correctly identified the issue.

#### 3.5. Models and Data

- **Base Model**: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct is used to initialize both conversation and feedback agents.
- **Training Data**: 20,000 prompts, consisting of 10,000 adversarial attack prompts from WildJailbreak and 10,000 borderline overrefusal prompts from OR-Bench-80K. Helpfulness prompts are not included in the RL training set.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Safety is measured by Attack Success Rate (ASR↓), overrefusal by Over-Refuse Rate (ORR↓). General capabilities are assessed using AlpacaEval 2.0, IF-Eval, GPQA, MMLU, and TruthfulQA. Adaptivity is measured by Feedback Trigger Rate (FTR↓).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across five diverse datasets demonstrate that WaltzRL significantly improves LLM safety alignment without degrading general capabilities.

#### 4.1. Safety and Overrefusal Performance

WaltzRL consistently outperformed all baselines in reducing both unsafe responses and overrefusals.
- **Attack Success Rate (ASR)**: On the WildJailbreak dataset, WaltzRL reduced ASR from 39.0% (baseline) to 4.6%. Similar reductions were observed across other adversarial datasets (e.g., from 40.4% to 6.2% on FORTRESS adversarial harmful).
- **Over-Refuse Rate (ORR)**: On the OR-Bench dataset, WaltzRL reduced ORR from 45.3% (baseline) to 9.9%. On FORTRESS benign prompts, ORR decreased from 6.0% to 5.4%.
- **Pareto Front Advancement**: The results indicate that WaltzRL effectively advances the Pareto front between helpfulness and harmlessness, demonstrating simultaneous improvement on both axes.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Baselines and Contributions

- **Safeguard Models**: The study confirmed that traditional safeguard models (e.g., Llama Guard) reduce ASR but increase ORR. This effect was more pronounced when applied to models already exhibiting low overrefusal, validating the paper's initial motivation.
- **Inference-Time Collaboration (No Training)**: Simply allowing the base LLM to act as both conversation and feedback agents at inference time showed some reduction in ASR and ORR compared to the standalone baseline. However, WaltzRL training further enhanced these improvements, highlighting the benefit of RL optimization.
- **Oracle Label-Converted Feedback**: A strong baseline using ground-truth labels converted into template feedback, while effective at reducing ASR, showed limited impact on ORR. This suggests that explicit and detailed textual feedback, rather than just binary instructions, is crucial for effectively steering a model to convert overrefusals into helpful responses.

#### 4.3. General and Instruction Following Capability

- Despite not including any helpfulness data during RL training, WaltzRL maintained the general capabilities of the conversation agent. Benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0, IF-Eval, GPQA Diamond, MMLU, and TruthfulQA showed minimal to no degradation in performance. This indicates that training a specialized feedback agent for safety can effectively decouple safety improvements from potential reductions in general helpfulness.

#### 4.4. Adaptivity and Latency

- WaltzRL significantly reduced the Feedback Trigger Rate (FTR) compared to untuned inference-time collaboration, leading to better efficiency. For general helpfulness prompts (e.g., AlpacaEval), the FTR was very low (6.7%), implying minimal latency impact for most queries. Even on challenging safety and overrefusal benchmarks, FTR remained below 50%, demonstrating a manageable impact on inference latency while providing adaptive intervention.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies and Analysis

- **Dynamic Improvement Reward (DIR)**: Ablations demonstrated that the DIR mechanism is critical for encouraging useful feedback. Without conditioning the DIR on label correctness (i.e., if the feedback agent accurately identifies the issue), label accuracy decreased during training, underscoring the importance of the multiplicative reward structure.
- **Multi-Agent Co-evolution**: Freezing the conversation agent during training led to worse outcomes compared to jointly training both agents, confirming the benefits of co-adaptation.
- **Two-Stage Training**: Forgoing the second stage of collaborative training resulted in significantly higher ASR and ORR, indicating that the two-stage approach is effective in enhancing safety and reducing overrefusal while maintaining label accuracy.
- **Mixed Trajectory Sampling**: Blending initial and final response trajectories for the conversation agent was found to be superior to using only one type, demonstrating its effectiveness in learning both to generate good initial responses and to incorporate feedback.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Examples

Qualitative examples provided in the appendix illustrated cases where the feedback agent successfully guided the conversation agent to transform an initial overrefusal into a compliant, helpful response, often by providing explicit outlines or even suggesting ideal phrasing. The feedback agent also demonstrated the ability to distinguish between prohibited harmful actions and permissible historical context or analysis.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Alignment Waltz" research presents a significant contribution to the field of large language model safety alignment with several potential impacts:

1. **Advancing the Helpfulness-Harmlessness Pareto Front**: The primary significance lies in demonstrating that it is possible to improve both the safety (reducing unsafe generations) and helpfulness (reducing overrefusals) of LLMs simultaneously. This challenges the prevailing notion of an inherent trade-off and offers a pathway towards more balanced and robust models. By enabling models to generate constructive responses to sensitive prompts instead of blanket refusals, WaltzRL improves user experience while maintaining safety.

2. **Novel Collaborative Multi-Agent Paradigm**: This work introduces a collaborative, positive-sum multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for safety alignment. This approach contrasts with traditional competitive (zero-sum) multi-agent setups or single-agent deployment models. The explicit incentivization of cooperation between a conversation agent and a specialized feedback agent allows for a more nuanced and adaptive approach to content moderation.

3. **Dynamic Improvement Reward (DIR)**: The proposed Dynamic Improvement Reward is a key methodological innovation. By rewarding the feedback agent based on the actual improvement its suggestions bring to the conversation agent's subsequent responses, it fosters the generation of genuinely useful and actionable feedback. This mechanism drives the learning of sophisticated collaborative behavior.

4. **Adaptive and Nuanced Safety Interventions**: Unlike static safeguard models that globally filter and reject content, WaltzRL's adaptive feedback mechanism allows for dynamic, context-aware intervention. The feedback agent only engages when necessary, preserving latency for benign queries and offering specific guidance for improvement rather than blunt rejection for problematic ones. This can lead to more constructive interactions with users, particularly for dual-use prompts.

5. **Preservation of General Capabilities**: A notable finding is that WaltzRL enhances safety without degrading the model's general instruction-following and knowledge capabilities, even though helpfulness data was not explicitly part of the RL training for safety. This suggests that specializing agents for specific alignment goals (e.g., safety in the feedback agent) can be an effective strategy to avoid negative transfer or performance degradation in other areas.

6. **Enhanced Robustness**: By requiring an attacker to "jailbreak" two distinct, collaboratively trained agents, the framework inherently offers a more robust defense mechanism against adversarial attacks compared to single-model systems.

7. **Future Research Directions**: The work opens avenues for future research, including exploring multiple rounds of feedback for more complex revisions, developing generalist feedback agents capable of interacting with various conversation agents, and evaluating the framework against advanced, adaptive adversarial attacks. The insights gained from WaltzRL could also inform the design of future alignment techniques that prioritize collaborative intelligence for safer and more helpful AI.
