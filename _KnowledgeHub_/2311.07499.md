# Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap with Dynamic Compliance Tuning for Industrial Insertion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley and Autodesk Research developed a framework to bridge the sim-to-real gap for contact-rich industrial insertion tasks, achieving robust zero-shot transfer by dynamically tuning robot compliance based on desired contact forces and learned motion plans, outperforming traditional baselines across various complex assembly scenarios.

## Problem

- High-dimensionality and nonlinearity of contact dynamics present a challenge for automating industrial manipulation tasks requiring precise contact forces.
- The 'sim-to-real gap' due to inaccurate simulation of contact dynamics hinders the direct transfer of policies learned in simulation to real robots.
- Tuning compliance control gains (e.g., stiffness) for real robots is difficult, often task-dependent, and existing learning methods struggle with zero-shot transfer.

## Method

- A ==decoupled learning framework== consisting of a **Force Planner** and a **Gain Tuner** is proposed, both trained exclusively in simulation.
- The Force Planner, based on a ==Decision Transformer==, simultaneously predicts robot motion (Cartesian displacement) and ==desired contact force== for the next time step.
- The Gain Tuner dynamically adjusts ==admittance control gains== (stiffness) to ensure the real robot's contact force tracks the desired force planned by the Force Planner.

## Results

- Achieved high success rates (**70-100%**) in zero-shot transfer for tight 3D-printed peg-and-hole insertions (**0.02 mm** to **0.05 mm** clearances), significantly outperforming baseline methods.
- Demonstrated robust generalization to complex electronic connectors (**100%** and **90%** success rates) and real-world skateboard truck assembly tasks (**70%** to **90%** success rates) involving deformable parts and negative clearances.
- Ablation studies confirmed the Gain Tuner's ability to responsively adjust stiffness to track scaled desired forces, indicating its adaptive control mechanism.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the planning of robot motion and desired contact force from the dynamic adjustment of compliance gains enables more robust sim-to-real transfer.
> - Dynamically tuning admittance control gains allows the robot to adapt its interaction force in the real environment to match a desired force profile, mitigating simulation inaccuracies.
> - Training both modules with supervised learning on simulation data, augmented with domain randomization and noise, facilitates zero-shot transfer without real-world fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a significant challenge within robotics: the "sim-to-real gap" in contact-rich manipulation tasks, particularly in industrial assembly.

#### 1.1. Challenges in Contact-Rich Manipulation

Industrial robots are increasingly tasked with complex manipulation requiring physical interaction with the environment, such as parts assembly and object pushing. These tasks necessitate precise application of contact forces. However, the inherent high-dimensionality and nonlinearity of contact dynamics present a considerable challenge for automation. Traditional approaches often rely on manual design of robot skills, which is time-consuming and lacks scalability across diverse task settings.

#### 1.2. Learning-Based Approaches and Sim-to-Real Gap

Recent advancements in learning-based methods, including imitation learning and reinforcement learning (RL), offer a promising alternative for automating the acquisition of these complex skills. While early studies focused on direct learning on physical robots, the emphasis has shifted towards simulation-based learning due to concerns regarding training safety and efficiency. However, accurately simulating complex contact dynamics remains difficult, as simulations are sensitive to parameters like surface friction, material stiffness, and specific geometries (e.g., narrow clearances, irregular connectors). These discrepancies create a substantial sim-to-real gap, impeding the direct transfer of simulation-learned policies to real-world robots. Prior efforts to address this gap include domain randomization, system identification, and fine-tuning with real-world data, but these often have limitations in achieving robust, zero-shot transfer for highly precise, contact-rich tasks.

#### 1.3. Compliance Control and Gain Tuning

For contact-rich manipulation, compliance control is frequently employed to facilitate safe interaction with the environment. Admittance and impedance control are common strategies that generate compliant robot motion based on external forces or trajectory deviations. A key challenge lies in tuning the compliance control gains (e.g., stiffness and damping matrices) for real robots. These gains are difficult to set for stability and often require adjustment for different tasks or even different phases within a single task. Learning-based approaches have been proposed to automatically acquire these gains, but these learned gains often suffer from the sim-to-real gap, necessitating further fine-tuning on real-world data. Some research has explored jointly learning robot motion and variable gains, demonstrating advantages over fixed-gain baselines, yet direct, zero-shot transfer to complex real-world tasks without fine-tuning remains an unresolved issue. Existing online optimization methods for gain adjustment tend to be computationally intensive, limiting their adaptation frequency.

This work specifically focuses on bridging the sim-to-real gap for tight insertion tasks (clearances less than 0.1 mm, or even negative clearances with deformable receptacles) by developing a framework that can learn manipulation skills exclusively from simulated data and transfer them robustly to real-world scenarios without fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust framework for learning contact-rich manipulation skills for real-world tasks using only simulated data, enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer without requiring any fine-tuning on the physical system.

#### 2.1. Problem Statement

The motivation stems from the significant challenges in automating industrial assembly tasks, particularly those involving tight insertions. These tasks are characterized by narrow or negative clearances, which lead to complex contact dynamics that are difficult to model accurately in simulation. The discrepancy between simulated and real-world contact behaviors creates a substantial sim-to-real gap, preventing the effective transfer of policies learned in simulation to real robots. Furthermore, the appropriate tuning of compliance control gains for real robots, which are crucial for safe and effective contact, is challenging and often task-dependent. Existing methods struggle with zero-shot transfer due to these issues.

#### 2.2. Core Hypothesis

The core hypothesis of this paper is that by dynamically adjusting a robot's compliance control gains during task execution, it is possible to adaptively modulate the contact force in the new environment to match a desired contact force. This adaptation can generate robot trajectories similar to those trained in simulation, thereby narrowing the sim-to-real gap.

#### 2.3. Proposed Solution Overview

To achieve this, the authors propose a novel framework consisting of two main components:
1. **Force Planner:** This component is responsible for planning both the robot's motion (incremental Cartesian displacement) and the desired contact force for the subsequent time step, given the current robot state and a desired future return.
2. **Gain Tuner:** This component dynamically adjusts the admittance control gains of the robot. Its purpose is to ensure that the actual contact force experienced by the robot in the real world tracks the desired contact force planned by the Force Planner. The Gain Tuner makes these adjustments based on the desired force, planned motion, current state, and historical trajectory data, which implicitly encodes environmental properties.

Both modules are designed to be trained exclusively in simulation using supervised learning and are intended for direct transfer to real-world scenarios without further fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on a decoupled learning framework where a "Force Planner" determines desired robot motion and contact force, and a "Gain Tuner" adaptively adjusts compliance gains to track the desired force.

#### 3.1. Admittance Control Framework

The system utilizes an admittance controller to manage robot actions. The controller maps desired pose and velocity (x_d, ẋ_d) to compliant robot motion (x_c, ẋ_c) based on a mass-spring-damping model: M(ẍ_c - ẍ_d) + D(ẋ_c - ẋ_d) + K(x_c - x_d) = f. Here, M, K, and D represent inertia, stiffness, and damping matrices, respectively, assumed to be positive definite diagonal matrices to ensure stability. The damping matrix D is computed as D = 4√MK, resulting in an overdamped system. The paper focuses on planning for the stiffness matrix K, referring to its diagonal entries k as admittance gains.

#### 3.2. Problem Setup and Task Definition

The real robot setup involves a UR10e robot with an embedded force/torque (F/T) sensor at the wrist, grasping a peg for insertion into a fixed hole. No camera is used; decision-making relies on F/T sensor data and robot proprioception, assuming nominal hole location knowledge but uncertainty in exact pose. The task is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with an 18-dimensional state S (peg pose, velocity, contact force) and a 12-dimensional action space A (incremental Cartesian motion ∆x and diagonal stiffness entries k). The reward R is defined as the negative squared Euclidean distance between the robot's current position and a fixed target point inside the hole.

Three sets of challenging insertion tasks are defined:
1. **Tight 3D-printed peg-and-holes:** Rectangular and round, with clearances of 0.05 mm and 0.02 mm.
2. **Complex electronic connectors:** Standard Ethernet and waterproof types.
3. **Real-world skateboard truck assembly:** Hanger insertion into a deformable receptacle and kingpin insertion through a tight rubber bushing, both involving negative clearances.

#### 3.3. The Force Planner

The Force Planner (FP) is designed to generate the desired robot motion (∆x) and the desired next contact force (f_d) simultaneously. It is trained using a Decision Transformer (DT) architecture, which leverages the GPT model to autoregressively predict actions based on historical trajectories and a desired future return. The extended action space for training includes both robot motion and desired force, A' = [∆x, f_d]. For training, a historical trajectory of window size H (τ^fp_t) is used, comprising states, extended actions, and returns. The FP model FP(∆x_t, f^d_{t+1}|x_t, ẋ_t, f_t, R_t, τ^fp_t) predicts ∆x_t and f^d_{t+1}. Training involves minimizing a mean-squared error (MSE) loss between predicted and actual values.

#### 3.4. The Gain Tuner

The Gain Tuner (GT) serves as a gain scheduler, dynamically adjusting the admittance control gains (k_t) to track the desired contact force (f^d_{t+1}) planned by the Force Planner. The core idea is that by adjusting k_t, the contact force generated by the robot in the real environment can be modulated to match the desired force from simulation. The GT model, GT(k_t|f^d_{t+1}, ∆x_t, x_t, ẋ_t, τ^gt_t), learns to predict k_t. It takes as input the historical trajectory (τ^gt_t), current state (x_t, ẋ_t), current robot motion (∆x_t), and the planned next desired force (f^d_{t+1}). The historical trajectory is used to approximate environmental properties E. During training, the actual next force (f_{t+1}) from the dataset replaces f^d_{t+1}, and an MSE loss is applied between predicted and actual admittance gains.

#### 3.5. Data Collection and Augmentation

Both the Force Planner and Gain Tuner are trained via supervised learning on simulation data. The simulation environment models a square peg-and-hole in the PhysX physics engine. A reinforcement learning (RL) agent, specifically Soft-Actor-Critic (SAC), is used with a curriculum-based approach to generate the dataset. Initially, the agent learns insertion with a 0.5 mm clearance, then fine-tunes on a more challenging task with 0.3 mm clearance. The replay buffer from the second phase forms the training dataset.

To enhance robustness and sim-to-real transferability, domain randomization and data augmentation techniques are applied:
- **Domain Randomization:** Applied to the peg's initial pose and hole location.
- **Data Augmentation:** Applied to contact force data. A scaling factor (0.4 to 1.4) is introduced to account for discrepancies in admittance command interpretation between simulation and reality. Zero-mean Gaussian noise (standard deviation of 1 N) is injected to model real-world F/T sensor noise. Dynamics properties like mass and friction were not randomized to avoid simulation instability.

#### 3.6. Training Details

Both GT and FP adopt a model architecture similar to Decision Transformer (GPT-2). For GT, robot states, motions, previous gains, and desired forces are mapped to 128-dimensional embedding spaces before being processed by GPT-2. The FP uses similar architecture with state, extended action, and return modalities. A window size H of 20 is used for both models. Training involves batch sizes of 64 over 200,000 steps, with a learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁴.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the sim-to-real robustness and generalizability of the proposed method against two baselines: a Decision Transformer (DT) and an RL policy, both of which output robot motion and admittance gains simultaneously.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on a UR10e robot with a wrist-mounted F/T sensor. The peg's initial position was randomized. The tasks included 3D-printed peg-and-holes (0.05 mm and 0.02 mm clearance), electronic connectors, and skateboard truck assembly, some involving deformable parts and negative clearances.

#### 4.2. Tight 3D-Printed Peg-and-Hole Insertions

For 3D-printed tasks, which involve clearances as small as 0.02 mm, the proposed method demonstrated zero-shot transfer with high success rates:
- Rectangular 0.05 mm clearance: 90% (9/10)
- Round 0.05 mm clearance: 100% (10/10)
- Rectangular 0.02 mm clearance: 70% (7/10)
- Round 0.02 mm clearance: 90% (9/10)

In contrast, both baseline DT and RL models struggled, achieving significantly lower success rates (e.g., 1/10 for rectangular 0.05 mm, 0/10 for rectangular 0.02 mm). Analysis of contact force profiles and admittance gains showed that the Gain Tuner adaptively adjusted stiffness to track the planned force, whereas baselines exhibited noisy or unstable gain patterns, leading to diminished performance. The method's X-axis force tracking was less accurate than Z-axis, attributed to simulation contact model inaccuracies and higher real-world sticking forces.

#### 4.3. Electronic Connectors

The method generalized effectively to different, non-convex geometries of electronic connectors (Ethernet and waterproof), achieving:
- Ethernet connector: 100% (10/10)
- Waterproof connector: 90% (9/10)

Baselines performed better on these tasks compared to the tight peg-in-hole scenarios (e.g., DT 8/10 for Ethernet, RL 6/10 for waterproof) but were still outperformed by the proposed method.

#### 4.4. Skateboard Truck Assembly

For real-world skateboard truck assembly tasks, involving deformable parts and negative clearances, the proposed method maintained strong performance:
- Hanger insertion (deformable, negative clearance): 90% (9/10)
- Kingpin insertion (metal-to-metal, negative clearance): 70% (7/10)

Baselines often applied excessive force, leading to part deformation and sticking, resulting in lower success rates (e.g., DT 7/10 for hanger, RL 3/10 for kingpin). The kingpin task's lower success rate for the proposed method was primarily due to initial high stiffness predictions from the Gain Tuner triggering protective stops, though it still surpassed baselines.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

1. **Gain Tuner Responsiveness to Desired Force:** By scaling the desired force output from the Force Planner (factors 0 to 2) while keeping robot motion constant, the study showed the Gain Tuner's ability to dynamically adjust admittance gains. For a desired force factor of 0, the Gain Tuner rapidly reduced stiffness, keeping subsequent contact forces below 10 N. As the scaling factor increased, the Gain Tuner proportionately elevated stiffness to track the planned force, demonstrating its adaptive capability.
2. **Fine-tuning the Force Planner:** Fine-tuning the Force Planner with 10 real-world trajectories from the 0.05 mm rectangular peg-in-hole task improved performance on difficult tasks. For the 0.02 mm rectangular peg-in-hole, the success rate increased to 90%, and for kingpin insertion, it reached 100%. This suggests that while the original Gain Tuner effectively modulates contact force, fine-tuning the Force Planner can yield more realistic force plans (e.g., in the X-direction for rectangular tasks) and enhance overall performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap

The research presents a significant advancement in addressing the sim-to-real gap for contact-rich robotic manipulation. By decoupling the learning of robot motion and desired force (Force Planner) from the dynamic adjustment of compliance control gains (Gain Tuner), the proposed framework allows for robust transfer of skills learned entirely in simulation to complex real-world tasks. The ability to dynamically modulate contact force based on a desired force profile, even in environments with different physical properties than simulation, directly mitigates the impact of inaccuracies in simulated contact dynamics.

#### 5.2. Generalizability and Robustness

The demonstrated zero-shot transferability across a diverse set of challenging insertion tasks, including those with extremely narrow clearances (down to 0.02 mm), deformable receptacles, and negative clearances, highlights the method's generalizability and robustness. This capability is particularly relevant for industrial applications where precise assembly tasks are common, but manual tuning of robot parameters or extensive real-world training is impractical. The framework's superior performance compared to end-to-end learning baselines (Decision Transformer and RL Policy) emphasizes the benefit of its decomposed architecture and dynamic compliance tuning.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its successes, the method has limitations. The Force Planner, being trained solely in simulation, may not always generate optimally realistic desired contact forces for all real-world deployment scenarios due to inherent variations in task settings. Additionally, the current approach simplifies the insertion task by assuming the peg is already grasped by the robot. Future work is proposed to explore methods for efficiently adapting the Force Planner online to generate more realistic force plans and to incorporate visual servoing for peg grasping and initial alignment, further enhancing the framework's autonomy and applicability. This research provides a foundational step towards more generalized and adaptable robotic manipulation systems capable of handling complex physical interactions without extensive real-world data collection or fine-tuning.
