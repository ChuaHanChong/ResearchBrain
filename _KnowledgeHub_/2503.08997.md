# Unified Locomotion Transformer with Simultaneous Sim-to-Real Transfer for Quadrupeds

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NVIDIA AI Technology Centre and Nanyang Technological University developed the Unified Locomotion Transformer (ULT), a framework that integrates knowledge transfer and policy optimization into a single network for quadrupedal robots. This approach enables zero-shot sim-to-real deployment on a Unitree A1 robot, demonstrating robust locomotion across various terrains while matching the performance of an "Oracle" policy with comparable data efficiency.

## Problem

- Traditional deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) teacher-student frameworks for quadruped locomotion involve complex, sequential training stages that are often data-inefficient.
- The sequential knowledge distillation process limits the student policy's robustness and generalization, especially when deploying data-hungry transformer-based controllers.
- Existing transformer-based locomotion controllers require extensive data generation and multi-stage supervised training, increasing computational cost and pipeline complexity for sim-to-real transfer.

## Method

- Introduced the **Unified Locomotion Transformer** (ULT) framework, which integrates policy optimization and knowledge transfer into a single transformer-based network.
- Developed an "Action Mixer" mechanism to dynamically combine teacher-guided and student-driven actions during training, facilitating simultaneous optimization of both policies.
- Incorporated privilege information as an additional modality in the transformer's input sequence, enabling continuous, indirect guidance during the unified training phase.

## Results

- The ULT framework achieved locomotion performance comparable to an "Oracle" policy, demonstrating high data efficiency in simultaneously optimizing teacher and student policies.
- It outperformed existing supervised and joint knowledge transfer baselines, showing enhanced robustness and generalization for both teacher and student policies across five terrain types.
- Successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer was achieved on a Unitree A1 robot, enabling robust omnidirectional locomotion on diverse physical terrains without additional fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A balanced exploration ratio (optimal at "alpha"=0.6) between teacher-guided and student-driven actions during unified training is essential for achieving high performance in both policies.
> - Integrating next state-action prediction and action imitation losses with the PPO objective is critical for the transformer to efficiently learn state transition dynamics and leverage privilege-guided actions.
> - Causal masking in the transformer architecture enables the student policy to operate solely on proprioceptive observations for zero-shot real-world deployment, despite benefiting from privilege information during training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadrupedal robot locomotion has seen significant advancements, particularly with the integration of deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques. Traditional controllers, reliant on dedicated models and heuristics, often struggle with dynamic and unpredictable environments due to their limited adaptability and requirement for extensive prior knowledge. The contemporary paradigm involves learning controllers in simulated environments, which can then be transferred to physical robots using various knowledge transfer methods and "privilege information" to bridge the sim-to-real gap.

A prominent method for knowledge transfer is the teacher-student framework. In this setup, a teacher policy is initially trained in simulation with access to "privilege information" (e.g., ground-truth dynamics, terrain profiles), which is generally unavailable in real-world deployment. An optimal locomotion strategy is learned by this teacher. Subsequently, a student policy is trained, typically through supervised knowledge distillation, to mimic the teacher's actions using only real-world accessible proprioceptive observations. This process often involves offline data collection or online adaptation, such as through algorithms like Data Aggregator (DAgger).

However, this sequential training approach is frequently data-inefficient and can suffer from performance limitations where the student's robustness and generalization are constrained by the teacher's performance and the diversity of the training data. Efforts to address this include approaches like DreamWaQ, which utilizes an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and context estimation to allow the agent to explore with indirect guidance while leveraging privileged information.

More recently, transformer models, known for their self-attention mechanisms, have been introduced into robotics for both low-level control and high-level decision-making, demonstrating capabilities in handling variable context lengths and multi-modal inputs. While transformers excel at capturing temporal information compared to recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and temporal convolutional networks (TCNs), their data-hungry nature presents challenges for training in robotics applications. Existing transformer-based locomotion controllers often require extensive data generation and multi-stage supervised training, increasing computational cost and complexity in pipeline management. This paper positions itself within this context by aiming to streamline the knowledge distillation process for transformer-based quadruped locomotion controllers, addressing the inefficiencies and complexities of multi-stage training while leveraging privilege information.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the limitations observed in current knowledge distillation techniques, particularly within the context of transformer-based locomotion policies for quadruped robots. The classical teacher-student framework, while effective in bridging the sim-to-real gap, presents several inefficiencies:

1. **Sequential Training Complexity:** The traditional pipeline requires distinct, sequential stages: first, training a teacher policy with privileged information, and then, a separate stage of supervised knowledge distillation to train a student policy using accessible observations. This multi-stage process necessitates additional setup to manage multiple models and large volumes of generated data.
2. **Data Inefficiency:** Sequential training, especially for locomotion control, is generally not data-efficient. The performance and robustness of the student policy are inherently limited by the quality and diversity of the trajectories generated by the pre-trained teacher, leading to potential degradation in performance when encountering situations outside the training data distribution in real-world deployment. For transformer-based models, which are inherently data-hungry, this limitation is further exacerbated, often requiring millions of additional timesteps for transfer.
3. **Inefficient Privilege Information Usage:** While privilege information is crucial for efficient learning of optimal locomotion strategies in simulation, its utilization in the teacher-student framework is primarily restricted to the teacher's initial training. The transfer process then focuses on imitating the teacher's actions without directly incorporating the privilege information for student exploration or policy optimization in a unified manner.

Against this background, the key objective of this research is to **simplify the knowledge distillation process by developing a unified architecture that allows for the simultaneous optimization of both teacher and student policies within a single network.** The authors aim to achieve this by:

- **Unifying Knowledge Transfer and Policy Optimization:** Integrating these processes into a single network to eliminate the need for dedicated teacher network design and multi-stage training.
- **Leveraging Privilege Information within a Unified Framework:** Introducing privilege information as an additional modality into the transformer's observation space. This allows for its continuous, indirect guidance during the student policy's exploration, aiming to generate more diverse trajectories and improve generalization.
- **Achieving Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Designing a system capable of direct deployment on physical robots without further fine-tuning, by ensuring that the student policy relies only on proprioceptive observations, while still benefiting from the teacher's guidance during the unified training phase.
- **Improving Sample Efficiency:** By combining reinforcement learning with next state-action prediction and action imitation within a single training stage, the goal is to discover state transition dynamics and observation probabilities more efficiently, thereby reducing the total number of generated trajectories required.

In essence, the motivation is to create a more efficient, less complex, and more robust method for training advanced transformer-based locomotion controllers for quadruped robots, facilitating their practical deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose the Unified Locomotion Transformer (ULT) framework, designed to unify knowledge transfer and policy optimization into a single network. The approach is built upon a transformer architecture and optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) combined with additional losses.

#### 3.1. Simulation Environment

The simulation environment is implemented using Isaac Gym and IsaacGymEnvs, enabling large-scale parallel training.

- **Terrain and Curriculum:** The policy is trained on a curriculum of five terrain types: smooth slope, rough slope, stairs up, stairs down, and discrete obstacles. Each type has 10 incremental difficulty levels. Agents progress to the next level upon achieving 80% of the maximum linear velocity tracking reward and regress if they fall below 25%.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** To enhance policy robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, DR is applied to key dynamics parameters (e.g., Kp/Kd scale, friction, motor strength, payload, CoM offset, external pushes, gravity, system delay). Linear and angular velocity commands are also sampled and randomized.

#### 3.2. Observations and Actions

- **Privilege Information (e_t):** Used by the teacher policy to access hidden environmental information, including randomized dynamics parameters (d_t), ground-truth robot states (s_t, e.g., base velocity, orientation), and precise surrounding height map (m_t). This information, though inaccessible in the real world, aids in efficient learning.
- **Proprioceptive Observation (o_t):** Used by the student policy, relying solely on onboard sensors. It includes joint position (q), joint velocity (˙q), angular velocity (ω), gravity vector (g), binary foot contact (c), and user commands (cmd = [vx, vy, ωz]). To capture temporal context, a rolling window of 15 historical observations and previous actions (a_{t-1}) is concatenated to form a sequence T, resulting in an observation space of R^(49x15).
- **Actions (a_t):** Both policies output desired joint positions (q=a_t) at a frequency of 50-100 Hz. These are passed to a higher-frequency PD controller (Kp=30, Kd=0.7) for torque output.

#### 3.3. Reward Function

A standard reward function for omnidirectional locomotion is used, encouraging commanded velocity tracking and penalizing vertical body velocity, body rotation, high joint acceleration, excessive power consumption, action rate changes, foot slip, and collisions.

#### 3.4. Model Architecture

ULT is based on a vanilla transformer with multihead attention blocks and causal masking.

1. **Tokenization:** Input trajectory T, consisting of concatenated state-action pairs (o_t, a_{t-1}), is tokenized into `z_t` and then projected into embedding space `h_t` via a shared linear layer.
2. **Privilege Information Encoding:** Privilege information `e_t` is processed by a three-layer MLP (environmental factor encoder μ) to project it into the same embedding space, `h_e`.
3. **Transformer Processing:** The full sequence `H = [h_0..., h_t, h_e]` (with `h_e` at the end to prevent information leakage to proprioceptive observations via causal masking) is fed into the transformer to produce `ˆH`.
4. **Next State-Action Prediction:** The transformer's output `ˆH` is used to predict future state-action trajectories. Specifically, `ˆh_t` (from the transformer's output) is decoded via another linear layer to `ˆz_{t+1}`, predicting the next state-action pair. A next state-action prediction loss (`L_n`) calculates the L2 norm between predicted and actual state-action pairs. This loss encourages the transformer to learn the robot's state and action transitions.
5. **Action Output:**
 - **Student Action (ˆa_t):** Directly extracted from the predicted next state-action pair `ˆz_{t+1}`.
 - **Teacher Action (¯a_t):** Generated from `ˆh_e` (the processed privilege information token) through a separate MLP policy network.
 - **Action Imitation Loss (L_a):** Measures the L2 difference between the teacher's action (¯a_t) and the student's action (ˆa_t).
6. **Unified Transformer Loss (L_ULT):** Combines `L_n` and `L_a` with a weighting factor β: `L_ULT = L_n + βL_a`.

#### 3.5. Action Mixer and Unified Training

To enable simultaneous optimization without a pre-trained teacher, an "Action Mixer" is introduced during training. With X parallel agents, an agent mask M (threshold α) determines whether a teacher action (¯a_i) or a student action (ˆa_i) is executed: `a_i = (¯a_i if M_i < α else ˆa_i)`. A higher `α` means more teacher involvement. The mask is frequently resampled for exploration.

The overall training objective combines the PPO RL loss (`L_RL`) with the transformer loss (`L_ULT`): `L = L_RL + λL_ULT`. This allows for joint optimization, leveraging state transition learning, action imitation, and guidance from privileged information in a single training phase.

#### 3.6. Direct Sim-to-Real Deployment

For real-world deployment, the framework removes the privileged environmental token (`h_e`) from the input sequence. Due to causal masking during training, the student policy (generating `ˆa_i`) can directly operate using only historical proprioceptive information, achieving zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ULT primarily focuses on three metrics: average linear velocity tracking return, average angular velocity tracking return (both per step for task performance), and final total episode reward return (for overall locomotion quality). All results are normalized against a pre-trained "Oracle" policy (similar to) and averaged over 5000 trajectories across various terrain types.

#### 4.1. A. Action Mixer Ratio ($\alpha$)

Experiments with varying action mixer ratios ($\alpha$) demonstrated its influence on performance:
- **Low $\alpha$ (e.g., $\alpha < 0.4$):** Teacher policy performance suffered due to insufficient trajectories for optimal learning, despite access to privilege information.
- **High $\alpha$ (e.g., $\alpha > 0.8$):** Student policy performance decreased because it became overly reliant on teacher guidance, limiting its exploration and ability to handle out-of-distribution situations.
- **$\alpha = 1$:** Produced a strong teacher policy but a poorly performing student due to a purely supervised training lacking student-driven exploration. This resulted in a low student survival rate.
- **$\alpha = 0.6$:** Was identified as the optimal ratio, allowing both teacher and student policies to achieve high performance.

This finding indicates that a balanced exploration between teacher-guided and student-driven trajectories is crucial for the unified training process.

#### 4.2. B. Comparison with Baselines

ULT was compared against several knowledge transfer baselines:
- **Supervised Transfer (Offline-Only, Online-Only, Two-Stages):** These methods, based on direct action imitation, generally required more trajectories and often struggled, especially the offline-only variant on rough terrains, where the student's survival rate was low. Online and two-stage methods improved performance but were still outperformed by ULT.
- **Joint Transfer:** This method, combining RL exploration with online supervised transfer, performed similarly to vanilla PPO, struggling with increased environmental challenges and requiring manual tuning of the joint ratio.
- **CENet (DreamWaQ):** This auto-encoder model, which uses VAE loss for next observation, base velocity, and latent space estimation with an asymmetric actor-critic, showed strong performance, exhibiting only slight disadvantages compared to ULT. This indicates a shared principle of benefiting from understanding state-action transitions.
- **PPO (Vanilla):** Training ULT solely on proprioceptive observation and RL loss (equivalent to $\alpha = 0$ with teacher head and other loss modules disabled) resulted in a student-only training, which performed less effectively due to the absence of privileged information and transformer-specific losses.

**Overall, ULT demonstrated superior performance for both teacher and student policies, achieving levels comparable to the Oracle policy while using the same number of trajectories as the Oracle training.** This indicates its high efficiency in knowledge transfer and simultaneous optimization, simplifying the training pipeline.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of ULT's core components:
- **Removal of Next State-Action Prediction ($L_n$):** Performance decreased for both teacher and student policies.
- **Removal of Action Imitation ($L_a$):** Performance also decreased, highlighting the importance of guided learning.
- **Removal of Both ($L_n$ and $L_a$):** Led to a significant drop in performance, similar to the $\alpha = 0$ case (vanilla PPO).

These results confirm that both the next state-action prediction module and the action imitation module are essential for ULT's effectiveness, contributing to its capability to understand state-action transitions and benefit from high-quality, privilege-guided actions. The absence of privilege information makes it difficult to optimize next state-action prediction effectively.

#### 4.4. D. ULT with Supervised Transfer

When a student policy was trained with $\alpha = 1$ (purely supervised, teacher-only trajectories), its initial performance was poor. Although an additional online supervised transfer phase recovered some performance, it remained inferior to the fully integrated ULT framework. This finding underscores the importance of the "Action Mixer" and the unified training approach, where simultaneous optimization and mixed exploration during the single training phase are more effective than sequential, isolated transfer stages.

#### 4.5. E. Physical Deployment

The trained ULT policy was successfully deployed directly on a Unitree A1 robot equipped with a Jetson AGX Orin Developer Kit, demonstrating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The policy ran at a control frequency of 50Hz, with Kp=30 and Kd=0.7. The robot exhibited robust locomotion on various terrains and with omnidirectional movements, validating the framework's practicality in the real world.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Unified Locomotion Transformer (ULT) framework represents a methodological advancement in the training and deployment of deep reinforcement learning policies for quadrupedal robots. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

1. **Simplified Training Pipeline:** By unifying the processes of knowledge transfer and policy optimization into a single network and a single training phase, ULT eliminates the need for sequential teacher-student training stages. This reduces the complexity of the training setup, streamlines the development workflow, and potentially lowers the barrier to adopting advanced, data-hungry models like transformers in robotics.
2. **Increased Sample Efficiency:** The joint optimization of reinforcement learning with next state-action prediction and action imitation, combined with mixed exploration guided by privilege information, allows ULT to achieve optimal teacher and student policies with a reduced volume of trajectory data compared to multi-stage baselines. This efficiency can translate to faster iteration cycles in research and development.
3. **Robust Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The framework's design, which leverages causal masking and integrates privilege information as an observation modality during training, enables direct deployment on physical robots without further fine-tuning. This "zero-shot" capability is crucial for practical robotic applications, as it saves significant time and resources typically spent on real-world adaptation.
4. **Enhanced Policy Generalization:** The unified training process, benefiting from both privilege-guided teacher actions and student exploration, contributes to the generation of more diverse trajectories. This diversified experience, coupled with the transformer's inherent ability to capture temporal and multimodal information, is intended to lead to more robust and generalizable locomotion policies capable of handling complex and varied real-world terrains.
5. **Efficient Utilization of Privilege Information:** ULT's method of incorporating privilege information as an additional modality during the single training phase allows this valuable data to continuously guide the learning process of the student policy, improving the overall effectiveness of knowledge transfer.

The potential impact of ULT extends to accelerating the development and deployment of agile and robust quadruped robots in challenging environments. By simplifying the underlying machine learning pipeline, it could enable roboticists to focus more on higher-level tasks and applications rather than the intricate details of policy transfer.

The authors also identify future research directions, such as exploring continual learning and generalization capabilities through large language models (LLMs) for handling a wider variety of information and transferring policies to different tasks and robot embodiments. This suggests that the unified approach could serve as a foundation for more generalist robotic controllers, further broadening the scope of autonomous quadruped applications.
