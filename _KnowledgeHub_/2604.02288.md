# Unifying Group-Relative and Self-Distillation Policy Optimization via Sample Routing

> [!summary] Summary
> Sample-Routed Policy Optimization (SRPO) unifies Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) for large language model post-training. This framework uses adaptive sample routing and entropy-aware dynamic weighting to improve early training efficiency, enhance long-horizon stability, and achieve higher peak performance on various benchmarks.

## Problem

- Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) uses coarse, sequence-level credit assignment, leading to inefficient updates for failed rollouts and slower convergence.
- Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) offers fast early convergence but experiences instability and performance collapse during prolonged training due to ambiguous signals on correct samples and degrading teacher quality.
- Post-training methods for LLMs often struggle to balance the stability of reward-aligned policy gradient methods with the fine-grained guidance of self-distillation.

## Method

- Introduces **SRPO** (==Sample-Routed Policy Optimization==), an on-policy framework that unifies GRPO and SDPO by adaptively applying them.
- Employs a ==sample routing mechanism== that directs correct samples to a GRPO-based objective and incorrect samples with available teacher information to an SDPO-based objective.
- Integrates an ==entropy-aware dynamic weighting== mechanism within the self-distillation branch to reduce reliance on unreliable (high-entropy) teacher signals, improving late-stage stability.

## Results

- **SRPO** achieved an average accuracy of **77.4%** on Qwen3-8B across five benchmarks, surpassing GRPO by **3.4** percentage points and SDPO by **6.3** percentage points.
- The method demonstrated rapid early performance gains similar to SDPO, combined with long-horizon stability and higher peak accuracy, avoiding the performance collapse seen in SDPO.
- **SRPO** reduced per-step compute time by **17.2%** compared to GRPO and **9.4%** compared to SDPO after 10 hours of training, while also yielding moderate and efficient response lengths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - GRPO provides stable, reward-aligned updates suitable for successful behaviors, while SDPO offers dense, logit-level corrections effective for repairing specific errors in failed trajectories.
> - SDPO's late-stage instability arises from optimization ambiguity when distilling correct samples and the decreasing reliability (increasing entropy) of the self-teacher's signal over time.
> - Dynamically routing samples to the most appropriate optimization signal (GRPO for correct, SDPO for incorrect with reliable teacher) and weighting distillation targets by confidence can combine the benefits of both approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of post-training large language models (LLMs) using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). RLVR has become a standard method for enhancing LLMs' reasoning and problem-solving capabilities. The paper focuses on two prominent RLVR approaches: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO), identifying their complementary strengths and weaknesses.

GRPO is a widely adopted policy-gradient method known for its simplicity and stability. It estimates advantages by normalizing outcome rewards across a group of rollouts, providing reward-aligned but coarse-grained credit assignment. This sequence-level assignment, while suitable for successful rollouts, uniformly penalizes or reinforces all tokens in a rollout, potentially diminishing sample efficiency and slowing convergence by lacking focus on specific token-level deviations in failed trajectories.

In contrast, recent work has explored on-policy and self-distillation methods to provide denser, logit-level guidance. SDPO is a notable self-distillation approach that uses a feedback-conditioned self-teacher to provide more precise optimization targets, leading to faster early convergence in complex domains. However, SDPO has been observed to suffer from late-stage instability and performance collapse under prolonged training. This instability has been attributed in some concurrent work to the suppression of epistemic verbalization, while this paper proposes a complementary diagnosis centered on the distillation signal itself.

This research bridges the gap between these two paradigms by proposing a unified framework that selectively applies the strengths of GRPO and SDPO based on the learning status of individual samples. It aims to combine the rapid early improvement observed in SDPO with the long-horizon stability characteristic of GRPO, thereby advancing the efficiency and robustness of LLM post-training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified policy optimization framework for post-training large language models that leverages the complementary strengths of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) while mitigating their respective limitations.

The motivation for this work stems from identified challenges in existing RLVR methods:

1. **Limitations of GRPO**:
 - **Coarse Credit Assignment**: GRPO assigns a single scalar advantage uniformly to all tokens within a rollout. For successful rollouts, this is generally appropriate. However, for failed rollouts, this uniform penalty lacks the specificity needed to pinpoint and correct individual token-level errors, leading to inefficient policy updates and slower convergence, as it dilutes gradients across causally irrelevant tokens.
 - **Suboptimal Sample Efficiency**: The uniform credit assignment diminishes the efficiency with which the model learns from specific mistakes, hindering the speed of improvement.

2. **Limitations of SDPO**:
 - **Late-Stage Instability and Collapse**: While SDPO achieves rapid early convergence by providing dense logit-level supervision, it frequently exhibits performance collapse during prolonged training, often being surpassed by GRPO. The authors trace this instability to two intrinsic flaws:
 - **Optimization Ambiguity on Correct Samples**: Applying self-distillation to already-correct samples can be counterproductive. Forcing a successful rollout to match a different successful "sibling" rollout (used as a teacher) can impose arbitrary logit-level preferences between reward-equivalent reasoning paths, introducing noise and ambiguity into the optimization process. This is supported by diagnostic experiments showing that applying SDPO only to correct samples degrades performance.
 - **Degradation of Self-Teacher Signal Quality**: As training progresses and the student policy improves, the gap between the self-teacher and student narrows. Concurrently, the self-teacher's token-level entropy rises, indicating increasingly uncertain predictions. This progressive degradation in the informativeness and reliability of the distillation signal contributes directly to SDPO's late-stage instability.

Based on these observations, the authors motivate their approach by recognizing the complementary nature of GRPO and SDPO. GRPO's sequence-level, reward-aligned updates are robust for anchoring successful behaviors, while SDPO's dense, logit-level correction is more effective for repairing localized errors in failed trajectories, especially when restricted to such cases to avoid ambiguity. The core motivation is to combine these advantages through a mechanism that adaptively applies the most suitable optimization signal to each sample based on its correctness and the availability of reliable teacher information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Sample-Routed Policy Optimization (SRPO), is a unified on-policy framework designed to combine the strengths of GRPO and SDPO. It operates by routing each generated sample to the most appropriate optimization branch based on its outcome (correct or incorrect) and the availability of teacher information. Additionally, SRPO incorporates an entropy-aware dynamic weighting mechanism within the self-distillation component to address the issue of unreliable teacher signals.

**A. Foundation: GRPO and SDPO**

SRPO builds upon the principles of GRPO and SDPO:

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**: For a group of $G$ rollouts $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ for a given prompt $x$, GRPO calculates a sequence-level advantage $A_{GRPO,i} = (r_i - \bar{r})/(\sigma_r + \epsilon)$, where $r_i$ is the reward for rollout $i$, $\bar{r}$ and $\sigma_r$ are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group. This advantage is then used in a clipped surrogate objective, applying a uniform scalar value to every token in the rollout. This provides reward-aligned reinforcement or suppression for entire rollouts.
- **Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO)**: SDPO augments the reward signal with dense logit-level supervision. It constructs a feedback-conditioned self-teacher from the same model, typically by conditioning on privileged context like a successful sibling rollout ($f_i$). The student policy is then trained to match the self-teacher's distribution along its own generated trajectory by minimizing a logit-level divergence (e.g., KL divergence) at each token position $t$: $L_{SDPO}(\theta) = \sum_t KL(\pi_\theta(\cdot | x, y_{i,<t}) \parallel \text{stopgrad}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | x, f_i, y_{i,<t})))$. This provides dense, token-level guidance.

**B. Sample-Level Routing**

SRPO introduces a routing mechanism to direct each rollout to either a GRPO branch or an SDPO branch. For each rollout $y_i$, two binary indicators are used:
- $c_i = 1$ if $y_i$ is correct, $0$ otherwise.
- $m_i = 1$ if teacher information (e.g., a successful sibling rollout) is available for $y_i$, $0$ otherwise.

The routing masks are defined as:
- $z_{SDPO,i} = (1 - c_i) \cdot m_i$: A rollout is routed to the SDPO branch only if it is *incorrect* AND *teacher information is available*.
- $z_{GRPO,i} = 1 - z_{SDPO,i}$: All other rollouts (correct rollouts, or incorrect rollouts without teacher information) are routed to the GRPO branch.

This design ensures that:
- **Correct rollouts** always go to the GRPO branch, leveraging its stable, reward-aligned reinforcement and avoiding the ambiguity of self-distillation on already correct samples.
- **Incorrect rollouts with teacher information** go to the SDPO branch, benefiting from its dense, logit-level correction for specific errors.
- **Incorrect rollouts without teacher information** (e.g., when all rollouts in a group are incorrect) fall back to the GRPO branch, as no informative self-distillation signal can be constructed.

The paper conceptualizes both GRPO and SDPO as providing advantage estimators, albeit at different granularities (sequence-level and reward-derived for GRPO; logit-level and teacher-derived for SDPO). Sample routing selects the appropriate estimator.

**C. Dynamic-Weighted SDPO (DW-SDPO)**

To address the degradation of the self-teacher's signal quality, particularly as entropy rises during training, SRPO equips the SDPO branch with an entropy-aware dynamic weighting mechanism. For each token $t$ in a rollout $i$ routed to the SDPO branch, the self-teacher's distribution $q_{i,t}(v) = \pi_\theta(v | x, f_i, y_{i,<t})$ is used to calculate its entropy $H_{i,t}$.

An unnormalized weight $\tilde{w}_{i,t} = \exp(-\beta H_{i,t})$ is computed, where $\beta > 0$ controls sensitivity to entropy. These weights are then normalized across all valid SDPO tokens to maintain the overall loss scale. The weighted token loss is $\ell_{DW-SDPO,i,t} = w_{i,t} \ell_{SDPO,i,t}$. This mechanism downweights distillation targets from high-entropy (uncertain) predictions while emphasizing low-entropy (confident) ones, aiming to preserve reliable corrective signals.

**D. Training Objective**

The final training objective combines the GRPO and DW-SDPO losses, normalized by the total number of tokens routed to each branch:

$L_{final} = \frac{\sum_{i,t} z_{GRPO,i} \ell_{GRPO,i,t} + \sum_{i,t} z_{SDPO,i} \ell_{DW-SDPO,i,t}}{\sum_{i,t} z_{GRPO,i} + \sum_{i,t} z_{SDPO,i}}$

This objective adaptively balances the influence of the two branches: early in training, when failures are common, the SDPO branch contributes more. As the policy improves, the GRPO branch's influence increases, anchoring the updates to the reward objective.

**E. Experimental Setup**

- **Models**: Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B instruct-tuned base models.
- **Datasets**: Five benchmarks: Chemistry, Physics, Biology, Materials (science question-answering from SciKnowEval), and Tool Use (tool-calling from ToolAlpaca). Train-test splits follow SDPO protocol.
- **Baselines**: Strengthened GRPO (with asymmetric clipping, unbiased advantage normalization, off-policy correction) and SDPO (using successful sibling rollouts as teacher information).
- **Hyperparameters**: Inherited most hyperparameters from SDPO and GRPO baselines. SRPO uses a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-6}$ and a dynamic-weighting temperature $\beta=1$. Training is on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that SRPO achieves improved early training efficiency, enhanced long-horizon stability, and a higher peak performance compared to both GRPO and SDPO.

**A. Performance on Benchmarks**
- **Overall Performance**: On Qwen3-8B, SRPO increased the 10-hour average accuracy across five benchmarks to 77.4%, surpassing GRPO (74.0%) by 3.4 percentage points and SDPO (71.1%) by 6.3 percentage points. On Qwen3-4B, SRPO achieved 74.2%, outperforming GRPO (69.7%) by 4.5 points and SDPO (66.7%) by 7.5 points.
- **Training Dynamics**:
 - SDPO exhibited rapid early improvement but saturated early, with 5h and 10h averages being identical, and often experienced performance degradation or collapse in later stages (e.g., Tool Use benchmark).
 - GRPO showed more steady improvement but eventually plateaued.
 - SRPO demonstrated a balance, matching SDPO's rapid early gains while maintaining stable improvement over longer horizons, ultimately achieving higher peak performance. For instance, on Chemistry with Qwen3-8B, SRPO reached 83.0% at 10h, exceeding SDPO (80.6%) and GRPO (78.9%).
- **Adaptive Behavior**: SRPO effectively exploits self-distillation when beneficial (early training, incorrect samples) and relies on GRPO's reward-aligned reinforcement when self-distillation is less effective or ambiguous (late training, correct samples), preventing drift.

**B. Ablation Study**
- **Sample Routing vs. Advantage-Level Mixing**: An "Advantage Mix" control (linear combination of GRPO and SDPO advantages) was compared against SRPO without dynamic weighting. Advantage Mix performed slightly better at 1h (+0.7 percentage points) but significantly fell behind SRPO w/o dynamic weighting by 2.5 points at 5h and 3.3 points at 10h. This suggests that mixing signals at the advantage level propagates noise from degrading SDPO signals into the learning process in later stages, whereas sample routing reduces this interference by confining SDPO to failed samples.
- **Effect of Dynamic Weighting**: Adding dynamic weighting to SRPO improved average performance by 0.4 points at 1h, 0.7 points at 5h, and 1.8 points at 10h. This widening gain indicates that entropy-aware weighting is increasingly beneficial as the self-teacher signal becomes noisier in later training, by emphasizing confident corrections and suppressing uncertain targets.

**C. Response Length and Compute Time**
- **Response Length**: GRPO produced the longest responses, SDPO the shortest, while SRPO yielded moderate response lengths. This moderate length avoids the verbosity of GRPO (which can inflate inference costs) and the excessive brevity of SDPO (linked to degraded epistemic reasoning).
- **Per-Step Compute Time**:
 - At 1h, SRPO incurred a 17.4% overhead relative to GRPO (83.4s vs. 71.0s per step), but was slightly faster than SDPO (83.4s vs. 85.9s).
 - Over longer training horizons, SRPO's compute cost decreased. At 5h, it was 4.9% faster than GRPO and 6.7% faster than SDPO (78.3s vs. 82.4s and 83.9s).
 - At 10h, SRPO demonstrated a 17.2% advantage over GRPO and 9.4% over SDPO (75.8s vs. 91.5s and 83.7s).
- **Explanation for Compute Time Trend**: Early in training, the higher frequency of failed samples means the SDPO branch (with its additional self-teacher log-probability computation) is more active. As training progresses and the policy improves, fewer samples are routed to the SDPO branch, reducing this overhead. Additionally, SRPO's shorter response lengths compared to GRPO contribute to overall computational efficiency.

**D. Routing Statistics Over Training**
- Initial training showed approximately 40% of samples routed to SDPO and 60% to GRPO. As training advanced, the fraction of samples routed to SDPO steadily decreased while the GRPO fraction increased. This empirically supports the adaptive mixing behavior, where SDPO's contribution diminishes as the policy matures and generates more correct rollouts, demonstrating the framework's ability to self-modulate the influence of self-distillation. The fraction of samples with constructible teacher information remained high, indicating that the shift in routing was primarily due to increasing correctness, not teacher unavailability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance of SRPO lies in its contribution to the robustness and efficiency of post-training large language models using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). By systematically identifying and addressing the intrinsic limitations of both Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO), SRPO offers a unified framework that combines their complementary advantages.

**Key aspects of its significance include:**

- **Improved Performance and Stability**: SRPO consistently achieves higher peak performance compared to state-of-art baselines like GRPO and SDPO, while also demonstrating both rapid early improvement and long-horizon stability. This addresses a critical trade-off in current RLVR methods, where fast initial gains often come at the cost of later instability.
- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency**: By routing samples to the most appropriate optimization signal (dense logit-level correction for failed samples, reward-aligned reinforcement for correct ones), SRPO optimizes learning from each interaction, potentially leading to more efficient use of training data and computational resources to reach a target performance level.
- **Robustness to Teacher Signal Degradation**: The introduction of entropy-aware dynamic weighting in the self-distillation branch is significant. It demonstrates a method for maintaining the utility of self-distillation even as the self-teacher's signal becomes noisier or less informative, extending the effective training horizon of distillation-based approaches.
- **Balanced Response Generation**: SRPO's ability to yield moderate response lengths, intermediate between the verbosity of GRPO and the brevity of SDPO, is impactful. This can mitigate issues such as inflated inference costs from overly long responses and potential degradation of reasoning capability associated with excessively short, less epistemic outputs.
- **Computational Efficiency**: The observed reduction in per-step compute time over longer training horizons implies a more resource-efficient approach for achieving high-performance LLMs, especially as the model improves and the overhead of self-distillation diminishes.
- **Generalizable Framework**: The design principles of sample routing and adaptive weighting could be extended to other RLVR settings or environments with richer feedback, offering a versatile approach for combining different forms of supervision.

The potential impact of this work includes the development of more robust, efficient, and higher-performing LLMs in domains requiring complex reasoning and problem-solving, such as scientific question-answering and tool use. By providing a method that overcomes the inherent trade-offs between stability and fine-grained guidance, SRPO could facilitate broader adoption and more effective application of RL-based post-training for language models.
