# Recovery RL: Safe Reinforcement Learning with Learned Recovery Zones

> [!summary] Summary
> Recovery RL presents a safe reinforcement learning algorithm featuring a dual-policy architecture for task and recovery and leveraging offline data for learned recovery zones. It achieves a 2 to 20 times more efficient trade-off between task successes and constraint violations in simulations and 3 times greater efficiency on a physical robot, outperforming prior methods.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces safety challenges for real-world deployment in robotics due to extensive exploration leading to unsafe behaviors.
- Existing safe RL methods often optimize task performance and safety jointly, which can lead to suboptimality due to conflicting objectives.
- Learning about constraints typically requires numerous online constraint violations, which are costly, time-consuming, and impractical in real-world robotic systems.

## Method

- A ==dual-policy architecture== employs a ==task policy== for reward maximization and a separate ==recovery policy== that activates to prevent constraint violations when the risk is high.
- ==Offline data== containing constraint violations are used to pre-train a ==safety critic== and the recovery policy, defining ==recovery zones== and reducing the need for costly online unsafe exploration.
- The task policy's training uses an ==action relabeling== mechanism, where it learns from its *proposed* actions even when the recovery policy executes a different action to maintain safety.

## Results

- **Recovery RL** demonstrated **2× to 20×** better efficiency in balancing task successes and constraint violations in simulation, and **3×** greater efficiency on a physical robot compared to baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both **offline pre-training** of the safety components and the **action relabeling** mechanism for task policy updates are critical for the algorithm's performance.
- The approach successfully applied to complex, high-dimensional visuomotor tasks, including **contact-rich manipulation** and **image-based navigation** on a physical dVRK robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling task and safety objectives into distinct policies (task and recovery) avoids inherent conflicts in joint optimization, leading to more efficient learning and safer exploration.
> - Pre-training safety mechanisms with offline data significantly reduces the necessity for costly online constraint violations, making real-world RL deployment more practical.
> - Action relabeling for the task policy allows it to learn effectively as if operating in a 'safer' environment, improving its ability to maximize reward under safety constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in acquiring new skills across various robotic domains, including navigation, locomotion, and manipulation. However, a primary challenge hindering its widespread deployment in real-world applications is safety. Learning new tasks often necessitates extensive exploration within uncertain environments, which can lead to suboptimal or unsafe behaviors. Such behaviors can result in damage to the robot or its surroundings, create a mess, or interrupt the learning process, requiring human intervention to rectify the situation. This creates a fundamental trade-off: exploration is necessary for learning, but safety requires limiting exploration.

Prior research in safe RL has approached this challenge through several strategies. One common method involves integrating constraint satisfaction directly into the task objective, often by modifying policy optimization procedures using techniques like trust regions, Lagrangian relaxation, or Lyapunov functions. While these approaches offer generality, they can introduce suboptimality in the learned task policy due to the inherent conflict between maximizing task reward and satisfying constraints. Additionally, learning about constraints through environmental interaction often requires a significant number of constraint violations during the learning phase, which is undesirable in real-world scenarios.

Another avenue of research involves explicitly restricting policy exploration to a safe subset of the Markov Decision Process (MDP) using an auxiliary policy or shielding mechanism. Methods in this category often rely on approximate knowledge of system dynamics or precise a priori knowledge of constraints, which can be challenging or impossible to obtain in high-dimensional observation spaces like images. Some work also explores the use of reset policies or hand-designed safety controllers activated under specific conditions. Furthermore, leveraging demonstrations, particularly constraint-satisfying examples, has been explored to enable safer exploration, often within model predictive control (MPC) frameworks that require dynamics models.

This work contributes to the latter category by proposing an algorithm that designs a recovery mechanism for safe exploration, but differs from prior work in two main aspects: its ability to leverage offline data to learn about constraints before online interaction and its architectural separation of task and safety objectives into distinct policies. This addresses the limitations of joint optimization and the necessity for extensive online constraint violations seen in other approaches, while extending applicability to high-dimensional and unknown dynamics settings where precise analytical safety guarantees are difficult to establish.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a safe reinforcement learning algorithm that effectively navigates the trade-off between task-driven exploration and constraint satisfaction, particularly in scenarios where unconstrained exploration can lead to undesirable outcomes. The authors identify two primary limitations in existing safe RL approaches that motivate their work:

1. **Inherent Objective Conflict in Joint Optimization:** Many prior safe RL methods attempt to simultaneously optimize for task performance and constraint satisfaction within a single policy optimization framework. This joint optimization can lead to suboptimality because the goals of exploring for task improvement and restricting exploration for safety are often conflicting. The single policy must balance these objectives, which can compromise its ability to excel at either. The motivation is to overcome this conflict by disentangling these objectives, allowing the task policy to focus solely on reward maximization and a separate mechanism to handle safety.

2. **Requirement for Extensive Online Constraint Violations:** Learning about system dynamics and the states leading to constraint violations, especially from high-dimensional observations, often requires the agent to experience numerous unsafe situations during online learning. In real-world robotic applications, such violations can cause damage, create messes, and necessitate laborious human intervention to reset the environment, thereby bottlenecking the learning process. The motivation is to reduce the reliance on costly online violations by allowing the agent to learn about potential hazards from prior, potentially human-supervised, or previously collected offline data. This pre-training can inform the agent about dangerous zones without directly experiencing uncontrolled incidents.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a novel algorithm, Recovery RL, with two key algorithmic ideas:

1. **Dual-Policy Architecture:** The algorithm employs two distinct policies: a *task policy* ($\pi_{\text{task}}$) that focuses solely on maximizing the task reward without direct consideration for safety, and a *recovery policy* ($\pi_{\text{rec}}$) that takes control when the task policy is likely to lead to constraint violations. This separation enables each policy to specialize in its respective objective, aiming to prevent the suboptimality that can arise from jointly optimizing conflicting goals. The recovery policy effectively defines a "safer" MDP for the task policy to explore.

2. **Leveraging Offline Data for Learned Recovery Zones:** Recovery RL utilizes a set of offline data, which may contain examples of desired behaviors or past constraint violations, to pre-train a *recovery set* and the *recovery policy*. The recovery set identifies regions in the MDP where future constraint violations are probable. This offline pre-training allows the agent to learn about potential dangers and how to recover from them before extensive online interaction, thereby reducing the number of uncontrolled violations during actual learning. Both the recovery set and policy are then refined online with new experiences.

By pursuing these objectives, Recovery RL aims to achieve a more efficient trade-off between safety and task performance, enabling safer and more practical deployment of RL agents in real-world robotic systems, particularly those with complex dynamics and visual observations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Recovery RL addresses the problem of constrained reinforcement learning within the framework of a Markov Decision Process (MDP) augmented with a binary constraint cost function $C(S)$, which indicates whether a state is constraint-violating. Episodes terminate upon a constraint violation. The objective is to find an optimal policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes expected return $R_\pi$ while ensuring the expected discounted probability of constraint violation $Q^{\pi}_{\text{risk}}(s_0, a_0)$ remains below a predefined threshold $\epsilon_{\text{risk}}$.

The core of Recovery RL is a composite policy ($\pi$) constructed from two sub-policies and a safety critic: a task policy ($\pi_{\text{task}}$) for maximizing rewards, a recovery policy ($\pi_{\text{rec}}$) for ensuring safety, and a safety critic ($\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$) to estimate future risks.

**A. Training a Safety Critic:**
Recovery RL learns a safety critic $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}(s_t, a_t)$, parameterized by $\phi$, which estimates the discounted future probability of constraint violation under the current composite policy $\pi$. This critic is trained by minimizing a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss based on a modified Bellman equation for constraint costs:
$\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}(s_t, a_t) = c_t + (1 - c_t)\gamma_{\text{risk}} E_{\pi}[\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1})|s_t, a_t]$. Here, $c_t=1$ if $s_t$ is violating, and $0$ otherwise, and $\gamma_{\text{risk}}$ is a discount factor for risk. A target network is used to stabilize training.

**B. Defining a Recovery Set and Policy:**
The learned safety critic $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$ is used to define a *safe set* ($T^{\pi}_{\text{safe}}$) and a *recovery set* ($T^{\pi}_{\text{rec}}$). A state-action pair $(s, a)$ is considered safe if $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}(s, a) \le \epsilon_{\text{risk}}$. Otherwise, it belongs to the recovery set.

The composite policy $\pi$ determines actions as follows:
If the action $a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t$ proposed by the task policy $\pi_{\text{task}}$ at state $s_t$ is within the safe set (i.e., $(s_t, a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t) \in T^{\pi}_{\text{safe}}$), then $a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t$ is executed.
If the proposed action $a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t$ is within the recovery set (i.e., $(s_t, a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t) \in T^{\pi}_{\text{rec}}$), then the recovery policy $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ is activated, and an action $a^{\pi_{\text{rec}}}_t$ sampled from $\pi_{\text{rec}}( \cdot |s_t)$ is executed.

The recovery policy $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ is also an RL agent, trained to minimize $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}(s, a)$, effectively guiding the agent back to regions where the risk of constraint violation is below $\epsilon_{\text{risk}}$.

**C. Action Relabeling for Task Policy Training:**
A critical aspect of Recovery RL is how $\pi_{\text{task}}$ is updated. When $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ takes control, the observed transition $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}, r_t)$ would typically be used to update $\pi_{\text{task}}$. However, $a_t$ in this case is $a^{\pi_{\text{rec}}}_t$, not $a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t$. To ensure $\pi_{\text{task}}$ learns effectively within the "safer" environment implicitly defined by the recovery mechanism, Recovery RL relabels the action for $\pi_{\text{task}}$'s training. The task policy is updated with the transition $(s_t, a^{\pi_{\text{task}}}_t, s_{t+1}, r_t)$, even if $a^{\pi_{\text{rec}}}_t$ was the action executed in the environment. This means $\pi_{\text{task}}$ effectively learns from the outcomes of its *intended* actions as if the environment had been modified by the safety shield.

**D. Offline Pretraining:**
Before any online interaction, Recovery RL leverages an offline dataset ($D_{\text{offline}}$) containing examples of constraint violations. This data, which can be collected under human supervision or from prior robotic experience, allows the agent to learn about constraints without incurring numerous online violations.
- The safety critic $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$ is pre-trained by minimizing the MSE loss (Equation IV.2) on batches sampled from $D_{\text{offline}}$.
- The recovery policy $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ is also pre-trained using $D_{\text{offline}}$.
Following pretraining, all three components ($\pi_{\text{task}}$, $\pi_{\text{rec}}$, and $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$) are updated online using experience collected by the composite policy $\pi$.

**E. Practical Implementation:**
- **Recovery Policy ($\pi_{\text{rec}}$):** Both model-free and model-based approaches are explored.
 - *Model-free recovery:* Implemented by performing gradient descent on $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}(s, \pi_{\text{rec}}(s))$, similar to the DDPG algorithm, to minimize the risk estimate.
 - *Model-based recovery:* Utilizes Model Predictive Control (MPC) over a learned dynamics model $f_\theta$. The MPC objective minimizes the sum of $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$ values over a planning horizon. For lower-dimensional tasks, the PETS algorithm (probabilistic ensemble of neural networks) is used. For visual observations, a VAE-based latent dynamics model is employed.
- **Task Policy ($\pi_{\text{task}}$):** The maximum entropy RL algorithm Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is used. In some challenging environments, $\pi_{\text{task}}$ is also initialized with a small set of task-specific demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Recovery RL was evaluated across six simulation domains and one physical robot experiment, comparing its performance against five prior safe RL methods: Unconstrained, Lagrangian Relaxation (LR), Safety Q-Functions for RL (SQRL), Risk Sensitive Policy Optimization (RSPO), Reward Penalty (RP), and Critic Penalty Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO).

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
The simulation domains included three 2D navigation tasks (Navigation 1, Navigation 2, Maze), two contact-rich manipulation tasks (Object Extraction, Object Extraction (Dynamic Obstacle)), and one image-based visual navigation task (Image Maze). The primary evaluation metric was the ratio of cumulative task successes to cumulative constraint violations.

- **Overall Performance:** Recovery RL, with both model-free and model-based recovery policies, generally outperformed comparison algorithms. For 2D navigation and visual navigation tasks, Recovery RL significantly outscored prior methods.
- **Manipulation Tasks:** In the Object Extraction environments, Recovery RL with a *model-based* recovery policy demonstrated significantly superior performance compared to all prior algorithms. Recovery RL with a model-free recovery mechanism, however, did not perform as well in these complex tasks. This suggests that for contact-rich or higher-dimensional tasks, the explicit planning capability of model-based recovery might better compensate for potential inaccuracies in the safety critic $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$.
- **Trade-off Efficiency:** Results indicated that Recovery RL achieved a more efficient trade-off between task successes and constraint violations, demonstrating 2 to 20 times better efficiency than the next best prior method in simulation domains. Comparison methods often achieved similar task completion rates but incurred substantially more constraint violations. Analysis of cumulative successes and violations (Figures 8, 9 in supplementary material) showed that Recovery RL often attained comparable or higher success rates while consistently exhibiting fewer violations. The reward learning curves (Figure 10 in supplementary material) suggested that Recovery RL converged to higher quality solutions more quickly, conditional on not violating constraints, indicating more efficient learning.

**B. Physical Robot Experiment:**
Recovery RL was tested on an image-based obstacle avoidance task using the da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK). The robot's end-effector needed to reach a target position without touching red 3D printed obstacles, using overhead RGB image observations.

- **Performance:** Recovery RL substantially outperformed the Unconstrained and LR baselines, demonstrating approximately 3 times more efficient trade-off between successes and violations compared to the next best prior algorithm. This finding suggests the applicability of Recovery RL to visuomotor control tasks on physical robots in real-world settings.
- **Constraint Violations (Physical Robot):** Supplementary results (Figure 11, 12) further highlighted that Recovery RL achieved a better balance of higher successes and lower violations compared to the baselines on the physical system, while also learning more efficiently in terms of cumulative reward.

**C. Ablation Studies:**
Experiments on the Object Extraction task investigated the contribution of Recovery RL's individual components:

- **Offline Pretraining:** Removing offline pretraining for $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ and $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$ led to significantly worse performance, underscoring the importance of learning about safety before interacting with the environment.
- **Online Updates:** While offline pretraining was critical, removing online updates for $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ and $\hat{Q}^{\pi}_{\phi, \text{risk}}$ resulted in a smaller performance reduction, indicating that the initial learning from offline data provides a robust foundation.
- **Action Relabeling:** The action relabeling mechanism for training $\pi_{\text{task}}$ (where $\pi_{\text{task}}$ is updated with its *proposed* action even if $\pi_{\text{rec}}$ executed a different action) was found to be crucial. Without it, Recovery RL's performance deteriorated significantly, with very few task successes.
- **Offline Data Quantity:** Recovery RL maintained good performance even with a relatively small offline dataset (1000 transitions) for the Object Extraction task, with performance degrading notably only when the dataset size was reduced below this threshold.

**D. Sensitivity Experiments:**
The study also explored the sensitivity of Recovery RL and comparison algorithms to hyperparameter choices. Recovery RL exhibited less sensitivity to variations in $\gamma_{\text{risk}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{risk}}$ compared to baselines like RP, RCPO, and LR, which were highly sensitive to the penalty parameter $\lambda$.

**E. Analysis of Violations:**
An analysis of constraint violations (Table I in supplementary material) revealed that in most tasks, violations occurred even when the recovery policy had been activated. This suggests that the safety critic was generally accurate in predicting future violations, but the recovery policy was not always able to prevent them, potentially due to the complexity of recovery maneuvers or approximation errors. This finding indicates that Recovery RL could potentially be used to identify situations requiring human intervention.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Recovery RL holds significance for the field of safe reinforcement learning, particularly for real-world robotic applications. The proposed algorithmic framework addresses several critical challenges that have limited the practical deployment of RL.

**Improved Safety and Learning Efficiency:** By separating the objectives of task performance and constraint satisfaction into distinct task and recovery policies, Recovery RL demonstrates a more efficient trade-off between learning new skills and maintaining safety. The empirical results, showing 2-20 times greater efficiency in simulation and 3 times greater efficiency on a physical robot compared to strong baselines, suggest a notable advancement in balancing these often conflicting goals. This efficiency means robots can potentially learn complex tasks with fewer hazardous incidents and less human supervision, accelerating the development cycle for autonomous systems.

**Leveraging Offline Data for Proactive Safety:** The ability to pre-train the safety critic and recovery policy using offline data of constraint violations is a key innovation. This allows the system to learn about dangerous states and recovery strategies before extensive online interaction, mitigating the risk of costly and time-consuming real-world damage or disruptions. This is particularly impactful for high-cost or delicate robotic systems where uncontrolled exploration is prohibitive. The finding that Recovery RL performs well even with relatively small offline datasets further enhances its practical applicability.

**Robustness and Applicability to Complex Domains:** The successful application of Recovery RL to contact-rich manipulation and image-based visual navigation tasks, including experiments on a physical robotic platform (dVRK), indicates its robustness and scalability to complex, high-dimensional state spaces where exact dynamics models or precise constraint specifications are often unavailable. The observation that model-based recovery can be particularly effective in these challenging environments highlights the potential for combining learning-based safety with predictive control paradigms.

**Insights into Safety Critic Performance:** The analysis showing that violations often occur even when the recovery policy is activated provides valuable insight into the safety critic's predictive capabilities. It suggests that the system can often foresee impending danger, even if recovery actions are not always successful. This insight could lead to future developments, such as using Recovery RL as a mechanism to signal the need for human intervention or to further refine recovery strategies in situations identified as high-risk but difficult to navigate autonomously.

**Potential for Broader Impact:** Recovery RL's architectural elegance in decoupling task and safety concerns, combined with its demonstrated empirical performance, could pave the way for more widespread adoption of RL in safety-critical domains such as industrial automation, autonomous driving, and medical robotics. Future work could focus on establishing formal safety guarantees for this framework, exploring its utility in scenarios with non-catastrophic constraint violations, and applying it to large-scale robot learning problems. The research contributes to building more reliable and trustworthy autonomous agents that can learn and operate safely in unpredictable real-world environments.
