# Robustness-Based Synthesis for Time Window Temporal Logic Specifications via Mixed-Integer Linear Programming

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Worcester Polytechnic Institute developed the first Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) based framework for synthesizing controllers from Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL) specifications by optimizing a quantitative robustness measure. Their approach, which includes a task-adaptive Model Predictive Control (MPC) strategy, demonstrated increased computational efficiency over STL-based methods and successful adaptation to disturbances in closed-loop control.

## Problem

- Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL) lacked a quantitative robustness measure and a direct optimization-based synthesis procedure, limiting its application in robust control.
- Synthesizing control policies for complex sequential tasks using temporal logics often leads to computationally expensive optimization problems, particularly for Model Predictive Control (MPC).
- Cyber-physical systems require robust control policies that can adhere to complex spatio-temporal constraints and adapt to disturbances in real-time.

## Method

- Formulated the problem of synthesizing control inputs for discrete-time linear systems to satisfy ==Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL)== specifications by encoding its ==min-max-based robustness (ρ)== as a ==Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP)==.
- Developed both an ==open-loop trajectory optimization== and a closed-loop ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== framework, where the latter re-solves the MILP at each step.
- Implemented a ==task-adaptive prediction horizon== within MPC, utilizing the TWTL's ==Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA)== to dynamically adjust the optimization window, alongside a ==warm-start strategy== for improved online computational efficiency.

## Results

- The Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) encoding for TWTL robustness was proven correct (**Theorem 4.1**), ensuring that any feasible solution with positive robustness guarantees specification satisfaction.
- Direct TWTL MILP encoding demonstrated faster solve times for specifications with **four or more** sequential subtasks compared to translating TWTL into STL and then solving the STL-based MILP.
- The developed Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework, utilizing a task-adaptive horizon, successfully handled unpredicted disturbances and significantly reduced total MILP solve time over a run compared to a fixed-horizon MPC strategy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The quantitative min-max robustness for TWTL specifications can be formally encoded into a solvable Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP), where a positive robustness value guarantees Boolean satisfaction.
> - Directly encoding TWTL for control synthesis offers computational advantages over translating TWTL into other temporal logics like STL, particularly for specifications involving multiple sequential tasks.
> - The Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA) associated with TWTL enables a task-adaptive prediction horizon in Model Predictive Control, allowing the optimization problem size to dynamically shrink as tasks are completed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of cyber-physical systems (CPS) frequently involves complex tasks requiring adherence to spatio-temporal constraints. Examples include autonomous robots executing sequential tasks within specific timeframes or surveillance drones monitoring regions for defined durations. To formally specify such high-level tasks, temporal logics (TLs) have become a standard tool.

Among concrete-time temporal logics, Signal Temporal Logic (STL) and Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) are widely used. Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL) emerged more recently as an alternative designed to compactly express sequential tasks using a dedicated concatenation operator. A notable characteristic of TWTL is that the complexity of its automaton translations is independent of the magnitude of the time bounds, making it advantageous for automata-based planning and synthesis.

Synthesizing control policies from TL specifications typically involves an optimization-based approach, which is facilitated by a quantitative semantics, or "robustness measure," that quantifies Boolean satisfaction as a real value. This measure allows for optimizing the satisfaction margin (e.g., distance from obstacles) alongside other objectives (e.g., path length, energy consumption), and naturally supports Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulations. For STL, the classical robustness measure has been encoded as Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) constraints, leading to receding-horizon MPC frameworks where the MILP is re-solved at each step with a fixed prediction horizon.

Prior to the work described in this paper, TWTL lacked a dedicated robustness measure, which hindered its application in robustness-optimizing synthesis. A recent development, by some of the current authors, introduced two quantitative semantics for TWTL—a min-max-based robustness (ρ) and an Arithmetic Geometric Mean (AGM) robustness—along with runtime monitors. While TWTL has been used for monitoring or as a basis for sampling-based planning, a direct synthesis procedure to optimize its robustness measure was absent. This paper addresses that gap by leveraging the previously introduced ρ robustness for TWTL to develop an MILP-based synthesis procedure.

The work differentiates itself from other TWTL applications by focusing on direct synthesis using system dynamics models rather than probabilistic or sampling-based planning methods. It also extends existing MILP encoding approaches for STL, adapting them to TWTL's distinct operators while introducing enhancements for MPC, particularly concerning prediction horizon management not typically available in general STL contexts without explicit automaton structure.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, optimization-based synthesis procedure for Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL) specifications applied to discrete-time linear systems. This involves leveraging a recently introduced quantitative semantics (robustness, ρ) for TWTL to formulate control synthesis as a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP).

The motivation for this work stems from several key areas:

- **Compact Specification of Complex Tasks:** Robot tasks in cyber-physical systems often involve sequential actions with explicit timing constraints, such as a drone dwelling in multiple regions or a multi-robot team completing sub-tasks in a specific order and time window. TWTL provides a compact and expressive language for encoding these high-level spatio-temporal specifications, making it a suitable candidate for formal methods in control.

- **Need for Quantitative Satisfaction (Robustness):** Boolean satisfaction (either a task is met or not) is often insufficient for real-world applications where noise, disturbances, and model uncertainties are present. A quantitative measure, or robustness degree, allows for optimizing a system's adherence to specifications. Maximizing robustness enables the system to maintain a margin of satisfaction, making plans more resilient to real-world imperfections and disturbances. This quantitative approach also allows for trade-offs with other performance objectives, such as energy consumption or path length.

- **Enabling Optimization-Based Synthesis:** The existence of a robustness measure allows the synthesis problem to be cast as an optimization problem. For linear systems and specific forms of predicate functions, this translates into a Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP), which can be solved using established solvers to find control inputs that maximize the robustness degree. Before this work, such an MILP-based synthesis procedure for TWTL, optimizing its robustness, was unavailable.

- **Closed-Loop Control and Disturbance Rejection:** Open-loop control sequences, while potentially optimal for a fixed initial state, cannot react to unforeseen disturbances or model inaccuracies during execution. This necessitates a closed-loop control strategy. Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a suitable framework, as it repeatedly re-solves an optimization problem based on the current system state, allowing for online adaptation and disturbance rejection. The development of a TWTL-based MPC formulation is thus a critical objective.

- **Computational Efficiency for MPC:** A significant challenge in MPC for temporal logic specifications is the computational cost of repeatedly solving large optimization problems. For logics like STL, fixed prediction horizons can lead to large, computationally expensive MILPs. A motivation for this work is to develop an MPC strategy that leverages TWTL's inherent structure, specifically its Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA), to reduce the computational burden per step. This includes the development of a "task-adaptive horizon" and a warm-start strategy to make the MPC more tractable for real-time applications.

- **Comparison with Existing Logics:** The work also seeks to demonstrate the potential efficiency advantages of directly encoding TWTL specifications in MILPs compared to translating TWTL into other logics like STL and then encoding those. This highlights the benefits of TWTL's specific structural properties.

In summary, the authors aim to introduce the first comprehensive framework for robustness-maximizing control synthesis for TWTL, addressing both open-loop and closed-loop scenarios, while focusing on computational efficiency for practical implementation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper integrates the quantitative semantics of Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL) with Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) to synthesize control inputs for discrete-time linear systems. The approach covers both open-loop and closed-loop (Model Predictive Control - MPC) formulations.

#### 3.1. Dynamical System Model

The system considered is a discrete-time Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) system: $x_{t+1} = Ax_t + Bu_t$, with observable outputs $o_t = Cx_t$. The state $x_t$ and control input $u_t$ are constrained to convex polytopes $X$ and $U$, respectively. Atomic propositions ($\pi_A$) in the TWTL specification are associated with satisfaction regions $A \subseteq O$, defined by affine predicate functions $h(o) = c_A^T o - b_A$. This assumption simplifies the encoding of propositional satisfaction into linear inequalities.

#### 3.2. Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL)

The paper utilizes the TWTL syntax, which includes standard Boolean operators ($\land, \lor, \neg$), a hold operator ($H_d s$) for repeated satisfaction over a duration $d$, a within operator ($[\phi]_{[a,b]}$) to bound satisfaction of $\phi$ to a time window $[a,b]$, and a concatenation operator ($\phi_1 \cdot \phi_2$) to specify sequential satisfaction. A key aspect of TWTL is its translation to a Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA) whose state set size is independent of the magnitude of the time bounds, a property exploited in the MPC formulation. The total time horizon $T$ of a formula $\phi$ (denoted $||\phi||$) is recursively computed.

#### 3.3. TWTL Robustness (ρ)

The core of the synthesis methodology relies on the min-max-based robustness measure $\rho$, previously introduced in. This robustness function is defined recursively for each TWTL operator. For atomic propositions, $\rho(o_t, \pi_A) = h(o_t)$. For $H_d \pi_A$, it is the minimum of $h(o_t)$ over the $d$ time steps. Boolean operators translate to minimum (for $\land$) or maximum (for $\lor$) of subformula robustness values. Negation simply negates the robustness. The within operator involves a maximization over possible start times within its interval, while concatenation involves a maximization over possible split points of an inner minimum of the two subformulae's robustness values. A large negative constant, $\rho_\perp$, is used for unsatisfiable time window constraints. A crucial property is its soundness: $\rho(\text{o}, \phi) > 0 \implies \text{o} \models \phi$, meaning positive robustness guarantees Boolean satisfaction.

#### 3.4. MILP Encoding of TWTL Robustness

The paper's central methodological contribution is encoding the recursive definition of $\rho$ into a set of Mixed-Integer Linear (MIL) constraints. For each sub-formula $\psi$ and time interval $[t_1, t_2]$, a continuous variable $r(\psi, t_1, t_2)$ is introduced to represent its robustness. The objective of the MILP is to maximize the top-level robustness $r(\phi, 0, T)$. The encoding for each operator involves:

- **Predicate:** Direct equality $r(\pi_A, t) = h(o_t)$. No binary variables.
- **Hold:** A set of linear inequalities $r(H_d \pi_A, t_1, t_2) \le r(\pi_A, t_1+k)$ for $k \in [0,d]$. No binary variables.
- **Negation:** An equality $r(\neg \phi, t_1, t_2) = -r(\phi, t_1, t_2)$. No binary variables.
- **Conjunction:** A set of linear inequalities $r(\phi_1 \land \phi_2, t_1, t_2) \le r(\phi_i, t_1, t_2)$. No binary variables.
- **Disjunction:** Uses one binary variable ($z_{\lor}$) and "big-M" constraints to select the maximum of subformula robustness values.
- **Within:** Uses $b-a+1$ binary variables ($z_W^t$) and big-M constraints to select the maximizing start time for $\phi$ within the window $[a,b]$.
- **Concatenation:** This is the most complex, requiring $2 \times (t_2 - t_1)$ binary variables ($z_C^t, z_S^t$) and auxiliary variables ($r_C^t$) to encode the inner minimum and outer maximum over split points, again using big-M constraints.
A large positive constant $M$ is used in big-M constraints, which is derived from the bounds of achievable robustness $\rho_\top, \rho_\perp$. The paper proves the correctness of this MILP encoding (Theorem 4.1), demonstrating that any feasible solution with a positive objective value guarantees Boolean satisfaction.

#### 3.5. Synthesis Settings

- **Open-Loop Synthesis (Problem 3.1):** This formulation maximizes $r(\phi, 0, T)$ subject to system dynamics and all TWTL robustness constraints over the entire horizon $T$. It computes the full control sequence $u_0, \dots, u_{T-1}$ once at $t=0$.

- **Receding-Horizon MPC Synthesis (Problem 3.2):** This closed-loop formulation addresses the limitations of open-loop control by re-solving an MILP at each time step $t$.
 - **Residual Formula (Definition 5.1):** At each step $t$, the current DFA state $q_t$ determines the "residual formula" $\phi_t$, representing the part of the original specification yet to be satisfied. For typical sequential TWTL formulas, this means identifying the active sub-task. Extracting $\phi_t$ is an $O(k)$ operation, where $k$ is the number of sub-tasks.
 - **Task-Adaptive Horizon (Definition 5.2):** Instead of a fixed horizon $T$, MPC uses a task-adaptive horizon $H_t$. This horizon is set to $\min(b_i - (t-t_s^i), T-t)$, where $b_i$ is the original end of the current task window, $t_s^i$ is its start time, and $T$ is the overall formula horizon. This typically results in a much smaller prediction horizon compared to fixed-horizon approaches, resetting at each task transition. This feature is enabled by TWTL's DFA structure.
 - **Warm-Start Strategy:** To reduce the online computational cost, the MILP is "warm-started."
 - **Offline Precomputation:** Parametric MILP constraint matrices are precomputed for each DFA state $q \in Q$, as they depend on $\phi_t$ and $H_t$ but not $x_t$.
 - **Online Update:** At each step, the initial state $x_t$ is updated ($O(n)$ cost), the prediction window shrinkage is handled by fixing binary variables for expired time steps ($O(1)$ cost), and task transitions involve swapping to the precomputed MILP matrices for the new DFA state ($O(n)$ RHS update cost). Modern MILP solvers efficiently exploit warm starts.

#### 3.6. MPC Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

The paper details an algorithm for the task-adaptive receding-horizon MPC. It initializes the DFA and warm-start solution. In each step, it identifies the active task, computes the task-adaptive horizon, loads the relevant precomputed MILP, updates it with the current state and window, solves the MILP (warm-starting), applies the first control input, and updates the DFA state. If the MILP becomes infeasible or robustness falls below zero, the process stops. Theorem 5.1 guarantees that if the algorithm consistently finds positive robustness solutions, the overall specification is satisfied.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the robustness-based synthesis for TWTL specifications:

- **Correctness of MILP Encoding for TWTL Robustness:** The paper establishes a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) encoding for the min-max-based robustness (ρ) of TWTL formulas. This encoding is proven correct by structural induction (Theorem 4.1), demonstrating that any feasible solution to the MILP with a positive objective value guarantees Boolean satisfaction of the TWTL specification. This establishes a foundational link between quantitative robustness and Boolean satisfaction within an optimization framework for TWTL.

- **Development of Open-Loop and Closed-Loop Synthesis Frameworks:**
 - **Open-Loop Trajectory Optimization:** An open-loop MILP formulation is presented that optimizes the full control sequence from an initial state to maximize the robustness of the TWTL specification. This provides a baseline for control synthesis.
 - **Receding-Horizon MPC:** A closed-loop, receding-horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework is developed. This framework re-solves an MILP at each time step using the current measured state, enabling reactivity to disturbances.

- **Efficiency Benefits of Direct TWTL Encoding vs. STL Translation:**
 - A comparative analysis (Fig. 2) demonstrates that directly encoding TWTL specifications into MILP for robustness optimization generally performs more efficiently than translating TWTL specifications into semantically equivalent STL and then solving the resulting STL-based MILP.
 - For a smaller number of concatenated tasks, the solve times for both approaches were comparable, with STL sometimes exhibiting slightly faster performance. However, for four or more sequential subtasks, the direct TWTL encoding yielded significantly faster solve times. This is attributed to the reduction in the number of binary variables required by the TWTL encoding compared to the translated STL formula, particularly concerning the concatenation operator. The paper notes that the open-loop TWTL MILP has $O(kT)$ binary variables, dominated by concatenations, though future work aims to reduce this to $O(k \log T)$.

- **Effectiveness of Closed-Loop MPC for Disturbance Rejection:**
 - The closed-loop MPC formulation is shown to be effective in handling disturbances. In an experimental setup (Fig. 3), an open-loop controller failed to meet its specification after an unpredicted disturbance, while the MPC-enabled controller successfully adapted its plan and completed all tasks. This highlights the practical importance of the reactive nature of the developed MPC.

- **Performance Advantages of Task-Adaptive Horizon in MPC:**
 - A key feature of the MPC formulation is the "task-adaptive horizon," which exploits the TWTL DFA structure to determine the active sub-task and limit the prediction horizon to the remaining window of that current task, rather than the full formula horizon ($T$).
 - Comparative experiments (Fig. 4) demonstrate that this DFA-based task-adaptive horizon strategy significantly reduces the total MILP solve time over the course of a run compared to a fixed-horizon approach (analogous to previous STL MPC methods). The benefit of the task-adaptive horizon increased as the number of sequential tasks grew. This implies that the MILP solved at each step is generally smaller and therefore quicker to solve.

- **Computational Tractability of MPC Online Overhead:**
 - The MPC approach incorporates a warm-start strategy and leverages offline precomputation of parametric MILP matrices for each DFA state. This strategy contributes to an $O(n)$ online overhead per MPC step (excluding the MILP re-solve itself). This observation indicates that the per-step updates necessary for the MPC loop are computationally efficient, and the re-solve times tend to decrease over the execution horizon as tasks are completed and the remaining problem shrinks.

In summary, the research successfully provides a direct MILP-based synthesis for TWTL, demonstrates its operational advantages over STL translation, and presents an MPC framework with tailored efficiency improvements (task-adaptive horizon, warm-starts) for reactive control in dynamic environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of cyber-physical systems (CPS) and formal methods for control synthesis by establishing a robust, optimization-based framework for Time Window Temporal Logic (TWTL) specifications. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Robust Control Synthesis for TWTL:** This work addresses a previous limitation of TWTL by providing the first MILP-based synthesis procedure that optimizes its quantitative semantics (robustness). This allows engineers and researchers to leverage TWTL's expressive power and compact nature for task specification, coupled with the ability to synthesize control policies that maximize satisfaction margins, leading to more resilient system behaviors in uncertain environments.

- **Bridging a Theoretical and Practical Gap:** By integrating the recently developed robustness measure for TWTL with MILP-based control synthesis, the paper bridges a gap between theoretical advancements in temporal logic semantics and practical applications in optimal control. This makes TWTL a more complete tool for the design and verification of autonomous systems.

- **Enhanced Efficiency for Sequential Task Specifications:** The demonstrated efficiency advantage of direct TWTL MILP encoding over TWTL translated to STL for tasks with multiple concatenated subgoals is significant. For complex, multi-stage missions common in robotics and autonomous systems, TWTL can now offer a computationally more efficient specification and synthesis pathway, potentially enabling more intricate task designs.

- **Improved Model Predictive Control (MPC) for Temporal Logic:** The introduction of a task-adaptive horizon within the MPC framework, which directly exploits the Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA) structure of TWTL, represents a notable advancement. This innovation allows the prediction horizon to be dynamically adjusted based on the current active sub-task, leading to significantly smaller and faster-to-solve MILPs at each control step. This computational efficiency is crucial for real-time and online decision-making in dynamic CPS environments, where fixed-horizon MPC approaches can become intractable.

- **Practical Applicability in Dynamic Environments:** The ability of the closed-loop MPC to adapt and replan in response to disturbances is critical for deploying autonomous systems in real-world scenarios. By consistently maximizing robustness, the synthesized controllers are designed to maintain a safety margin, thereby improving the reliability and safety of operations for applications such as surveillance, exploration, and automated manufacturing.

- **Foundation for Future Research and Development:** This work lays a robust foundation for several future research directions. These include exploring logarithmic encoding techniques to further reduce binary variables in the MILP, extending the framework to optimize other TWTL robustness measures (e.g., Arithmetic Geometric Mean) via different programming paradigms (e.g., MISOCP), incorporating formula rewriting strategies for dynamic simplification, and expanding to multi-agent planning and task allocation problems.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the utility of Time Window Temporal Logic in control synthesis, offering a robust, efficient, and adaptable methodology for designing autonomous systems capable of executing complex spatio-temporal tasks in dynamic and uncertain environments.
