# Do You Know Where Your Camera Is? View-Invariant Policy Learning with Camera Conditioning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at TTIC, Waymo, Johns Hopkins University, and Toyota Research Institute developed a method to improve view-invariant policy learning in robotics by explicitly conditioning policies on camera geometry using per-pixel Plücker embeddings. This approach consistently enhanced robustness and generalization for imitation learning policies across diverse viewpoints, reducing the need for extensive training data.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning policies exhibit brittle performance when camera viewpoints shift from training to deployment, often leading to failure.
- Policies trained with fixed third-person cameras struggle to generalize to diverse perspectives, limiting real-world applicability and cross-embodiment transfer.
- Current methods often force policies to implicitly infer complex camera geometries from RGB images alone, which is data-inefficient and prone to relying on spurious background cues.

## Method

- The research proposes integrating explicit ==camera geometry== into imitation learning policies using per-pixel Plücker embeddings, which define 3D rays for each pixel.
- Plücker embeddings are combined with visual inputs through either "early fusion" (concatenating with raw RGB) or "late fusion" (concatenating with visual latents from pretrained encoders).
- New manipulation tasks with "randomized" background settings were developed to rigorously evaluate policies' true geometric understanding and viewpoint generalization capabilities.

## Results

- Explicit camera conditioning consistently improved success rates (up to **34.8%**) across multiple imitation learning models and simulation tasks, showing superior robustness and generalization in randomized environments where static background cues were absent.
- The approach demonstrated improved data efficiency, requiring significantly fewer training camera views, and its benefits were validated through real-world experiments on a UR5 robot arm, where conditioned policies achieved higher success rates.
- Ablations found Delta End-Effector Pose the best-performing action space, and random cropping applied jointly to RGB and Plücker maps consistently boosted success rates across tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly conditioning robot policies on camera geometry, rather than relying on implicit inference from images, is a more robust and data-efficient approach for viewpoint invariance.
> - Per-pixel Plücker embeddings effectively encode camera extrinsic and intrinsic parameters into a representation compatible with visual inputs.
> - Policies without explicit geometric conditioning often exploit static background cues for implicit pose inference, failing when these cues are randomized.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper positions itself within the vibrant and rapidly evolving fields of imitation learning, robot manipulation, and robust computer vision. The core problem it addresses – viewpoint invariance in robot policies – is a long-standing and critical challenge for deploying robots in unstructured, dynamic, and varied real-world environments.

**Imitation Learning (IL):** The paper builds upon significant recent advancements in imitation learning, particularly the development of high-capacity models like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), Diffusion Policy (DP), and SmolVLA. These models, often leveraging large, diverse datasets (e.g., RT-1, RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA), have shown promising generalization capabilities for complex manipulation tasks. However, a fundamental limitation highlighted by the authors is their typical training setup with fixed third-person viewpoints, leading to deployment failures when camera poses shift.

**View-Invariant Robot Policies:** The quest for policies robust to viewpoint changes is a central theme in robot learning. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized:
- **3D and Depth-Based Methods:** These methods, which lift sensor data into explicit 3D representations (e.g., voxel grids, learned feature fields, point clouds) or synthesize canonical views, offer inherent robustness to camera pose. However, they rely on high-quality depth sensors, which are often expensive and less common than standard RGB cameras.
- **RGB-Only Methods:** This category, which the paper primarily focuses on, is attractive due to the ubiquity and lower cost of RGB cameras and the vast availability of RGB-based datasets. Within RGB-only approaches, two main strategies have emerged:
 - **Implicit Learning via Randomization/Augmentation:** This involves randomizing camera viewpoints during data collection (e.g., DROID, RoboSet) or training, or synthesizing novel views (e.g., using NeRF, 3D Gaussian Splatting). The implicit assumption is that the policy will learn to infer the camera's relative pose from visual cues. The authors identify a key weakness here: if background cues are static, policies might take a "shortcut" by inferring pose from these cues rather than true geometric understanding, leading to failure when backgrounds change.
 - **Implicit Learning via Self-Supervision:** Methods utilizing cross-view correspondence, contrastive learning, or test-time adaptation aim to learn viewpoint-invariant visual features.

**The Paper's Niche:** This work directly contrasts with the implicit learning paradigm by advocating for **explicit conditioning on camera geometry**. The authors argue that neglecting readily available geometric information (camera intrinsics and extrinsics) is a missed opportunity. Given the data-scarce nature of robotics, expecting a single end-to-end model to simultaneously infer complex camera pose *and* learn sophisticated manipulation policies is a significant burden. By explicitly providing this information, the paper aims to decouple these tasks, leading to more data-efficient and robust learning. This approach is novel in its direct application of per-pixel Plücker embeddings to RGB-based robot control policies, bridging the gap between advanced 3D vision techniques and practical robot learning challenges. It extends the concept of encoding camera geometry, previously seen in multi-view transformers and 3D reconstruction, to the domain of visuomotor control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a fundamental challenge in deploying robot policies trained with imitation learning: their brittle performance under viewpoint shifts. Most state-of-the-art imitation learning policies are trained with fixed third-person camera viewpoints. When these policies are deployed in real-world environments, it's often impossible to precisely replicate the training camera poses due to physical constraints or the need for diverse perspectives, leading to significant performance degradation or outright failure. This problem is further compounded in scenarios requiring cross-embodiment transfer, where camera configurations can vary dramatically.

The authors identify a critical opportunity: **accurate camera geometry (intrinsics and extrinsics) is increasingly accessible**. This information can be derived from training datasets, estimated through classical hand-eye calibration, SLAM methods, or modern learning-based techniques. The prevailing practice of neglecting this explicit geometric information, forcing policies to implicitly infer it from RGB images alone, is deemed a "significant missed opportunity."

This observation leads to the paper's core investigative question: **Should an end-to-end robot policy implicitly infer camera geometries during training, or should it explicitly condition on these geometries when they are readily available?**

The authors contend that, while architectural simplicity of end-to-end learning is attractive in data-rich domains, robotics is inherently data-scarce. Asking a single model to simultaneously perform complex camera pose estimation *and* learn intricate manipulation policies imposes a severe trade-off between learnability and data efficiency. Therefore, the **key objective** of this work is to thoroughly investigate the benefits of explicit camera conditioning on the robustness and generalization capabilities of robot policies under viewpoint variations.

Specifically, the paper aims to:
- Propose a **novel camera conditioning method** that effectively integrates explicit camera geometries, via per-pixel Plücker embeddings, into common imitation learning policies.
- Conduct a **comprehensive empirical analysis** to demonstrate the benefits of this approach for viewpoint generalization, identifying factors like action space, data augmentation, encoder pretraining, and data scaling.
- Introduce **new benchmark tasks** in RoboSuite and ManiSkill, designed to rigorously evaluate viewpoint generalization, by creating "fixed" and "randomized" scene variants that decouple background cues from camera pose.

By addressing these objectives, the authors seek to provide a more direct, data-efficient, and robust pathway to view-invariant robot control using readily available geometric information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this paper centers on integrating explicit camera geometry into existing imitation learning policies using a novel conditioning method and evaluating its efficacy through rigorous simulation and real-world experiments.

**A. Plücker Embedding as Camera Conditioning:**
The core of the proposed method is to provide detailed camera geometry information at the pixel level, compatible with visual inputs.
- **Plücker Coordinates:** The authors employ a per-pixel ray-map where each entry is a six-dimensional Plücker coordinate `r = (d, m)`. `d` is a unit direction vector of the ray, and `m = p × d` is the moment vector, where `p` is any point on the ray (e.g., the camera center). This representation uniquely defines an oriented 3D line, is homogeneous, and invariant to the choice of point `p` along the ray, making it robust for representing pixel-aligned rays in 3D space.
- **Computation:** Given camera intrinsic matrix `K` and extrinsics `(R, t)` (rotation and translation), the world-frame ray direction `d_w` for each pixel `(u, v)` is calculated. The camera center `C = -R^T t` is then used to compute the moment vector `m = C × d_w`. This creates a fixed `H × W × 6` Plücker map for each image frame.
- **Integration with Policies:** Two strategies are proposed for integrating this Plücker map:
 - **Policies without pretrained encoders (e.g., Diffusion Policy):** The `H × W × 6` Plücker map is directly concatenated channel-wise with the `H × W × 3` RGB image, forming a `H × W × 9` input. The first convolutional layer of the image encoder is modified to accept this 9-channel input. This is termed "early fusion."
 - **Policies with pretrained encoders (e.g., ACT, SmolVLA):** A small, independent convolutional network processes the Plücker map, encoding it into a latent representation. This Plücker latent is then concatenated channel-wise with the image latent (output of the pretrained visual encoder) before being fed into the policy head. This is termed "late fusion."

**B. Benchmarks and Task Setup:**
To rigorously evaluate viewpoint generalization, the authors introduce six new manipulation tasks:
- **RoboSuite Tasks:** Lift, Pick Place Can, Assembly Square.
- **ManiSkill Tasks:** Push, Lift Upright, Roll Ball.
Each task is designed with two variants:
- **"Fixed" Setting:** The robot, table, and floor are static relative to each other, allowing policies to potentially infer camera pose from background cues.
- **"Randomized" Setting:** The robot's position/orientation and the table's position/orientation are randomized relative to the floor. This crucial variant explicitly decouples background cues from the camera's pose relative to the robot, forcing policies to rely on true geometric understanding.
- **Data Collection Strategy:** A "stair-shaped" data collection method is adopted to promote compositional generalization. Each demonstration episode is recorded with `n` cameras. Crucially, consecutive episodes `i` and `i+1` share `n-m` camera poses, while `m` new camera poses are introduced in episode `i+1`. This ensures exposure to overlapping but evolving viewpoints. (Defaults: `n=3, m=1` for most; `n=4, m=2` for harder tasks).
- **Camera Pose Randomization:** Camera poses are randomized across azimuth (180 degrees), elevation (30-60 degrees), and the 3D point they focus on, creating diverse viewing conditions.

**C. Evaluation Protocol:**
- **Simulation Experiments:** Policies (ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA) are trained with 3 random seeds (except SmolVLA, 1 seed). The last 10 checkpoints from each run are evaluated on 50 distinct initial position-camera pose pairs. This results in 1500 (or 500 for SmolVLA) rollouts per setting, ensuring statistical robustness. Environment random seeds are fixed for fairness across comparisons.
- **Real-World Experiments:** Conducted on a UR5 robot arm with a three-finger gripper across three tasks (Pick Place, Plate Insertion, Hang Mug). 200 demonstrations per task are collected using VR teleoperation. Three articulated cameras are used, with poses estimated by AprilTags, following the `n=3, m=1` randomization strategy. To mimic the "randomized" simulation setting, the background is covered with green cloth to remove static visual cues. Evaluation involves 21 rollouts per setting (7 random poses for each of 3 cameras, using 2/3 cameras per rollout). A "half-success" criterion is introduced to provide more nuanced performance metrics for difficult real-world tasks.

**D. Ablation Studies:**
The authors systematically investigate various architectural and training design choices through ablation studies:
- **Random Cropping:** Evaluates the effect of applying random cropping to both images and Plücker maps simultaneously.
- **Action Space:** Compares performance across Absolute End-Effector Pose, Delta End-Effector Pose, Absolute Joint Position, and Delta Joint Position.
- **Encoding Methods:** Compares linear projection of the extrinsic matrix, early fusion (pixel-wise concatenation), and late fusion (latent concatenation).
- **Pretrained Vision Encoders:** Assesses the impact of no pretraining, R3M weights, and ImageNet pretraining on the image encoder.
- **Scaling:** Studies how the number of training camera views affects performance with and without conditioning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several compelling findings, consistently demonstrating the benefits of explicit camera conditioning for view-invariant imitation learning.

- **Consistent Performance Improvement Across Models and Tasks:** Explicit camera pose conditioning significantly increases success rates across all evaluated imitation learning policies (ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA) and all six simulation tasks (Table I). Improvements ranged from modest (e.g., 0.4% for Diffusion Policy on Assembly Square) to substantial (e.g., 34.8% for SmolVLA on Lift), indicating broad applicability and effectiveness.

- **Robustness to Background Changes:** The "randomized" environment setting, which removes static background cues that policies might exploit for implicit pose inference, clearly highlights the weakness of unconditioned policies. As shown in Figure 6, baseline policies without extrinsics conditioning perform significantly worse in randomized setups. Crucially, conditioning on camera pose restores and often surpasses performance in both fixed and randomized settings, demonstrating true viewpoint generalization decoupled from spurious visual cues.

- **Benefit of Random Cropping:** Applying random cropping to both the image and the corresponding Plücker map consistently improves performance across tasks (Figure 8). This suggests that cropping acts as an effective data augmentation strategy, simulating a greater diversity of camera parameters and views, thereby enhancing generalization.

- **Optimal Action Space:** Policies generally perform best when actions are represented as **Delta End-Effector Pose** (Figure 7). This implies that predicting relative end-effector movements is more conducive to generalization in these tasks. Importantly, camera conditioning boosts performance across all tested action spaces (Absolute/Delta End-Effector Pose, Absolute/Delta Joint Position), underscoring its general utility.

- **Encoding Method: Late Fusion Preferred with Pretrained Encoders:** The ablation study on encoding methods (Figure 9) reveals that "late fusion" (encoding Plücker map and image separately and then concatenating their latents) generally outperforms "early fusion" (pixel-wise concatenation before the visual encoder) for policies employing pretrained encoders. Linear projection of the extrinsic matrix provided no observable benefit. The authors hypothesize that early fusion might put pretrained encoders out-of-distribution, whereas separate processing allows the pretrained encoder to operate on familiar image inputs.

- **Limited Impact of Pretrained Vision Encoders:** Surprisingly, the level of pretraining for the image encoder (no pretraining, R3M, or ImageNet) had little significant effect on the success rate when camera conditioning was applied (Figure 10). This implies that the explicit geometric information provided by the Plücker embedding is the dominant factor for achieving viewpoint invariance, reducing the reliance on visual priors from large-scale image pretraining for inferring camera pose.

- **Improved Data Efficiency and Scaling:** The scaling experiments (Figure 11) demonstrate a critical advantage: explicit camera conditioning drastically reduces the number of unique camera views required during training to achieve a given performance level. For example, policies without conditioning needed "several times more cameras" to match conditioned policies. Even with a large number of cameras, conditioning still provides a small but consistent improvement, highlighting its lasting value.

- **Real-World Validation:** The findings are successfully translated to real-robot experiments (Pick Place, Plate Insertion, Hang Mug) using a UR5 arm (Figure 13). For both ACT and Diffusion Policy, conditioning on camera poses consistently improved both binary success rates and the more nuanced "half-success" metrics, validating the simulation results in a practical, physical setting. The challenges noted for Diffusion Policy (stochasticity in precision tasks) are also an interesting side observation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of viewpoint generalization for imitation learning policies. Its impact can be multifaceted, spanning methodological advancements, practical implications for robot deployment, and guiding future research directions.

- **Methodological Breakthrough in View-Invariant Learning:** The most immediate impact is the introduction of a novel and highly effective method for achieving view-invariant robot control. By leveraging per-pixel Plücker embeddings to explicitly condition RGB-only policies on camera extrinsics, the authors provide a direct and robust mechanism for injecting geometric awareness. This bypasses the limitations of purely implicit learning methods, which often struggle with data efficiency or collapse when environmental cues are removed. It effectively brings sophisticated 3D geometric understanding, previously limited to depth-based or dedicated 3D vision models, to the more ubiquitous RGB-only domain of robot control.

- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization for Robot Deployment:** The demonstrated ability of conditioned policies to generalize across diverse viewpoints, especially in "randomized" environments where policies cannot cheat by relying on static background cues, is paramount for real-world robotic applications. Robots often need to operate in environments with varying camera placements or under dynamic conditions where consistent viewpoints are not guaranteed. This work offers a practical solution to make state-of-the-art policies (ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA) robust enough for such deployments, greatly increasing their utility and reliability.

- **Improved Data Efficiency in Robotics:** The scaling law experiments reveal a profound impact on data collection. By requiring significantly fewer training camera views to achieve comparable performance, explicit camera conditioning can dramatically reduce the onerous data collection efforts in robotics, which is a notoriously data-scarce field. This data efficiency translates into faster policy development and training, lowering the barrier to entry for complex manipulation tasks.

- **Guidance for Policy Design and Training:** The comprehensive ablation studies offer valuable insights for researchers and practitioners. The findings regarding optimal action spaces (Delta End-Effector Pose), effective data augmentation (random cropping), and suitable integration methods (late fusion with pretrained encoders) provide concrete guidelines for designing and training more robust and performant robot policies. The observation that explicit conditioning can diminish the reliance on extensive visual pretraining for pose inference is also a significant insight.

- **New Benchmarks for Future Research:** The release of six new, challenging simulation benchmarks (RoboSuite and ManiSkill tasks with "fixed" and "randomized" variants) is a valuable resource for the community. These benchmarks offer a principled and standardized way to evaluate viewpoint generalization, fostering further research and comparative analysis in this critical area.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper acknowledges limitations, such as imperfections in current pose estimation methods. This opens immediate avenues for research into robust pose estimation techniques that can feed into this framework, and strategies to mitigate the impact of pose estimation errors. Furthermore, the natural extension to generalize across different camera intrinsics (beyond just extrinsics) is an exciting future direction.

In summary, "Do You Know Where Your Camera Is?" provides both a strong conceptual argument and compelling empirical evidence for explicitly incorporating camera geometry into RGB-only robot control policies. Its contributions pave the way for more robust, data-efficient, and generalizable robot learning systems, accelerating the transition of advanced robotic capabilities from laboratories to real-world applications.
