# Ego4WAM: What Matters When Scaling Egocentric Human Data for Robot Learning?

> [!summary] Summary
> Ego4WAM is a controlled study of how egocentric human data helps robot learning. It holds a dual-branch world-action model backbone fixed while varying human-robot alignment, data duration, task diversity, action supervision and training stage. Aligned demonstrations fix failures on unseen objects and scenes, and video-only pre-training on 15K hours builds a strong dynamics prior. With only about 100 robot hours, the model reaches a 20.29 average RoboDojo score.

## Problem

- Prior egocentric scaling studies report aggregate curves over total hours, entangling alignment, behavioral coverage, supervision type and scale, so the property actually driving robot gains stays unknown.
- Human-to-robot transfer is often judged by open-loop human-action prediction error, which does not reveal whether benefits persist under closed-loop robot deployment.
- Reliable human action labels require accurate hand and camera pose reconstruction, which is costly and error-prone, leaving most egocentric video unused by imitation-style pipelines.

## Method

- Egocentric data pyramid: robot-aligned EgoAlign demonstrations (about 4h), about 12K hours of quality-filtered multi-task video-action data, and about 120K hours of video-only footage, each sample kept at its most reliable supervision level.
- ==FastWAM==-based ==world-action model== with a video branch and an action branch linked by ==shared attention==; the base variant conditions actions on current frames, the joint variant also on predicted future visual tokens.
- ==Unified 32D bimanual action space== (joints, gripper, 9D end-effector per arm) that supervises humans only on end-effector and gripper, trained in three stages: ==video pre-training==, ==world-action mid-training==, ==robot post-training==.

## Results

- On Place into the Basket, object-OOD human demonstrations lift object-OOD success from **10% to 60%**, and scene-OOD demonstrations lift scene-OOD success from **0% to 20%**.
- Video-only pre-training raises the RoboDojo average score from **6.39 to 14.13** without action labels; mid-training without pre-training reaches only **10.28**, and joint attention pushes the score to **20.29**.
- With 20 robot demos on Fold Cloth, multi-task mid-training recovers **80% in-distribution and 80% object-OOD** success; scaling from 500 to 6K tasks raises Long-Horizon from **12.0 to 16.2** but lowers Precision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Total hours is a poor scaling axis: data duration and task diversity buy different capabilities, and sparse long-tail tasks can hurt precision-sensitive manipulation.
> - Action-free human video is not a weak substitute: building the world-modeling branch first makes later action learning far more effective than action-labeled data alone.
> - Conditioning actions on predicted futures only pays off once the world model is strong, so architecture ablations run without pretraining can understate joint designs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Robot demonstrations are expensive to collect, and robot-only policies have little experience of objects or scenes absent from their training. In Ego4WAM, a person's first-person, or egocentric, demonstration can supply some of that missing experience without collecting matching robot demonstrations. On the real-robot Place into the Basket task, object-OOD success rises from 10% to 60% when object-OOD human demonstrations are added. Scene-OOD success rises from 0% to 20%, with a larger increase in task progress. Here, OOD means the manipulated object or the surrounding environment is absent from robot training. These results hold for this task and these held-out conditions. They do not show that human data replaces robot data.

Real-robot evaluation on Place into the Basket with human data under OOD settings. Rows show robot ID data only (300 episodes), then that data plus EgoAlign ID, EgoAlign object-OOD, or EgoAlign scene-OOD human data. The bars report success rate and normalized task score, a 0–100 measure of task-specific partial progress. With robot data only, object-OOD reaches 10% success and a score of 20, and scene-OOD reaches 0% and 0. Adding object-OOD human demonstrations yields 60% and 67.5 on object-OOD. Adding scene-OOD human demonstrations yields 20% and 47.5 on scene-OOD.

### 2. Learning what scenes do from video

Reliable human action labels require additional reconstruction and quality control. The authors therefore let the model learn from what human data can reliably provide. It first predicts how a scene changes from video, then uses action labels when they are available, and finally adapts to robot control.

The model is a world-action model: one branch predicts future visual states, while another predicts robot actions. Training has three stages:

- **Video pre-training** learns future-scene prediction from egocentric video.
- **Video-action mid-training** jointly learns video and action prediction from reliable action-labeled data. Human actions supervise only the shared end-effector and gripper components, not robot-specific joint commands.
- **Post-training** adapts the model to robot demonstrations.

The pre-training collection combines 12K hours of action-valid video, used with video-only supervision, with about 3K additional video-only hours, for 15K hours in total. So the video-pretraining objective did not use human action labels, even though many of its videos had them.

On the RoboDojo benchmark, video-only pre-training improves the official average score from 6.39 to 14.13 and the average success rate from 3.15% to 9.45%. The score is RoboDojo's reported progress score. Success rate is the percentage of task rollouts that complete the task. Both averages cover the benchmark's full evaluation suite.

Adding mid-training after pre-training yields a smaller increase, to 15.04/10.78%. Mid-training without prior video pre-training reaches 10.28/6.42%. This comparison is not compute-matched, because the video-only setup uses more egocentric hours.

### 3. Letting actions look ahead

The second comparison changes how attention works during post-training. In the joint-attention variant, action prediction can also use predicted future visual representations. Starting from the same video-pretrained model, joint attention reaches a 20.29 score and 14.35% success, compared with 14.13 and 9.45% for base attention.

The authors interpret this as evidence that future visual representations help action prediction, and that a stronger dynamics prior makes those representations more useful. The comparison establishes a benefit for joint over base attention in this setting. The paper does not report the corresponding joint-attention comparison without video pre-training. The result therefore does not by itself establish that video-pretraining quality is the only reason for the gain.

Ablation study of egocentric training stages on RoboDojo. Each category column reports a Score and SR (success rate) pair. The Average pair is 6.39/3.15 with no pre- or mid-training, 14.13/9.45 with video pre-training only and base attention, 15.04/10.78 with pre-training plus mid-training and base attention, and 20.29/14.35 with pre-training and joint attention. The joint row shares its video-pretrained starting point with the base row. These are benchmark measurements, not a direct measurement of the learned dynamics prior.

### 4. Closing

Ego4WAM shows that the value of human data depends on which objects and tasks it covers, whether its actions align with robot behavior, what supervision can be trusted, and when the data enters training. Egocentric video can teach scene dynamics without human action supervision, and that knowledge improves robot policies in the tested settings. Robot data still provides embodiment grounding: the reported Ego4WAM data scale is about 0.1K robot hours and 15K egocentric hours.
