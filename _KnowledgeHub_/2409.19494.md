# OptiGrasp: Optimized Grasp Pose Detection Using RGB Images for Warehouse Picking Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> OptiGrasp predicts 6D suction grasp poses from a single RGB image, dropping depth sensors entirely. A frozen Depth Anything DINOv2 backbone plus a fine-tuned DPT decoder feeds an Affordance Grasp Head that outputs three dense maps: grasp score, pitch, and yaw. Trained only on 400,000 synthetic shelf images, it transfers zero-shot to a real warehouse robot at 82.3% pick success.

## Problem

- Prior suction-grasp methods depend on depth sensors, which are costly, high-latency, interference-prone, and inaccurate on transparent, reflective, or fine-textured object surfaces.
- RGB-only grasping stays hard because grasp quality hinges on 3D structure that is difficult to recover from one view, especially for object categories outside training.
- Existing learned suction policies predict only a translation point, ignoring gripper approach angle, and need real-world data or fine-tuning to close the sim-to-real gap.

## Method

- **OptiGrasp**'s ==Affordance Grasp Head== on a frozen ==Depth Anything== ==DINOv2== ViT-base backbone with a fine-tuned ==DPT decoder==, taking RGB plus a target segmentation mask from STOW.
- Three dense per-pixel ==affordance maps== (grasp score, pitch, yaw, both angles bounded to ±30 degrees), trained with ==Huber loss== summed over masked pixels of all maps.
- ==Analytic affordance grasp score== combining anomaly, depth-consistency, depth-variability, normal-consistency, and inclination terms, used to auto-label 400,000 domain-randomized shelf scenes with 350+ scanned objects.

## Results

- Zero-shot real-robot picking reached **81.9%** over **215** attempts on **170** unique objects: **90.9%** easy, **82.7%** medium, **73.3%** hard, versus **57.2%** centroid and **40.5%** DexNet 3.0.
- Removing the pitch and yaw heads dropped accuracy from **81.9%** to **67.4%**, confirming that predicting approach angle, not just grasp location, drives most of the gain.
- Feeding DexNet 3.0 estimated depth from Depth Anything gave only **54.3%** versus **50.4%** with camera depth, while OptiGrasp scored **78.0%** on the same **230** objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Depth-estimation foundation-model weights carry transferable 3D structure priors; reusing them as a frozen backbone bridges sim-to-real without any real-world fine-tuning.
> - Better depth is not the bottleneck: swapping in estimated depth barely helped a depth-based baseline, so representation quality matters more than sensing modality.
> - Predicting approach orientation alongside contact position is essential in densely packed bins; position-only affordance maps lose roughly 15 points of success rate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly for object picking in warehouse environments, requires capabilities such as low hardware requirements, generalization to novel products, and robustness across varied settings. A common approach in this domain involves the use of depth sensors to acquire structural information for grasp prediction. However, depth sensors exhibit several limitations, including high cost, operational latency, susceptibility to multi-device interference, restricted range and resolution, and inaccuracies when interacting with transparent, highly reflective, or finely textured surfaces. These limitations impede their widespread adoption in industrial applications.

Despite these challenges, relying solely on RGB input for grasp pose prediction has been difficult because grasping fundamentally depends on the 3D structure of objects, which is not directly evident from a single RGB image. This difficulty is compounded when generalization to objects outside the training set is required.

Recent advancements in computer vision, particularly with the development of large-scale foundation models, have demonstrated an enhanced capacity to infer 3D structure and scene understanding from RGB images alone. This research builds upon these developments by integrating such foundation models to address the limitations of depth-based perception in robotic grasping. Existing suction grasping methods often utilize RGB-D or depth-only inputs, as summarized in the paper’s related work (Table I). Methods like SuctionNet, DexNet 3.0, Zeng, and DYNAMO-GRASP frequently leverage depth information. While approaches like SimSuction use RGB-D to predict both translation and rotation, this work aims to achieve similar or superior performance using only RGB input. The paper positions OptiGrasp as a method that leverages the generalization abilities of pre-trained vision models to improve suction-based grasping by providing detailed affordance maps from RGB images, thereby offering an alternative to depth-sensor-reliant systems and addressing the sim-to-real gap without real-world fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method, named OptiGrasp, for detecting optimal grasp poses for suction grippers using only a single-view RGB image. This method aims to address the challenges associated with robust robotic picking in dynamic warehouse environments.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Eliminate Dependence on Depth Sensors:** A core motivation is to overcome the inherent drawbacks of depth sensors. These drawbacks include high acquisition and setup costs, potential latency, interference between multiple devices, restricted operational range and resolution, and inaccuracies when dealing with transparent, highly reflective surfaces, or objects with fine textures and edges. By developing an RGB-only solution, the researchers aim to provide a more economical and technically less complex perception system for industrial robots.
- **Enhance Generalization to Novel Objects:** Warehouse environments require robots to handle a vast and constantly changing inventory. Traditional methods often struggle to generalize effectively to novel objects or object categories not encountered during training. The research seeks to leverage the generalization capabilities of foundation models, which have demonstrated a strong understanding of 3D structure from RGB images across diverse scenes, to enable robust grasping of previously unseen objects without explicit retraining or fine-tuning.
- **Achieve Robust Performance in Cluttered Environments:** The goal is to develop a system that can reliably identify optimal grasp points and approach angles (6D pose: translation, pitch, yaw) for objects situated within cluttered containers or industrial shelving units, replicating realistic warehouse conditions. This includes handling objects with varying geometries and surface properties.
- **Facilitate Sim-to-Real Transfer without Fine-tuning:** Collecting real-world training data for robotic grasping is a labor-intensive and costly process. A significant objective is to train the model exclusively on synthetic datasets and demonstrate its ability to transfer these learned capabilities to real-world robotic systems with zero-shot generalization, bypassing the need for real-world fine-tuning.
- **Provide Detailed Grasp Affordance Maps:** The motivation extends to generating explicit affordance maps that guide the robot in selecting precise grasp points and their corresponding approach angles. This includes predicting the optimal translation, pitch (β), and yaw (γ) angles at a pixel level. While the roll angle is considered flexible due to suction cup symmetry, accurate pitch and yaw are crucial for successful suction.
- **Address Challenges of 3D Structure Inference from RGB:** The inherent difficulty of inferring 3D object structure from 2D RGB images for grasping tasks is a central motivation. The approach is inspired by foundation models that have shown aptitude in this area, utilizing their pre-trained weights to enable the network to perceive necessary 3D information from visual cues.

In summary, the research is motivated by the desire to create an efficient, cost-effective, and highly generalizable robotic picking system for warehouse automation by moving away from depth-sensor reliance and towards advanced RGB-based perception powered by foundation models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The OptiGrasp methodology is a learning-based pipeline designed to predict grasp poses for suction grippers using only a single-view RGB image. The system is trained exclusively on synthetic data and aims for zero-shot transfer to real-world applications.

**A. Network Structure**
The OptiGrasp network architecture integrates components from established computer vision models. It utilizes a pre-trained DINOv2 model, specifically its Vision Transformer (ViT-base) architecture from Depth Anything, as its backbone. This backbone processes the input single-view RGB images to generate dense feature maps. The weights of the DINOv2 backbone are kept frozen during training. The output from DINOv2 is then passed to a Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) model, which is refined during the training process.
Following the DPT, an "Affordance Grasp Head" module takes the refined features and a segmentation mask of the target object (obtained from STOW) as input. This head generates three dense prediction maps, each the same size as the input image:
1. **Grasp Location Affordance Map:** Estimates the likelihood of a successful suction grasp at each pixel.
2. **Pitch Angle Affordance Map:** Predicts the optimal pitch angle (β) for the gripper's approach at each pixel.
3. **Yaw Angle Affordance Map:** Predicts the optimal yaw angle (γ) for the gripper's approach at each pixel.

The pitch and yaw angles are constrained within a range of (-30, 30) degrees due to bin constraints and dynamic scenario considerations, though the system can tolerate up to ±15 degrees beyond this range through suction deformation. For a given grasp attempt, the system selects the pixel with the highest score from the grasp location affordance map and then retrieves the corresponding pitch and yaw angles from their respective maps to determine the final 6D grasp pose (translation and orientation). The depth required for re-projection is inferred, as precise depth is not critical, given the end effector's movement to a pre-grasp pose followed by 6D pose waypoints.

**B. Simulation Environment and Data Generation**
To circumvent the high costs and effort associated with real-world data collection, the researchers developed a simulated environment. This setup mimics an industrial warehouse shelf arrangement, similar to that used in DYNAMO-GRASP. The simulation incorporates over 350 diverse 3D object models sourced from Google’s scanned objects.
For each scene configuration, objects are randomly placed within the bins, with the number of objects determined by the bin's volume and space limitations. Each object receives random translations within bin boundaries and random rotations. After object placement, the system collects depth data, segmentation information, and single-view RGB data.
To enhance the model's robustness and generalization to real-world conditions, domain randomization techniques are applied. These include varying friction coefficients, object sizes, and object weights. The simulation runs in parallel across 75 different environments, spawning 30 objects at a time with convex decomposition for fine-grained collision models. After collecting data from every 10 scene configurations, a new set of 30 objects is introduced to maintain object diversity.

**C. Data Labeling**
The labeling process is central to generating the ground truth for training the affordance maps. For each object in the simulated environment, the affordance grasp score *F(β, γ)* (described below) is evaluated across a wide range of pitch (β) and yaw (γ) angle combinations. To ensure accurate score computation, the object's point cloud is rotated to align with the suction cup's approach angle for each angle combination.
The dataset consists of approximately 400,000 instances. For each instance, *F(β, γ)* is calculated for every combination of β and γ angles. The optimal *F(β, γ)* for each object is identified by iterating through all possible angle configurations, updating the best *F(β, γ)* if a higher score is found. This process generates three affordance labels for each object configuration: the optimal pitch, yaw, and the corresponding maximum affordance grasp score. The labels for pitch and yaw are generated at 5-degree intervals within the (-30, 30) degree range.
This label generation process was highly parallelized using eight NVIDIA A10G Tensor Core GPUs to ensure efficiency.

**D. Affordance Grasp Score**
The affordance grasp score *F(β, γ)* quantifies the quality of a potential suction grasp for a given pitch and yaw angle. It is defined by the following equation:

*F(β, γ) = k1Sa − k2Cd − k3Vd + k4Sn + k5Sc*

Where:
- *Sa* is the **Normalized Anomaly Score**, indicating how much a point deviates from expected depth.
- *Cd* is the **Depth Consistency Cost**, measuring depth variation along the suction cup's perimeter.
- *Vd* is the **Depth Variability Cost**, represented by the standard deviation of depth values.
- *Sn* is the **Normal Consistency Score**, indicating the alignment of surface normals with the gripper's approach vector.
- *Sc* is the **Angle Inclination Score**, reflecting the angle of the surface relative to the suction cup.
The *ki* terms are positive weighting factors that balance the contribution of each component. These scores collectively aim to identify robust and reliable suction grasp points.

**E. Training**
The OptiGrasp model was trained for 70 epochs using eight NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs. The Adam optimizer was employed, coupled with a learning rate scheduler for adaptive adjustment. Training was performed exclusively on the generated synthetic single-view RGB images.
The Huber loss function (*Lδ*) was used to calculate the loss for all three affordance maps (grasp location, pitch, and yaw). The loss is computed across all segmented pixels within the target object's mask. The threshold parameter *δ* for the Huber loss was set to 1.0. The strategy relies on sim-to-real transfer, evaluating the model in real-world scenarios without any fine-tuning on real images.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive evaluations both in simulation and with a real robot setup, demonstrating the performance and generalization capabilities of OptiGrasp.

**A. Real Robot Setup and Evaluation**
Experiments were performed using a Universal Robots UR16e robot equipped with a custom industrial vacuum suction gripper (Schmalz SCTSi-EIP 4 vacuum ejector). Motion planning utilized RRT Connect and OMPL. The robot operated within an industrial shelving unit designed to simulate warehouse environments. Object sets were categorized into "easy" (bottles, boxes), "medium" (bottles, boxes with irregularities or transparent sections), and "hard" (minimal graspable areas, deformable materials). A grasp was considered successful if the suction seal was established, and the object was lifted. The perception system's state was restored after each trial for reproducibility.

**B. Real-World Performance and Comparison**
OptiGrasp demonstrated an overall success rate of 81.9% across 215 pick attempts involving 170 unique objects in a cluttered warehouse scene. The success rates across object difficulty categories were:
- **Easy Object Set:** 90.9%
- **Medium Object Set:** 82.7%
- **Hard Object Set:** 73.3%

Ablation studies highlighted the importance of angle prediction:
- **OptiGrasp w/o angle:** Performance dropped to 67.4% success, indicating that predicting pitch and yaw angles significantly contributes to grasping success.

The performance of OptiGrasp was compared against two baseline methods:
- **Centroid method (RGB input):** Achieved a 57.2% success rate.
- **DexNet 3.0 (Depth input):** Achieved a 40.5% success rate.
OptiGrasp demonstrated superior performance compared to both baselines in real-world scenarios.

**C. Accuracy Comparison with Depth-Based Methods (Table IV)**
Further comparisons were made using human expert assessment across 230 real-world objects:
- **OptiGrasp (RGB input):** Achieved 78.0% grasp accuracy.
- **DexNet 3.0 (Depth from Depth Anything):** Achieved 54.3% accuracy.
- **DexNet 3.0 (Depth from depth camera):** Achieved 50.4% accuracy.
- **DYNAMO GRASP (Depth from depth camera):** Achieved 48.5% accuracy.
These results indicate that while using depth estimated by Depth Anything improved DexNet 3.0's performance over raw camera depth, OptiGrasp, utilizing RGB input directly, still significantly outperformed all depth-based methods. This finding suggests that depth data alone may be insufficient for accurate grasp point prediction, and the comprehensive visual understanding derived from the pre-trained backbone contributes more effectively.

**D. Ablation Study on Backbone and DPT Configurations (Table V)**
An ablation study investigated the impact of different backbone and DPT configurations:
- **OptiGrasp (Depth-Anything backbone, fine-tuned DPT):** Achieved 88.9% synthetic success rate and 82.3% real-world success rate. This configuration corresponds to the main OptiGrasp method presented.
- **OptiGrasp (Depth-Anything backbone, DPT not fine-tuned):** Achieved 78.4% synthetic and 82.3% real-world success rate. The identical real-world success rate suggests that fine-tuning DPT primarily benefits synthetic performance, with less impact on zero-shot real-world transfer for this specific configuration.
- **Models trained with DinoV2 backbone and DPT (not from Depth Anything, with or without fine-tuning):** Showed significantly lower performance. For example, a model with a fine-tuned DinoV2 backbone and DPT not fine-tuned achieved 71.4% synthetic and 42.8% real-world success. A model with DinoV2 backbone not fine-tuned and DPT not fine-tuned achieved 71.0% synthetic and 25.0% real-world success.
These results underscore the importance of leveraging pre-trained weights from foundational models like Depth Anything for effective sim-to-real transfer and robust real-world performance. Training from scratch without such a backbone resulted in substantially reduced accuracy.

**E. Failure Cases**
The analysis of failure cases revealed three primary categories:
1. **Suction cup incompatibility:** Objects with porous, highly irregular, or overly textured surfaces that prevent the formation of an effective suction seal.
2. **Visual limitations from single-view:** In some instances, a single RGB view may not provide enough information to identify viable graspable areas, especially for complex object geometries or occluded parts.
3. **Object dynamics:** Movements of objects within the storage bin during the grasp attempt could destabilize the gripper, leading to unsuccessful picks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OptiGrasp research demonstrates a significant advancement in robotic grasping by successfully implementing a system that relies solely on RGB images for suction pose detection, mitigating the limitations associated with depth sensors.

The potential impact and significance of this work include:

- **Cost Reduction and Simplified Deployment:** By eliminating the need for expensive and complex depth sensors, OptiGrasp offers a more economical solution for industrial warehouse picking robots. This reduction in hardware requirements can lead to lower initial investment costs and simplified system integration and maintenance, making robotic automation more accessible for various industrial applications.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** The leverage of pre-trained foundation models from computer vision, particularly the Depth Anything model, enables OptiGrasp to exhibit strong generalization to novel and diverse objects not included in the synthetic training dataset. This capability is critical for warehouse environments that handle a wide variety of products with frequent introductions of new items, reducing the need for extensive retraining or fine-tuning for every new product.
- **Increased Robustness in Varied Settings:** The demonstrated high success rates in cluttered warehouse scenes and across objects of varying difficulty suggest improved robustness of the grasping system. This contributes to more reliable and versatile robotic operations in complex, real-world industrial settings where objects may be densely packed or have challenging geometries.
- **Advancement in Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer from synthetic training data to real-world robot operation without any real-world fine-tuning represents an important contribution to addressing the sim-to-real gap. This approach minimizes the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection and annotation, accelerating the development and deployment of robotic solutions.
- **Foundation for Future Robotic Perception:** This work establishes a precedent for utilizing advanced visual foundation models for direct perception in robotic manipulation tasks. It suggests that highly generalized visual features learned from vast datasets can be effectively adapted for specific robotic affordance predictions. This could inspire further research into RGB-only perception systems for other robotic capabilities, potentially leading to a broader shift away from specialized sensors.
- **Expansion of Grasping Capabilities:** The method's ability to accurately predict both grasp locations and approach angles (pitch and yaw) is crucial for robust suction grasping, especially for objects with irregular surfaces or in constrained environments. This fine-grained control improves the likelihood of successful picks for a wider range of objects.

In conclusion, OptiGrasp contributes to the development of more adaptable, cost-effective, and efficient robotic picking systems for logistics and supply chain management by demonstrating a viable and effective RGB-only solution for robust suction grasping. Future work, including the integration of common sense reasoning from vision-language models and extension to other gripper types, indicates potential for further expanding the impact of this approach.
