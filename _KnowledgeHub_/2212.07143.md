# Reproducible scaling laws for contrastive language-image learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers systematically investigated scaling laws for contrastive language-image models using public LAION datasets, revealing how performance scales with model size, data quantity, and training samples, and crucially, how pre-training data distribution influences task-specific scaling behaviors. This open-source study provides a reproducible framework for understanding large-scale multimodal AI, indicating that gains continue with scale, but the optimal scaling strategy depends on the underlying dataset.

## Problem

- Prior research on scaling laws primarily focused on uni-modal models (language or vision) and often relied on private datasets and proprietary models.
- The influential CLIP model demonstrated strong multimodal capabilities, but its training dataset (WIT-400M) was private, hindering systematic, reproducible investigations of scaling trends in this domain.
- A lack of open and reproducible large-scale studies made it difficult for the broader research community to understand and verify fundamental scaling properties of multimodal AI models.

## Method

- The authors conducted a systematic empirical study of ==scaling laws for contrastive language-image learning== using OpenCLIP models (ViT-B/32 to ViT-g/14) and publicly available LAION datasets (LAION-80M, LAION-400M, LAION-2B).
- Models were trained using an open-source framework on supercomputing infrastructure, varying ==model size, data size, and total samples seen== (up to 34 billion) to explore scaling dimensions.
- Performance was evaluated comprehensively across diverse downstream tasks, including ==zero-shot classification, image/text retrieval, linear probing==, and full fine-tuning, to identify generalizable scaling trends.

## Results

- Performance across zero-shot classification, retrieval, linear probing, and fine-tuning consistently follows **power-law relationships** with increased scale, confirming scaling laws for multimodal models.
- OpenCLIP models (LAION-trained) demonstrate stronger scaling for zero-shot image retrieval tasks compared to OpenAI's CLIP, while OpenAI's CLIP shows stronger scaling for zero-shot classification (e.g., OpenCLIP α=**−0.11** vs. CLIP α=**−0.16** on ImageNet).
- Derived power laws enable predictions for larger scales, estimating that a ViT-G/14 CLIP model trained on **68 billion samples** could achieve **81.9%** zero-shot ImageNet accuracy and **7**6.99%** Recall@5** on MS-COCO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scaling laws observed in unimodal learning extend to contrastive language-image models, with consistent power-law improvements across diverse downstream tasks as model size, data size, and samples seen increase.
> - The specific pre-training data distribution critically influences scaling behavior and task-specific performance; for instance, models trained on LAION datasets show stronger scaling for retrieval tasks, while OpenAI's CLIP (trained on private WIT-400M) exhibits stronger scaling for zero-shot classification.
> - Performance improvements plateau if one scaling dimension (model size, data quantity, or training steps) is fixed while others are increased, highlighting the necessity of balanced scaling across all dimensions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research positions itself squarely within the burgeoning field of "scaling laws" in deep learning, particularly for large pre-trained models. The concept of scaling laws, which posits that model performance reliably improves following power-law relationships as a function of training set size, model size, and compute, gained significant traction with works on large language models (LLMs) like GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020; Kaplan et al., 2020) and subsequent studies on compute-optimal LLMs (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Similar scaling behaviors were later observed in vision-only models (Zhai et al., 2022; Riquelme et al., 2021).

However, the authors identify a critical gap: prior work on scaling laws primarily focused on uni-modal (language or vision) learning and often relied on private datasets and models. The seminal work on CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) demonstrated the power of contrastive language-image pre-training for multimodal understanding, achieving unprecedented zero-shot transfer capabilities. Yet, the original CLIP models were trained on a private dataset (WIT-400M), making it challenging for the broader research community to systematically investigate scaling trends in this crucial multimodal domain.

This paper directly addresses this limitation. By leveraging the recently released, publicly available LAION datasets (LAION-400M and LAION-5B) and the open-source OpenCLIP repository, the authors conduct the first systematic and *reproducible* investigation of scaling laws for contrastive language-image learning. This work not only extends the empirical validation of scaling laws to the multimodal domain but also pioneers a new standard for transparency and accessibility in large-scale AI research. It pushes the boundaries of open science by enabling others to replicate and build upon these resource-intensive experiments, fostering a more inclusive and collaborative research environment. Furthermore, the discovery that scaling behavior can differ based on the training data distribution (LAION vs. WIT) adds a new dimension to scaling law research, emphasizing the critical, often under-explored, role of dataset design in model generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are:

1. **Systematically Investigate Scaling Laws for Contrastive Language-Image Learning:** The core goal is to understand how the performance of multimodal models, specifically CLIP-like architectures, scales with increases in model size, data size, and the total number of samples seen during training. This extends prior scaling law studies, which predominantly focused on uni-modal models.
2. **Ensure Reproducibility and Accessibility:** A central motivation is to conduct this large-scale study using only public datasets (LAION) and open-source training code (OpenCLIP). This directly contrasts with previous private-data-driven studies, aiming to make scaling law research more accessible and verifiable for the broader scientific community.
3. **Evaluate Performance Across Diverse Downstream Tasks:** The study aims to identify scaling trends not just for pre-training loss but for various practical downstream applications, including zero-shot classification, image retrieval, linear probing (few-shot and full-dataset), and end-to-end fine-tuning. This provides a comprehensive view of how scaling impacts real-world utility.
4. **Compare Open-Source vs. Proprietary Training Distributions:** A significant objective is to compare the scaling behavior of models trained on the public LAION dataset with those from OpenAI's private WIT-400M dataset (using their released CLIP models). This comparison seeks to understand if and how the underlying training data distribution influences the observed scaling laws.
5. **Provide Guidance for Future Large-Scale Model Development and Dataset Design:** By identifying consistent scaling laws and bottleneck effects, the authors aim to offer valuable empirical guidance to practitioners on how to optimally allocate computational resources for future large-scale multimodal model training and how to design more effective pre-training datasets.

The overarching motivation stems from the high cost and resource intensiveness of training large neural networks. Reliable scaling laws offer a predictive framework to extrapolate performance, determine optimal training regimes, and avoid wasteful experimentation, especially as experiments become increasingly expensive. By making this research open and reproducible, the authors seek to accelerate progress in multimodal AI by enabling more researchers to engage with and understand these fundamental properties of large models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a systematic empirical approach to investigate scaling laws, focusing on three key dimensions of scale: model size, data size, and the number of samples seen during training.

#### 3.1. Datasets

- **Pre-training:** The study exclusively uses publicly available datasets: LAION-400M and LAION-5B. Specifically, the English subset of LAION-5B, referred to as LAION-2B (2.32 billion image-text pairs), is the largest dataset utilized. LAION-80M, an 80-million sample subset of LAION-400M, is also used to represent smaller data scales. These datasets were compiled from Common Crawl, filtered using a pre-trained OpenAI ViT-B/32 model.
- **Evaluation (Downstream Tasks):** A comprehensive suite of downstream tasks is used, including:
 - **Zero-shot Classification:** ImageNet, ImageNet distribution shift datasets (ImageNet-V2, ImageNet-R, ImageNet-Sketch, ObjectNet, ImageNet-A), and the Visual Task Adaptation Benchmark (VTAB+), comprising 35 diverse tasks.
 - **Retrieval:** MS-COCO and Flickr30K for both image and text retrieval.
 - **Linear Probing (Few-shot and Full):** ImageNet, CIFAR100, and VTAB+.
 - **Fine-tuning:** ImageNet (including a two-stage approach with ImageNet-12k as an intermediate step) and a joint fine-tuning experiment on eight diverse tasks (Cars, DTD, EuroSAT, GTSRB, MNIST, RESISC45, SUN397, SVHN).
- **Duplication Check:** A perceptual image hash library (pHash) was used to check for overlaps between pre-training and downstream datasets, finding generally low overlap (around 1%) with slightly higher rates for ImageNet-R (3.80%) and ImageNet-Sketch (5.15%), but concluding that these do not significantly alter results based on re-evaluation and prior work.

#### 3.2. Model Architectures and Scaling Dimensions

- **Model Architectures:** OpenCLIP models with various Vision Transformer (ViT) visual encoders are used: ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14, ViT-H/14, and ViT-g/14. The corresponding text encoders are scaled in accord.
- **Scaling Dimensions:**
 - **Model Scale:** Ranging from ViT-B/32 to ViT-g/14.
 - **Data Scale:** LAION-80M, LAION-400M, LAION-2B.
 - **Samples Seen:** 3 billion (3B), 13 billion (13B), and 34 billion (34B) samples seen during pre-training. Due to compute constraints, larger models (H/14, g/14) were tested with a more restricted set of samples seen (e.g., 34B for H/14, 13B for g/14 on LAION-2B).

#### 3.3. Training Procedure

- **Open-Source Framework:** All models are trained using the open-source OpenCLIP repository.
- **Distributed Training:** Experiments were conducted on supercomputers, primarily JUWELS Booster at JSC, utilizing up to 1520 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. PyTorch DDP (DistributedDataParallel) was used for data parallel training.
- **Batch Size:** Large global batch sizes (86K-88K samples) were used for efficient distributed training. Control experiments confirmed that variations in batch size within the tested range (32K-88K) did not significantly affect performance, thus not confounding scaling observations.
- **Learning Rate Schedule:** A cosine annealing learning schedule was adapted to the number of samples seen for each experiment to prevent suboptimal training.
- **Optimizer:** InfoNCE loss, AdamW optimizer (β1=0.9, β2=0.98, weight decay=0.2).
- **Precision:** Mixed precision training was used. For larger models (ViT-L/14, H/14, g/14), bfloat16 was employed over float16 to mitigate training instabilities (loss spikes, NaNs) due to its wider dynamic range, an important practical insight.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** A small number of hyperparameters were tuned for each scale point to optimize validation loss and ensure training stability, while generally following the original CLIP training procedure.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Methods

- **Zero-shot:** For classification, class embeddings are computed by averaging prompt embeddings, and images are classified by cosine similarity. For retrieval, cosine similarity is used to rank images/texts, reporting Recall@K (K=5).
- **Linear Probing:** Linear classifiers (softmax regression) are trained on frozen image features. Hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs) are swept per model and dataset.
- **Fine-tuning:** Models are fine-tuned on ImageNet and other datasets. For some tasks, vision encoder parameters are fine-tuned while classification heads (initialized from zero-shot text encoder) are frozen. Model patching via weight interpolation is also explored for robustness.
- **Power Law Fitting:** Performance is plotted against total compute (GMAC per sample x samples seen) on a log-log scale. Power law relationships ($E = \beta C^\alpha$) are fitted to the Pareto frontier of available models to derive scaling coefficients ($\alpha$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several crucial findings regarding scaling laws in contrastive language-image learning:

1. **Consistent Power Law Scaling:** Performance across a broad range of downstream tasks – including zero-shot classification, retrieval, linear probing (few-shot and full), and fine-tuning – consistently improves with increased scale (model size, data size, and samples seen). This improvement follows a power-law relationship, confirming that scaling laws observed in uni-modal learning extend to the multimodal domain.
2. **Task-Dependent Scaling Behavior and Dataset Influence:** A key discovery is that the scaling behavior differs significantly depending on the downstream task and, critically, on the pre-training dataset.
 - **Zero-shot Classification:** OpenAI CLIP models (trained on private WIT-400M) exhibit stronger scaling for zero-shot classification (e.g., ImageNet, ImageNet robustness datasets) compared to OpenCLIP models (trained on LAION-400M/2B). For instance, on ImageNet, $\alpha_{CLIP} = -0.16$ vs $\alpha_{OpenCLIP} = -0.11$.
 - **Zero-shot Retrieval:** Conversely, OpenCLIP models (LAION-trained) show stronger scaling for zero-shot image retrieval (MS-COCO, Flickr30K) compared to OpenAI CLIP. For MS-COCO, $\alpha_{OpenCLIP} = -0.08$ vs $\alpha_{CLIP} = -0.05$.
 - The authors hypothesize that these differences stem from the distinct curation procedures of the training datasets: WIT-400M might be more aligned with ImageNet-like classification, while LAION (filtered based on CLIP's similarity measures) might favor retrieval tasks.
3. **Bottleneck Effects:** The study identifies bottleneck behaviors where performance improvement plateaus if one scaling dimension is fixed while others are increased. For example, smaller models (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16) on LAION-2B require a sufficient number of samples seen (e.g., 34B) to fully realize the benefits of the larger dataset, which would otherwise be hidden. Similarly, ViT-L/14 on LAION-400M eventually becomes data-bottlenecked, requiring a larger dataset (LAION-2B) to continue scaling benefits from more samples seen. This highlights the importance of scaling all dimensions in concert.
4. **Predictions for Larger Scales:** Based on the derived power laws, the study provides performance predictions for even larger, untried scales. For instance, a ViT-G/14 CLIP model trained on 68B image-text samples from scratch is estimated to achieve 81.9% zero-shot top-1 accuracy on ImageNet and 76.99% Recall@5 on MS-COCO.
5. **Robustness Correlation:** For OpenCLIP models trained on LAION, improving accuracy with scale consistently leads to corresponding improvements on robustness benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet robustness datasets), suggesting a beneficial alignment between in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization with scaling.
6. **Fine-tuning Performance:** Scaling continues to improve accuracy even when models are fine-tuned on downstream tasks. While specific trends vary, the pre-training dataset still influences the slope of the accuracy-compute relationship, often favoring WIT-400M for classification-oriented fine-tuning.

In summary, the research empirically validates the existence of power-law scaling in contrastive language-image learning across a wide range of tasks and adaptation methods, while critically revealing that the specific training data distribution significantly shapes these scaling properties, leading to differential task-specific performance gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications for the field of AI, particularly in the development of large-scale multimodal models:

1. **Democratization of Scaling Law Research:** By conducting a large-scale scaling law study using entirely public datasets (LAION) and open-source code (OpenCLIP), the authors set a new standard for reproducibility and transparency. This directly enables broader participation from the research community, allowing academics and smaller labs to verify, extend, and build upon these findings without access to proprietary data or compute. This open approach is crucial for accelerating scientific progress and fostering trust in the results.
2. **Guidance for Efficient Resource Allocation:** The derived power laws and identified bottleneck effects offer invaluable guidance for practitioners designing future large-scale multimodal models. They can now make more informed decisions about how to balance increases in model size, data quantity, and training duration for a given compute budget to achieve optimal performance on desired tasks, potentially saving immense computational resources and time.
3. **Emphasis on Dataset Design:** The most profound insight is the finding that the training data distribution profoundly influences scaling behavior and task-specific performance. This shifts focus from merely *scaling up* to *scaling up intelligently* by considering the properties of the pre-training dataset. This will likely spur new research into "foundation datasets" – how to curate, filter, and compose datasets to optimize for specific downstream generalization capabilities and scaling characteristics. It suggests that future gains might come not just from larger models or more data, but from *better* data.
4. **Advancement of Multimodal AI:** By confirming robust scaling laws in the contrastive language-image domain, the study provides a clearer roadmap for achieving higher performance in multimodal AI. The predictions for even larger models, suggesting that models trained solely on unlabeled data can achieve competitive performance, motivate continued investment in scaling without relying on expensive supervised pre-training stages or private datasets.
5. **Foundation for Robustness Research:** The observation that improved accuracy with scale correlates with better robustness provides a strong incentive to continue scaling these models, with the promise of more reliable and generalizable AI systems. It also opens avenues for future research into scaling laws specifically for robustness metrics.
6. **Open Science Advocacy:** The paper itself, including its acknowledgments, serves as a powerful testament to the value of community effort, open-source tools, and publicly available infrastructure (like supercomputing centers and hosting platforms). It demonstrates how collaborative, open science can yield results comparable to, and in some aspects surpass, those from closed, proprietary environments.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge limitations, including the sparse sampling density of the scale space due to compute constraints, which limits the precision of hyperparameter tuning and extrapolation. The duplication check, while performed, could be more elaborate. Future work directions include denser sampling for more optimal compute configurations, deriving scaling laws for robustness benchmarks, investigating modality-specific scaling of vision and text encoders, and most importantly, systematically studying the effect of pre-training dataset composition on scaling behavior across various tasks. These future directions underscore the transformative potential of this open and reproducible research for shaping the trajectory of large-scale AI development.
