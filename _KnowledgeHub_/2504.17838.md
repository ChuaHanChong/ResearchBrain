# CaRL: Learning Scalable Planning Policies with Simple Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> CaRL introduces a simple, route completion-based reward function for reinforcement learning in autonomous driving, achieving superior performance and scalability on the CARLA and nuPlan benchmarks. The method attains a 64 Driving Score on CARLA longest6 v2 and 91.3 Closed-Loop Score on nuPlan Val14 (non-reactive traffic), while demonstrating 7-17 times faster inference on nuPlan than other baselines.

## Problem

- Rule-based autonomous driving planners struggle to scale to the "long tail" of complex and rare driving scenarios.
- Imitation learning suffers from distribution shift during closed-loop inference, leading to compounding errors and suboptimal performance.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches for autonomous driving rely on complex, hand-shaped reward functions that hinder scalability with large mini-batch sizes, introduce suboptimal tradeoffs, and are often capped by rule-based expert performance.

## Method

- A novel, simple ==reward function== is proposed, primarily based on route completion, with multiplicative soft penalties and terminal penalties for infractions, avoiding complex additive terms and implicit rule-based dependencies.
- The approach optimizes ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== hyperparameters and leverages ==Distributed Data Parallelism (DD-PPO)== to efficiently train with significantly larger mini-batch sizes (up to 16384) and data samples (up to 1 billion).
- Extensive simulator optimizations for CARLA and nuPlan, including specialized environments, pre-computed routes, and ==Asynchronous Collection PPO (AC-PPO)==, enable efficient and scalable on-policy data collection.

## Results

- CaRL achieved a **64 Driving Score** on the CARLA longest6 v2 benchmark, outperforming prior RL methods (e.g., 22 DS for Roach) and the state-of-the-art imitation planner (62 DS for PlanT), with reduced collision rates.
- On the nuPlan Val14 benchmark, CaRL attained the highest Closed-Loop Score among learning-based planners (**91.3** non-reactive, **90.6** reactive), surpassing the previous best by 1.7 and 7.9 points respectively.
- The approach demonstrated significant inference efficiency, running **7-17 times faster** on nuPlan compared to other baselines (14 ms/step), attributed to its small 2M parameter network, and showed a **33 DS improvement** in CARLA when scaling training from 10 million to 300 million samples.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simplifying the reward function to focus on route completion and multiplicative penalties drastically improves PPO's scalability with larger mini-batch sizes, enabling more efficient distributed training.
> - Complex, hand-shaped rewards can lead PPO to undesirable local optima, while a principled, goal-oriented reward design better aligns the learning objective with the target driving metric.
> - Efficient and scalable data generation in simulators, combined with robust distributed RL training, is crucial for learning generalized and high-performance planning policies in autonomous driving.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust and scalable planning systems is a central challenge in autonomous driving. Traditionally, planning in AD has relied on **rule-based approaches**. While these methods offer high reliability in common scenarios, they struggle to scale to the "long tail" of complex and rare driving situations, requiring extensive manual engineering of scenario-specific rules.

An alternative is **imitation learning (IL)**, where a neural planner learns to mimic expert demonstrations. IL scales well with data but suffers from the problem of **distribution shift**: during closed-loop inference, the agent may encounter states not seen during open-loop training, leading to compounding errors and suboptimal performance compared to rule-based or hybrid methods.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)** emerges as a promising paradigm to overcome the limitations of both rule-based and imitation learning approaches. RL agents learn through trial and error in a closed-loop environment, optimizing a reward signal. This avoids the distribution shift inherent in IL and offers a scalable alternative to handcrafted rules. However, a significant hurdle in RL for driving has been the design of an appropriate **reward function**. Early RL work used simple rewards, but these were found insufficient for complex environments with dynamic actors. Consequently, contemporary RL approaches for driving, such as Roach and Think2Drive, have adopted complex, "shaped" rewards that are additive combinations of multiple terms (e.g., progress, speed, position, orientation, comfort). These rewards often implicitly rely on rule-based "desired" states, introducing local optima, demanding careful tuning of tradeoffs, and ultimately upper-bounding performance by the suboptimality of the underlying rules.

CaRL directly addresses this critical issue within the RL for AD landscape. The paper identifies that these complex reward designs, while seemingly offering denser feedback, hinder scalability, particularly when training with larger mini-batch sizes using algorithms like PPO. By proposing a fundamentally simpler reward structure, CaRL demonstrates that RL can indeed achieve state-of-the-art performance and scale efficiently, thereby pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with purely learning-based approaches in complex driving simulators like CARLA and nuPlan. It challenges the prevailing notion that complex rewards are necessary for learning in dynamic driving environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a **scalable reinforcement learning policy for privileged planning in autonomous driving**, specifically focusing on addressing the limitations imposed by conventional complex reward designs. The authors are motivated by several key observations and problems within the current RL for AD paradigm:

1. **Scalability of Rule-Based vs. RL**: Rule-based planners are effective for regular driving but fail to scale to the "long tail" of driving scenarios. RL, in principle, offers scalability through data-driven learning.
2. **Limitations of Imitation Learning (IL)**: IL suffers from distribution shift, leading to performance degradation in closed-loop inference. RL, being a closed-loop training method, avoids this issue.
3. **Problems with Complex, Shaped Rewards in RL**: State-of-the-art RL methods for driving typically use complex, shaped rewards (e.g., summing progress, position, orientation, comfort rewards). The authors demonstrate that these complex rewards:
 - **Hinder Scalability with Mini-Batch Size**: PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) struggles to optimize these rewards effectively when the mini-batch size is increased. Larger mini-batch sizes, while crucial for efficient distributed training and data scaling, cause the optimization to get stuck in undesirable local minima (e.g., waiting at green lights for easy rewards).
 - **Introduce Suboptimal Tradeoffs**: The additive nature of complex rewards requires careful tuning of weights between different terms, which can introduce conflicting objectives and unintended agent behaviors.
 - **Depend on Rule-Based Experts**: Many "desired" states used in shaped rewards are computed using rule-based planners, effectively capping the performance of the RL agent to that of the expert rules.
4. **Lack of Efficient RL Frameworks**: There is a scarcity of publicly available, efficient RL codebases for simulators like CARLA and nuPlan, hindering reproducible research and further advancement.

Therefore, the **key motivation** is to overcome these challenges by proposing a **new reward design** that is simple, principled, and **scales well with increased mini-batch sizes**, enabling efficient distributed training and learning from significantly larger datasets. This aims to unlock the full potential of RL for developing highly performant and generalized planning policies for autonomous driving.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of CaRL's methodology lies in its **novel reward design** coupled with **systematic optimization for large-scale RL training**.

#### 3.1. Simple Reward Design (CaRL Reward)

The authors propose a simple, intuitive reward function based primarily on **route completion (RC)**, avoiding the complex additive terms of prior work. The reward at time step $t$ is defined as:
$r_t = RC_t \times \prod p_t - T$
Where:
- $RC_t$: Percentage of the route completed during the current time step. This is the primary source of positive reward.
- $\prod p_t$: A multiplicative product of "soft penalty factors" $p_t \in$. If a soft constraint (e.g., speeding, driving outside lanes, comfort violation) is violated, $p_t$ becomes less than 1, reducing the effective route completion reward. Crucially, $p_t > 0$ for penalties the agent cannot avoid early in training to ensure some reward signal.
- $T$: A terminal penalty applied at the end of an episode, dependent on the type of infraction. Hard penalties (e.g., collision, running a red light, off-road) simply terminate the episode, yielding no further reward and thus enforcing constraints. $T=1$ for collisions and red light infractions, and $T=0$ otherwise, to induce an ordering.

This design adheres to three principles: (1) finite obtainable reward, preventing loopholes; (2) specification of *what* to do, not *how*, by avoiding rule-based components; and (3) alignment of the global optimum with the target metric (Driving Score).

#### 3.2. PPO Hyperparameter Optimization and Scalability

- **Addressing Mini-Batch Size Issues**: The authors empirically demonstrate that while the complex Roach reward performs poorly with increased mini-batch sizes (256 to 1024), their simple reward *improves* significantly, outperforming complex rewards. This enables scaling to much larger mini-batch sizes (up to 16384).
- **Adopting Atari Hyperparameters**: The research switches from Roach's default CARLA hyperparameters to the PPO Atari hyperparameter set, which allows for higher learning rates and fewer off-policy steps per iteration. This significantly reduces training time and improves performance.
- **Distributed Data Parallelism (DD-PPO)**: Leveraging the ability to use large mini-batch sizes, CaRL scales training efficiently using DD-PPO. This involves parallelizing data collection across many environments and distributing model inference/training across multiple GPUs (e.g., single 8-GPU node). This allowed scaling to 300 million samples in CARLA and 500 million samples in nuPlan.

#### 3.3. Simulator Optimizations (CARLA)

To make on-policy RL feasible and efficient in the computationally expensive CARLA leaderboard 2.0 environment, several engineering optimizations were implemented:
- **CARLA Environment Specialization**: Each simulator instance is specialized to a particular town, avoiding town reloading during resets, which significantly speeds up episode initialization.
- **Pre-computed Routes**: A* route planning, typically slow, is pre-computed and stored on disk.
- **Asynchronous Collection PPO (AC-PPO)**: The authors introduce AC-PPO, which removes collection synchronization issues in non-homogeneous environments like CARLA by running forward passes concurrently with batch size 1, implemented in C++ (LibTorch) to bypass Python's GIL.
- **Scenario Generation**: An automatic script generates a large volume of unique training routes and scenarios, overcoming the limited number of manually labeled scenarios and preventing overfitting.
- **CPU Mode**: CARLA is run in CPU mode (`-nullrhi`) to save GPU memory.

#### 3.4. Architecture and Input

- **Architecture**: A small CNN processes bird's-eye-view (BEV) semantic segmentation as input, predicting action via a Beta distribution and value via a separate MLP.
- **Layernorm**: Adding layernorm before ReLU activations is found to improve performance, hypothesized to maintain network plasticity essential for non-stationary RL problems.
- **Beta Distribution Restriction**: The Beta distribution parameters ($\alpha, \beta$) are restricted to $\in [1, \infty)$ to ensure unimodal action distributions, avoiding degenerate or multimodal behaviors seen with values $\in (0, 1)$.
- **Expanded Field of View (FOV)**: The input BEV observation range is significantly increased (e.g., 78m front, 50m back) with reduced resolution (2 pixels/meter) to allow the agent to perceive distant, fast-approaching vehicles. Crucially, the A* route mask is only rendered inside intersections to avoid biasing the agent's lane choice.

#### 3.5. nuPlan Adaptations

For the nuPlan simulator, specific adaptations were made:
- **Survival Bonus**: Due to nuPlan's fixed 15-second scene duration, a constant survival bonus is added to the reward to incentivize collision avoidance after route completion, when the primary RC reward signal would otherwise cease.
- **Action Space**: The continuous action space is mapped to longitudinal acceleration and steering rate for nuPlan's kinematic bicycle model, integrating control into the planner.
- **Input**: BEV input channels are adapted for nuPlan's map elements (e.g., route roadblocks, speed limits as polylines).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates CaRL's superior performance and scalability across two challenging autonomous driving benchmarks: CARLA and nuPlan.

#### 4.1. CARLA Longest6 v2 Benchmark

- **Significant Performance Improvement**: CaRL achieves a **64 Driving Score (DS)** on the longest6 v2 benchmark, significantly outperforming prior RL methods: Roach (22 DS) and Think2Drive (7 DS). This represents a 42 DS improvement over Roach and 57 DS over Think2Drive.
- **Improved Safety**: CaRL particularly reduces pedestrian (Ped) and vehicle (Veh) collisions compared to other RL baselines (e.g., 0.01 Ped collisions vs. 0.97 for Think2Drive, 0.36 Veh collisions vs. 2.55 for Think2Drive).
- **Competitive with Imitation Learning**: CaRL (64 DS) slightly outperforms PlanT (62 DS), the state-of-the-art imitation planner, while using a smaller model and less inference compute.
- **Performance vs. Rule-Based**: While still slightly trailing the highly scenario-specialized rule-based PDM-Lite (73 DS), CaRL achieves a much faster average driving speed (16.4 km/h vs. 12.5 km/h for PDM-Lite), as indicated by its 4.5 times lower min-speed infractions (MS). PDM-Lite's high performance is partly attributed to its access to privileged scenario information not available to learning-based methods.
- **Inference Efficiency**: RL-based methods are generally more efficient. CaRL runs 2 times faster at inference (8 ms/step) than PDM-Lite (18 ms/step) and is comparable to other RL baselines.
- **Scalability Demonstration**: The core finding regarding scalability is that the simple reward allows PPO to learn effectively with large mini-batch sizes (e.g., 1024 to 16384), leading to a massive performance improvement of 33 DS when scaling from 10 million to 300 million samples.

#### 4.2. nuPlan Val14 Benchmark

- **Best Learning-Based Performance**: With minimal adaptations, CaRL achieves the highest Closed-Loop Score (CLS) among all learning-based planners on nuPlan Val14, scoring **91.3 in non-reactive traffic and 90.6 in reactive traffic**.
- **Superior to Prior Learning-Based Methods**: This performance is 1.7 points better in non-reactive and 7.9 points better in reactive traffic than the previous best learning-based approach, Diffusion Planner.
- **Competitive with Rule-Based**: CaRL significantly closes the gap with rule-based methods like PDM-Closed (92.8 non-reactive, 92.1 reactive CLS), particularly in reactive traffic where learning-based methods typically struggle.
- **Enhanced Performance with Tuned Reward**: A further model, CaRL (nuPlan tuned), which modifies the reward to closely reflect nuPlan's CLS metric tradeoffs and trains with 1 billion samples, achieves even higher scores of 93.87 (non-reactive) and 93.12 (reactive), **outperforming PDM-Closed** and marking a new state-of-the-art for purely learning-based methods.
- **Extreme Inference Efficiency**: CaRL is remarkably fast on nuPlan, running **7-17 times faster** than other baselines (14 ms/step), attributed to its small 2M parameter network.
- **Controller Impact**: The authors also highlight that the choice of controller can significantly impact reported scores, showing that a more accurate iLQR controller can raise the human performance upper bound on nuPlan by almost 3 CLS points compared to the default LQR.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "CaRL: Learning Scalable Planning Policies with Simple Rewards" carries significant implications for the field of autonomous driving and reinforcement learning:

1. **Paradigm Shift in Reward Design**: CaRL fundamentally challenges the prevailing approach of using complex, hand-shaped, and often rule-dependent reward functions in RL for autonomous driving. By demonstrating that a simple reward function based primarily on route completion, coupled with multiplicative penalties and terminal states for infractions, can achieve superior performance and scalability, the paper advocates for a more principled and less heuristic-driven reward design. This can simplify the reward engineering process and lead to more robust agents.

2. **Unlocking Scalability in RL for AD**: The key finding that simple rewards enable effective training with large mini-batch sizes is crucial. This directly facilitates the use of distributed data parallelism, allowing RL agents to learn from orders of magnitude more data (e.g., 300M in CARLA, 500M-1B in nuPlan). This increased data exposure is vital for agents to learn complex, long-tail behaviors, traditionally a weakness of rule-based systems.

3. **Advancing State-of-the-Art in Learning-Based Planning**: CaRL sets a new benchmark for learning-based privileged planning in both CARLA and nuPlan. Its substantial performance gains over prior RL and even imitation learning methods, particularly in challenging reactive traffic scenarios, signifies a major step forward for end-to-end learning in autonomous driving.

4. **High Inference Efficiency**: The resulting CaRL policies are highly efficient at inference time, being significantly faster than many competitive baselines. This is a critical factor for real-world deployment in resource-constrained automotive platforms.

5. **Fostering Reproducible Research**: The authors commit to releasing their code, which is a commendable effort to promote transparency and allow other researchers to build upon their work. This is particularly important in an area like RL for driving, which often suffers from benchmarking errors and lack of reproducible results.

6. **Insights into RL Optimization**: The paper provides valuable insights into PPO's behavior with different reward structures and mini-batch sizes, highlighting how gradient smoothing can exacerbate local minima issues with complex rewards. The architectural findings (e.g., layernorm for plasticity, Beta distribution parameterization) also contribute to best practices in applying RL to continuous control tasks.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Accelerated Development**: The simplified reward design and demonstrated scalability could significantly accelerate the development and iteration cycle for RL-based AD planners.
- **Improved Robustness and Generalization**: By enabling training on vast datasets, CaRL-like approaches could lead to more robust and generalizable policies that handle a wider array of scenarios than handcrafted rules.
- **Foundation for Sim2Real Transfer**: While not directly addressing Sim2Real transfer in this paper, the improved simulation performance and scalability provide a stronger foundation for future work in transferring these learning-based policies to real-world vehicles.
- **Industry Adoption**: The high performance, efficiency, and principled nature of CaRL make it a promising candidate for further exploration and adaptation by industry, potentially influencing future AD software stacks.

The work does acknowledge limitations, such as focusing on urban driving, not addressing Sim2Real directly, specific scenario types in CARLA, and comfort parameter tuning. However, these are presented as promising directions for future work, indicating the foundational nature of this contribution to scalable and effective reinforcement learning for autonomous driving.
