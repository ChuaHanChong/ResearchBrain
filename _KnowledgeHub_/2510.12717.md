# Residual MPC: Blending Reinforcement Learning with GPU-Parallelized Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A GPU-parallelized residual architecture tightly integrates Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) for robotic locomotion, enabling real-time MPC within RL training. This method extends the robot's trackable velocity range by up to 78% and facilitates zero-shot adaptation to novel terrains and gaits, including self-collision avoidance, validated on the MIT Humanoid.

## Problem

- Traditional Model Predictive Control (MPC) struggles with model inaccuracies, environmental disturbances, and the computational burden of complex, real-world constraints in dynamic robotic locomotion.
- Standalone Reinforcement Learning (RL) often produces physically implausible behaviors, lacks interpretability, and requires extensive reward engineering for stable robotic control.
- Integrating high-frequency, whole-body MPC into RL training loops for legged robots was computationally prohibitive, limiting the exploration of tightly coupled hybrid approaches.

## Method

- A GPU-parallelized floating-base kinodynamic ==Model Predictive Control== (**MPC**) is implemented using CusADi and NVIDIA cuDSS, allowing thousands of 100 Hz MPC instances to run concurrently in simulation.
- A residual policy architecture blends torques from the MPC and a learned Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy, where the RL component provides corrective actions to the stable MPC baseline.
- The RL policy uses a joint-space action representation with a minimal reward function, and training is conducted in IsaacGym with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

## Results

- The GPU-parallelized MPC achieved approximately **2.5** times faster evaluation than CPU-parallelized approaches, enabling concurrent 100 Hz MPC solves for thousands of environments in RL training.
- The residual policy extended the trackable velocity range of the robot by up to **78%** for forward velocity, learned to avoid self-collisions, and demonstrated zero-shot adaptability to unseen gaits and uneven terrains.
- Hardware validation on the MIT Humanoid confirmed stable locomotion, and analysis revealed emergent physically plausible strategies like heel-toe transitions and active ankle control not present in the baseline MPC.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - GPU parallelization of MPC is crucial for enabling tight integration with RL, significantly reducing training time and making high-frequency model-based components feasible within learning loops.
> - Providing a strong MPC prior to the RL policy dramatically enhances sample efficiency, guides exploration towards physically plausible behaviors, and achieves higher asymptotic rewards compared to end-to-end RL.
> - The residual architecture allows the learned policy to selectively address the inherent weaknesses of simplified MPC, such as handling complex non-differentiable constraints like self-collision and adapting to unforeseen environmental changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic locomotion control primarily revolves around two paradigms: **Model Predictive Control (MPC)** and **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**. Each offers distinct advantages and disadvantages, and a significant challenge in robotics research is to combine their strengths while mitigating their weaknesses.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC)** is valued for its interpretability, tunability, and strong grounding in physical models. It can generate physically consistent and constraint-satisfying control inputs by explicitly predicting system evolution over a finite horizon. However, MPC is inherently sensitive to model inaccuracies (e.g., terrain variations, actuator nonlinearities) and environmental disturbances. Its reliance on frequent, real-time optimization also limits the complexity of models and constraints that can be incorporated, often requiring simplifications that reduce robustness in real-world scenarios.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, in contrast, excels at learning robust and adaptive behaviors by optimizing control policies directly from data, often in diverse simulated conditions. RL policies can generalize to unseen terrains and adapt to phenomena difficult to model explicitly. Nevertheless, RL suffers from a "black-box" nature, making interpretation and debugging challenging. It also requires extensive reward engineering and can produce physically implausible or unstable behaviors if not carefully constrained, sometimes exploiting simulation artifacts rather than learning robust dynamics.

The present work fits into the growing research area of **blending MPC and RL** to harness the best of both worlds. Prior approaches to this synthesis include:
- **Hierarchical architectures:** A high-level RL policy learns to output parameters for a lower-level MPC controller.
- **Learning to track MPC outputs:** Policies learn to track predicted states or commands generated by MPC.
- **Embedding learned dynamics/constraints:** Incorporating learned models into the MPC formulation itself.

This paper specifically explores a **parallel or residual policy architecture**, where a fixed control prior (MPC in this case) provides a baseline, and a learned policy makes corrective modifications. While residual learning has been explored with simpler controllers like LQR or pre-trained policies [23–28], integrating a *high-frequency, whole-body MPC* within the RL training loop presents a significant computational bottleneck. Previous attempts to combine MPC and RL in this manner for legged robots, such as Jenelten et al., relied on CPU clusters and evaluated MPC at a much lower frequency (2-3 Hz), leading to weeks of training time.

This work directly addresses this computational challenge by **GPU-parallelizing the MPC formulation**, allowing thousands of MPC instances to be solved concurrently at 100 Hz *within the RL training loop*. This is a crucial advancement, as it enables "in-the-loop" training with a complex, real-time predictive controller at the torque-control level, a first for legged systems to the authors' knowledge. By doing so, the authors aim to combine the interpretability and constraint handling of model-based control with the adaptability and robustness of RL, while overcoming the computational hurdles that previously limited such tight integration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research is to overcome the inherent limitations of both standalone Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) when applied to dynamic robotic locomotion. The authors recognize that:

- **Standalone MPC** is powerful but constrained by model accuracy and real-time computation. Even minor model mismatches or environmental disturbances can destabilize the controller, and incorporating complex, real-world constraints (like self-collision) makes the optimization problem computationally prohibitive at high frequencies.
- **Standalone RL** can yield robust behaviors but often lacks interpretability, suffers from out-of-distribution failures, and demands intensive, often counter-intuitive, reward engineering and curriculum design, sometimes leading to physically implausible solutions.

Given these limitations, the **key objectives** of this work are to:

1. **Develop a tightly integrated control architecture** that blends the outputs of MPC and RL at the torque-control level, leveraging the strengths of each paradigm.
2. **Overcome the computational bottleneck** of incorporating high-frequency MPC into RL training by developing a GPU-parallelized MPC formulation capable of concurrent evaluation across thousands of agents. This is a critical enabler for the proposed residual architecture.
3. **Enhance sample efficiency and asymptotic reward convergence** in RL training by using the MPC as a strong control prior, guiding the policy towards desirable behaviors from the outset and reducing the need for extensive reward engineering.
4. **Expand the operational capabilities** of legged robots beyond standalone MPC, specifically by improving the range of trackable velocity commands and enabling the satisfaction of complex, non-differentiable constraints (e.g., self-collision avoidance) that are difficult to encode in model-based formulations.
5. **Demonstrate zero-shot adaptability** to unforeseen changes in gaits and uneven terrain, without requiring additional training or domain randomization specific to these conditions.
6. **Maintain interpretability and tunability** of the core model-based controller while augmenting it with the adaptive capabilities of RL.
7. **Validate the proposed architecture on hardware** to demonstrate its practical viability for real-world robotic systems, specifically the MIT Humanoid.

Ultimately, the motivation is to achieve more robust, adaptive, and deployable locomotion controllers for complex robotic platforms by intelligently combining the best features of model-based and data-driven control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around a **residual policy architecture** that blends the outputs of a GPU-parallelized Model Predictive Control (MPC) with a learned Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy at the torque-control level.

#### 3.1. MPC Formulation for Legged Locomotion

- **Kinodynamic Floating-Base MPC:** The authors formulate a "floating-base kinodynamic" optimization problem for the MIT Humanoid, striking a balance between oversimplified single rigid-body models and computationally prohibitive whole-body formulations. It considers only the dynamics of the floating base by projecting the full equations of motion, reducing the dimensionality and complexity.
- **Decision Variables:** The optimization is over generalized coordinates (q), velocities ($\dot{q}$), and ground reaction forces (F) for a horizon of T=12 timesteps.
- **Objective Function:** Minimizes the squared error between current and desired states (positions, velocities, forces). Desired trajectories for base height, linear/angular velocities, and joint positions are generated using simple extrapolation heuristics from a command vector.
- **Constraints:** Includes initial state, integration dynamics, floating-base dynamics (Eq. 4), contact constraints (friction cone, zero velocity at contact), swing foot height (defined by a fifth-order Bezier curve with controllable takeoff velocity, touchdown velocity, and swing height), and joint position/velocity limits.
- **Simplifications:** The formulation deliberately omits reasoning about individual joint dynamics/torques within the MPC (relying on inverse dynamics post-solve), bounds on floating-base states, and self-collision constraints to reduce computational complexity. These limitations are intended to be compensated by the residual RL policy.

#### 3.2. GPU-Parallelized Solver Strategy

- **Real-time Iteration (RTI) Scheme:** To achieve high-frequency control, a single Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) iteration is performed per MPC solve, directly trading optimality for responsiveness. This converts the Nonlinear Program (NLP) into a single Quadratic Program (QP).
- **OSQP Solver:** The QP subproblems are solved using the Operator-Splitting Quadratic Program (OSQP) library, which employs the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). ADMM is chosen for its efficiency, as its KKT matrix is seldom modified, allowing for computationally inexpensive projection and matrix-vector operations after an initial factorization.
- **GPU Parallelization with CusADi and cuDSS:**
 - **CusADi:** A code-generation framework used to generate parallelized CUDA kernels for the auxiliary functions of the MPC solver (e.g., initial guess computation, parameter updates, KKT system construction, Ruiz equilibration, ADMM updates, RNEA for inverse dynamics). This offloads these calculations to the GPU for concurrent execution across thousands of simulated environments.
 - **NVIDIA cuDSS:** Used for the most computationally intensive steps: sparse LDL factorization and linear solves of the KKT matrix. This leverages NVIDIA's optimized library for parallel GPU computation of these operations, avoiding millions of instructions in custom-generated kernels and sharing memory efficiently on the GPU.
- **Control Loop:** The MPC calculates desired joint positions, velocities, and feedforward torques from the first timestep of its solution. These are then combined with PD feedback to generate the commanded joint torques.

#### 3.3. Residual Policy Architecture

- **Neural Network:** A simple Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with three hidden layers (256 Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) nodes each) is used as the residual policy.
- **Observations:** The policy observes a comprehensive state vector, including base position/orientation, joint positions, base linear/angular velocities, joint velocities, contact phase variables, and a value from the MPC (o $\in \mathbb{R}^{54}$).
- **Actions:** The policy outputs corrective actions for the 10 leg joints, focusing on locomotion. Arm actions were initially tested but found to have minimal impact, with simple box constraints proving sufficient for the arms in MPC.
- **Reward Design:** A *minimal* set of rewards is used to guide the RL training, aligning with general locomotion objectives rather than complex, task-specific shaping. This includes penalties for action rate, torque, self-collision, and termination, alongside positive rewards for linear velocity tracking, angular velocity tracking, orientation, height, and joint regularization. Notably, self-collision penalties and termination conditions are non-differentiable and sparse, making them ideal targets for RL. The simplicity aims to demonstrate the MPC prior's guidance with minimal reward tuning.
- **Blending Strategy (Joint-Action, Torque-Blending):** Three blending strategies were investigated:
 1. Joint action, joint blending: Policy modifies MPC's joint setpoint.
 2. Joint action, torque blending: Policy outputs joint setpoints relative to default positions, then converted to torques and added to MPC torques. Chosen for robustness.
 3. Torque action, torque blending: Policy directly outputs torques added to MPC torques.
 The "joint action, torque blending" strategy was selected. Here, the residual policy produces joint setpoints, which are then converted into torques (Equation 23). These residual torques are then directly added to the MPC-computed torques (Equation 24) with a scalar blending factor $\lambda = 0.1$. This ensures that even if MPC outputs become unstable, the residual policy's actions remain within a reasonable range relative to a nominal joint configuration.
- **Training Procedure:**
 - **Simulation Environment:** IsaacGym is used for high-performance GPU-based physics simulation, running up to 2048 parallel environments.
 - **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a clipped objective and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is used.
 - **Warm Start:** The policy network is zero-initialized at the start of training to ensure it initially has minimal effect on the stable baseline MPC controller.

#### 3.4. Hardware Validation

The final residual policy is deployed on the MIT Humanoid hardware. The MPC runs onboard at 100 Hz using OSQP on the CPU, and the learned residual network's outputs are added to the MPC torques, demonstrating sim-to-real transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings across the technical and performance aspects of the proposed Residual MPC framework.

#### 4.1. GPU-Parallelization of MPC

- **Computational Efficiency:** The parallelization of the MPC formulation, leveraging CusADi for code generation and NVIDIA cuDSS for sparse linear algebra, achieved a significant speedup. GPU evaluation was approximately 2.5 times faster than an optimistically estimated CPU-parallelized approach for 1,000 environments.
- **Real-time Integration:** While still computationally intensive (a single simulation step for 1,000 environments takes roughly 0.5 seconds), this GPU acceleration enabled the evaluation of high-frequency (100 Hz) MPC instances concurrently within the RL training loop. This was a critical enabler for the residual architecture, overcoming the computational barrier observed in prior work.
- **QP Iterations:** Through ablation studies, it was determined that 25 QP iterations were sufficient to achieve a high survival rate (90%) under initial disturbances, demonstrating a good trade-off between optimality and computational cost.

#### 4.2. Training and Blending Strategy

- **Blending Strategy Comparison:** The authors investigated three blending strategies. They found that a "joint-space action representation" for the residual policy (where the policy outputs joint setpoints) performed significantly better than a "torque-space action representation" (where the policy directly outputs torques). The "joint-action, torque-blending" strategy was chosen for its robustness, as it ensures reasonable policy outputs even if the underlying MPC solution diverges, making actions relative to default joint positions.
- **Sample Efficiency and Asymptotic Reward:** The residual architecture demonstrated substantially higher sample efficiency and converged to a greater asymptotic reward compared to a standalone end-to-end RL policy trained with identical rewards and environmental conditions (Fig. 7). This highlights the benefit of the MPC control prior in guiding the learning process.
- **Qualitative Behavior and Sim-to-Real Potential:** The most striking difference was in the *learned locomotion behaviors*. The end-to-end RL policy learned an "aphysical gliding" motion, rapidly oscillating the ankle joints, which is unlikely to transfer safely to hardware (Fig. 8B). In contrast, the residual policy, guided by the MPC prior, exhibited clear, physically plausible stepping behavior at the same frequency as the MPC (Fig. 8A). Quantitatively, the residual policy showed lower medians and spreads for joint velocities, torques, mechanical power, and ground reaction forces, suggesting higher viability for sim-to-real transfer (Fig. 8C).
- **State Space Exploration:** Visualizations using UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) confirmed that the MPC prior significantly biased the policy's exploration. The residual policy's visited state distribution remained close to the initial MPC distribution throughout training, whereas the end-to-end policy converged to an entirely different and often less desirable region of the state space (Fig. 9). This demonstrates the MPC's role as a "warm start" for RL, shaping exploration towards physically grounded behaviors.

#### 4.3. Performance and Adaptability

- **Expanded Trackable Velocities:** The residual policy significantly extended the range of trackable velocity commands beyond the capabilities of the baseline MPC, achieving approximately 78% improvement in forward velocity ($v_x$), 12% in lateral velocity ($v_y$), and 9% in yaw angular velocity ($\omega_z$) (Fig. 10).
- **Self-Collision Avoidance:** Crucially, the residual policy learned to avoid self-collisions (e.g., knee-to-knee collisions during aggressive turns), a constraint that was not explicitly included in the simplified MPC formulation and is computationally expensive to encode in model-based methods. The residual policy achieved this by modifying MPC outputs to raise the base height and adjust swing trajectories (Fig. 11).
- **Zero-Shot Adaptability:**
 - **Unseen Gaits:** The residual policy demonstrated zero-shot adaptation to changes in the MPC's requested contact schedule, such as double stance and flight phases, which it had never experienced during training (Fig. 12). The standalone MPC failed under these conditions, but the residual policy stabilized the robot.
 - **Uneven Terrain:** Without any fine-tuning or domain randomization, the residual policy successfully navigated various uneven and sloped terrains, while the MPC baseline quickly failed due to incorrect assumptions about ground height (Fig. 13). The policy also adapted immediately when MPC parameters like swing height were modified to handle more pronounced steps.
- **Hardware Validation:** The residual architecture was successfully deployed on the MIT Humanoid, demonstrating stable forward, lateral, and turning locomotion (Fig. 14). This validates the sim-to-real transfer potential, although the authors noted discrepancies between the custom simulator and hardware, indicating an existing sim-to-real gap.

#### 4.4. Residual Policy Analysis

- **Asymmetric Activity:** The residual policy's torque contribution was found to be asymmetric with respect to commanded velocities. It was more "active" for forward velocity than backward, and for specific lateral/angular velocity combinations that increased the risk of self-collision (Fig. 15). This indicates the residual policy is selectively engaged where the MPC is weakest or where unmodeled constraints become critical.
- **Modulation Near Contact Switches:** Analysis revealed a significant increase in the residual torque ratio near contact switches (just before planned touchdown). The cosine similarity between residual and MPC torques showed that the residual torques often *opposed* the MPC outputs near these critical contact events (Fig. 16).
- **Emergent Locomotion Strategies:** The residual policy exhibited emergent heel-toe transitions and more pronounced ankle strategies (larger reaction forces at touchdown, earlier takeoff of the foot center) that were not present in the flat-footed MPC or explicitly rewarded (Fig. 17). This suggests the RL component learns critical contact dynamics and ankle strategies for robust ground interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic locomotion by effectively bridging the gap between model-based control and reinforcement learning.

#### 5.1. Significance

1. **Computational Breakthrough:** The development of a GPU-parallelized MPC framework that can execute high-frequency optimal control problems concurrently within an RL training loop is a major contribution. This breaks a long-standing computational bottleneck, enabling a new class of hybrid MPC-RL controllers that were previously infeasible due to computational demands.
2. **Synergistic Control Paradigm:** The "Residual MPC" architecture successfully combines the interpretability, physical grounding, and constraint handling of MPC with the robustness, adaptability, and generalization capabilities of RL. It demonstrates that a physically consistent prior can effectively guide learning, producing superior results than either standalone method.
3. **Reduced Reward Engineering:** By providing a strong model-based prior, the residual architecture significantly reduces the burden of complex reward engineering and curriculum design, which are notorious challenges in end-to-end RL. The policy is biased towards desirable, physically plausible behaviors, even with minimal reward shaping.
4. **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The work shows that the residual policy can overcome inherent limitations of the simplified MPC, such as self-collision avoidance and adaptation to unseen gaits and uneven terrain, without explicit modeling or extensive domain randomization during training. This zero-shot adaptation is particularly impactful for deployment in unstructured environments.
5. **Improved Sim-to-Real Potential:** By steering RL towards physically consistent behaviors and preventing the exploitation of simulation artifacts (as seen in the "gliding" behavior of end-to-end RL), the residual architecture inherently improves the likelihood of successful sim-to-real transfer, making learned controllers safer and more reliable for hardware.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **More Capable Legged Robots:** This approach could lead to the development of more robust, agile, and versatile legged robots capable of operating in highly dynamic and unstructured real-world environments with greater autonomy and less human intervention.
2. **Generalization to Complex Robotic Systems:** The methodology for GPU-parallelizing optimal control and integrating it with RL through a residual architecture is not limited to legged locomotion and could be extended to other complex robotic systems involving manipulation, aerial robotics, or human-robot interaction where high-fidelity control and adaptability are crucial.
3. **New Research Directions in Hybrid Control:** The work opens several avenues for future research, including:
 - **Learning MPC Parameters:** Training RL to directly output or adapt MPC parameters (e.g., cost function weights, contact schedules) dynamically.
 - **Uncertainty Estimation:** Using the value or sensitivity of the MPC solution to estimate model uncertainty and adaptively balance the influence of the learned policy versus the model-based controller.
 - **Novel Network Architectures:** Designing neural networks that inherently mimic the structure and operations of optimal control solvers (e.g., factorization and iterative updates), potentially leading to even faster and more interpretable learning.
4. **Safer and More Reliable Robot Deployment:** By grounding learning in physical models and enabling robust adaptation, this framework contributes to the development of controllers that are not only high-performing but also safer and more reliable for deployment on expensive and delicate robotic hardware.

In conclusion, "Residual MPC" offers a compelling blueprint for how advanced model-based control and modern reinforcement learning can be effectively combined to push the boundaries of robotic capabilities.
