# STEAM: Self-Supervised Temporal Ensemble Advantage Modeling for Real-World Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed STEAM, a self-supervised framework that uses temporal ordering within expert demonstrations to generate frame-level advantage scores for robot learning. This approach achieved up to 59% higher success rates compared to baselines in real-world manipulation tasks, effectively distinguishing task-advancing actions in mixed-quality datasets and improving execution throughput.

## Problem

- Effectively utilizing large, heterogeneous robot datasets (expert, suboptimal rollouts, human corrections) due to varying quality within episodes.
- High supervision cost for current reward or value models (human annotations, hand-crafted rewards) limits scalability in robot learning.
- Existing progress estimators often suffer from noise, lack of physical grounding, and overestimation of advantages on out-of-distribution (OOD) states.

## Method

- A self-supervised approach, **STEAM**, derives ==frame-level advantages== by learning ==normalized temporal offsets== from expert demonstration frames, including reversed pairs to model regressions.
- An ensemble of ==distributional temporal offset predictors== is trained, and the final advantage is computed as the ==minimum across ensemble members== to ensure conservative, robust estimation on OOD data.
- These learned frame-level advantages are converted into binary optimality labels and integrated with ==Classifier-Free Guidance Reinforcement Learning (CFGRL)== to prioritize high-quality transitions during policy training.

## Results

- STEAM reliably distinguishes task-advancing actions at a frame level, with ASTEAMA_{STEAM}ASTEAM curves and distributions clearly differentiating expert, successful, and failed robot rollouts across various manipulation tasks.
- Integration of STEAM advantages into policy learning frameworks led to significant performance gains, with success rates increasing by up to **59%** (e.g., **92.3%** for bimanual towel folding) and improved execution throughput compared to baseline methods.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that both the fine-grained discretization of temporal offsets (e.g., **N=32** bins) and the conservative ensemble aggregation (e.g., **M=3** members) are essential for STEAM's superior performance, especially in mitigating OOD overestimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The relative temporal order of frames within expert trajectories serves as a powerful, label-free, and self-supervised signal for fine-grained credit assignment in robot tasks.
> - A conservative ensemble aggregation strategy (taking the minimum advantage across multiple predictors) is crucial to prevent overestimation on out-of-distribution samples, leading to more reliable training signals.
> - Discretizing continuous temporal offsets into bins and normalizing them by trajectory length allows for consistent and robust modeling of progress across varying episode durations and complexities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly leveraging large, heterogeneous datasets that combine expert demonstrations with suboptimal policy rollouts and human corrections. A significant challenge in this context is effectively utilizing these mixed-quality data sources, as traditional trajectory-level filtering can discard useful segments or retain detrimental ones. This necessitates fine-grained, frame-level credit assignment to differentiate between task-advancing and regressive actions.

Current approaches often involve a two-stage pipeline: first learning an intermediate reward or value signal, and then converting it into an advantage for policy learning (e.g., weighted behavior cloning or offline reinforcement learning). These reward/value models typically rely on substantial supervision such as human annotations, hand-crafted reward functions, or pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs). However, these methods face several limitations:
1. **High Supervision Cost**: Manual annotations and hand-crafted rewards are labor-intensive and impede scalability. Cross-trajectory calibration further complicates these efforts.
2. **Noise and Lack of Physical Grounding**: VLM-based reward models, despite broad pretraining, can produce noisy frame-level signals, especially when visual-language priors are not physically grounded in robotic tasks.
3. **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization**: Many progress estimators assume an absolute or monotonic notion of progress, which is often not applicable to real-world rollouts that may involve temporary regressions, recoveries from mistakes, or deviations from the expert distribution. Applying learned models to such OOD states can lead to overestimation of advantages, generating misleading training signals.

STEAM positions itself in this landscape by proposing a label-free, self-supervised alternative to derive frame-level advantages. Instead of learning an absolute reward or value, it focuses on the relative temporal order of frames within expert trajectories. This approach aligns with prior work that leverages temporal structure for self-supervised video representation learning and dense reward signals (e.g., TimeRewarder, ReWiND, VLAC). However, STEAM distinguishes itself by directly predicting per-frame advantages for offline data quality assessment, rather than solely generating dense rewards for online reinforcement learning, and by employing a conservative ensemble strategy to mitigate OOD overestimation, a common problem in reward modeling. It builds upon ideas of using relative progress (like ARM, which uses manual annotations) but removes the need for such external labels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for robust, label-free, frame-level advantage estimation in real-world robot learning. The motivation stems from the inherent challenges of effectively utilizing heterogeneous robot datasets, which frequently contain a mix of useful progress and suboptimal behaviors (stalls, corrections, failures).

The specific motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

- **Address the Need for Fine-Grained Credit Assignment**: As robot foundation models increasingly rely on large, diverse datasets, distinguishing high-quality, task-advancing actions from suboptimal ones at a frame-level becomes crucial. Trajectory-level filtering is insufficient for data that changes quality within a single episode. The goal is to provide a granular assessment for more effective policy learning.
- **Overcome Limitations of Existing Reward/Value Models**:
 - **Reduce Supervision Reliance**: Traditional methods require significant human effort (hand-crafted rewards, human annotations) or extensive pretraining (VLM-based models). A key motivation is to develop a self-supervised approach that eliminates the need for such external supervision, thereby improving scalability.
 - **Enhance Robustness to Noise and OOD Data**: Learned reward/value models can be noisy, lack physical grounding, or exhibit overconfidence on states outside their training distribution. This overestimation can lead to false-positive training signals that guide policy optimization towards undesirable behaviors. The objective is to design a system that is resilient to these issues.
- **Leverage Inherent Temporal Structure as Supervision**: Instead of external labels, the authors propose to utilize the relative temporal order of frames within expert demonstrations as a dense, self-supervised signal. This intrinsic signal is local to each trajectory and avoids the complexities of cross-trajectory calibration or pre-defined global progress curves.
- **Enable Conservative Advantage Estimation**: Recognizing the issue of overestimation when applying models trained on expert data to OOD (e.g., suboptimal rollout) samples, a core objective is to incorporate a mechanism that produces conservative, robust advantage estimates. This leads to the ensemble-based approach to suppress false positives.
- **Improve Policy Learning from Mixed-Quality Data**: The ultimate goal is to integrate these learned frame-level advantages into policy learning frameworks, such as Classifier-Free Guidance Reinforcement Learning (CFGRL), to selectively prioritize high-quality transitions from mixed-quality datasets (expert demonstrations, human corrections, policy rollouts) and achieve substantial improvements in robot policy performance in real-world settings.

In summary, STEAM aims to provide an accurate, scalable, and robust method for identifying "good" actions within diverse robot datasets by exploiting temporal cues, ultimately leading to more effective robot policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

STEAM (Self-supervised Temporal Ensemble Advantage Modeling) is a label-free framework designed to learn frame-level advantages from expert demonstrations and robustly score mixed-quality data for policy refinement. The methodology involves three main stages: defining a self-supervised temporal learning target, training an ensemble of distributional temporal offset predictors, and integrating the resulting advantages into a policy learning framework.

#### 3.1. Normalized Temporal Offset as Learning Target

- **Self-Supervised Temporal Targets**: The core idea is to derive supervisory signals from the inherent temporal progression within expert trajectories. For any two frames, $f_{k,i}$ and $f_{k,j}$, from an expert episode $\tau_k$, the temporal offset is defined as $\Delta(f_{k,i}, f_{k,j}) = j - i$. Positive offsets indicate forward progress. To also learn about regressive behaviors, reversed frame pairs from successful trajectories are used, which create pseudo-failure sequences with negative offsets, eliminating the need for explicit failure demonstrations.
- **Trajectory-Length Normalization**: Raw temporal offsets are not comparable across trajectories of varying lengths. To address this, offsets are normalized by the ratio of a maximum episode length ($L_{max}$) to the current episode length ($L_{\tau_k}$): $\tilde{\Delta}_{\tau_k}(i, j) = (j - i) \cdot \frac{L_{max}}{L_{\tau_k}}$. This normalization standardizes the temporal offset targets, ensuring consistent supervision regardless of episode duration.

#### 3.2. Distributional Temporal Offset Predictor

- **Categorical Prediction and Bin Assignment**: Directly predicting continuous temporal offsets is challenging for long, high-frequency robot episodes. Instead, the continuous normalized offsets $\tilde{\Delta}_{\tau_k}(i, j)$ are bounded within $[-\tilde{\Delta}_{max}, \tilde{\Delta}_{max}]$ and discretized into $N$ evenly spaced bins. This creates a one-hot target vector $\tilde{\Delta}_B^{\tau_k}(i, j)$ of length $N$. An ensemble of $M$ predictors, $p_{\theta}(\Delta | f_{k,i}, f_{k,j}, \ell)$, is trained. Each predictor maps an expert frame pair $(f_{k,i}, f_{k,j})$ and its natural language instruction $\ell$ to a categorical distribution over these $N$ temporal bins. Training is performed by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between the one-hot target and the predicted categorical distribution (Equation 3).
- **Advantage Modeling**: A scalar advantage score is derived from the learned distributional predictor to quantify a frame's contribution to task progress. For a frame $f_{k,i}$, the model predicts the temporal offset to a future frame $f_{k,i+H}$ (using a fixed lookahead $H$). The predicted categorical distribution is converted into an expected bin index. The advantage score $A(f_{k,i}; \theta)$ is then computed as the normalized expected bin index minus the ground-truth quantized temporal offset (Equation 4). This formulation implicitly rewards efficient execution (shorter, faster expert demonstrations) by the trajectory-length normalization.
- **Ensemble for Overestimation Control**: To counteract the tendency of a single predictor to overestimate advantages on out-of-distribution (OOD) samples (common in mixed-quality rollouts), an ensemble strategy is employed. $M$ independent temporal offset predictors are trained with distinct random initializations. The final STEAM advantage, $A_{STEAM}(f_{k,i}; \Theta)$, is defined as the minimum predicted advantage across all ensemble members (Equation 5). This conservative minimum aggregation aims to suppress false positives by penalizing high variance among ensemble members in unfamiliar state spaces.

#### 3.3. Policy Training with $A_{STEAM}$

- **Optimality Labeling**: After training the STEAM ensemble, $A_{STEAM}$ scores are computed for all frames in the heterogeneous training data (expert demonstrations, policy rollouts, human corrections). These continuous advantages are converted into binary optimality labels $o_{k,i} \in \{0, 1\}$. To account for differing advantage distributions across data sources, quantile thresholding is applied separately to expert data ($D_{exp}$) and non-expert data ($D_{nexp}$). A frame $f_{k,i}$ is labeled as optimal if its $A_{STEAM}$ score is above a source-specific q-quantile threshold ($\delta_q$) (Equation 6).
- **Integration with CFGRL**: These binary optimality labels $o_{k,i}$ are then used as the optimality condition within Classifier-Free Guidance Reinforcement Learning (CFGRL). This guides the policy to prioritize action generation towards samples identified as high-quality local progress by STEAM. During training, a flow-matching policy $v_{\phi}$ is conditioned on these labels, with a random dropout probability for the conditioning label to enable classifier-free guidance. During inference, a guided velocity field $v_{cfg}$ extrapolates towards the high-optimality label (o=1) using a guidance scale $w \geq 1$ to steer generated actions (Equations 9-11 in Appendix A).

The visual and language backbones for the advantage predictors are SigLIP-SO400M and Gemma-3-270M, respectively. The policy backbone is $\pi_0$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates STEAM across four real-world robot manipulation tasks: bimanual towel folding, chip checkout, cola restocking, and single-arm pick-and-place. The experiments address three key questions regarding STEAM's ability to distinguish frame quality, improve policy performance, and the impact of its design choices.

#### 4.1. STEAM Can Distinguish Task-Advancing Actions (Q1)

- **Qualitative Assessment (Figure 4, 8, 9, 10)**: Visualizations of $A_{STEAM}$ curves demonstrate its capability to provide fine-grained, frame-level assessment of task execution.
 - **Expert Demonstrations**: Advantages remain consistently high, with transient drops only for necessary retries or minor adjustments.
 - **Successful Rollouts**: Advantages are generally lower and more fluctuating than expert data, reflecting slower but still effective execution.
 - **Failed Rollouts**: Advantages rapidly drop to near-zero or negative values after the policy enters a failure state, indicating stagnation.
 - **Human Correction Episodes**: Initial drops in advantage during policy failure are followed by clear recovery after human intervention, demonstrating sensitivity to recovery phases.
 - Detailed visualizations for chip checkout, cola restocking, and pick-and-place further support these observations, showing how STEAM captures grasping failures, slow progress, handovers, and successful recoveries across tasks.
- **Quantitative Assessment (Figures 5 and 6)**:
 - **Frame-level Probability Density (Figure 5)**: Expert demonstrations show a high concentration of $A_{STEAM}$ near +1, indicating proficient execution. Successful rollouts exhibit a shift towards lower positive values. Failed rollouts show a prominent peak around or below zero, reflecting lack of progress. Human correction episodes present a broader distribution, combining lower autonomous advantages with higher recovery advantages. This consistently differentiates data quality across all tasks.
 - **Episode-wise Sum of Advantages (Figure 6)**: The cumulative sum of $A_{STEAM}$ effectively discriminates between successful and failed episodes. Failed rollouts consistently have significantly lower sums. Differences in cumulative advantages between tasks (e.g., higher for towel folding/chip checkout failures than cola/pick-and-place failures) are attributed to distinct failure modes (late-stage vs. initial grasp failures), further demonstrating STEAM's sensitivity to task progression.

#### 4.2. STEAM Enhances Policy Performance (Q2)

- **Comparison with Baselines (Table 1)**: STEAM consistently achieves the highest performance across all four tasks when compared to Behavior Cloning (BC), HG-DAgger, and RECAP.
 - **Success Rate**: STEAM significantly improves success rates, e.g., by 59% (to 92.3%) for towel folding, 54.3% (to 93.8%) for chip checkout, 23% (to 75%) for cola restocking, and 16.2% (to 80%) for pick-and-place over baselines.
 - **Progress Score and Throughput**: Beyond success rates, STEAM also improves task progress scores and execution throughput. For instance, in towel folding, STEAM increases throughput to 58 successful episodes/hour, whereas RECAP (which failed to filter slow-progress frames) resulted in lower throughput than BC. This indicates STEAM's ability to prune stagnant, low-quality frames, leading to more efficient policy execution.
- **Impact of Data Combinations (Figure 7)**:
 - STEAM effectively filters high-quality frames from human corrections and autonomous rollouts, significantly broadening the policy's state-action coverage and improving success rates compared to training solely on expert demonstrations (e.g., STEAM (Full) vs. STEAM (Exp) for towel folding).
 - For tasks with highly proficient and clean expert data (e.g., pick-and-place), STEAM (Exp) might lead to a slight performance decline compared to BC, as filtering might shrink an already small, high-quality dataset. However, when combined with imperfect rollout and correction data (STEAM (Full)), performance recovers and surpasses BC, demonstrating the benefit of leveraging diverse data sources with robust advantage signals.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies on STEAM Design Choices (Q3)

- **Effect of Bin Count N (Table 2)**: Increasing the number of bins ($N$) from 2 to 32 significantly improves policy performance (e.g., success rate from 27.3% to 92.3% for towel folding). This indicates that finer-grained temporal progress modeling provides a more informative advantage signal.
- **Effect of Ensemble Size M (Table 3)**:
 - Increasing ensemble size from $M=1$ (single predictor) to $M=3$ (default) substantially improves success rate from 72.7% to 92.3%. This confirms that conservative ensemble aggregation effectively suppresses false-positive signals from overconfident individual predictors on OOD transitions.
 - Further increasing to $M=5$ does not yield additional performance gains, suggesting that $M=3$ strikes a balance between performance and computational efficiency.
- **Effectiveness of Conservative Ensemble Aggregation (Figure 11, Appendix E.4)**: Qualitative analysis demonstrates how individual ensemble members can diverge and overestimate advantages (e.g., one predictor remaining confidently high during a regression phase). The minimum aggregation strategy of STEAM successfully suppresses these false positives, resulting in a more accurate advantage curve that reflects actual task progress.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of STEAM represents a significant contribution to real-world robot learning, particularly in addressing the challenges associated with heterogeneous and mixed-quality datasets. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Reduced Annotation Burden and Increased Scalability**: By leveraging the inherent temporal structure of expert demonstrations as a self-supervised signal, STEAM eliminates the need for manual reward annotations or hand-crafted reward functions. This drastically reduces the human supervision burden, making robot policy learning more scalable and applicable to a wider range of complex, long-horizon tasks where dense reward engineering is impractical.
- **Robustness to Mixed-Quality Data**: STEAM's ability to provide fine-grained, frame-level advantage estimates allows for the effective utilization of suboptimal policy rollouts and human corrections. This is crucial for broadening state-action coverage and improving policy robustness, as it enables the system to intelligently select useful segments from imperfect trajectories rather than discarding entire episodes.
- **Mitigation of Overestimation in OOD States**: The conservative ensemble aggregation strategy is a key innovation for real-world application. It directly addresses the problem of overconfident reward/value models on out-of-distribution data, which can lead to false-positive training signals and suboptimal policy behavior. By suppressing these overestimations, STEAM provides more reliable guidance for policy optimization.
- **Improved Policy Performance in Real-World Tasks**: The empirical results demonstrate substantial improvements in success rates, task progress scores, and execution throughput across diverse real-world manipulation tasks. This suggests that STEAM is a practical and effective framework for enhancing robot learning systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work opens avenues for future research. The authors note limitations such as STEAM's current inability to differentiate unequal contributions of different task phases to overall progress. Integrating "structure-aware" methods that assign varying reward signals to task stages (e.g., SPARS) could be a direction. Additionally, incorporating robot state information beyond visual observations could enhance STEAM's capacity to identify subtle, non-visually observable errors.

In conclusion, STEAM advances the field by offering a self-supervised, robust, and scalable method for credit assignment in robot learning, paving the way for more efficient and effective utilization of diverse real-world robot data.
