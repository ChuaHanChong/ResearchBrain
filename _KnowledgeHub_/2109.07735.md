# Decentralized Control of Quadrotor Swarms with End-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A decentralized quadrotor swarm controller learned end-to-end with multi-agent PPO, mapping each drone's own state plus a permutation-invariant encoding of its K nearest neighbors directly to motor thrusts. Trained on a billion steps in a noise-modeled Crazyflie simulator, it flies dense formations, swaps, avoids moving obstacles, pursues evaders, scales to 128 drones, and transfers zero-shot to real hardware.

## Problem

- Classical multi-quadrotor planners need full global state and heavy offline computation, are brittle under partial observability, and scale combinatorially with team size.
- Voronoi-cell and velocity-obstacle collision avoidance heavily constrain the configuration space, forbidding aggressive maneuvers and occasionally deadlocking, or require explicit neighbor models.
- Centralized Q-networks scale poorly since input size grows with team count, and graph networks need multi-round message passing too slow for nano-quadrotor control rates.

## Method

- Fully decentralized policy maps proprioceptive state plus relative positions and velocities of K neighbors to four motor thrusts, trained by ==PPO== within the asynchronous ==SampleFactory== framework.
- Two ==permutation-invariant== neighborhood encoders: a ==deep-sets encoder== averaging per-neighbor embeddings, and an ==attention encoder== softmax-weighting neighbors by learned relevance scores.
- Reward sums goal-distance, collision indicator plus smooth linear-falloff ==proximity penalty==, and stabilizing auxiliary terms; simulator injects motor lag, thrust and sensor noise, and randomized collision impulses.

## Results

- Attention beat deep sets, plain MLP, and blind baselines over **1B** steps; non-permutation-invariant MLP failed collision avoidance, showing permutation invariance is necessary.
- Scaling from **8** to **128** drones raised collisions to **8.63** per minute per drone, but **200M** extra steps (**20%** of training, ~**4 hours**) cut this to **1.37**.
- On real Crazyflies a ~**10^3**-parameter 16x8 deep-sets policy ran at **500Hz**, reaching **4 m/s** and **7 m/s^2** versus **1 m/s** and **3 m/s^2** for the PID plus buffered-Voronoi baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Permutation- and scale-invariant neighbor encoders let one policy generalize across team sizes; attention adds value mainly in dense configurations where neighbor importance differs.
> - Learned attention weights prioritize neighbors on collision courses, indicating relative velocity matters more than distance for reactive avoidance.
> - Modeling motor lag, sensor noise, and crude randomized collision impulses suffices for zero-shot transfer; unmodeled downwash was tolerated, and tiny networks preserved performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of multi-robot systems, particularly drone swarms, is a critical area of robotics research with extensive applications in fields like area coverage, reconnaissance, and logistics. Historically, the planning and control of drone teams have relied on **classical methods** such as Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT), Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM), and kinodynamic planning. While these methods can generate collision-free trajectories, they suffer from several significant limitations:
- **Requirement for full state information:** They often necessitate a complete understanding of the environment and all agents' states.
- **Extensive computational resources:** Planning in advance for large teams can be computationally intensive, limiting real-time applicability.
- **Curse of dimensionality:** The complexity grows combinatorially with the number of robots, hindering scalability.
- **Brittleness in partially observable environments:** Their performance degrades when full information is unavailable.
- **Dependence on precise dynamic models:** Kinodynamic planners require accurate models of drone dynamics.
- **Constraints on maneuvers:** Classical collision avoidance techniques (e.g., n-body collision avoidance, Buffered Voronoi Cells, chance-constrained safety sets) often overly constrain the control space, preventing aggressive maneuvers or leading to deadlocks.

In response to these challenges, **learning-based approaches** have emerged, leveraging the power of deep learning and reinforcement learning (RL). Prior work has explored imitation learning from centralized planners and various forms of RL for single and multi-agent quadrotor control. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have also shown promise in parameterizing policies for swarm control due to their ability to handle permutation invariance and local information aggregation. However, many existing learning-based methods either struggle with direct control (e.g., operating at a higher abstraction level than direct motor thrusts), lack demonstrated zero-shot transferability to real hardware for complex swarm behaviors, or face computational overheads (like multi-layer GNNs requiring frequent message exchanges) that make them impractical for resource-constrained platforms.

This paper positions itself as a significant advancement by presenting a **fully learned, end-to-end deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approach** that directly controls motor thrusts based solely on local observations. Crucially, it claims two novel contributions that address the aforementioned limitations:
1. It is the first approach to demonstrate scalable coordinated flight of drone swarms in a realistic physical simulation achieved through end-to-end reinforcement learning with direct thrust control.
2. It is the first to demonstrate that the learned policies can successfully transfer zero-shot to physical drone teams.

By tackling direct thrust control, decentralization with local observations, and robust sim-to-real transfer, this work aims to bridge the gap between theoretical multi-agent control and practical, agile, real-world drone swarm deployment, offering a compelling alternative to traditional planning and control methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **learn decentralized quadrotor swarm controllers that are zero-shot transferable to real quadrotors** through large-scale multi-agent end-to-end deep reinforcement learning. This central goal is driven by several motivations aimed at overcoming the limitations of existing methods:

1. **Overcome Limitations of Classical Planning:** The authors are motivated by the inherent drawbacks of state-of-the-art classical planning algorithms, which often demand full state information, extensive computational resources, and precise dynamic models. These limitations severely restrict their applicability in real-time, partially observable, and dynamic environments, especially as swarm size increases. The objective is to develop a system that requires minimal computation during execution and operates solely on local observations.
2. **Enable Scalable Decentralized Control:** A primary motivation is to create controllers that scale effectively with the size of the swarm without suffering from the curse of dimensionality. The decentralized approach, where each drone relies only on its own state and local neighborhood information, is key to achieving this scalability and mirroring real-world constraints where global state information is often unavailable.
3. **Achieve Agile and Aggressive Maneuvers:** Unlike classical methods that often constrain the control space to guarantee safety, this research aims to learn policies that can perform aggressive maneuvers in tight formations, break and re-establish formations, and coordinate efficiently in complex tasks like pursuit-evasion. The end-to-end DRL approach, directly controlling motor thrusts, is expected to fully exploit the quadrotor's dynamic capabilities.
4. **Facilitate Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A critical practical motivation is to achieve seamless transfer of learned policies from simulation to physical hardware without any additional real-world training or fine-tuning. This requires a highly realistic simulator that accounts for hardware imperfections, making the learned policies robust to real-world noise and disturbances.
5. **Progress Towards Hardware-Agnostic Deployment:** By learning policies from scratch without relying on tuned PID controllers or specific drone models, the method aims to be model-agnostic. This means policies could theoretically be learned for quadrotors with different physical parameters by simply updating the simulator, paving the way for more flexible and adaptable swarm systems.

In essence, the research seeks to demonstrate that deep reinforcement learning can provide a robust, scalable, and agile control solution for drone swarms, directly addressing the practical challenges of deploying multi-UAV systems in complex, dynamic, and partially observable real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on **multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (DRL)**, leveraging a highly realistic simulator for training and subsequent zero-shot transfer to physical quadrotors.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The task involves controlling a team of $N$ quadrotor drones in a simulated 3D environment. Each quadrotor $i$ learns a decentralized control policy $\pi_\theta(a^{(t)}|o^{(t)})$, which maps its local observations $o^{(t)}_i$ at time $t$ to continuous motor thrusts $a^{(t)}_i$. The objective is to minimize distances to desired formation positions while avoiding collisions with other drones and obstacles.
- **Quadrotor State:** $s^{(t)}_i = (p, v, R, \omega)$, representing position, linear velocity, rotation matrix, and angular velocity, respectively, relative to its goal.
- **Observations:** $o^{(t)}_i = (s^{(t)}_i, \eta^{(t)}_i)$, where $\eta^{(t)}_i$ includes relative positions ($\tilde{p}^{(t)}_{ij}$) and velocities ($\tilde{v}^{(t)}_{ij}$) of $K$ nearest neighbors. $K$ is kept small ($K \ll N$) for scalability.
- **Actions:** Continuous actions $a^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^4$ are transformed into non-negative motor thrusts $f^{(t)} \in^4$.

#### 3.2. Simulation and Sim-to-Real Considerations

The control policies are trained in a custom-built simulation environment based on prior work, augmented for swarms. A crucial aspect for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is the realistic modeling of hardware imperfections:
- **Drone Dynamics:** Modeled after the Crazyflie 2.0 platform.
- **Non-ideal Motors:** Includes motor-lag and thrust noise.
- **Imperfect Observations:** Noise is injected into position, orientation, and velocity estimations, with parameters derived from real quadrotor data.
- **Collision Model:** A randomized model applies opposite forces and torques upon collision to preserve momentum, encouraging collision avoidance.

#### 3.3. Training Setup

- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), implemented using the high-throughput asynchronous RL framework "SampleFactory" to support large-scale multi-agent learning (thousands of simulated quadrotors simultaneously).
- **Reward Function:** A composite reward $r^{(t)} = r^{(t)}_{pos} + r^{(t)}_{col} + r^{(t)}_{aux}$ is designed:
 - $r^{(t)}_{pos}$: Negative squared distance to the target position.
 - $r^{(t)}_{col}$: Penalizes actual collisions and includes a smooth proximity penalty with linear falloff to encourage minimal separation.
 - $r^{(t)}_{aux}$: Auxiliary rewards penalize high angular velocity, high motor thrusts, and large rotations about horizontal axes to facilitate stable flight learning.
- **Diverse Training Scenarios:** Policies are trained in a rich set of procedurally generated scenarios to promote robustness and generalization:
 - **Static Formations:** Various 2D and 3D geometric shapes (grid, circle, cylinder, cube), including a "same goal" scenario demanding high density.
 - **Dynamic Formations:** Shrinking separations, teleporting formations, and "swarm-vs-swarm" goal swaps requiring aggressive maneuvers through each other.
 - **Evader Pursuit:** Swarm pursues a moving target with Lissajous or Bezier curve trajectories.
- **Obstacle Avoidance:** For scenarios with dynamic obstacles, quadrotor observations are augmented with obstacle state (radius, relative position, velocity), processed by an additional MLP.

#### 3.4. Model Architectures

Policies are parameterized by neural networks that output parameters of a Gaussian distribution for continuous actions.
- **Core Structure:** Quadrotor's own state ($s$) and neighborhood information ($\eta$) are embedded into vectors ($e_s$, $e_\eta$) before being fed into a final fully-connected network ($\phi_a$) to produce action means.
- **Neighborhood Encoders (Permutation Invariant):**
 - **Deep Sets:** Applies a learned transformation $\psi_\eta$ to each neighbor's features, followed by mean aggregation to achieve permutation and scale invariance.
 - **Attention-based:** Computes attention weights for each neighbor based on the current quadrotor's state and neighbor observations, allowing the policy to prioritize important neighbors. This produces a weighted sum of neighbor embeddings.
- **Value Function:** Shares the same architecture as the policy but outputs a single value estimate.

#### 3.5. Physical Deployment

- **Hardware:** Crazyflie 2.0 nano-quadrotors, known for their resource constraints (168MHz microcontroller, 192KB RAM).
- **Model Simplification:** An ablation study was conducted to train a significantly smaller version of the Deep Sets model (16 and 8 neurons in hidden layers for self and neighbor encoders respectively) to fit the hardware's computational and memory limits.
- **Sensing:** Vicon system provides 100 Hz position and velocity updates (with low-pass filtering). IMU measurements are integrated by the 500 Hz on-board controller.

This comprehensive methodology, spanning advanced simulation, sophisticated DRL techniques, and careful consideration for hardware deployment, is designed to achieve robust and transferable swarm control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from extensive simulations and successful physical deployments, validating the efficacy and transferability of their DRL approach for quadrotor swarm control.

#### 4.1. Model Architecture Study (Simulation)

- **Permutation Invariance is Crucial:** Baselines like a "blind" agent (no neighborhood info) and a plain Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) (non-permutation invariant) failed to effectively avoid collisions. This underscored the necessity of permutation-invariant architectures for swarm control.
- **Deep Sets vs. Attention:** Both Deep Sets and attention-based encoders demonstrated effective goal-reaching and collision avoidance. However, the **attention-based architecture consistently outperformed Deep Sets**, especially in tasks requiring dense swarm configurations (e.g., "same goal" scenario). Attention policies achieved better formation density while minimizing collisions, indicating a more sophisticated understanding of neighbor interactions. This led to its selection for further simulated experiments.

#### 4.2. Attention Weights Study (Simulation)

- **Intelligent Neighbor Prioritization:** The attention mechanism learned to assign higher importance (attention scores) to neighbors that were both closer and on a collision course (i.e., whose velocity vectors pointed towards the current agent).
- **Velocity Over Distance:** Analysis showed that relative velocity towards the agent was often prioritized over mere proximity, implying that the policy learns a sophisticated risk assessment. This demonstrates that the attention mechanism effectively captures dynamic relevance in a local neighborhood.

#### 4.3. Scaling (Simulation)

- **Generalization to Larger Swarms:** Policies trained on $N=8$ quadrotors with $K=6$ visible neighbors did not generalize well to much larger swarm sizes ($N=16, 32, 48, 64, 128$) without further training, as the change in state distribution led to increased collisions.
- **Effective Fine-tuning:** A relatively short **fine-tuning phase** (an additional $2 \times 10^8$ steps, roughly 20% of original training time) with the new, larger swarm size significantly improved performance. Even with 128 drones, policies effectively avoided collisions, with increased collision counts often attributed to cascade effects rather than fundamental policy failure. This confirms the scalability of the approach for large teams when the observation space remains fixed at $K$ neighbors.

#### 4.4. Obstacle Avoidance (Simulation)

- **Robustness to Dynamic Obstacles:** By augmenting observations with obstacle information, the policies successfully learned to maintain dense formations and avoid collisions with dynamically moving spherical obstacles. The performance remained comparable to scenarios without obstacles, demonstrating the adaptability of the learned controllers to richer, more complex environments.

#### 4.5. Physical Deployment (Crazyflie 2.0)

- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The most significant finding is the successful **zero-shot transfer** of policies, learned entirely in simulation, to highly resource-constrained Crazyflie 2.0 nano-quadrotors. This was achieved using a much smaller Deep Sets model (16x8 neurons) specifically designed for the hardware's computational limits.
- **Demonstrated Behaviors:** Policies successfully executed various tasks on physical drones, including station keeping, following moving targets, maintaining dynamic geometric formations, and "swarm-vs-swarm" goal swapping with up to 8 quadrotors.
- **Robustness on Hardware:** The physical drones demonstrated resilience, recovering from aerodynamic disturbances (downwash) and non-destructive collisions, showcasing the robustness ingrained by the noisy simulation environment.
- **Comparison to Classical Methods (Buffered Voronoi Cells + PID):**
 - The neural controllers generated significantly **more aggressive and agile maneuvers**, reaching velocities of up to 4 m/s and accelerations of 7 m/s² during goal swaps, completing tasks much faster.
 - In contrast, the classical method was more conservative, with slower movements (max 1 m/s, 3 m/s²) and a tendency to constrain trajectories predominantly to a 2D plane, whereas neural controllers utilized all three spatial dimensions for more efficient navigation.

These results collectively demonstrate that end-to-end DRL, when combined with realistic simulation, offers a powerful paradigm for developing highly effective, scalable, and physically deployable decentralized controllers for complex quadrotor swarm behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of multi-robot systems and drone swarm control, with substantial implications for future applications and foundational research.

1. **Pioneering Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Swarms:** The most impactful contribution is the demonstration of **zero-shot transferability** of end-to-end DRL policies from simulation to physical quadrotor swarms for complex, coordinated behaviors. While sim-to-real for single robots exists, extending this to scalable, decentralized swarms performing aggressive maneuvers, with direct thrust control, is a challenging feat. This breakthrough validates the efficacy of realistic simulation environments (including hardware imperfections) as a viable training ground for real-world robotics.

2. **Scalable and Decentralized Control:** By relying solely on local observations and permutation-invariant neural network architectures (Deep Sets and Attention), the method inherently supports decentralized control. This approach scales effectively, as demonstrated by the ability to fine-tune policies for up to 128 drones with minimal additional training and a fixed computational burden per quadrotor. This addresses the "curse of dimensionality" that plagues many classical multi-robot planning techniques, opening doors for very large swarm deployments.

3. **Agile and Aggressive Flight Capabilities:** The end-to-end DRL approach, directly controlling motor thrusts, allows the policies to exploit the full dynamic capabilities of the quadrotors. This results in highly agile and aggressive maneuvers, demonstrated by high speeds, accelerations, and complex formation changes, which are often restricted or impossible with classical methods that prioritize safety through constrained control spaces. This is critical for applications requiring rapid response or high dynamic performance.

4. **Model-Agnostic Control:** Since the policies learn to fly from scratch without pre-tuned PID controllers or specific kinematic/dynamic models, the approach is fundamentally model-agnostic. This implies that the same training methodology could be applied to different quadrotor platforms by merely updating the simulator's physical parameters, significantly reducing development time and effort for new hardware.

5. **Robustness and Real-World Applicability:** The training in environments with injected noise (motor lag, thrust noise, sensor noise) and simulated collisions enables the learned policies to be robust to real-world disturbances like aerodynamic downwash and minor physical contacts. This enhances the practical viability and safety of deploying these swarms in unpredictable environments.

6. **Potential for Broad Applications:** The capabilities demonstrated (area coverage, reconnaissance, pursuit-evasion, dynamic formation flying, obstacle avoidance) are directly applicable to a wide range of real-world scenarios. These include search and rescue operations, environmental monitoring, precision agriculture, package delivery, industrial inspection, and even entertainment (e.g., drone light shows). The real-time, decentralized nature makes them suitable for dynamic and time-sensitive tasks.

In conclusion, this paper marks a significant step towards the robust, autonomous, and scalable deployment of quadrotor swarms in the real world. By successfully marrying large-scale DRL with realistic simulation and demonstrating direct hardware transfer, it lays a strong foundation for future research in adaptive multi-agent control and the practical realization of complex drone swarm behaviors.
