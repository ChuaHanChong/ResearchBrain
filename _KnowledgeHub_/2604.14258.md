# GFT: From Imitation to Reward Fine-Tuning with Unbiased Group Advantages and Dynamic Coefficient Rectification

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Zhejiang University developed Group Fine-Tuning (GFT), a post-training framework reinterpreting Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) from a Reinforcement Learning perspective to overcome single-path dependency and gradient explosion. GFT consistently improved performance across math-reasoning benchmarks, achieving a +60.69% accuracy gain on TabMWP for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, while enhancing generalization and mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

## Problem

- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) suffers from single-path dependency, restricting learning to exact expert trajectories, leading to entropy collapse and hindering generalization and subsequent RL exploration.
- SFT's implicit reward function can cause gradient explosion due to inverse-probability weighting (1/π_θ(y|x)), resulting in unstable optimization and catastrophic forgetting when the model assigns low probability to valid expert tokens.
- The common sequential pipeline of SFT followed by Reinforcement Learning (RL) often faces a 'synergy dilemma,' yielding diminished improvements compared to RL alone due to SFT's tendency to over-constrain the policy to a narrow manifold.

## Method

- ==Group Advantage Learning (GAL)== expands SFT datasets into diverse 'hybrid response groups' (expert, teacher distillation, self-generated samples) and computes ==standardized advantage scores== to enable contrastive learning and foster exploration beyond single expert paths.
- ==Dynamic Coefficient Rectification (DCR)== introduces an adaptive weighting scheme that applies a dynamic threshold to token probabilities, stabilizing optimization by bounding the ==inverse-probability term== and preventing gradient explosion for low-confidence tokens.
- The final **GFT** objective unifies GAL and DCR, allowing contrastive supervision across multiple trajectories while bounding update magnitudes for low-probability tokens, formalized as a generalized and stabilized ==policy-gradient method==.

## Results

- GFT consistently outperformed all baselines, including strong SFT variants and RL methods like GRPO, across various model scales and math-reasoning benchmarks, achieving specific gains like **+60.69%** on TabMWP for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both Group Advantage Learning (GAL) and Dynamic Coefficient Rectification (DCR) are crucial components, with GAL improving performance on complex reasoning tasks and DCR enhancing training robustness by preventing volatility.
- GFT significantly mitigated catastrophic forgetting, preserving base model capabilities (comparable or better than **GRPO** on general reasoning tasks), and acted as a stronger initializer for subsequent RL, enhancing the performance ceiling of sequential **SFT + GFT + GRPO** pipelines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) can be formally reinterpreted as a special case of policy gradient optimization within a reinforcement learning framework, revealing intrinsic issues like single-path dependency and gradient explosion.
> - Addressing SFT's single-path dependency requires expanding the learning signal beyond exact expert matches, while mitigating gradient explosion necessitates adaptive weighting of updates for low-probability tokens to ensure stability.
> - Maintaining policy entropy and solution diversity during fine-tuning is crucial for both robust generalization and for providing a more effective and stable initialization for subsequent reinforcement learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges within the post-training phase of large language models (LLMs), specifically concerning Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). SFT is recognized for its efficiency in injecting knowledge and instruction-following abilities by leveraging expert demonstrations. However, it is prone to issues such as catastrophic forgetting of general-purpose representations, degraded out-of-distribution generalization, and a tendency to constrain the model's policy to a narrow manifold, thereby reducing policy entropy and solution diversity. This narrow policy space can subsequently hinder the exploration required by downstream RL.

Conversely, RL is effective at guiding models to explore broad policy spaces, optimize based on reward signals, and learn robust, generalizable strategies. Despite its strengths, RL is computationally expensive and can struggle to acquire complex skills from an uninitialized state.

The existing landscape includes hybrid post-training approaches that combine SFT and RL. However, these often encounter a "synergy dilemma," where SFT's overfitting can create a rigid policy that limits the exploration space for subsequent RL, and reasoning pattern mismatches can impede effective policy alignment. Efforts to mitigate this dilemma include interleaved updates, preference optimization methods, single-stage hybrid methods (like SRFT and UFT), and frameworks that mix supervised and reinforcement objectives. Some methods, such as Dynamic Fine-Tuning (DFT), Proximal SFT, and Anchored SFT, focus on gradient-level stabilization, often by reweighting or introducing trust-region constraints. Group-based RL methods like GRPO also aim to stabilize training by normalizing advantages within generated groups.

This work contributes to this landscape by providing a theoretical analysis that reinterprets SFT as a special case of on-policy RL with a sparse implicit reward and unstable inverse-probability weighting. Based on this analysis, it proposes a unified post-training framework, Group Fine-Tuning (GFT), designed to address the intrinsic limitations of SFT directly. GFT aims to balance efficient knowledge injection with robust generalization and improve compatibility with subsequent RL training, without explicit external feedback signals, by transforming the rigid imitation objective into a group advantage learning framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified post-training framework that can effectively inject knowledge into large language models while maintaining robust generalization capabilities and improving compatibility with subsequent reinforcement learning.

This objective is motivated by several identified limitations and challenges inherent in current LLM post-training paradigms:

1. **Limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
 - **Catastrophic Forgetting and Generalization Degradation:** SFT's strict imitation objective can overwrite general-purpose representations acquired during pretraining, leading to a loss of prior knowledge and diminished out-of-distribution generalization. This is supported by empirical observations where SFT can degrade performance relative to base models on certain tasks.
 - **Policy Entropy Collapse:** SFT tends to over-constrain the model's policy to a narrow manifold dictated by expert demonstrations. This reduces policy entropy and solution diversity, which is crucial for exploration in downstream RL tasks.
 - **Unstable Optimization:** The training process of SFT can be unstable, potentially leading to issues like gradient explosion.

2. **The "Synergy Dilemma" with Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - While SFT and RL have complementary strengths, their sequential application often results in diminished returns. SFT's tendency to rigidify the policy and narrow the exploration space hinders the effectiveness of subsequent RL algorithms, which rely on diverse exploration to optimize robust strategies.

3. **Theoretical Diagnosis of SFT's Limitations:**
 - A core motivation for the proposed solution stems from a theoretical analysis that reinterprets SFT as a specific instance of policy gradient optimization within an RL framework. This analysis reveals two fundamental flaws in SFT from an RL perspective:
 - **Single-Path Dependency:** SFT utilizes an extremely sparse implicit reward function, `r(x, y) = I[y = y*]`, which provides a learning signal only when the model's output *exactly* matches the expert trajectory. This restricts learning to a single path, limits exploration, and contributes to entropy collapse.
 - **Gradient Explosion:** SFT's gradient updates are scaled by an inverse-probability weight, `1/πθ(y|x)`. When the model assigns a very low probability to a valid or expert token, this weight can become excessively large, leading to gradient explosion, unstable optimization, and a tendency toward mechanical memorization and overfitting.

By systematically diagnosing these intrinsic deficiencies of SFT, the authors aim to develop a unified framework that directly mitigates them, thereby enabling more efficient knowledge injection, robust generalization, and improved compatibility for subsequent RL training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Group Fine-Tuning (GFT) framework is designed to address the intrinsic limitations of SFT identified through the training-dynamics analysis. GFT integrates two complementary mechanisms: Group Advantage Learning (GAL) and Dynamic Coefficient Rectification (DCR).

#### 3.1. Group Advantage Learning (GAL)

GAL aims to overcome the single-path dependency of SFT by expanding the learning signal beyond a single expert trajectory. For each input query `x`, GFT constructs a "hybrid response group" `Gx = {y1..., yK}`. This group is designed to be diverse and comprises three types of data sources:
- **Expert Demonstrations (`y_exp`):** These provide ground truth responses, ensuring a valid optimization direction.
- **Teacher Distillations (`y_demo`):** Outputs from other powerful models introduce diverse reasoning paradigms, helping to break the single-path dependency.
- **Self-Generated Samples (`y_sample`):** Responses generated by the model's own rollouts offer on-policy feedback, enabling the model to rectify intrinsic errors and reinforce successful self-exploration.

To unify learning from these diverse sources, each response `yk` in the group `Gx` is assigned a scalar reward `R(yk)`. A standardized advantage score `A(yk)` is then computed using the formula:

`A(yk) = (R(yk) - μ(Gx)) / (σR(Gx) + ϵ)`

where `μ(Gx)` and `σR(Gx)` are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group, respectively, and `ϵ` is a small constant for numerical stability. This normalization process centers and scales the rewards, producing a relative, contrastive signal. This mechanism enables the model to discern and prioritize high-quality responses, thereby integrating imitation, distillation, and self-improvement within a stable objective and preserving exploration during early post-training.

#### 3.2. Dynamic Coefficient Rectification (DCR)

DCR addresses the instability caused by the inverse-probability term `1/πθ(y|x)` in SFT-style optimization, which can lead to gradient explosion. This instability arises when the model explores uncertain responses (small `πt`) or when it initially assigns low probability to valid but unfamiliar expert/teacher tokens.

Inspired by gradient clipping techniques, DCR introduces a rectification function `C(πt)` that adaptively bounds the importance weight. The function is defined as:

`C(πt) = sg(πt) if πt < τ`
`C(πt) = 1 if πt ≥ τ`

Here, `πt` represents the predicted token probability, `τ` is a confidence threshold, and `sg(·)` denotes the stop-gradient operator. For low-confidence tokens (where `πt < τ`), this mechanism suppresses the explosive `1/πt` term by effectively clipping the coefficient, preventing large gradient updates. For confident predictions (where `πt ≥ τ`), the gradient remains unchanged, preserving full learning strength for knowledge transfer. This dual behavior stabilizes updates during exploration while ensuring efficient knowledge injection.

#### 3.3. Final GFT Objective

By combining GAL and DCR, the final training objective of GFT is formulated in its gradient form as:

`∇θ L = E_{yk ∈ Gx} [ A(yk) * (C(π) / πθ(yk|x)) * ∇log πθ(yk|x) ]`

This gradient expression is designed to resolve the two identified intrinsic limitations of SFT: the group-wise advantage weighting introduces contrastive supervision across multiple trajectories, and dynamic coefficient rectification bounds the update magnitude for low-probability tokens, thereby preventing gradient explosion. The paper provides both sequence-level and token-level formulations for this objective.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated GFT across multiple model families (Qwen2.5-Math, LLaMA-3, DeepSeekMath) and math-reasoning benchmarks, comparing it against standard SFT, strong SFT variants (DFT, ASFT, PSFT), and RL baselines (GRPO).

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Data Efficiency

- GFT consistently surpassed all baselines on both standard and competition-level math tasks, achieving the best average performance across diverse model scales.
- GFT demonstrated strong data efficiency; with 10k training examples, it matched or exceeded baselines trained with 100k examples.
- Mixing distillation data yielded only marginal changes for both SFT and GFT, suggesting GFT's gains were primarily from its proposed mechanisms rather than additional data. GFT(no mix) sometimes outperformed mixing strategies, particularly for smaller, heterogeneous models like Llama-3.2-3B, indicating robustness to distribution mismatch from teacher models.
- GRPO performance was observed to be close to GFT, attributed to both methods converting sparse rewards into more informative signals and GRPO's implicit update stabilization partially overlapping with DCR's effect.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- Ablations on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B showed distinct contributions from GAL and DCR.
- Removing GAL resulted in the sharpest performance decline, especially on the most difficult benchmark (Olympiad), affirming its importance for complex reasoning through group-based contrastive feedback.
- Removing DCR primarily impacted robustness (Minerva), consistent with its role in rectifying gradient explosion and stabilizing optimization.
- Learning dynamics plots (Figure 3) corroborated these findings: removing DCR led to severe training volatility, while removing GAL resulted in slow, suboptimal convergence. GFT, with both components, achieved efficient and stable optimization.

#### 4.3. Compatibility with SFT and RL

- Sequential training studies demonstrated GFT's improved compatibility with RL. GFT provided a stronger "cold start" and more stable optimization for subsequent RL (GRPO) compared to the conventional "SFT→RL" pipeline.
- GFT+GRPO significantly outperformed SFT+GRPO on harder benchmarks. This was attributed to GAL preventing policy collapse and maintaining a multi-solution distribution, leading to more diverse rollouts and stronger advantage signals for GRPO.
- DCR's bounding of per-token updates prevented over-sharpening of an SFT-initialized policy, maintaining a higher-entropy regime and preserving rollout diversity, which is beneficial for GRPO.
- The pipeline SFT+GFT+GRPO achieved the highest performance, suggesting that SFT provides a reliable initialization point, GFT restores exploration capabilities without drifting, and GRPO then leverages higher-quality trajectories.

#### 4.4. Catastrophic Forgetting Analysis

- GFT substantially mitigated catastrophic forgetting on general reasoning benchmarks (MAWPS, SVAMP, MMLU-STEM) compared to SFT. SFT exhibited significant degradation, while GRPO and GFT largely preserved base model capabilities, with GFT even improving MMLU-STEM.
- KL divergence analysis (Figure 5) showed that SFT incurred the highest distributional drift from the base model, while GRPO and GFT maintained significantly lower KL levels. This stability in GFT was attributed to GAL reinforcing high-quality outputs in a reward-driven manner (avoiding abrupt shifts) and DCR suppressing gradient explosions from extreme tokens (preventing drastic policy drift).

#### 4.5. Diversity of GFT

- Evaluations using Pass@k (Pass@128, Pass@256) as a proxy for solution coverage showed that GFT achieved the highest Pass@k scores across benchmarks compared to Distillation and GRPO.
- This indicates GFT's ability to balance solution diversity with correctness. It learns diverse modes from teacher distributions (like distillation) while using within-group advantages to compare student samples against teacher traces, pushing the student towards high-reward diverse modes.

#### 4.6. Hyperparameter Analysis

- **Group Composition Ratio (N_demo : N_sample):** With a fixed group size of K=8, a ratio of 2:6 (2 teacher distillations, 6 self-generated samples) was found to be optimal. This suggested that a minimal number of demonstrations anchored correctness, while abundant self-samples provided richer contrastive signals for advantage learning.
- **Clipping Threshold (τ):** Accuracy exhibited an inverted U-shape as τ increased. Small τ led to insufficient clipping and unstable updates, while large τ over-clipped informative gradients, reducing learning efficiency. An optimal balance was found at τ ≈ 0.7, indicating DCR's robustness across a range of τ values.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work contributes to the field of large language model post-training by offering a principled framework that addresses several long-standing challenges associated with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and its integration with reinforcement learning (RL).

The theoretical reinterpretation of SFT as a sparse-reward, unstable on-policy RL problem provides a foundational understanding of its limitations, specifically single-path dependency and gradient explosion. This perspective motivates a targeted approach to improvement.

The proposed Group Fine-Tuning (GFT) framework, through its two core mechanisms—Group Advantage Learning (GAL) and Dynamic Coefficient Rectification (DCR)—demonstrates an ability to:
1. **Enhance Knowledge Injection and Generalization:** By moving beyond rigid imitation to a contrastive learning signal from diverse response groups, GFT allows for more efficient knowledge transfer while mitigating overfitting and improving out-of-distribution generalization.
2. **Improve RL Compatibility:** GFT serves as a stronger initializer for subsequent RL training, effectively mitigating the "synergy dilemma." By preserving policy diversity and stabilizing updates, it prevents SFT from creating a rigid policy that would hinder RL exploration. This suggests more effective and stable multi-stage post-training pipelines.
3. **Mitigate Catastrophic Forgetting:** The design of GFT significantly reduces the catastrophic forgetting observed in SFT, allowing models to retain general-purpose reasoning capabilities while specializing in target tasks.
4. **Balance Diversity and Correctness:** GFT's ability to achieve higher Pass@k scores indicates that it can maintain a broader range of high-quality solutions, reconciling the trade-off between policy precision and exploration diversity.

The findings suggest that GFT offers a more robust and unified post-training paradigm for LLMs, capable of integrating the strengths of SFT (efficient knowledge injection) with RL-like benefits (robust generalization, exploration) within a single stage. This could lead to the development of more capable and adaptable LLMs that exhibit improved performance across diverse tasks and are less susceptible to common post-training pitfalls.

**Limitations acknowledged by the authors include:**
- Evaluation primarily focused on mathematical reasoning tasks with objective correctness, suggesting further exploration is needed for open-ended tasks with subjective rewards.
- Constructing response groups introduces marginal data preparation overhead compared to standard SFT.
- Experiments were limited to models up to 8B parameters, requiring further validation on larger models (70B+ parameters).
