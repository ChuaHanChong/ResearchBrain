# Versatile Loco-Manipulation through Flexible Interlimb Coordination

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the RAI Institute, UC Berkeley, and Cornell University developed Reinforcement Learning for Interlimb Coordination (ReLIC), a system enabling versatile loco-manipulation through flexible assignment of robot limb roles. It achieved a 78.9% success rate across 12 diverse real-world tasks on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot, dynamically switching gaits and coordinating manipulation while locomoting.

## Problem

- Seamlessly integrating locomotion and manipulation for autonomous robots in complex, unstructured environments remains a major challenge.
- Prior methods struggle with fixed limb roles and lack adaptability for dynamic task demands, often failing when limbs need to alternate functions (e.g., a leg switching from support to manipulation).
- Learning-based approaches often lack robustness and versatility for complex whole-body control, while model-based methods struggle with scaling to unstructured, dynamic scenarios.

## Method

- **ReLIC** uses a ==hierarchical framework== that translates diverse task descriptions into end-effector targets and dynamically assigns limbs for either manipulation (==model-based control==) or locomotion (==reinforcement learning==).
- An ==adaptive hybrid controller== combines a model-based manipulation module for precision with an RL-trained locomotion policy for robustness, allowing seamless, dynamic role switching.
- The system employs extensive ==domain randomization== and a novel ==motor calibration== procedure during simulation training to ensure robust sim-to-real transfer and supports task specification via direct targets, contact points, or natural language.

## Results

- ReLIC achieved an overall success rate of **78.9%** across **12** diverse real-world loco-manipulation tasks on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot.
- The system demonstrated instant and dynamic switching between four-legged trotting and various three-legged bouncing gaits without pauses, maintaining precise end-effector tracking during locomotion.
- ReLIC outperformed end-to-end RL and model predictive control baselines, which largely failed due to unstable gait generation, inaccurate tracking, or lack of support for interlimb manipulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hybrid control architecture that decouples manipulation (model-based) and locomotion (RL) while maintaining inter-module coordination provides superior versatility and robustness compared to monolithic end-to-end learning or purely model-based approaches.
> - Dynamic gait regularization, combined with a novel motor calibration procedure, is crucial for achieving stable and precise interlimb coordination and effective sim-to-real transfer for complex loco-manipulation tasks.
> - Flexible task specification via high-level inputs (contact points, language) can be effectively grounded into low-level robot actions through a hierarchical system, significantly enhancing usability and autonomy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Loco-manipulation, the ability to combine locomotion and manipulation, is a critical capability for autonomous robots operating in complex, unstructured environments. This field has seen significant advancements in both locomotion and manipulation independently. However, the seamless integration and flexible coordination of these two capabilities remain a major open challenge in robotics.

Prior research in loco-manipulation can be broadly categorized into two main streams:

- **Model-based approaches:** These often rely on whole-body control frameworks and trajectory optimization, leveraging precise dynamics models and structured task representations. While effective in controlled settings with known object geometries and contact conditions, these methods often struggle to scale to unstructured, dynamic environments due to their reliance on accurate models and scene knowledge. Examples include work on wheeled mobile manipulators, humanoid robots, and legged systems that plan contact sequences offline.
- **Learning-based approaches:** These methods offer improved adaptability and reduced engineering overhead by leveraging large-scale data and reinforcement learning. However, many existing learning-based solutions are limited to specific tasks or assume fixed roles for robot limbs (e.g., arms for manipulation, legs for locomotion). The complexity of learning holistic whole-body control often leads to policies that are not robust or versatile enough for dynamic, multi-limb coordination. End-to-end learning methods, for instance, often struggle with unstable gait generation and inaccurate end-effector tracking in complex scenarios.

This paper addresses a crucial gap: the lack of systems capable of *flexible interlimb coordination* where limb functions are dynamically assigned based on task demands, rather than being fixed or heuristically predetermined. Current systems often fall short when tasks require limbs to alternate roles (e.g., a leg shifting from locomotion support to manipulation) or to perform coordinated manipulation with an arm and a leg simultaneously while maintaining mobility. The challenge is magnified in unstructured environments where static planning or predefined modes are insufficient.

ReLIC directly tackles this by proposing an adaptive controller that dynamically assigns limbs for either manipulation or locomotion. This moves beyond the limitations of "fixed limb roles" or "predefined task sets" that characterize much of the prior work. It also builds on existing work in interlimb coordination in animals and humans, translating that biological versatility into robotic control, particularly for multi-legged robots and those equipped with arms. By focusing on a hybrid approach that decouples subproblems (manipulation and locomotion) while maintaining inter-module coordination, ReLIC seeks to achieve the adaptability of learning-based methods without sacrificing the robustness and precision often associated with model-based control for manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable **versatile loco-manipulation through flexible interlimb coordination** for autonomous robots operating in unstructured environments. The authors explicitly state their goal is to overcome the limitations of prior work, which is often constrained to specific tasks or predetermined limb configurations.

The key motivations behind ReLIC are:

- **Addressing the need for adaptability:** Complex real-world tasks often demand dynamic assignment of limb functions. For instance, an arm-mounted quadruped might need to use its arm and a leg to grasp a large object while moving, requiring the remaining legs to maintain stable locomotion. Existing methods often fail to adapt to such dynamic requirements.
- **Bridging locomotion and manipulation:** While significant progress has been made in locomotion and manipulation individually, their seamless integration remains a challenge. The paper aims to develop a controller that can robustly generate manipulation behaviors while simultaneously ensuring stable gaits, without relying on rigid heuristics or sacrificing inter-module coordination.
- **Improving generalization and robustness:** Many learning-based approaches are limited to specific tasks or struggle with real-world deployment due to sim-to-real gaps. ReLIC seeks to learn a robust and transferable policy in simulation that can effectively handle diverse and complex tasks in the real world.
- **Enabling flexible task specification:** To make robots truly autonomous and useful in varied scenarios, they must be able to interpret and execute tasks specified through different modalities, from direct targets to natural language instructions. ReLIC aims to support these diverse input types.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to push the boundaries of robotic autonomy, allowing legged manipulators to perform a broader range of complex, real-world tasks with unprecedented flexibility and robustness in how they utilize their entire body.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ReLIC (Reinforcement Learning for Interlimb Coordination) proposes a novel approach to achieve versatile loco-manipulation through a hierarchical framework and an adaptive, hybrid controller design.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Framework

The system operates on two levels:

- **Task Level:** This level describes diverse loco-manipulation tasks using a unified target-driven representation. User descriptions, irrespective of modality, are converted into end-effector targets for designated limbs and a desired torso target. A binary mask `m` dynamically indicates whether each limb is assigned to manipulation (1) or locomotion (0).
- **Command Level:** This level translates the task-specific targets and limb assignments into low-level motor commands for the robot, ensuring stable mobility and task execution through flexible limb coordination.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Control for Flexible Interlimb Coordination

The core of ReLIC is an adaptive controller designed to seamlessly bridge locomotion and manipulation. Unlike monolithic end-to-end policies, ReLIC decouples these into two interacting modules:

- **Manipulation Module (Model-Based, MB):** Given the target-driven task representation, manipulation is handled by a model-based controller. This can range from an inverse kinematics (IK) solver to an impedance controller, prioritizing task success. This module generates actions `a_MB` for limbs assigned to manipulation.
- **Locomotion Module (Reinforcement Learning, RL):** For locomotion, which requires high robustness and adaptability to dynamic behaviors, a policy `π(·|s, m)` is trained using reinforcement learning. This module focuses on maintaining stable gaits. It generates actions `a_RL` for limbs assigned to locomotion.

The two modules communicate through the robot's current state `s` and the limb assignment mask `m`. The final action `a` is a masked combination of the outputs from both modules: `a = m ◦ a_MB + (1 − m) ◦ a_RL`. This design allows for dynamic role switching without relying on rigid heuristics.

**Gait Regularization:** To ensure stable locomotion across different limb coordination patterns (e.g., four-legged trotting vs. three-legged bouncing), ReLIC incorporates gait regularization during RL training. This uses contact-time metrics to enforce desired gait patterns, which are simpler and more stable than phase-based regularization. For four-legged locomotion, a symmetric trotting gait is encouraged. For three-legged locomotion (when one leg is lifted for manipulation), a cyclic bouncing gait is enforced among the remaining three supporting feet.

#### 3.3. Learning Transferable Policy in Simulation

The locomotion policy is trained entirely in simulation using **IsaacLab** and **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. Key strategies for sim-to-real transfer include:

- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive randomization of robot dynamics, terrain properties, and external disturbances (e.g., pushes) is applied during training to enhance robustness to real-world variability.
- **Motor Calibration:** To address the significant sim-to-real gap, particularly related to unmodeled variations in motor parameters (e.g., time-dependent torque limits), a novel motor calibration procedure is employed. After initial simulation training, the policy is deployed on the real robot to collect calibration data (joint positions, velocities, commanded/actual torques). CMA-ES is then used to optimize torque limits as functions of joint state from this real-world data. The policy is subsequently fine-tuned in simulation using these calibrated parameters, minimizing the Wasserstein distance between real and simulated rollouts.

#### 3.4. Task Interface for Versatile Loco-Manipulation

ReLIC supports three modalities for user task specification, which are translated into the task-level representation:

- **Direct Targets:** Manual specification of target trajectories for designated limbs, often via teleoperation (e.g., PS5 joystick for torso velocity, 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse for 6-DoF end-effector poses).
- **Contact Points:** Users specify key contact points and associated motions. Target trajectories are then generated via motion planning. This involves fusing RGB-D images and proprioception to create a 3D point cloud, allowing users to select arm and leg contact points and pre/post-contact directions. Inverse kinematics calculates desired body poses.
- **Language Instructions:** Free-form natural language descriptions are used to infer contact points and trajectories. This is achieved by interfacing with Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Specifically, SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2) segments the scene into object masks from RGB observations, and GPT-4o then processes these masks, identifies task-relevant objects based on language instructions, and selects appropriate contact points for the arm and legs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of ReLIC was rigorously evaluated on a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped robot equipped with a Spot Arm and a gripper, across 12 diverse real-world tasks.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Analysis on Interlimb Coordination

- **Gait Transitions:** ReLIC demonstrated the ability to instantly and dynamically switch between four-legged trotting and various three-legged bouncing gaits (with different legs lifted for manipulation). These transitions occurred without requiring the robot to pause or reset its stance, a notable improvement over prior work that often relies on predefined state machines or pauses for mode switching.
- **End-Effector Motions:** While walking with three support legs, the robot successfully used its arm and the lifted leg to independently track rhombus-shaped trajectories. The low mean Cartesian error across both effectors confirmed precise interlimb coordination during dynamic locomotion, highlighting the controller's ability to simultaneously manage manipulation and mobility.

#### 4.2. Loco-Manipulation Task Solving

- **Task Suite:** The 12 real-world tasks were designed to test diverse and complex interlimb coordination, categorized into:
 - **Mobile interlimb coordination:** Manipulating large objects while navigating (e.g., Yoga Ball, Shipping Box).
 - **Stationary interlimb coordination:** Coordinated arm/leg manipulation while maintaining static balance (e.g., Tire Pump, Trash Bin, Deck Box, Small Bin).
 - **Foot-assisted manipulation:** Tasks where a leg aids the arm for improved efficiency or robustness (e.g., Tool Chest, Storage Bin, Chair, Basket, Drawer, Laundry Bag).
- **Performance:** ReLIC achieved an impressive overall success rate of **78.9%** across all tasks and input modalities.
 - **ReLIC-Direct** (using direct target specification) achieved the highest success rates, succeeding in over 80% of trials in 9 out of 12 tasks, demonstrating the controller's robustness across varied, multi-stage, long-horizon tasks.
 - **ReLIC-Contact** and **ReLIC-Language** (using contact points and natural language, respectively) achieved comparable results, validating the effectiveness of the hierarchical framework in translating high-level user commands into actionable targets for the ReLIC controller.
- **Baseline Comparison:**
 - An **end-to-end reinforcement learning (E2E RL)** baseline failed on all tasks due to unstable gait generation and inaccurate end-effector tracking, highlighting the challenges of learning holistic policies for complex interlimb coordination.
 - A **model predictive control (MPC)** baseline, lacking support for interlimb manipulation or three-leg locomotion, failed on most tasks, only succeeding by chance on one task without coordinated intent. This underscores the advantage of ReLIC's adaptive and flexible approach.

#### 4.3. Failure Analysis

An analysis of failure modes for ReLIC-Contact revealed three primary sources:

- **Perception Errors:** Inaccuracies in 3D point clouds or state estimation led to failures, emphasizing the dependency on robust perception.
- **Tracking Errors/Balance Loss:** Despite accurate perception, the ReLIC controller sometimes struggled with tracking under extreme or unstable body configurations, especially when unpredictable external forces (e.g., stepping on a pedal) challenged balance.
- **Inaccurate Contact/Manipulation Errors:** Unintended manipulation errors, such as losing grip on an object (e.g., Yoga Ball) or unintentional kicks, posed significant challenges requiring real-time feedback and fine-grained contact reasoning.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Appendix G)

Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of specific methodological components:

- **Motor Calibration:** Removing motor calibration (ReLIC w/o Motor Calib.) significantly reduced tracking accuracy and stability, leading to compensatory motions and balance struggles.
- **Gait Regularization:** Without gait regularization (ReLIC w/o Gait Regular.), the learned gait degenerated into repetitive bouncing motions in both quadrupedal and tripedal modes, resulting in poor stability and consistent failures in tracking tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ReLIC represents a significant step forward in the field of loco-manipulation, addressing key limitations of existing approaches and offering a versatile, robust framework for complex robotic tasks.

- **Enabling Versatile Autonomy:** The most impactful contribution is the ability to achieve flexible interlimb coordination, allowing robots to dynamically reassign limb roles (locomotion vs. manipulation) based on task demands. This adaptability is crucial for operating in unstructured, dynamic environments where predefined limb functions are inadequate.
- **Hybrid Control Architecture:** The hybrid approach, seamlessly integrating a model-based controller for precise manipulation with an RL policy for robust locomotion, offers a powerful paradigm. This effectively decouples complex subproblems, leveraging the strengths of both model-based accuracy and learning-based adaptability, without sacrificing coordination.
- **Robust Real-World Performance:** Demonstrating high success rates across 12 diverse and challenging real-world tasks on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot validates the practical applicability and robustness of ReLIC. This is a substantial achievement, particularly given the difficulties typically associated with sim-to-real transfer for complex dynamic behaviors.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The robust sim-to-real transfer, facilitated by comprehensive domain randomization and a novel motor calibration procedure, is a critical enabler for deploying learned policies on physical hardware. This addresses a major hurdle in robotics research.
- **Flexible Task Specification:** The support for various user input modalities, including direct targets, contact points, and natural language instructions, significantly enhances the usability and accessibility of loco-manipulation systems, moving towards more intuitive human-robot interaction. The integration of VLMs for language grounding is particularly forward-looking.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** This work is poised to inspire further research at the intersection of reinforcement learning and whole-body control, especially in exploring more dynamic and nuanced forms of interlimb coordination.

Despite its advancements, ReLIC has acknowledged limitations. The open-loop nature of target generation from contact points and language instructions could be brittle for tasks requiring real-time adaptation. The manipulation controller's reliance on a standard IK solver may also limit its scope for dynamic behaviors or complex collision avoidance. Future work could focus on integrating more expressive, perhaps learned, manipulation controllers, exploring feedback-driven target generation, and generalizing ReLIC to broader forms of interlimb coordination (e.g., arms supporting locomotion) or other robot morphologies. Nevertheless, ReLIC stands as a compelling demonstration of advanced robotic capabilities, highlighting the critical role of flexible interlimb coordination in pushing the boundaries of autonomous robot operation.
