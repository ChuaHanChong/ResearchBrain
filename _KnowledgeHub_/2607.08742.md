# ContactMimic: Humanoid Object Interaction via Contact Control

> [!summary] Summary
> CONTACTMIMIC introduces a framework enabling humanoid robots to perform object interaction tasks with precise, runtime-controllable physical contact by integrating explicit part-level binary contact commands with keypoint trajectories. The system successfully demonstrates contact-conditioned behavior on a Unitree G1 robot for various tasks, achieving object manipulation without requiring task-specific rewards.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid motion tracking systems primarily rely on kinematic keypoint or joint trajectory imitation, treating physical contact as an incidental outcome.
- For complex loco-manipulation tasks (e.g., wiping, sitting, pushing), accurate poses alone are insufficient if the robot does not make the appropriate physical contact.
- Current keypoint-only trackers often require additional fine-tuning with task-specific rewards to perform contact-critical operations, reducing generality.

## Method

- The **CONTACTMIMIC** framework augments traditional keypoint trajectory input with desired per-time-step binary contact labels for specific body parts.
- A novel ==data augmentation scheme== (contact-label flipping, object removal, inflated geometry) is used to decouple contact behavior from keypoint geometry during training.
- Reinforcement learning policies are trained with custom ==contact-aware rewards== that incentivize matching commanded binary contact labels and appropriate contact distances.

## Results

- In simulation, policies consistently achieve contact controllability, with measurable changes in contact metrics (e.g., higher contact bodies/impulses, greater object displacement) when contact is commanded.
- Real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot confirms successful contact control for tasks like wiping a whiteboard or sitting, with high success rates across different contact commands.
- Compared to keypoint-only trackers, CONTACTMIMIC achieves comparable **Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE)** but significantly higher contact bodies and impulses, successfully performing object manipulation without task-specific rewards.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit contact commands provide fine-grained control over robot interaction, allowing for distinct behaviors (e.g., sitting vs. squatting) for similar keypoint trajectories.
> - Breaking the natural correlation between keypoint trajectories and contact patterns through data augmentation is crucial for policies to learn genuine contact-conditioned behavior.
> - Policies can implicitly encode the robot's runtime contact state from proprioceptive observations, even without explicit contact sensors as input.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics extensively explores policies for imitating human motion, often through reinforcement learning by tracking kinematic trajectories such as keypoints or joint targets [1–6]. While these methods are effective for replicating the general shape of a motion, they frequently treat physical contact with the environment as an incidental outcome of geometry matching rather than a controllable, integral component of the task. This limitation becomes apparent in complex loco-manipulation tasks like wiping a whiteboard, sitting on a chair, or pushing furniture, where the utility and success of the task are fundamentally defined by the specific body parts making contact with objects, and when.

Prior work has explored various aspects related to contact in robotics and character animation. Some systems, such as ResMimic, incorporate contact-tracking rewards but typically condition only on general motion and object trajectories, which does not provide the fine-grained, explicit control over contact desired for many interaction scenarios. In classical robotics, methods like trajectory optimization [31–33] plan through contact but generally require contact schedules to be predefined at planning time, lacking runtime adaptability. Physics-based character animation [34–36] also conditions motion on scene contact, but this is usually for maintaining physical plausibility rather than offering an explicit control knob to the user. Similarly, learning-based human-object interaction (HOI) synthesis methods [27, 37–39] utilize contact as an intermediate representation or affordance to ensure physically plausible motion, rather than as a direct, user-controllable input.

CONTACTMIMIC addresses this gap by proposing a framework that integrates explicit part-level binary contact commands directly into the policy's input alongside keypoint trajectories. This approach aims to decouple contact behavior from mere keypoint geometry, thereby enabling precise physical contact and offering runtime contact controllability. By doing so, this work extends beyond existing keypoint-only trackers that often necessitate task-specific rewards for contact-relevant tasks, and moves towards a more generalized approach where successful manipulation can be achieved simply by matching keypoint and contact trajectories. The authors posit that this explicit conditioning provides a more versatile and intuitive interface for humanoid interaction with complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning framework, CONTACTMIMIC, that enables humanoid robots to perform object interaction tasks with precise and controllable physical contact. The authors highlight a critical limitation in existing humanoid motion tracking systems: their reliance on kinematic keypoint or joint trajectory imitation, which often overlooks the crucial role of explicit physical contact in achieving meaningful whole-body loco-manipulation tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from the observation that for many practical tasks, simply reaching a correct pose, as dictated by keypoint tracking, is insufficient if the robot does not make the appropriate physical contact. For instance, a robot might move its hand close to a whiteboard, but without explicit contact, it cannot actually wipe it. Similarly, "sitting on a chair" implies weight transfer and contact with the seat, which differs significantly from merely "squatting" above it. Current humanoid trackers, being predominantly keypoint-aware, are unable to intrinsically understand or control these contact nuances. Consequently, even advanced keypoint-only trackers often require additional fine-tuning with task-specific rewards to perform contact-critical operations effectively. This adds complexity and reduces the generality of such systems.

To overcome these challenges, the research proposes a "contact-conditioned keypoint tracker." The key objectives are:
1. **Introduce explicit contact commands:** To augment the traditional keypoint trajectory input with desired per-time-step binary contact labels for specific body parts. This additional information is intended to disambiguate tasks where keypoint geometry might be similar but contact patterns differ (e.g., wiping vs. waving, sitting vs. squatting).
2. **Enable fine-grained control:** To provide more precise control over the robot's motion and interaction, such as allowing the robot to sit in a chair with or without leaning on the backrest, by simply toggling a contact command.
3. **Reduce reliance on task-specific rewards:** To enable the training of policies for object manipulation tasks (e.g., lifting a box) by solely tracking keypoint and contact trajectories, without the need for additional, custom-designed task-specific reward functions. This aims to simplify the development of policies for new interaction tasks.
4. **Decouple keypoint and contact behaviors:** To address the inherent correlation between keypoint trajectories and contact patterns in raw human-object interaction data. Without this decoupling, a policy might learn to infer contact from keypoints rather than obeying explicit contact commands. This necessitates a novel data augmentation scheme to generate motion pairs with similar keypoints but differing contact patterns.
5. **Design effective contact-aware rewards:** To incorporate specific reward terms during policy training that encourage the robot to match commanded contact patterns, encompassing both binary contact label assignment and appropriate contact distances.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to advance humanoid capabilities towards more purposeful and versatile interaction with diverse environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CONTACTMIMIC framework focuses on training a contact-conditioned policy, denoted as πθ(at|pt, ¯kt, ¯ct), which takes proprioception (pt), reference keypoint position targets (¯kt), and a binary reference contact label map (¯ct) as input. The policy outputs target joint angles that are translated into torques by a 1 kHz PD controller, with the policy operating at 50 Hz. The methodology is structured around two main stages: data generation and policy training.

**Stage 1: Contact-Conditioned Motion Retargeting & Augmentation**

This stage focuses on preparing training data that includes both keypoint trajectories and explicit contact labels, crucially breaking the natural correlation between them.

- **Reference Data Source:** The work utilizes human-object interaction clips from the HUMOTO dataset.
- **Motion Retargeting:** The OmniRetarget method is employed to transfer human motions onto the G1 humanoid robot, generating reference robot configuration trajectories (¯q1:T).
- **Contact Information Extraction:** From the retargeted trajectory, reference contact labels (¯ct,b,p) are extracted. A robot body part `b` is marked as in contact with an object semantic part `p` if its distance to the object surface falls below a 1 cm threshold. The overall body-level contact label (¯ct,b) is then derived as the maximum contact state across all object parts (maxp ¯ct,b,p).
- **Trajectory Augmentation:** To ensure the policy learns to genuinely decouple contact behavior from keypoint geometry, several augmentation strategies are applied to generate diverse motion pairs:
 - **Contact-label flipping:** The original motion trajectory is retained, but the task-relevant contact labels are inverted. The object remains in the simulation, but the policy is explicitly commanded *not* to make contact.
 - **Object removal:** The interaction object is entirely removed from the scene. Correspondingly, all target contact labels are set to zero, while the original keypoint trajectory is preserved. This forces the robot to track keypoints without making contact.
 - **Inflated geometry:** During a second retargeting pass, the collision geometry of specific target object parts is expanded isotropically by 5 to 10 cm. This perturbation forces the retargeter to generate a new keypoint trajectory where the robot bodies are routed away from the object surface, thus preventing contact. The corresponding contact labels are then zeroed out. These augmentations can be combined to create more complex training scenarios, such as pairing a distant trajectory with a true contact label, which simulates situations with slight keypoint misspecification where contact is still desired.

**Stage 2: Policy Training & Deployment**

This stage involves training the policy using reinforcement learning and deploying it on the physical robot.

- **Reinforcement Learning Setup:** Policies are trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm within the Isaac Lab simulation environment. Training occurs across 4096 parallel environments, with a policy step rate of 50 Hz. At each episode reset, the environment randomly assigns either the default trajectory or one of its augmented counterparts, ensuring the policy experiences both contact and no-contact target labels during training.
- **Reward Design:** The total reward (rt) is a sum of standard tracking terms, regularization terms, and novel contact-aware rewards:
 - **Tracking Rewards:** Standard terms adopted from prior work that incentivize matching global anchor position/orientation, body positions/orientations, and body linear/angular velocities. These terms typically use Gaussian kernels.
 - **Regularization Rewards:** Penalties for high action rates, joint limit violations, and undesired contacts with the terrain.
 - **Contact-Aware Rewards (Proposed):**
 - **Contact label assignment (rlmt):** Rewards the policy for accurately matching the commanded reference contact label (¯ct,b,p) with the actual contact state (ct,b,p). This can be calculated as:
 - Balanced accuracy: 0.5 * (True Positive Rate + True Negative Rate), the default.
 - TP-FP: True Positive Rate - λ * False Positive Rate (λ=1.0), used for sparse-contact motions to provide a stronger gradient.
 - **Contact distance (rcdt):** Composed of two sub-terms: one (rcd,+t) that encourages parts intended for contact to be close to the object surface (using a Gaussian on distance), and another (rcd,-t) that penalizes unintended contact by encouraging non-contact parts to stay far from the surface (using an indicator function for distances below 0.05m).
- **Policy Architecture and Observations:** Both the actor and critic networks are Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with hidden dimensions and ELU activations. The actor's observations (ot) concatenate proprioception (joint positions/velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity), reference keypoint targets, and the binary reference contact label map (¯ct). Uniform observation noise is added during training. The critic receives noise-free observations plus privileged information (base linear velocity).
- **Domain Randomization:** To improve sim-to-real transfer, various physical parameters like robot link masses, joint friction, and object friction/mass are randomized at the start of each episode.
- **Real-World Deployment:** The trained policies are deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. The policy runs onboard at 50 Hz, generating target joint positions for the robot’s 1000 Hz joint-level PD controller. No external motion capture or vision systems are used during deployment; the policy relies solely on onboard proprioception, the pre-recorded reference, and the explicit contact-label command.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across 10 diverse human-object interaction motions in simulation and 5 motions on a real Unitree G1 robot provided several key findings:

- **Contact Controllability in Simulation:**
 - The policies consistently demonstrated contact controllability. For the same keypoint trajectory, toggling the per-part contact label from "contact ✔" to "contact ✘" resulted in a measurable change in robot behavior.
 - When contact ✔ was commanded, the robot established task-relevant contacts, reflected by higher "contact bodies" (number of robot parts in contact) and "contact impulse" metrics. These metrics decreased significantly when contact ✘ was commanded. This trend held true even when keypoints were artificially moved far from the object (T_kp=far_), indicating that the policy followed the explicit contact command rather than inferring contact from keypoint proximity.
 - For motions involving object manipulation (e.g., "Kick chair," "Pick up box"), the object displacement was notably greater when contact ✔ was commanded, demonstrating that CONTACTMIMIC successfully enables manipulation without requiring task-specific rewards.
 - Key-joint torques also changed predictably based on the contact command, reflecting the robot's physical adaptation (e.g., increased hip torque when not leaning on a backrest).

- **Contact Control Transfers to the Real World:**
 - Real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 robot for five motions (wipe whiteboard, sit in front of table, lean on backrest I/II, sit and squat) confirmed contact controllability.
 - The robot qualitatively and quantitatively matched the commanded contact behavior (✔ or ✘) with high success rates (e.g., 5/5 or 9/10 successful trials for most conditions, as per Table 2).
 - For instance, when commanded to "wipe whiteboard" with contact ✔, the robot applied sustained pressure, leaving a visible trace. With contact ✘, it hovered just above the board with no effect. For sitting, contact ✔ led to weight transfer onto the seat, while contact ✘ resulted in a dynamically balanced squat posture at the same height without bearing weight.

- **Keypoint Control Alone Is Insufficient:**
 - A comparative analysis with BeyondMimic, a state-of-the-art keypoint-only tracker, revealed that while both methods achieved comparable Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE), CONTACTMIMIC produced significantly higher "contact bodies" and "contact impulses."
 - This indicates that keypoint tracking alone, even with high accuracy, is insufficient for tasks requiring meaningful physical contact. BeyondMimic failed to establish consistent task-relevant contacts and could not manipulate objects in tasks like "Pick up box," whereas CONTACTMIMIC successfully performed these manipulations.

- **Necessity of Data Augmentation:**
 - An ablation study demonstrated the critical importance of the proposed trajectory augmentation scheme (contact-label flipping, object removal, inflated geometry).
 - Policies trained without these augmentations exhibited poor contact control. When commanded with contact ✘, the "no-augmentation" variant often continued to make contact or failed to reduce contact metrics significantly, indicating that the policy ignored the explicit contact command and continued to infer contact from the correlated keypoint trajectories (Fig. 5, Table 9).
 - The augmentation scheme successfully broke the correlation between keypoints and contacts, forcing the policy to learn genuine contact-conditioned behavior.

- **Policy Representations Encode Runtime Contact State:**
 - Even without explicit contact sensors as policy inputs, the internal representations of CONTACTMIMIC's policy (raw observations and hidden layer 2 features) proved highly predictive of the robot's actual runtime contact state.
 - Linear probing showed high F1 scores (e.g., >0.9 for many motions) for predicting task-relevant contact, significantly outperforming chance performance and the F1 from the reference label alone (Table 4). This suggests that the policy can infer its contact state from proprioceptive observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CONTACTMIMIC represents a notable advancement in humanoid robotics by providing a framework for explicit, runtime-controllable physical contact during human-object interaction. Its primary significance lies in addressing a fundamental limitation of traditional keypoint-only motion tracking, where contact is often an incidental byproduct rather than a direct, controllable variable.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Enhanced Humanoid Autonomy and Versatility:** By enabling humanoids to precisely control contact, the system moves towards more nuanced and capable interaction with the physical world. This allows robots to perform tasks such as wiping, pushing, or sitting with the appropriate physical engagement, rather than just approximating the desired geometry.
- **Simplified Policy Design for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The ability to achieve complex object manipulation tasks (e.g., lifting a box) solely by tracking keypoint and explicit contact trajectories, without the need for additional, task-specific rewards, simplifies the policy engineering process. This could significantly reduce the development effort for new loco-manipulation skills.
- **Improved Safety and Reliability:** Explicit contact control can lead to safer interactions, as the robot is aware of and can modulate its contact forces and locations. This may also improve task reliability by ensuring critical contacts are established and maintained as intended.
- **Foundation for More Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:** Providing a simple and general interface for expressing contact-rich tasks via contact labels could make it easier for human operators or higher-level planners to specify desired robot behaviors. This is a step towards humanoids interacting with their environment "purposefully rather than incidentally."
- **Advancement in Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The success of contact controllability in both simulation and on the physical Unitree G1 robot, supported by robust data augmentation and domain randomization, demonstrates the practicality of the approach for real-world robotic deployment.

**Acknowledged Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations, which also point to avenues for future research:
1. **Motion-Specific Policies:** The current framework trains a separate policy for each motion. Developing a single universal contact-conditioned tracker that can generalize across a wide range of motions would be a natural and impactful next step.
2. **Data Dependency:** The reliance on high-quality human-object interaction datasets like HUMOTO limits the diversity of interactions that can currently be covered. Future work could explore methods that leverage more abundant, in-the-wild video data to expand the range of learnable skills.
3. **Hardware Validation Scope:** The real-world evaluation was conducted on five motions using a single robot platform (Unitree G1). Broader hardware validation across different humanoid platforms and a wider array of tasks would be beneficial to confirm the generality and robustness of the approach.

Overall, CONTACTMIMIC provides a robust methodology for integrating explicit contact control into humanoid motion tracking, offering a pathway toward more capable, versatile, and intuitively controllable robotic systems.
