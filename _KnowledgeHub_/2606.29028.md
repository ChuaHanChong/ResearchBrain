# Keypose Exploration: Efficient Automatic Trajectory Labelling and Cross-Embodiment Policy Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> A method is introduced for automatically extracting keyposes from robotic demonstrations using vision-language models and trajectory analysis, then leveraging these labels within a Diffusion Policy. This approach, enhanced with a kinematic reachability filter, improves zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer for multimodal tasks, achieving up to an 9.1% increase in success rate on the Square task for a UR5e robot while reducing performance variance.

## Problem

- Manual or heuristic-based keypose annotation for robotic manipulation is inefficient and lacks scalability or generalizability for diverse tasks.
- Learning robust policies for long-horizon robotic tasks is complex, and traditional keypose methods are bottlenecked by data preparation.
- Achieving zero-shot cross-embodiment policy transfer, especially for tasks with multimodal solutions, is challenging due to varying robot kinematics and the need to disambiguate diverse strategies.

## Method

- An automatic pipeline integrates ==Vision-Language Models== for semantic event detection with classical ==trajectory analysis== for precise temporal correction of keyposes in grasp-related tasks. A ==Consistency Model== serves as the keypose predictor.
- Keyposes are incorporated as explicit conditions into a ==Diffusion Policy==, guiding action generation for low-level control.
- A ==Propose-Filter-Generate== mechanism applies a precomputed ==kinematic reachability map== to candidate keyposes, prioritizing those feasible for the target robot to enable zero-shot transfer.

## Results

- Policies trained with automatically extracted keyposes performed comparably to or slightly better than standard Diffusion Policy baselines on the source Franka Panda robot (e.g., **100%** vs **98.7%** on Can, **87.3%** vs **86.2%** on Square).
- For zero-shot transfer on the multimodal Square task to UR5e, the keypose-guided policy with reachability filtering (KP+Reach) increased success from **73.8%** (DP) to **82.9%** and significantly reduced variance from **10.3%** to **4.4%**.
- The effectiveness of reachability filtering is limited by large kinematic gaps; for Kinova3 on the Square task, where few keyposes were reachable, KP+Reach showed minimal improvement (**24.2%** vs **22.0%** for DP).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Automatic keypose extraction can be achieved by combining high-level VLM semantic understanding with low-level trajectory analysis, reducing reliance on manual effort or task-specific heuristics.
> - Explicitly conditioning generative policies on keyposes helps to disambiguate multimodal action distributions and enables more precise control during critical task phases.
> - Integrating kinematic reachability constraints into keypose selection is a viable strategy for improving zero-shot policy transfer across different robot embodiments by biasing the policy towards feasible actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation for long-horizon tasks presents a significant challenge due to the complexity of learning continuous policies over extended timeframes. Within this domain, keypose-based manipulation has emerged as a strategy to simplify policy learning by decomposing tasks into a sequence of critical end-effector poses or waypoints, known as keyposes. These keyposes serve as sub-goals, guiding the robot's actions and improving task interpretability.

A fundamental prerequisite for keypose-based approaches is the accurate and reliable identification of these critical poses within demonstration data. Historically, this has been achieved through methods requiring human intervention, such as manual annotation of demonstration videos or the development of hand-crafted, task-specific heuristics (e.g., based on gripper state transitions or velocity thresholds). While manual annotation offers flexibility, it is time-consuming and computationally expensive for large datasets. Heuristic-based methods, conversely, are inflexible and require re-engineering for each new task. This bottleneck in keypose extraction limits the scalability and generalizability of keypose-based robotic systems.

Concurrently, advances in generative imitation learning (IL), particularly with models like Diffusion Policy (DP) and Consistency Models (CMs), have demonstrated effectiveness in addressing challenges such as error accumulation and multimodal action distributions in robotic tasks. These models learn to generate action sequences from expert demonstrations, improving robustness and handling diverse task strategies. The integration of keyposes with such generative policies offers a potential pathway to leverage high-level semantic information for more structured and effective policy learning.

This paper addresses the identified gap by proposing an automated solution for keypose extraction, moving beyond manual and heuristic-based methods. It integrates vision-language models (VLMs) for high-level semantic understanding with classical trajectory analysis for precise temporal alignment. Furthermore, it explores how to exploit these automatically generated keypose labels within a generative policy framework (Diffusion Policy) to facilitate zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer. This is particularly relevant for scenarios with multimodal demonstration data, where different successful strategies exist, and for adapting policies to robots with varying kinematic structures. The work aims to enhance the practicality and applicability of keypose-based manipulation by reducing the data preparation burden and improving transferability across different robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research presented in this paper is motivated by two primary challenges in keypose-based robotic manipulation: the labor-intensive nature of keypose annotation and the underexplored potential of keyposes for cross-embodiment policy transfer, particularly in multimodal task settings. The authors position their work as a preliminary feasibility study to address these issues.

The detailed objectives and motivations are as follows:

1. **Objective 1: Develop an Automatic Trajectory Labelling Pipeline:**
 - **Motivation:** Existing methods for extracting keyposes from demonstration data are either inefficient or inflexible. Manual annotation is prohibitively slow and costly for large datasets, while hand-crafted heuristics require task-specific coding for every new scenario. This bottleneck hinders the widespread adoption and scalability of keypose-based methods for long-horizon tasks.
 - **Goal:** To create a pipeline that can automatically generate keypose labels from demonstration data with minimal human intervention. This pipeline should combine the semantic reasoning capabilities of vision-language models (VLMs) with the precision of classical trajectory analysis to identify critical interaction moments in grasp-related tasks. The design aims to require VLM inference on only a single demonstration per task, reducing computational overhead for repetitive demonstrations.

2. **Objective 2: Leverage Keypose Conditioning for Policy Design and Cross-Embodiment Transfer:**
 - **Motivation:** While keyposes are known to simplify policy learning for long-horizon tasks and improve interpretability, the extent to which their features can be exploited for advanced policy capabilities, such as adapting to new robot embodiments or handling multimodal task strategies, remains an open question. Different robots have distinct kinematic constraints, and a policy trained on one robot's demonstrations might struggle to transfer to another if the target robot cannot kinematically achieve certain keyposes.
 - **Goal:** To explore how a keypose conditioning mechanism can be integrated into a generative policy (specifically, a Diffusion Policy) to improve its performance, especially in zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer scenarios. This involves:
 - **Disambiguating Multimodal Distributions:** Investigating whether keyposes can help disambiguate qualitatively different strategies present in demonstration data, allowing the policy to select an appropriate mode of action.
 - **Addressing Kinematic Feasibility:** Incorporating a reachability filter into the keypose selection process. This filter would guide the policy towards kinematically feasible keypose candidates for a target robot, without requiring retraining on the target robot's data. This aims to improve the robustness and success rate of zero-shot transfer when kinematic differences exist.

3. **Objective 3: Conduct a Preliminary Feasibility Study:**
 - **Motivation:** Before developing a fully generalized system, it is essential to establish the viability of the proposed concepts. This involves validating that the automatically labelled data is of sufficient quality to train effective policies and that the keypose-guided transfer mechanism demonstrates a positive effect in controlled experimental settings.
 - **Goal:** To perform experiments on simulated robomimic tasks (Can and Square) to:
 - Validate that policies trained on automatically labelled keyposes perform comparably to a standard Diffusion Policy baseline.
 - Investigate the potential benefits of reachability-filtered keypose conditioning for zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, particularly on a task known to have multimodal solutions.

In essence, the research seeks to overcome practical barriers to scalable keypose-based robot learning and to enhance the adaptability of policies to new robot platforms by making keypose extraction automatic and by intelligently leveraging keypose information during policy execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology comprises a three-component pipeline: a VLM-guided subtask analysis, an automatic trajectory-based event correction, and a keypose-conditioned policy learning framework that incorporates reachability constraints for cross-embodiment transfer. The focus is on grasp-related tasks where keyposes correspond to interaction moments.

#### 3.1. A. Vision-Language Guided Subtask Analysis

1. **Subtask Design for Grasp-Related Tasks:** The process begins by providing a Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Qwen3-VL-235B, with a single demonstration video (uniformly sampled RGB frames) and task context. The VLM is guided by a structured prompt to decompose the demonstration into subtasks.
2. **Three-Phase Structure:** Each subtask is defined by a three-phase structure:
 - **Targeting Phase:** The robot approaches an object or location, ending when positioned for manipulation. No object interactions occur here.
 - **Interaction Phase:** The robot performs manipulation primitives: `grasp` (gripper closes), `release` (gripper opens), `detach` (object separates from surface after lifting), and `interact` (gripper-held object contacts a target object/surface, e.g., insertion).
 - **Result Phase (Optional):** Passive consequences, such as `land` (released object settles).
3. **VLM Output:** The VLM outputs a structured JSON detailing these subtasks, interaction primitives (with interacting entities, type, and VLM-estimated frame-level timestamps), and their phases. This output provides semantic event detection and initial temporal estimates.

#### 3.2. B. Automatic Trajectory-Based Event Correction

VLMs can introduce temporal misalignments. A hierarchical trajectory analysis framework corrects these errors by aligning VLM-estimated semantic events with precise physical motion patterns from raw trajectory data.

1. **Double-Layer Motion Segmentation:**
 - **Stage 1 - Per-Axis Segmentation:** For each Cartesian axis (x, y, z), Savitzky-Golay smoothing is applied to end-effector position data to denoise and derive velocity. Velocity thresholds (θk, task-dependent) classify each frame into one of three motion labels (+1 for up, -1 for down, 0 for stable). Each axis is then independently segmented into intervals of consistent motion direction.
 - **Stage 2 - Cross-Axis Combination:** Per-axis segments are merged into unified motion phases. Adjacent phases are combined unless an axis reverses direction. Short segments (e.g., <20 frames) and closely spaced boundaries are discarded to prevent fragmentation from brief contacts.
 - **Optional Layer - Subtle Motion Detection:** Within stable phases from Stages 1-2, the cumulative displacement over a sliding window is analyzed. If it exceeds a threshold (e.g., 10% of working range), the phase is reclassified as slow-moving. This ensures both fast and deliberate motions are captured.
 - **Gripper Timeline:** Gripper opening/closing transitions are segmented independently from gripper commands.
 - **Output:** A motion timeline (T_move) with intervals, start/end times, and motion signatures, and a gripper timeline (T_grip) with open/close intervals.
2. **Semantic-Motion Correspondence Rules:** Deterministic rules map the VLM-detected semantic events to precise frames using the established motion and gripper timelines:
 - `grasp`: First frame of the gripper closing interval.
 - `release`: First frame of the gripper opening interval.
 - `detach`: Maximum of the gripper closing end time and the first moving frame after the grasp.
 - `interact`: First frame of the last moving phase before release (when the gripper-held object makes contact).
 - `land`: End of the gripper opening interval or subtask boundary (for passively settling objects).
 These rules leverage specific motion signatures (e.g., downward z-motion for insertion) for context-aware alignment. The corrected keypose frames yield end-effector poses `k_i = [p_i, q_i, g_i]` (position, 6D orientation, 2-finger joint positions) as target vectors.

#### 3.3. C. Keypose Prediction Networks

Building on the BiKC framework, a Consistency Model (CM) serves as the keypose predictor.
1. **Training:** The CM is trained on the automatically extracted keypose labels. Given an observation `o`, an encoder produces an observation embedding `f_obs`. A conditional denoiser `f_θ(k_t, t, f_obs)` is trained via EMA-based consistency distillation to map noisy keyposes directly to clean target keyposes. An auxiliary head predicts the current subtask stage to stabilize the predictor.
2. **Inference:** The CM's single-step generation capability allows rapid sampling of `N_cand` candidate keyposes, each with a manifold-confidence score.

#### 3.4. D. Keypose-Guided Diffusion Policy with Reachability Constraints

The core policy is a keypose-guided conditional Diffusion Policy (DP), which uses keyposes as explicit conditions to decouple high-level mode selection from low-level control.
1. **Policy Architecture and Training:** The action trajectory policy `p(τ|o, k)` is conditioned on both the current observation `o` and a selected keypose `k`. The keypose `k` is feature-wise concatenated with `o` at each time step to form a combined condition `c`, which conditions a Transformer-based denoising backbone. The policy is trained using a standard noise prediction objective.
2. **Reachability Filtering and Transfer:** To enable zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer to new robots (e.g., Kinova3, UR5e) with different kinematics:
 - A precomputed `RichMap` is queried to determine if a given end-effector pose `k` is kinematically reachable by the target robot, yielding a binary suitability indicator `S(k; robot)`.
 - This suitability map reweights the source keypose distribution `p_source(k|o)` to `p_target(k|o) ∝ S(k; target) · p_source(k|o)`, prioritizing reachable keyposes.
 - **Propose–Filter–Generate Procedure:**
 1. **Propose:** `N_cand` (e.g., 24) candidate keyposes are sampled from the CM predictor `p_source(k|o)`, each with a confidence score.
 2. **Filter:** Candidates are clustered into orientation modes (up to three). Each mode is queried against the `RichMap`. The mode with the highest composite value (number of reachable candidates × best confidence) is selected, and its highest-confidence reachable candidate is chosen as `k*`. If no candidate is reachable, the procedure falls back to the highest-confidence overall candidate.
 3. **Generate:** The frozen Diffusion Policy generates the action trajectory conditioned on the selected `k*`.
This pipeline applies the reachability constraint in the keypose space, facilitating zero-shot transfer without retraining.

#### 3.5. E. Experimental Setup

- **Goals:** Validate labelled data quality, explore multimodal zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer.
- **Tasks:** Robomimic Can (pick-and-place, unimodal) and Square (peg insertion, multimodal).
- **Robots:** Source: Franka Panda. Targets: Kinova3, UR5e.
- **Observations:** Low-dimensional state vectors (object states, end-effector poses; 23D) to isolate keypose/reachability effects from visual domain shift.
- **Action Space:** 10D absolute end-effector pose.
- **Comparisons:**
 - **DP:** Standard Diffusion Policy baseline.
 - **KP:** Keypose-guided policy using automatically extracted keyposes, no reachability filtering.
 - **KP+Reach:** KP augmented with the propose–filter–generate pipeline and reachability filtering.
- **Parameters:** Specific task-dependent parameters for labelling (smoothing window `w`, velocity thresholds `θk`), `N_cand` for sampling, clustering thresholds, and `RichMap` resolution are detailed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted to validate the automatic labelling pipeline and investigate the utility of keypose conditioning for zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over 9 checkpoints (3 seeds × 3 epochs), each evaluated on 50 episodes.

#### 4.1. A. Labelling Validation

- **Panda Source Robot Performance:**
 - On the **Can** task (unimodal), the standard Diffusion Policy (DP) achieved a success rate of 98.7% ± 1.4%. The keypose-guided policy (KP) achieved 100%, and KP+Reach achieved 98.9% ± 1.4%. These results indicate that policies trained with automatically labelled keyposes perform comparably to, and in one instance slightly surpass, the DP baseline on the source robot for a unimodal task.
 - On the **Square** task (multimodal), DP achieved 86.2% ± 3.6%. KP achieved 84.2% ± 5.2%, and KP+Reach achieved 87.3% ± 3.5%. The performance of KP and KP+Reach is comparable to the DP baseline on the source robot, validating that the automatic labelling pipeline produces effective keypose annotations and that the KP-DP framework successfully trains on this data.
- **Interpretation:** The results on the Panda robot confirm the quality of the automatically generated keypose labels, demonstrating that they are sufficient to train policies that perform on par with a standard non-keypose-conditioned baseline. The slight variations are within typical run-to-run variations for these policies.

#### 4.2. B. Multimodal Feature for Policy Transfer

The **Square** task, with its intrinsically multimodal demonstration distribution (three distinct insertion orientations), served to evaluate the utility of keypose conditioning for cross-embodiment transfer.

- **Zero-Shot Transfer to UR5e:**
 - The DP baseline transferred to UR5e achieved 73.8% ± 10.3% success, indicating a notable drop from the source robot performance and high variability across training runs.
 - KP alone improved transfer stability to 75.8% ± 5.5% on UR5e, roughly halving the variance compared to DP.
 - KP+Reach further improved the success rate to 82.9% ± 4.4% on UR5e, bringing it closer to the Panda source-robot baseline.
- **Mechanisms of Improvement:**
 - **Mode Selection via Keypose Conditioning:** Keypose conditioning guides each policy rollout towards a specific insertion orientation, rather than an average. The reachability filter helps ensure that the selected mode is kinematically feasible for the target robot. Rollouts (e.g., Figure 3) showed KP+Reach successfully selecting different insertion orientations in independent trials, both leading to success.
 - **Conservative Behavior Near Keyposes:** The keypose-guided policy demonstrated more deliberate and cautious motion as the end-effector approached the predicted keypose compared to DP's faster, more direct execution. This caution during critical interaction moments (e.g., peg insertion) reduced positional error, mitigating failures caused by overly aggressive execution (e.g., misaligned contact).
- **Interpretation:** Keypose conditioning, especially when combined with reachability filtering, proved beneficial for zero-shot transfer on the multimodal Square task for UR5e. It enabled effective mode selection and promoted more precise, successful interactions.

#### 4.3. C. Kinematic Gap Limitations (Kinova3)

- **Zero-Shot Transfer to Kinova3:**
 - On the Square task, transfer performance to Kinova3 remained low across all methods: DP (22.0% ± 4.5%), KP (18.7% ± 3.7%), and KP+Reach (24.2% ± 4.2%).
- **Feasibility Analysis:** The reachability map revealed that for Kinova3, almost no sampled candidate keyposes (∼0.1% acceptance) were kinematically reachable. This suggests that Kinova3's reachable workspace and wrist limits were poorly matched to the task's required insertion orientations.
- **Interpretation:** When candidate keyposes are largely infeasible for the target robot, the reachability filter has minimal impact, effectively reducing KP+Reach to plain KP. The observed small improvement in KP+Reach for Kinova3 is within the experimental variance and does not indicate a significant benefit from the filter. This highlights that the effectiveness of reachability filtering is contingent on the existence of feasible candidate keyposes for the target embodiment; a large kinematic gap necessitates more than soft conditioning.

#### 4.4. D. Unimodal Task Transfer (Can)

- On the **Can** task (unimodal), the DP baseline showed strong transfer performance to Kinova3 (91.1% ± 5.5%) and UR5e (96.0% ± 2.2%). KP and KP+Reach generally trailed DP on transfer for this task (Kinova3: KP 84.7% ± 3.4%, KP+Reach 84.3% ± 2.5%; UR5e: KP 89.1% ± 2.8%, KP+Reach 91.3% ± 3.8%).
- **Interpretation:** For a task with a largely unimodal demonstration distribution and an already high-performing baseline, additional keypose conditioning may not offer significant benefits and can sometimes introduce minor disruptions, as the policy is already well-calibrated from observations alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several contributions that could influence the development and application of robot manipulation systems.

1. **Automation of Keypose Extraction:** The introduction of an automatic trajectory labelling pipeline addresses a significant bottleneck in keypose-based robotic manipulation. By combining VLM semantic understanding with deterministic trajectory analysis, the method offers a scalable alternative to manual annotation or task-specific heuristic coding. This capability can substantially reduce the human effort and computational cost associated with dataset preparation, enabling faster development and deployment of keypose-based policies for a wider range of grasp-related tasks. The potential to automatically derive structured subtask information from raw demonstrations could accelerate the creation of large-scale, annotated robot datasets.

2. **Validation of Automatically Generated Labels:** The experiments confirm that keypose labels generated by the automatic pipeline are of sufficient quality to train high-performing policies. The keypose-guided Diffusion Policy (KP-DP) achieved performance comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, a standard Diffusion Policy baseline on the source robot. This validation provides confidence in the utility of the automated labelling approach, suggesting that it can serve as a robust foundation for subsequent policy learning.

3. **Enhanced Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Transfer for Multimodal Tasks:** The work demonstrates a mechanism for improving zero-shot transfer for multimodal tasks by leveraging keypose conditioning and reachability filtering. For tasks with multiple successful strategies (e.g., different insertion orientations), keypose conditioning helps to expose and disambiguate these action modes. The integration of a reachability map further refines this process by guiding the policy toward kinematically feasible keyposes for the target robot, without requiring task-specific demonstrations or retraining on the new embodiment. This capability is significant for deploying robot skills across heterogeneous robot fleets, as it can reduce the need for extensive data collection and model adaptation for each new robot, thereby improving efficiency and reducing operational costs.

4. **Foundation for Future Research in Hierarchical and Adaptive Control:** The study's findings on mode selection and conservative behavior near keyposes suggest that explicit conditioning on high-level task structures can lead to more robust and deliberate low-level control. This paves the way for further research into hierarchical control systems where high-level semantic understanding (from VLMs and keyposes) can effectively guide and constrain low-level motor policies. The identified limitations, such as the scope of the keypose abstraction for non-prehensile tasks, the need for fully parameter-free labelling, and the challenge of overcoming large kinematic gaps, provide clear directions for extending this work towards more generalized subtask-level labelling, stronger integration of kinematic constraints, and vision-based policy adaptation for real-world scenarios.
