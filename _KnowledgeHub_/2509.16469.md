# A Framework for Optimal Ankle Design of Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework was developed for the optimal mechanical design of humanoid robot ankle joints, systematically comparing parallel kinematic architectures and actuator types. This methodology produced optimized parallel ankle designs that reduced a scalar performance cost by up to 41% compared to existing serial designs and 14% against conventionally engineered parallel solutions.

## Problem

- Current humanoid robot ankle designs, particularly traditional serial architectures, limit agility, stability, and dynamic performance, hindering operations in human-centric environments.
- Designing parallel ankle mechanisms is complex due to a large number of coupled geometric parameters, with performance metrics varying significantly across their operational workspace.
- A lack of systematic, quantitative methodologies exists for selecting optimal parallel architectures and tuning their parameters based on specific application requirements and performance indicators.

## Method

- Inverse kinematic models were developed for two representative parallel ankle mechanisms, SPU (linear actuated) and RSU (rotary actuated), including a novel reparameterization for the RSU to guarantee workspace feasibility.
- A multi-objective optimization algorithm (==NSGA-II==) was employed to synthesize geometric parameters, minimizing peak actuator forces/torques and velocities over realistic locomotion tasks.
- A scalar cost function was created to aggregate seven architecture-agnostic performance metrics (e.g., speed, torque, mass, compactness) for direct, quantitative comparison and ranking of optimized solutions across different architectures and actuators.

## Results

- The novel RSU reparameterization was validated to guarantee full workspace feasibility across the desired operational region, preventing kinematically impossible configurations during design.
- Optimized parallel ankle designs, notably the RSU mechanism with Synapticon ACTILINK JD 10 actuators, consistently achieved lower scalar costs across evaluated metrics compared to other architectures and actuators.
- Optimized parallel designs reduced the scalar cost by up to **41%** compared to the original serial ankle architecture and by up to **14%** over a conventionally engineered parallel RSU mechanism, validating the framework's effectiveness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parallel mechanisms offer inherent advantages over serial architectures for humanoid ankles, including increased rigidity, precision, and better mass distribution, but require sophisticated design approaches.
> - A systematic design framework combining kinematic modeling, workspace reparameterization, multi-objective optimization, and a scalar cost function can effectively overcome the complexity of parallel mechanism design.
> - Ensuring inverse kinematics existence across the entire operational workspace is crucial for parallel mechanisms and can be systematically achieved through dedicated parameter reparameterization, preventing kinematically infeasible designs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of agile and dexterous humanoid robots capable of operating safely and efficiently in human-centric environments is a significant objective in robotics research. Achieving such capabilities necessitates advancements not only in control algorithms but also in fundamental mechanical design, particularly concerning robot limbs that interact directly with the environment.

Within this context, two critical factors influencing robot agility are identified: the distribution of mass and the mechanical compliance of joints. Placing heavy actuators proximally, away from the robot's distal segments, improves energy efficiency by raising the center of mass (CoM), as demonstrated by previous research. Mechanical compliance, characterized by high backdrivability, is crucial for robust and safe interaction with unpredictable environments and for dissipating energy during dynamic movements, which contributes to more forgiving gait patterns and reduced damage risk.

Parallel mechanisms have emerged as a prominent architectural solution for addressing these design considerations, especially in the ankle joints of next-generation humanoid robots. Notable examples in contemporary robotics, such as Tesla’s Optimus, Agility Robotics’ Digit, and Fourier’s N1, have adopted parallel ankle designs. These mechanisms offer inherent advantages over traditional serial architectures, including increased structural rigidity, enhanced positioning precision (due to error averaging), reduced individual actuator size requirements (due to torque sharing among multiple kinematic chains), and improved dynamics resulting from the relocation of heavy components (actuators) closer to the robot’s main body.

However, the design of parallel mechanisms presents distinct challenges. Unlike serial chains where performance metrics can often be localized to individual joints, parallel structures involve a larger set of coupled geometric parameters, requiring a global optimization approach. Furthermore, the performance of parallel mechanisms, such as joint torques and velocities, can vary significantly across their operational workspace, complicating design and control. Existing research on geometric parameter synthesis for parallel mechanisms often employs optimization algorithms, but their specific application to humanoid ankle mechanisms remains limited. Additionally, while various kinematic structures have been surveyed, a systematic, quantitative methodology for selecting the most appropriate architecture and tuning its parameters based on specific application requirements and performance indicators is largely absent in current design practices.

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a unified methodology for the design and comparative evaluation of parallel ankle mechanisms. It aims to bridge the gap between theoretical advantages of parallel architectures and practical, quantitative design decisions by offering a systematic tool for architecture selection and parameter tuning. By focusing on two representative parallel architectures, SPU (Spherical-Prismatic-Universal) and RSU (Revolute-Spherical-Universal), and developing both kinematic solutions and an evaluation framework, the work contributes to providing a more informed and efficient pathway for developing advanced humanoid ankle systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to establish a unified and systematic framework for the optimal design and quantitative comparative evaluation of parallel ankle mechanisms for humanoid robots. This framework aims to support designers in making informed choices regarding kinematic architecture and geometric parameters based on application-specific performance requirements.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and opportunities identified in current humanoid robot ankle design:

- **Criticality of Ankle Design:** The ankle joint is recognized as a pivotal component in humanoid locomotion, serving as the primary interface for ground interaction. Its mechanical design significantly influences a robot's agility, stability, and overall dynamic performance. Improvements in ankle design can therefore yield substantial gains in a robot’s capabilities.
- **Advantages and Challenges of Parallel Mechanisms:** Parallel mechanism architectures offer significant benefits over serial designs, particularly for ankle joints, including improved structural rigidity, higher precision, better load distribution among actuators, and the ability to place heavy actuators proximally, thus reducing distal mass and improving energy efficiency and overall dynamics. This has led to their increasing adoption in advanced humanoid robots. However, the design of parallel structures is inherently complex, involving a large number of interconnected geometric parameters. Their performance metrics, such as joint torques and velocities, are often highly coupled and vary substantially across the workspace, making intuitive design difficult.
- **Lack of Systematic Design Tools:** Current design practices for parallel ankle mechanisms often rely on iterative engineering and qualitative assessments. While optimization algorithms exist for geometric parameter synthesis of parallel mechanisms, their application to humanoid ankle joints specifically remains underdeveloped. Furthermore, existing surveys of possible kinematic structures lack quantitative criteria to guide designers in selecting the most appropriate architecture for a given task or set of constraints. Consequently, designers currently lack a systematic methodology that integrates architecture selection with parameter tuning, guided by quantitative performance indicators tailored to specific applications.
- **Need for Cross-Architecture Comparison:** Without a common framework, comparing the performance of different parallel kinematic structures (e.g., linear vs. rotary actuated systems) or even parallel versus serial designs becomes challenging. A method that allows for consistent, quantitative comparison across diverse architectures is necessary to identify truly optimal solutions.

To address these motivations, the research pursues the following specific objectives:

1. **Develop Kinematic Models:** To derive closed-form inverse kinematic solutions for two representative parallel ankle mechanisms: the Spherical-Prismatic-Universal (SPU) mechanism, typically using linear actuators, and the Revolute-Spherical-Universal (RSU) mechanism, typically using rotary actuators. These models are fundamental for analyzing mechanism behavior across its operational range.
2. **Ensure Workspace Feasibility for RSU:** To introduce a novel parameterization for the RSU mechanism. This parameterization is specifically designed to inherently guarantee that the inverse kinematics problem admits solutions across the entire desired operational region, thereby preventing the design of kinematically infeasible configurations and accelerating the optimization process.
3. **Implement Multi-Objective Optimization:** To employ a multi-objective optimization algorithm to synthesize the geometric parameters of candidate ankle mechanisms. This optimization aims to minimize critical performance objectives, specifically peak actuator force (or torque) and peak actuator velocity, evaluated over a set of representative locomotion tasks.
4. **Create a Scalar Cost Function for Evaluation:** To define a scalar cost function that aggregates multiple, high-level, and architecture-agnostic performance metrics (e.g., speed, torque, backdrivability, compactness, mass, CoM height). This cost function facilitates a unified post-ranking and direct comparison of optimized solutions across different kinematic architectures and actuator types.
5. **Validate the Framework:** To validate the proposed methodology by applying it to a realistic scenario: the redesign of an existing humanoid robot’s ankle. This involves comparing the performance of optimized parallel designs against both the original serial ankle and a conventionally engineered parallel RSU design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper for designing and evaluating parallel ankle mechanisms is structured into two main stages: a multi-objective optimization stage and a scalar cost function-based evaluation stage. This decoupling aims to efficiently explore design spaces and then provide a unified ranking.

**Kinematic Analysis of Mechanisms (Section III):**
The framework begins with a detailed kinematic analysis of the two target parallel ankle mechanisms: the Spherical-Prismatic-Universal (SPU) and the Revolute-Spherical-Universal (RSU). Both mechanisms are modeled with the robot’s shin as the fixed world frame (W) and the foot as the end-effector (F), connected by three legs. A central universal joint (U0) defines the ankle’s roll (φ) and pitch (ϑ) orientation. The other two legs, indexed `i` in `{1, 2}`, define the specific parallel architecture. Both mechanisms are two-degrees-of-freedom (DOF) systems, as determined by the Gr¨ubler-Kutzbach criterion.

- **SPU Parallel Mechanism:** In the SPU mechanism, each of the two outer legs consists of a Spherical (S_i), Prismatic (P_i), and Universal (U_i) joint. Actuation is achieved by controlling the linear elongations (ζ_i) of the prismatic joints. The inverse kinematics (IK) problem for SPU is formulated to determine these elongations for a desired foot orientation (φ, ϑ). Loop closure equations are derived based on geometric points (e.g., S_i, U_i, U0) and constant geometric parameters. By taking the Euclidean squared norm of the loop closure equation, the dependency on universal joint angles (φ_i, ϑ_i) is eliminated, leading to a closed-form expression for ζ_i. The positive sign solution is adopted for design feasibility.
- **RSU Parallel Mechanism:** For the RSU mechanism, each outer leg comprises a Revolute (R_i), Spherical (S_i), and Universal (U_i) joint. Actuation is performed by controlling the angular displacements (α_i) of the revolute joints. Similar to the SPU, loop closure equations are established using geometric parameters (a_i, b_i, crank length c_i, rod length r_i, and revolute axis orientation ψ_i). Taking the Euclidean squared norm allows for the derivation of a closed-form IK for α_i. This solution, however, requires an existence condition (`|k_i / ρ_i| ≤ 1`) to be met, ensuring that the crank and rod can physically connect. If this condition is violated, no feasible solution exists for the given foot orientation.

**Reparameterization of the RSU Mechanism (Section III-C):**
A critical aspect of the RSU design is ensuring that the inverse kinematics existence condition is satisfied across the entire desired operational region (`Ω`) for the foot. Arbitrary selection of geometric parameters (`c_i`, `r_i`) does not guarantee this, potentially leading to a workspace with "holes" where the mechanism cannot reach. To address this, a novel reparameterization strategy is introduced:

1. **Crank Length Bounds:** The IK existence condition is reformulated to explicitly define the minimum possible crank length (`c_i,min`) required to cover the desired operational region `Ω`. This `c_i,min` is determined as the maximum value of a specific expression over `Ω`. A new parameter `γ_i ∈` is then introduced, allowing the crank length (`c_i`) to be expressed such that it is always greater than or equal to `c_i,min`, thus inherently fulfilling part of the existence condition.
2. **Rod Length Bounds:** With `c_i` defined by `γ_i`, the IK existence condition is then used to derive lower (`r_i,min`) and upper (`r_i,max`) bounds for the rod length (`r_i`). These bounds represent the intersection of all possible rod lengths across the operational region. A second parameter `δ_i ∈` is introduced to define `r_i` as a convex combination of `r_i,min` and `r_i,max`, ensuring `r_i` always lies within its feasible range. This reparameterization ensures that any chosen `γ_i` and `δ_i` will result in an RSU mechanism capable of reaching all orientations within `Ω`.

**Mechanisms Evaluation Framework (Section IV):**
The framework proceeds with two sub-stages:

- **Geometrical Parameters Optimization (Section IV-A):**
 - This stage formulates the design problem as a multi-objective optimization problem. The goal is to find geometric parameter vectors (`π`) that minimize two objective functions: peak actuator force/torque (`f_1(π)`) and peak actuator velocity (`f_2(π)`). These objectives are evaluated over a set of reference trajectory tasks (`K`), derived from simulated or experimental data, covering the desired operational range.
 - The design variables (`π`) include `W a_i`, `F b_i` for SPU, and `W a_i`, `F b_i`, `ψ_i`, `γ_i`, `δ_i` for RSU.
 - A feasible set (`Π`) is defined by constraints such as actuator ratings, hardware size limits, and collision avoidance. The RSU reparameterization automatically handles workspace feasibility for that architecture.
 - The Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) is employed to solve this multi-objective problem, generating a Pareto front of optimal trade-off solutions (`Π⋆`).
- **Scalar Cost Function for Comparative Evaluation (Section IV-B):**
 - To allow for direct, quantitative comparison and ranking of solutions across different architectures and actuators (e.g., SPU vs. RSU, different commercial actuators), a scalar cost function (`ξ`) is introduced.
 - **Performance Metrics:** Seven high-level performance metrics are defined, focusing on the external performance at the ankle’s universal joint (U0):
 1. **Speed [rad/s]:** Ankle roll and pitch angular velocities at nominal actuator speeds.
 2. **Torque [Nm]:** Ankle roll and pitch torques at nominal actuator forces/torques.
 3. **Backdriving Torque [Nm]:** Ankle torques required to overcome actuator static friction.
 4. **Manipulability Ratio [–]:** Measures the isotropy of the mechanism's motion capability.
 5. **Compactness [mm]:** Minimum radius of a vertical cylinder enclosing the mechanism in a neutral pose.
 6. **Actuation Mass [kg]:** Total mass of actuators and associated linkages (e.g., crank/rod for RSU).
 7. **CoM Height [mm]:** Vertical distance from ground to the actuators’ CoM in a neutral pose.
 - **Region-Based Weighting:** For metrics that vary across the operational region (Speed, Torque, Backdriving Torque, Manipulability Ratio), a weighted average (`μ`) and variance (`σ²`) are computed over a grid of foot orientations. A "core region" (weight `w=1`) encompasses task trajectories, while an "extended region" uses a smoothly decaying weight (`w`) to penalize poor performance outside the core but within the overall operational region.
 - **Cost Function Definition:** Each raw metric (`m_j`) is normalized to a `` range (`˜m_j`) using min-max scaling across the entire candidate population. The total scalar cost `ξ` is then computed as a weighted sum of these normalized metrics (`ξ = Σ η_j ˜m_j`), where `η_j` are user-defined weights reflecting application priorities. The solution with the lowest `ξ` is considered optimal.

**Implementation Details:** The framework is implemented using Grasshopper and Python, with the NSGA-II algorithm facilitated by the Tunny plugin. A set of reference tasks (dynamic walking, ramp walking, step-climbing) provides realistic torque and velocity profiles for optimization. Commercial actuators are selected based on preliminary power estimation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across the validation of its methodology and the comparative analysis of ankle designs.

**Validation of the RSU Reparameterization (Section V-A):**
The proposed reparameterization strategy for the RSU mechanism was validated by examining the mechanism's configuration space across varying desired operational regions. The results visually confirmed that the reparameterization successfully ensures that the entire target operational region remains within the area where the inverse kinematics (IK) problem is solvable. This implies that for any foot orientation within the specified region, the mechanism's crank and rod lengths will allow for a closed kinematic chain. It was observed that when the reparameterization parameters `γ_i` and `δ_i` approached zero, the operational region would effectively touch the singularity curves, indicating that the system was operating at its kinematic limits, which is consistent with the theoretical formulation. This validation demonstrates that the reparameterization effectively guarantees workspace feasibility and prevents the generation of kinematically impossible designs.

**Mechanisms Evaluation Framework Use-case (Section V-B):**
The framework was applied to a practical scenario: redesigning the ankle of an existing humanoid robot.
- **Reference Tasks and Operational Region:** The design was informed by a combination of experimental and simulated locomotion tasks, including dynamic walking, ramp ascent, and stair climbing. These tasks defined a required operational region for the ankle of [−35°, 35°] in roll and [−70°, 30°] in pitch, with a more constrained core region for weighted metric evaluation.
- **Actuator Selection and Optimization:** Following preliminary design space exploration to estimate power requirements, three different commercial rotary actuators (Maxon HEJ 70-48-50, MyActuator RMD-X6-P20-60, Synapticon ACTILINK JD 10) were selected for RSU designs, and one linear actuator (Wittenstein AL32) for SPU designs. Multi-objective optimizations were then conducted for each actuator-architecture combination, yielding Pareto fronts of optimal trade-off designs (e.g., Fig. 7).
- **Cost Function Analysis:** The scalar cost function, with uniformly distributed weights across the seven performance metrics, was computed for all solutions on the Pareto fronts. The analysis (Fig. 8) revealed a consistent performance advantage for RSU designs utilizing **Synapticon ACTILINK JD 10 actuators**, exhibiting the lowest average cost and the tightest variance among all tested actuator families. This suggests that the Synapticon actuators, within the RSU architecture, offered the most balanced performance across the chosen metrics.
- **SPU vs. RSU Comparison:** A detailed comparison between the best SPU and RSU candidates (Fig. 9) indicated that the RSU design generally **outperformed the SPU counterpart across most of the seven performance metrics**. While the SPU exhibited slightly lower performance variability across the operational region (narrower shaded area in the radar chart), its average performance (solid line) was inferior in multiple categories, making the RSU the more favorable solution in this specific use case.

**Validation of the Scalar Cost Function Approach (Section V-C):**
To assess the effectiveness of the automated design pipeline, the optimized solutions were compared against a conventionally engineered RSU mechanism. This "baseline" RSU was developed through an iterative CAD-based process, aiming to meet operational region coverage, avoid collisions, and satisfy joint requirements. When this conventionally engineered RSU was paired with each of the rotary actuators used in the study, its calculated scalar costs were consistently higher than those achieved by solutions within the optimized populations (indicated by arrows in Fig. 8). This result substantiates the advantage of the proposed automated, optimization-driven framework over conventional, iterative design methods.

**Comparison with the Original Serial Architecture (Section V-D):**
The scalar cost (`ξ`) was also computed for the original serial ankle architecture of the existing humanoid robot. This comparison demonstrated that **nearly all optimized parallel designs, regardless of the specific actuator chosen, achieved a lower total cost than the original serial design** (dashed line in Fig. 8). Quantitatively, the optimized RSU reduced the scalar cost by up to **41% compared to the original serial design** and by up to **14% compared to the conventionally engineered RSU**. This finding not only validates the ability of the framework to identify superior parallel designs but also confirms its applicability for evaluating and comparing serial mechanisms.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the proposed framework successfully generates kinematically feasible parallel ankle designs, identifies optimal actuator-architecture combinations (with Synapticon+RSU showing strong performance), and produces solutions that significantly outperform both traditionally engineered parallel and existing serial ankle mechanisms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for the systematic design and evaluation of parallel ankle mechanisms, contributing to the field of humanoid robotics in several significant ways:

- **Systematic and Quantitative Design:** The primary significance lies in providing a unified, quantitative, and systematic methodology for humanoid robot ankle design. This addresses a recognized gap in current practices, which often rely on iterative engineering and qualitative assessments. By integrating closed-form kinematics, a novel reparameterization, multi-objective optimization, and a scalar cost function, the framework offers a structured approach that moves beyond intuition-driven design toward empirically validated optimal solutions.
- **Informed Design Decisions:** The framework enables designers to make informed decisions by directly comparing the performance of different kinematic architectures (e.g., SPU vs. RSU) and various commercial actuators. The customizable weighting of performance metrics within the scalar cost function allows for application-specific prioritization (e.g., prioritizing backdrivability for safer interaction or compactness for slender designs), aligning the design process with specific robot tasks or operational environments.
- **Improved Robot Performance and Capabilities:** The validation results demonstrate that the optimized parallel ankle designs significantly outperform both the original serial ankle architecture and a conventionally engineered parallel design. A reduction in scalar cost by up to 41% over the serial design and 14% over a conventionally engineered parallel RSU indicates a substantial improvement in overall performance metrics. This can lead to the development of humanoid robots with enhanced agility, higher energy efficiency (due to improved mass distribution), increased compliance (contributing to safer human-robot interaction and robustness to environmental disturbances), and better dynamic capabilities for complex locomotion and manipulation tasks.
- **Enhanced Workspace Feasibility and Efficiency:** The novel RSU reparameterization is a notable contribution, ensuring that the designed mechanisms are inherently capable of covering the desired operational region. This preempts the design of kinematically infeasible configurations or those prone to singularities within the intended workspace, thereby streamlining the design process and reducing iterative adjustments.
- **Reduced Development Time and Cost:** By automating the exploration of a large design space and quantitatively ranking solutions, the framework has the potential to significantly accelerate the development cycle of humanoid robot limbs. This can reduce the time and resources traditionally associated with manual design, prototyping, and testing phases.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust foundation for future research in several areas. The authors identify collision checking over the full operational region and the direct embedding of the cost function into the optimization loop as immediate next steps. These advancements could further refine the optimality and practicality of the generated designs, pushing the boundaries of humanoid robot mechanical design.

In conclusion, this research provides a valuable tool for roboticists, offering a rigorous and systematic pathway to design and select superior parallel ankle mechanisms, which can directly contribute to the realization of more capable, agile, and robust humanoid robots for diverse applications.
