# TFP: Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies for Visuomotor Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NYU Shanghai developed Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies (TFP), which integrate an episode-local, Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) driven belief state into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies to directly modulate action generation. This framework achieved improved success rates on long-horizon tasks and enhanced robustness, demonstrably reducing stage-level memory failures in real-world robotic manipulation.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are reactive, predicting actions mainly from immediate observations, leading to failures in "stage-dependent manipulation" where visually similar states require different actions based on implicit task progress.
- Existing memory mechanisms in robotic policies often use discrete updates or act as passive history buffers, failing to provide a compact, dynamically updated state of task progress that accounts for elapsed physical time and specific manipulation events.
- Manipulating objects requires memory updates that are robust during stable phases (e.g., carrying an object) and rapidly adaptive during critical events (e.g., contact or release), a challenge not adequately met by prior work.

## Method

- **TFP (Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies)** augments a chunked VLA policy with an episode-local latent belief system, leveraging ==Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks== for continuous-time, dynamics-aware belief updates.
- The updated belief state directly modulates action generation within the ==flow-matching action decoder== using ==adaptive normalization (AdaLN)== style conditioning.
- An **Episode-Aware Temporal Batching (EATB)** strategy is introduced for training, maintaining episode-local hidden state continuity across long episodes while enabling efficient backpropagation.

## Results

- TFP improved the average success rate on LIBERO tasks to **98.75%** (from **96.9%** for π₀.₅) and achieved a **93.77%** robustness score on LIBERO-plus (compared to **91.4%** for π₀.₅).
- On the MIKASA-Robo ShellGameTouch task, TFP achieved a **75.0%** success rate, significantly outperforming reactive VLA baselines in tasks requiring hidden-state reasoning under occlusion.
- Real-world experiments on a Galaxea A1 robot showed TFP achieving **15/20** successes for object swapping and **18/20** for counting pick-and-place, substantially reducing "stage-level memory failures" compared to reactive baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks can learn dynamics-aware belief updates, allowing the memory to adaptively retain information during stable manipulation phases and rapidly integrate new evidence during critical events.
> - Directly conditioning the action generation with the learned task belief allows the policy to produce distinct actions for visually similar states, depending on the implicit stage of the task.
> - Explicitly accounting for elapsed physical time (Δt) in memory updates is crucial for maintaining a consistent task belief in chunked receding-horizon control, where intervals between policy queries can vary.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research operates within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Recent VLA policies, such as $\pi_{0.5}$, OpenVLA, Octo, and RT-2, have demonstrated capabilities in mapping language instructions and multimodal observations directly to robotic actions. These models often rely on a reactive paradigm, where actions are predicted predominantly from the current observation, instruction, and proprioceptive state.

However, a recognized limitation of these reactive policies is their insufficiency for "stage-dependent manipulation." In such scenarios, visually similar states may necessitate different actions based on latent task progress, previous interactions, or hidden subgoals. For example, in an object swapping task, the same visual scene might require moving an object to a buffer, moving another object to a target location, or terminating the task, depending on the implicit stage of the operation.

Existing approaches to incorporate memory into robot policies generally fall into two categories:
1. **History-based methods:** These store past observations, actions, or semantic summaries and retrieve or attend to them. Examples include HAMLET, MemoryVLA, and Causal Diffusion Policy. While providing rich episodic detail, these methods primarily serve as retrieval sources and do not explicitly form a compact, dynamically updated state of task progress.
2. **Recurrent latent states:** Methods like AVA-VLA and ReMem-VLA maintain a compact latent state that evolves over time. While providing an explicit memory state, their updates are typically tied to discrete frames, policy steps, or action chunks, without explicitly accounting for physical elapsed time between policy queries. In chunked receding-horizon control, the interval between policy queries can vary significantly, necessitating memory updates that are sensitive to actual physical time rather than just discrete steps.

This paper addresses the gap by proposing a memory mechanism that not only maintains a compact task belief but also features "dynamics-aware belief updates" that are sensitive to elapsed physical time and manipulation events. It leverages Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks, which model hidden state evolution with input-dependent time constants, to enable adaptive retention and revision of task progress information. The integration of this continuous-time, event-sensitive memory into a VLA backbone aims to enhance performance on tasks requiring context beyond immediate visual information.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are:

- **Addressing the Reactivity Limitation of Current VLA Policies:** Standard VLA policies, which predict actions based solely on current observations, struggle with tasks where identical or similar visual inputs demand different actions depending on the underlying task stage, historical interactions, or unobserved environmental changes. The paper aims to overcome this limitation by introducing a mechanism for maintaining and updating an internal representation of "task progress belief."

- **Developing Dynamics-Aware Belief Updates:** The motivation extends beyond simply adding memory. For robotic manipulation, a useful memory must possess specific temporal dynamics. During stable phases, such as object transport or occlusion, the policy should robustly preserve task-progress information. Conversely, near critical events like contact, object release, or subgoal completion, the policy needs to rapidly integrate new evidence and revise its belief. The objective is to enable memory updates that follow this event structure and adapt to the temporal dynamics of manipulation, including variable intervals between policy queries.

- **Leveraging Continuous-Time Inductive Bias for Memory:** The paper argues that chunked visuomotor control, where the physical time between policy queries can vary, requires a memory mechanism sensitive to elapsed physical time. Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks are identified as a suitable mechanism due to their ability to evolve hidden states with input-dependent time constants. This continuous-time inductive bias is motivated by the need for memory to persist over stable periods and update rapidly during events, irrespective of discrete step counts.

- **Directly Conditioning Action Generation with Task Belief:** A crucial objective is to ensure that the learned task belief actively shapes the generated actions. Unlike methods where memory serves as a passive history buffer or an auxiliary signal for perception, this work aims for the belief state to directly modulate the action distribution. This is motivated by the idea that different task beliefs for visually similar states should lead to distinct actions.

- **Improving Performance on Memory-Dependent, Long-Horizon, and Robustness-Oriented Tasks:** The overall motivation is to enhance the performance of VLA policies on tasks that inherently require sustained memory and contextual understanding, specifically long-horizon tasks, those involving occlusion or visual perturbations, and tasks with stage-dependent structure. This includes improving task success rates and robustness under challenging environmental conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies (TFP)**, augments a chunked VLA policy with an episode-local latent belief using Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) dynamics and integrates this belief directly into the flow-matching action decoder via adaptive modulation.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Setting and Notation

At each policy query $t$, the agent receives a visual observation $I_t$, robot state $s_t$, elapsed time $\Delta t_t$ since the previous query, language instruction $\ell$, and previous belief $h_{t-1}$. A vision encoder converts $I_t$ into visual tokens $V_t$. The policy then updates the latent belief $h_t$ and predicts an action chunk.

#### 3.2. B. Continuous-Time Belief Update

The core of TFP is its LTC-style recurrence for updating the latent belief $h_t$. First, a compact observation representation $x_t$ is formed by combining pooled visual tokens and the embedded robot state. The update proceeds as follows:
1. **Candidate Belief ($\hat{h}_t$)**: A candidate belief $\hat{h}_t$ is computed from the current observation $x_t$ and previous belief $h_{t-1}$ using a tanh-activated feedforward network.
 $\hat{h}_t = \tanh(W_h[x_t; h_{t-1}] + b_h)$
2. **Time Constants ($\tau_t$)**: A vector of input-dependent time constants $\tau_t$ is predicted, also based on $x_t$ and $h_{t-1}$, using a softplus activation to ensure positivity.
 $\tau_t = \text{softplus}(W_\tau[x_t; h_{t-1}] + b_\tau) + \epsilon$
3. **Retention Gate ($k_t$)**: The retention gate $k_t$ is calculated using an exponential decay function of the elapsed time $\Delta t_t$ and the predicted time constants $\tau_t$. This determines how much of the previous belief $h_{t-1}$ is retained.
 $k_t = \exp(-\Delta t_t / \tau_t)$
4. **Belief Update ($h_t$)**: The final belief $h_t$ is an interpolation between the previous belief $h_{t-1}$ and the candidate belief $\hat{h}_t$, weighted by $k_t$ and $(1 - k_t)$.
 $h_t = k_t \odot h_{t-1} + (1 - k_t) \odot \hat{h}_t$
This formulation implies that memory only changes when the candidate belief differs from the retained belief and the elapsed-time-dependent "write gain" ($g_t = 1 - k_t$) permits the update. The vector-valued $\tau_t$ allows different dimensions of the latent belief to operate on different effective timescales, enabling some channels to retain information longer (e.g., task context) and others to update more quickly (e.g., local perceptual evidence). This update is shown to be the exact discretization of a first-order continuous-time belief relaxation process and adheres to elapsed-time consistency (Appendix C).

#### 3.3. C. Belief-Conditioned Action Generation

The updated belief $h_t$ is directly injected into the flow-matching action decoder using an adaptive normalization (AdaLN) style modulation.
1. **Memory Projection ($m_t$)**: The belief $h_t$ is projected into the decoder conditioning space.
 $m_t = W_m h_t + b_m$
2. **Timestep Embedding ($z_\tau$)**: The flow-matching timestep $\tau$ is embedded using a multi-layer perceptron with positional encoding.
 $e_\tau = PE(\tau)$, $z_\tau = W_2 \sigma(W_1 e_\tau + b_1) + b_2$
3. **Combined Conditioning ($c_t$)**: The timestep embedding $z_\tau$ and memory projection $m_t$ are additively fused to form a combined conditioning vector $c_t$.
 $c_t = z_\tau + m_t$
4. **AdaLN Modulation**: This $c_t$ vector is used by AdaLN layers within the action decoder to predict feature-wise affine modulation parameters, which are then applied to normalized decoder activations. This allows the temporal context to directly shape the flow velocity output by the action head, influencing the generated action chunk.

#### 3.4. D. Training with Episode-Aware Temporal Batching (EATB)

To handle the challenge of training recurrent belief states while preserving hidden-state continuity across long episodes, TFP introduces Episode-Aware Temporal Batching (EATB). Instead of full-episode backpropagation (memory-intensive) or random chunk shuffling (breaks continuity), EATB samples $B$ active episodes and unrolls each for $K$ consecutive chunks in parallel. An episode-local hidden state $h^{(e)}_t$ is maintained for each episode, initialized to zero at episode boundaries, and carried forward across subsequent training segments. After parameter updates, the final hidden state of each segment is written back with gradient truncation, allowing numerical persistence of memory across segments while limiting backpropagation length.

#### 3.5. E. Adaptive Receding-Horizon Execution

During inference, TFP employs an adaptive receding-horizon executor. While the policy always predicts an action chunk of horizon $H$, the executor dynamically selects a prefix length $E_t \in [E_{min}, H]$ to execute before the next policy query. This decision is based on a risk metric $R_{t,r}$ that considers action jerk, gripper-transition boundaries, and continuity with previously executed actions. The key aspect for memory is that the next memory update receives the actual elapsed interval $\Delta t_{t+1}$ corresponding to the executed prefix length, enabling the LTC dynamics to be calibrated to real physical time.

#### 3.6. F. Comparison to Baselines

The paper systematically compares TFP's memory dynamics to:
- **GRUs**: TFP's retention gate is indexed by physical time ($\Delta t_t$), while standard GRUs are indexed by recurrent steps. Without explicit $\Delta t_t$ input, GRUs cannot enforce elapsed-time consistency.
- **Fixed-Decay Memory**: This is a special case of TFP where the time constant $\tau_t$ is a fixed constant $\tau_0$. TFP's learning of an input-dependent $\tau_t$ allows for adaptive retention.
- **State-Space Models (SSMs)**: While continuous-time SSMs can implement elapsed-time-dependent transitions, TFP uses LTC as a nonlinear, input-dependent belief fuser whose state directly conditions a generative action decoder, making it functionally distinct and action-relevant.

The computational overhead of LTC updates, memory projection, and AdaLN is described as small relative to the VLA backbone.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluate TFP across various simulation benchmarks and real-world tasks, focusing on its ability to improve manipulation performance, encode task-progress information, and benefit from continuous-time dynamics.

#### 4.1. A. Main Benchmark Results

- **LIBERO**: TFP improved the average success rate on LIBERO tasks from 96.9% (for $\pi_{0.5}$) to 98.75%. The most significant improvement was observed on the "long" split, where TFP achieved 97.0% compared to 92.4% for $\pi_{0.5}$. This result supports the hypothesis that maintaining latent task progress is beneficial for long-horizon manipulation.
- **LIBERO-plus**: On LIBERO-plus, which assesses robustness under various perturbations, TFP achieved an average robustness score of 93.77%, outperforming $\pi_{0.5}$ (91.4%). Improvements were particularly noted under noise (88.5% for TFP vs. 85.2% for $\pi_{0.5}$) and lighting perturbations (96.1% for TFP vs. 93.9% for $\pi_{0.5}$).
- **MIKASA-Robo ShellGameTouch**: This diagnostic task isolates belief-state filtering under occlusion. TFP achieved a success rate of 75.0%, which is a substantial improvement over the reported OpenVLA-OFT reference result of 47.0%. The paper notes that MemoryVLA reports 88.0% on this task, positioning TFP's result as an improvement over a reactive VLA in hidden-state reasoning, while also indicating a potential for complementary object-centric hidden-location binding mechanisms.
- **Real-world Galaxea A1 Robot**: On real-world object swapping and counting pick-and-place tasks, TFP demonstrated reduced "stage-level memory failures" compared to the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline. For object swapping, TFP achieved 15/20 successes versus 3/20 for $\pi_{0.5}$. For counting pick-and-place, TFP achieved 18/20 successes versus 8/20 for $\pi_{0.5}$. The failure analysis indicated that reactive baselines often repeated completed subtasks or switched to wrong stages, while TFP's failures shifted towards target grounding and low-level execution errors, suggesting improved task-progress tracking.

#### 4.2. B. Event-Sensitive Belief Dynamics

- **Irregular-Query Diagnostic**: Analysis of the LTC write-gain ($g_t$) during rollouts under adaptive receding-horizon execution (variable $\Delta t_t$) showed that write-gain changes near manipulation events (e.g., reaching, carrying, releasing, pushing) were approximately 6 times larger than during far non-event phases. This indicates that the learned memory actively adjusts its write/retain schedule in an event-sensitive manner, often slightly before simulator-side event labels.
- **Same Observation, Different Memory States**: An intervention study was conducted where the observation, robot state, and instruction were fixed, but the LTC hidden state $h_t$ was varied. This intervention led to substantial changes in the predicted 10-step action chunks (mean pairwise action distance of 0.0442), confirming that the LTC belief causally modulates action generation.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

- **Memory Dynamics**: Comparisons were made between TFP and variants using GRU and S4D for memory, all built on the same $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone and training protocol. TFP consistently outperformed GRU and S4D, particularly on object, goal, and long-horizon tasks on LIBERO, and in robustness on LIBERO-plus. This suggests that the gains are not solely from adding a generic recurrent state, but from TFP's continuous-time memory coupled with direct action-head conditioning.
- **Elapsed-Time Conditioning**: Comparing TFP with "TFP w/o $\Delta t$" (which uses a constant step size instead of the measured elapsed interval for memory updates) demonstrated the importance of elapsed-time conditioning. TFP w/o $\Delta t$ showed a drop in performance, especially on LIBERO Long and LIBERO-plus, indicating that the memory update benefits from receiving the actual physical time between policy queries.

#### 4.4. D. Limitations

The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Training Cost**: Recurrent full fine-tuning with TFP, even with EATB truncating gradients, remains computationally expensive, requiring significant GPU memory and time for large VLA backbones.
- **Hardware Scope**: Real-world evaluations were limited to tabletop single-arm manipulation, leaving more complex platforms (mobile manipulators, humanoids, dexterous hands) for future work.
- **Memory Regime for ShellGameTouch**: While TFP improved ShellGameTouch, the task's requirement for persistent categorical object-location binding is noted as a partial mismatch with TFP's primary design goal of continuous-time, event-sensitive manipulation dynamics. This indicates a potential complementary need for object-centric memory modules.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies (TFP) framework represents an advancement in the capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies for robotic manipulation. Its significance stems from several contributions:

1. **Addressing Stage-Dependent Manipulation**: TFP provides a principled approach to overcome the limitations of reactive VLA policies in stage-dependent tasks. By enabling policies to maintain and update an internal task-progress belief, it allows for appropriate actions even when current visual observations are ambiguous or identical across different task stages. This is crucial for complex, multi-stage, and long-horizon robotic tasks.

2. **Dynamics-Aware and Time-Consistent Memory**: The integration of Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) dynamics is a key innovation. This allows the memory to be explicitly sensitive to elapsed physical time and to adapt its retention or revision based on manipulation events. This continuous-time inductive bias is aligned with the temporal realities of robotic control, where policy queries can occur at irregular intervals. The mechanistic analyses confirm that this event-sensitive belief dynamics is learned and leveraged by the policy.

3. **Direct Action Conditioning**: The method's approach to injecting the updated belief directly into the flow-matching action decoder through AdaLN-style modulation is significant. This design ensures that the learned temporal context actively shapes the generated action chunks, moving beyond memory as a passive historical record or auxiliary input. This direct coupling enables visually similar observations to result in different actions based on differing internal task beliefs.

4. **Improved Robustness and Performance**: The consistent performance improvements on diverse benchmarks, including LIBERO (especially long-horizon tasks), LIBERO-plus (under noise and lighting perturbations), and MIKASA-Robo ShellGameTouch (occlusion-based memory), demonstrate the practical benefits of TFP. The real-world results on a Galaxea A1 robot further validate its effectiveness in reducing memory-related failures and improving task-progress tracking in physical environments.

5. **New Avenues for VLA Development**: TFP suggests that memory in VLA policies should function as an event-sensitive, action-conditioning state, preserving context during stable phases and revising belief during critical events. This paradigm can inform the design of future VLA architectures, prompting further research into combining dynamics-aware memory with other critical aspects like object-centric grounding, spatial generalization, and compositional planning. The identified limitations, such as training cost and the specific memory regime for certain tasks (e.g., categorical object-location binding), also point towards fruitful future research directions, including more efficient recurrent fine-tuning and hybrid memory architectures.

In summary, TFP offers a novel and effective method for endowing VLA policies with a more sophisticated understanding of task progress over time, moving beyond purely reactive control towards context-aware and event-sensitive robotic manipulation.
