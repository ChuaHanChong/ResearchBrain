# Large-Scale Long-Tailed Recognition in an Open World

> [!summary] Summary
> This research defines and addresses Open Long-Tailed Recognition (OLTR), a task integrating imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition. It introduces a dynamic meta-embedding algorithm with modulated attention, achieving consistent outperformance across new large-scale benchmarks like ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, and MS1M-LT, with notable gains in few-shot and open-set scenarios.

## Problem

- Real-world visual data naturally follows a "long-tailed" distribution, where many classes have few examples ("tail" classes) while few have many ("head" classes), hindering recognition system performance.
- Recognition systems must operate in an "open world" where they encounter instances of previously unseen "open" classes, requiring differentiation from known categories.
- Existing machine learning approaches address imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition in isolation, leading to suboptimal performance when all challenges are present simultaneously in natural data.

## Method

- The proposed **Open Long-Tailed Recognition (OLTR)** algorithm maps images to a feature space using a ==dynamic meta-embedding== that combines direct image features with memory-based visual concepts.
- Dynamic meta-embedding leverages a ==reachability calibrator== (based on distance to class centroids) to scale embeddings, indicating familiarity for known classes and signaling novelty for open-set instances.
- ==Modulated attention== is introduced, applying conditional spatial attention to self-attention maps, which helps maintain discriminability across head and tail classes by encouraging adaptive spatial feature usage.

## Results

- The method consistently achieved state-of-the-art performance across new large-scale OLTR benchmarks, including ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, and MS1M-LT.
- On ImageNet-LT, it attained a **35.6%** overall top-1 accuracy in closed-set evaluation and an F-measure of **0.474** in open-set evaluation, outperforming baselines.
- Significant performance gains were observed for few-shot and open-set instances, with notable improvements in identification rates for one-shot (**3.0%** gain) and zero-shot (**1.8%** gain) identities on the MS1M-LT MegaFace benchmark.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A unified approach to imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition is essential for realistic visual data, rather than addressing these challenges in isolation.
> - Combining direct features with meta-learned memory features allows for effective knowledge transfer from data-rich head classes to data-poor tail classes.
> - Dynamically scaling meta-embeddings based on feature proximity to known class centroids provides a robust mechanism for distinguishing between known (head/tail) and unknown (open-set) categories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visual recognition has made significant progress, particularly with deep learning models excelling at classifying common categories with abundant training data. However, real-world visual data naturally exhibit a "long-tailed" distribution, meaning a few "head" classes have many instances, while numerous "tail" classes have very few. Furthermore, recognition systems operate in an "open world" where instances of previously unseen, "open" classes may be encountered.

Traditional approaches in machine learning tend to address these challenges in isolation:
- **Imbalanced Classification** focuses on improving performance on minority classes within a closed set of known categories. Methods include re-sampling, re-weighting, and metric learning.
- **Few-Shot Learning** (often framed as meta-learning) aims to generalize from a very limited number of examples for novel classes. These methods typically assume a balanced training set for base classes and focus on novel classes.
- **Open-Set Recognition** (or out-of-distribution detection) aims to identify instances that do not belong to any of the known training categories. Techniques often involve confidence calibration or statistical modeling of known classes.

The presented work introduces "Open Long-Tailed Recognition" (OLTR) as a unified task that simultaneously addresses these three challenges. It recognizes that real-world data distributions involve head, tail, and open classes concurrently. Existing methods, by focusing on only one aspect, often perform suboptimally across the entire spectrum of class frequencies and novelty. This research positions itself as an effort to integrate solutions for imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition into a single, comprehensive algorithm suitable for natural, real-world data distributions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to define and address the task of Open Long-Tailed Recognition (OLTR), which reflects the characteristics of natural, real-world visual data. This involves learning from datasets where class frequencies follow a long-tailed distribution (containing many-shot "head" classes and few-shot "tail" classes) and where novel, previously unseen "open" classes can appear during evaluation. The overarching goal is to optimize classification accuracy over a balanced test set that includes head, tail, and open classes.

The motivation for defining and tackling OLTR stems from several observations:
- **Prevalence of Long-Tailed Data**: Visual categories in daily life inherently follow a long-tailed distribution, with some classes being common and many others being rare. Existing deep learning models are proficient with abundant "head" class data but struggle with scarce "tail" class data.
- **Open-Ended Nature of the World**: Recognition systems deployed in real environments must be able to encounter and acknowledge instances of categories they have never been trained on, rather than misclassifying them into known categories.
- **Limitations of Isolated Solutions**: Current research typically isolates the problems of imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition. Algorithms developed for one specific challenge often perform poorly when confronted with the full complexity of naturally distributed data. For instance, few-shot learning methods often assume balanced training data, and open-set methods may struggle when distinguishing unknown classes from known but data-scarce tail classes.
- **Practical System Requirements**: A practical recognition system must possess several capabilities simultaneously:
 - Classify accurately among common and rare categories.
 - Generalize category concepts from limited instances.
 - Identify and acknowledge instances of entirely new categories.

The key challenges identified for OLTR are:
- **Tail Recognition Robustness**: Maintaining high recognition accuracy as the number of training instances decreases from thousands (head) to just a few (tail). This requires effective knowledge sharing between data-rich head classes and data-poor tail classes.
- **Open-Set Sensitivity**: Accurately distinguishing unknown "open" classes from known "tail" classes, especially given the sparsity of examples for the latter. This necessitates a mechanism to indicate unfamiliarity or novelty.

To address these challenges, the research aims to develop an integrated algorithm that can manage imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition within a single framework. The underlying principle is to map images to a feature space where visual concepts can be related (for robustness) and novelty can be acknowledged (for sensitivity), balancing the requirements of closed-world classification with open-world awareness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed OLTR algorithm maps an input image to a feature space designed to facilitate conceptual relationships and acknowledge novelty. The model integrates two primary modules: dynamic meta-embedding and modulated attention.

#### 3.1. Dynamic Meta-Embedding

This module combines a direct image feature with an associated memory feature, where the feature norm is used to indicate familiarity with known classes.

- **Direct Feature ($\mathbf{v}_{\text{direct}}$)**: This is a standard embedding produced by the second-to-last layer of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). It is updated through stochastic gradient descent based on the classification loss. While effective for data-abundant head classes, it often lacks sufficient supervision for rare tail classes.

- **Memory Feature ($\mathbf{v}_{\text{memory}}$)**: To enrich the direct feature, especially for tail classes, a memory feature is introduced, inspired by meta-learning approaches. This feature relates visual concepts stored in a visual memory.
 - **Learning Visual Memory ($\mathbf{M}$)**: The visual memory $\mathbf{M} = \{\mathbf{c}_i\}_{i=1}^K$ consists of discriminative centroids for all $K$ training classes. Centroids are computed in two steps:
 1. **Neighborhood Sampling**: Mini-batches are composed of both intra-class and inter-class examples.
 2. **Propagation**: Direct features and centroids are alternately updated to minimize intra-class distance and maximize inter-class distance.
 - **Composing Memory Feature**: For an input image, $\mathbf{v}_{\text{memory}}$ is a weighted sum of the centroids: $\mathbf{v}_{\text{memory}} = \mathbf{o}^T \mathbf{M} = \sum_{i=1}^K o_i \mathbf{c}_i$. The coefficients $\mathbf{o}$ are "hallucinated" from the direct feature using a lightweight neural network, $\mathbf{o} = T_{\text{hal}}(\mathbf{v}_{\text{direct}})$. This mechanism transfers knowledge to tail classes.

- **Dynamic Meta-Embedding ($\mathbf{v}_{\text{meta}}$)**: This combines the direct and memory features and is dynamically calibrated for open-set recognition.
 - **Concept Selector ($\mathbf{e}$)**: A lightweight network $T_{\text{sel}}(\cdot)$ with a $\tanh(\cdot)$ activation function produces $\mathbf{e} = \tanh(T_{\text{sel}}(\mathbf{v}_{\text{direct}}))$. This allows for adaptive selection, where the direct feature is prioritized for head classes, and the memory feature is more influential for tail classes.
 - **Reachability Calibrator ($\gamma$)**: This scalar dynamically scales the meta-embedding and indicates familiarity to known classes. $\gamma$ is defined as the minimum distance between the direct feature and any class centroid in memory: $\gamma := \min_{i} \|\mathbf{v}_{\text{direct}} - \mathbf{c}_i\|_2$.
 - **Combination**: The dynamic meta-embedding is formulated as: $\mathbf{v}_{\text{meta}} = (1/\gamma) \cdot (\mathbf{v}_{\text{direct}} + \mathbf{e} \otimes \mathbf{v}_{\text{memory}})$. When $\gamma$ is small (meaning close to a known centroid), $1/\gamma$ is large, maintaining the embedding's magnitude. If $\gamma$ is large (far from known centroids, likely an open-set instance), the embedding is scaled down towards zero, signaling novelty.

#### 3.2. Modulated Attention

While dynamic meta-embedding facilitates knowledge sharing, modulated attention is designed to maintain discriminability between head and tail classes, particularly by encouraging different spatial feature usage.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of applying spatial attention directly to the input feature map, this method applies conditional spatial attention to a *self-attention map*.
 1. A self-attention map $SA(f)$ is computed from the input feature map $f$ via self-correlation.
 2. Modulated attention $MA(f)$ is then designed as conditional spatial attention applied to $SA(f)$.
 3. The final attention-enhanced feature map is $f_{\text{att}} = f + MA(f) \otimes SA(f)$. This encourages examples to select different spatial contexts based on their class characteristics, thus aiding discrimination.

#### 3.3. Learning

The entire model is trained end-to-end.
- **Cosine Classifier**: A cosine classifier is used, where both the meta-embeddings $\mathbf{v}_{\text{meta}}$ and the classifier's weight vectors $\mathbf{w}_k$ are normalized. A non-linear squashing function is applied to $\mathbf{v}_{\text{meta}}$ to amplify the effect of the reachability calibrator $\gamma$.
- **Loss Function**: The total loss $\mathcal{L}$ combines a cross-entropy classification loss ($L_{\text{CE}}$) with a large-margin loss ($L_{\text{LM}}$) between the meta-embeddings and the centroids:
 $\mathcal{L} = \sum_{n=1}^N L_{\text{CE}}(\mathbf{v}_{\text{meta}}^{(n)}, y_n) + \lambda \cdot L_{\text{LM}}(\mathbf{v}_{\text{meta}}^{(n)}, \{\mathbf{c}_i\}_{i=1}^K)$.
 $L_{\text{LM}}$ minimizes the distance between an embedding and its own class centroid while maximizing its distance from other centroids, separated by a margin.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed OLTR method was evaluated through extensive experimentation on newly curated large-scale benchmarks and compared against various state-of-the-art methods.

#### 4.1. Datasets

Three large-scale OLTR datasets were curated from existing representative datasets:
- **ImageNet-LT**: 115.8K images from 1000 categories, with a Pareto distribution ($\alpha=6$) ranging from 5 to 1280 images per class. ImageNet-2010 classes served as the open set.
- **Places-LT**: 184.5K images from 365 categories, also with a Pareto distribution ($\alpha=6$) ranging from 5 to 4980 images per class. Places-Extra69 images were used as the open set.
- **MS1M-LT**: 887.5K images and 74.5K identities sampled from MS1M-ArcFace, creating a long-tailed distribution. Evaluation was performed on the MegaFace benchmark, which has no identity overlap with the training set, allowing for assessment of generalization.
- **SUN-LT**: Used for direct comparison with specific prior works, featuring 1132 to 1 images per class.

#### 4.2. Evaluation Metrics

Performance was assessed under both closed-set (no unknown classes in test) and open-set (unknown classes present) scenarios.
- **Top-1 Classification Accuracy**: Reported overall and specifically for three subsets of classes based on training sample count: many-shot (>100 samples), medium-shot (20-100 samples), and few-shot (<20 samples).
- **F-measure**: Used for open-set evaluation to provide a balanced measure of precision and recall.

#### 4.3. Competing Methods

The proposed method was compared against representatives from:
- Metric learning (Lifted Loss)
- Hard negative mining (Focal Loss)
- Feature regularization (Range Loss)
- Few-shot learning (FSLwF)
- Long-tailed modeling (MetaModelNet, Model Regression)
- Open-set detection (OpenMax)

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

Experiments on ImageNet-LT isolated the contributions of each module:
- **Dynamic Meta-Embedding**: The combination of memory feature and concept selector significantly improved performance across all "shot" classes (many, medium, few). The reachability calibrator was particularly effective for few-shot classes, aiding discrimination between tail and open classes.
- **Modulated Attention**: Contributed more to the discrimination of many-shot and few-shot classes. It was found to be more effective than directly applying spatial attention on feature maps, suggesting that adaptive context selection is beneficial.
- **Reachability Calibration for Open-World Setting**: Additional experiments on CIFAR100 + TinyImageNet demonstrated that reachability calibration performed favorably compared to standard open-set methods like ODIN and Softmax Prediction.

#### 4.5. Result Comparisons

The method consistently outperformed state-of-the-art alternatives across all benchmarks.
- **ImageNet-LT (Table 3a)**: The proposed method achieved the highest overall top-1 accuracy (35.6% closed-set, 33.9% medium-shot, 17.4% few-shot open-set) and the highest F-measure (0.474). Other methods like Lifted Loss and Focal Loss boosted few-shot performance but often sacrificed many-shot accuracy. OpenMax improved open-set results but struggled with F-measure when compounded with tail classes.
- **Places-LT (Table 3b)**: Using a pre-trained ResNet-152 backbone, the method still demonstrated consistent superiority across all metrics and "shot" categories (e.g., 35.9% overall closed-set, 0.464 F-measure open-set).
- **MS1M-LT (Table 4, left)**: On the MegaFace identification benchmark, the method showed across-the-board improvements, with the most significant gains observed for one-shot (3.0% gain) and zero-shot (1.8% gain) identities.
- **SUN-LT (Table 4, right)**: The proposed method achieved the best accuracy (58.7%), improving 1.4% over MetaModelNet, while incurring less computational cost due to its non-recursive training procedure.
- **Fairness Indication**: On MS1M-LT, the method achieved improvements in identification rates for both 'male' and 'female' sub-groups, indicating a potential for effective fairness learning.

#### 4.6. Further Analysis

- **Memory Feature Infusion**: Visualization showed that the memory feature infused relevant visual concepts (e.g., "bird head," "dotted texture" for a 'cock' image), making meta-embeddings more informative.
- **Influence of Longtail-ness**: The method demonstrated moderate performance degradation as datasets became more imbalanced (decreasing Pareto distribution $\alpha$), suggesting effective knowledge transfer.
- **Open-Set Probability Threshold**: Performance changes steadily with the open-set threshold, indicating robustness to varying thresholds, which is attributed to the reachability estimator.
- **Number of Open Classes**: The method showed robustness to an increasing number of open classes.
- **Failure Cases**: A noted failure case involved slight confusion between many-shot and medium-shot classes due to feature infusion, where cross-category traits were sometimes transferred at the expense of fine-grained discrimination.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Open Long-Tailed Recognition (OLTR) as a task that more accurately reflects the challenges of visual recognition in real-world, naturally distributed data environments. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Formal Problem Definition**: By formally defining OLTR, the work clarifies a critical, yet previously fragmented, area of research. It highlights the necessity of simultaneously addressing imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition, providing a unified framework for future investigations.
- **Integrated Algorithmic Solution**: The proposed dynamic meta-embedding algorithm, coupled with modulated attention, offers a comprehensive solution. It demonstrates how to effectively share visual knowledge between data-rich head classes and data-poor tail classes (robustness) while maintaining sensitivity to distinguish known tail classes from completely unknown open classes (sensitivity). This integration is a departure from prior approaches that tackle these issues in isolation.
- **Curated Benchmarks**: The curation and release of three large-scale OLTR benchmarks (ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, MS1M-LT) are a significant contribution. These datasets provide standardized platforms for evaluating visual recognition algorithms under realistic, long-tailed, and open-ended conditions, filling a gap in existing benchmarks. This facilitates consistent and comparable research.
- **Performance Advancement**: The consistent outperformance of the proposed method against various state-of-the-art baselines across diverse domains (object, scene, face recognition) and different "shot" categories demonstrates its effectiveness. The improvements across many-shot, medium-shot, few-shot, and open classes indicate a balanced and robust recognition capability.
- **Practical Applicability**: The focus on real-world data distributions and the robust handling of various class types mean the research outcomes are more directly transferable to practical applications. This includes scenarios like autonomous driving, medical imaging, wildlife monitoring, and e-commerce, where encountering rare objects or entirely new categories is common.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The publicly available code, datasets, and models lower the barrier for other researchers to engage with the OLTR problem. This can spur further innovation in integrated learning approaches, potentially leading to more adaptable and intelligent visual systems. The work also points to future directions, such as exploring feature disentanglement to mitigate trade-offs in fine-grained discrimination.
- **Implications for Fairness**: The observation that the method provides across-the-board improvements for different demographic sub-groups in face recognition suggests a potential impact on developing more equitable AI systems by inherently addressing data imbalance.
