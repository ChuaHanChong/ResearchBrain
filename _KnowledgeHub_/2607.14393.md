# An offline approach to fNIRS-guided reinforcement learning for robot behavior

> [!summary] Summary
> The NEURO-LOOP framework enables robot reinforcement learning to be guided by offline functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) signals, improving learning rates in a simulated Fetch Pick-and-Place task. By augmenting state-action Q-values and trajectory priorities, the system leverages human neural feedback, especially when introduced early in the training process, to accelerate agent skill acquisition.

## Problem

- Traditional Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HITL-RL) methods often impose cognitive overload on human teachers and exclude individuals with motor or speech impairments due to reliance on explicit feedback.
- Implicit HITL-RL, while reducing cognitive load, still typically requires users to be able to move or speak, thereby not fully addressing accessibility issues.
- Real-time Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) systems, which use intrinsic brain signals as feedback, often face practical challenges related to immediate hardware setup and calibration, limiting deployability.

## Method

- Developed **NEURO-LOOP**, an offline framework integrating ==fNIRS==-derived brain signals into a ==Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient with Hindsight Experience Replay (DDPG-HER)== algorithm for robot learning.
- Employed a ==Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)== to classify fNIRS feature vectors into predicted optimality classes (binary, ternary, continuous) or an error value, accompanied by a confidence score.
- Augmented existing RL parameters (rewards, ==Q-values==, or ==trajectory priorities==) with a recalibrated neural value derived from the performance model's output to modulate the robot's learning process.

## Results

- Q-Augmentation and the combined "All" augmentation condition consistently outperformed the Baseline and other individual augmentation methods, showing improved **success rates** in robot learning.
- Learning speeds were **fastest** when utilizing fNIRS data from passive human interaction, attributed to the balanced distribution of optimal and suboptimal trajectories in this dataset.
- Ablation studies confirmed that performance gains were directly attributable to the neural signal, as increasing synthetic noise in the performance model’s output led to a degradation of performance where gains were initially observed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Augmenting state-action Q-values or applying a combination of all augmentation methods consistently led to superior learning performance compared to modifying rewards or only priorities.
> - Using fNIRS data collected during passive observation of agent behavior resulted in faster learning rates for the robot compared to data from active teleoperation, likely due to a cleaner, more balanced dataset.
> - Injecting the neural signal early in the training process (e.g., at a 0.0 success rate threshold) proved most effective, with benefits diminishing or disappearing if introduced too late in training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is positioned within the domain of Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HITL-RL), a field focused on integrating human feedback to train, finetune, and align autonomous agent behavior with user preferences. Traditional HITL-RL methods, such as learning from demonstration, preference learning, and intervention learning, often rely on explicit feedback (e.g., speech, physical demonstration, interface-based expressions). These explicit approaches can pose challenges, including potential cognitive overload for human teachers due to the need for sustained attention and expert feedback. Furthermore, they may introduce biases by assuming user reactions conform to general distributions and can exclude individuals with motor or speech impairments who cannot provide physical or vocal feedback.

Implicit HITL-RL seeks to mitigate the cognitive load issue by utilizing passive feedback, such as gestures, facial expressions, or voice inflections, to indirectly guide agent behavior. However, these methods typically still require users to be able to move or speak, thereby not fully addressing the problem of accessibility for individuals with impairments.

This paper addresses the limitations of both explicit and implicit feedback by exploring the use of brain signals as the primary feedback channel, a concept referred to as intrinsic feedback. Existing work in this area has explored electroencephalography (EEG)-based error-related potentials (ErrPs) to provide binary feedback for agents in various domains, including games and simulated robot environments. The current work extends this approach by employing functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). fNIRS captures a slower hemodynamic signal with higher spatial resolution compared to EEG and is noted for being potentially more robust to certain artifacts. The authors propose that fNIRS and EEG could be used in tandem in future multimodal systems.

A key distinguishing factor of this research is its focus on an *offline* approach. Unlike real-time brain-computer interface (BCI) systems that require immediate hardware setup and calibration, this work leverages prerecorded fNIRS data to modulate robot learning. This offline strategy aims to offer a practical alternative for scenarios where real-time BCI setups are impractical or only limited data is available, thereby enhancing deployability. The paper also extends existing methods for integrating feedback into RL algorithms by examining the augmentation of trajectory priorities and state-action Q-values, in addition to rewards, within the learning loop.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the identified limitations of existing Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HITL-RL) paradigms. Both explicit and many implicit feedback mechanisms often require active and sustained human engagement, which can lead to cognitive overload for the human teacher. Moreover, these methods frequently assume a general distribution of user reactions, potentially introducing bias, and critically, they can exclude individuals with motor or speech impairments, limiting the accessibility and inclusivity of human-robot interaction systems.

To address these limitations, the authors propose using intrinsic feedback derived from human brain signals as a direct and accessible channel for modulating robot learning. The rationale is that brain signals can convey user intent or evaluation without requiring explicit physical actions or verbal communication, thereby relaxing the constraints on human interaction and potentially enabling broader participation.

The key objectives of this study are:
- **Feasibility Assessment:** To investigate whether brain signals, specifically functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), can be effectively utilized to modulate robot learning in a simulated environment. This involves determining if fNIRS data can be decoded into a value reflecting agent performance and subsequently used to guide a robot's policy.
- **Comparison of Interaction Tasks:** To compare the learning efficacy of agents trained using fNIRS data collected during two distinct human interaction tasks: passive observation (where the participant merely watches the agent) and active demonstration (where the participant teleoperates the robot). This comparison aims to understand how the nature of human involvement influences the utility of the neural signal for learning.
- **Evaluation of Augmentation Methods:** To test various methods for integrating the neural signal into the Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithm. The research focuses on augmenting existing RL parameters—specifically reward, Q-value, and trajectory prioritization—rather than replacing them, to identify the most effective points of intervention for neural feedback.
- **Investigation of Model Granularity and Noise:** To examine how the granularity of the performance model (binary, ternary, or continuous labels for agent optimality) and the presence of synthetic noise in the neural classification output affect the agent's learning outcomes. This objective aims to characterize the robustness and sensitivity of the proposed framework.
- **Validation of Offline Approach:** To demonstrate that the framework is effective when using purely offline fNIRS data. This objective is crucial for validating an approach that offers a practical alternative to real-time Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) systems, which typically entail significant hardware and calibration overhead, thereby enhancing the deployability of such systems in various settings.
- **Ablation Study:** To conduct an ablation study to isolate the individual and combined contributions of each augmentation mechanism and to confirm that observed performance improvements are a direct result of the neural signal, rather than other experimental artifacts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces NEURO-LOOP, a framework designed to integrate offline neural data into a Reinforcement Learning (RL) loop. This framework comprises two primary components: neural classification and the RL algorithm itself.

**Robot Learning Environment and Algorithm:**
The robot agent is trained in OpenAI Gymnasium’s Fetch Pick-and-Place domain (v4), a robotic manipulation environment. The task involves the robot finding, picking up, and placing a black cube at a randomized location on a table. The environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
The chosen RL algorithm is Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient with Hindsight Experience Replay (DDPG-HER), which is augmented with Prioritized Experience Replay, as described in. This prioritization mechanism assigns higher priority to certain batched experiences for sampling, rather than uniform sampling.

**Neural Classification (Performance Model):**
A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) performance model, denoted as M_g(f_t) = ŷ_t, is used to map fNIRS feature vectors (f_t) to a predicted class (ŷ_t) representing agent optimality. The model also outputs a confidence score (c_t), which is the probability that the predicted class matches the true class for classifiers, or 1 minus the estimated action-selection error for regressors.

**Model Granularity:**
The performance model can be trained with different granularities for the optimality label (g):
- **Binary (g_b):** Labels actions as either Optimal or Not Optimal ({0, 1}).
- **Ternary (g_d):** Labels actions as Optimal, Suboptimal, or Not Optimal ({0, 1, 2}).
- **Continuous (g_e):** A regressor that predicts the error between the agent’s selected action and actions chosen by near-optimal policies.

**Dataset and Participants:**
The study utilizes a publicly available dataset that pairs fNIRS recordings with the behavior of an autonomous agent in a robot domain. For each timestep, the dataset includes an MDP tuple (state, action, reward) and the participant’s brain signal. Each MDP tuple is also labeled with three measures of the robot’s action optimality. Data from 21 participants (14 female, 11 male, ages 19–27) were analyzed. Each participant completed one or both of two interaction tasks, with at least 13 participants per condition.

**Interaction Tasks for Data Collection:**
- **Passive:** Participants observe the agent operating. State-action pairs are generated by a near-optimal policy that is forced to execute an approximately equal mix of optimal and suboptimal actions, ensuring each MDP tuple has a known performance label.
- **Active:** Participants teleoperate the robot, meaning the state-action pairs aligned with fNIRS data reflect their own selections, which often include suboptimal actions due to participants not being expert demonstrators.
- **Pooled:** Combines data from both active and passive interaction tasks.

**Finetuning Checkpoints:**
Prerecorded MDP tuples from the dataset are injected into the agent's learning process at specific success rate checkpoints during training. The agent initially learns from scratch. Upon reaching a predefined success rate threshold, it incorporates the offline dataset for further learning until the dataset is exhausted, then resumes online learning. This approach aims to investigate the optimal stage for neural signal injection (early, mid, or late-stage training).

**Tuning the Performance Model Output:**
The raw output of the performance model (ŷ or error) is recalibrated before being applied to the RL loop. The recalibrated neural value, n_t, is calculated as (x_t + c_t * μ_X[ŷ_t]) * β for classifiers, or (x_t + c_t * μ_X) * β for regressors. Here, x_t is the parameter to be augmented (Reward, Priority, or Q-value), c_t is the confidence score, μ_X is an array describing the expected value of the parameter for each optimality class, and β is a scaling factor. This n_t is then used to modulate the RL algorithm.

**Augmentation Techniques:**
Four primary conditions, built upon DDPG-HER with Prioritization, were tested, differing in how n_t modulates the RL process:
- **Baseline:** The prerecorded data is injected at finetuning checkpoints, but no neural augmentation is applied.
- **Prioritization:** n_t modulates the priority assigned to trajectories in the experience replay buffer. If no neural priority is available, the Temporal Difference (TD)-error (δ) serves as the fallback priority.
- **Reward Augmentation:** n_t directly modulates the reward signal received from the environment.
- **Q-Augmentation:** n_t modifies the state-action Q-value.
- **All:** This condition combines Prioritization, Reward Augmentation, and Q-Augmentation simultaneously.

**Model Noise:**
To evaluate the framework's robustness, synthetic noise was injected into the performance model’s output. For classifiers, outputs were flipped with a probability τ. For regressors, outputs were shifted by a random value between 0 and 1.

**Metrics:**
Agent performance was assessed by tracking its total episode return and success rate during evaluation cycles. Evaluations were conducted every 10,000 training steps, with each evaluation consisting of 25 episodes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of fNIRS-guided reinforcement learning in various configurations.

**A. Augmentation Methods**
- **Superior Performance:** Q-Augmentation and the "All" augmentation condition consistently outperformed the Baseline and other individual augmentation methods. This suggests that directly influencing the policy through Q-value modification or a combination of interventions is effective.
- **Modest Improvement:** Prioritization demonstrated a modest but consistent improvement over the Baseline across most interaction tasks. The authors suggest that this improvement might be less pronounced because the Baseline already incorporates TD-error-based prioritization, creating competition with an established ranking scheme.
- **No Significant Improvement:** Reward Augmentation showed no significant improvement compared to the Baseline, aligning with previous research that found neural feedback applied to the reward pipeline performed similarly to agents learning from a dense reward function.
- **Confidence:** All reported differences were determined using 95% confidence intervals.

**B. Model Granularity**
The effectiveness of different model granularities varied depending on the interaction task and augmentation method:
- **Passive Experiments:** Continuous performance models generally outperformed binary and ternary models when used with Q-augmentation. In other augmentation conditions, binary and continuous models performed similarly. Ternary models typically underperformed but still surpassed the Baseline in most conditions, with the exception of Reward Augmentation.
- **Active Experiments:** Binary and ternary models generally outperformed continuous models. Continuous models only marginally surpassed the Baseline in the "All" condition.
- **Pooled Experiments:** Binary models exhibited the strongest performance. Continuous and ternary models generally struggled in pooled data but still showed modest improvements over Baseline in specific conditions (e.g., ternary "All," and continuous Prioritization and Q-Augmentation).
- **Ternary Underperformance Explanation:** The overall weaker performance of ternary models was attributed to noisier outputs from the performance model, as evidenced by lower F1 scores (0.47–0.50 for three-class models) compared to binary models (0.67–0.72). Further support for this was found when injecting 0.5 model noise, causing binary performance in Passive and Pooled conditions to converge towards ternary levels.
- **Continuous in Active:** The poor performance of continuous granularity in Active tasks was hypothesized to be due to accumulated error within the demonstration data, making an error-based signal more challenging to interpret and learn from.
- **Prioritization Consistency:** Prioritization showed minimal variance in performance across different granularities, suggesting that a simple categorization into "good" or "bad" trajectories might be sufficient for this ranking mechanism, with the magnitude of optimality adding less value.

**C. Interaction Task**
The type of human interaction task significantly influenced learning speed:
- **Passive Interaction:** This task led to the fastest learning rates. The authors attributed this to the roughly 50/50 split between optimal and suboptimal trajectories in the passive dataset, which exposed the agent to a greater proportion of optimal performance early in training.
- **Active Interaction:** Active tasks resulted in slower learning. This was likely due to the higher noise inherent in teleoperated data, where participants, not being expert demonstrators, often generated suboptimal or failed trajectories.
- **Pooled Data:** Combining both interaction tasks resulted in learning speeds that were faster than Active alone but slower than Passive alone, consistent with its mixed nature.

**D. Ablation Studies**
- **Finetune Checkpoints:** Ablations revealed that injecting the neural signal early in the training process (at a 0.0 success rate threshold) yielded the best performance. While a 0.2 threshold still produced a measurable benefit, beyond this point, the neural signal ceased to improve learning outcomes.
- **Model Noise:** An ablation study on model noise demonstrated that in conditions with significant performance gains (e.g., Q-Augmentation), increasing synthetic noise in the performance model’s output led to a degradation of performance. Conversely, in conditions without significant gains (e.g., Reward Augmentation), added noise had little to no effect. This pattern supports the conclusion that the observed performance improvements are directly attributable to the neural signal itself, rather than being artifacts of the framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates the viability of utilizing fNIRS signals to augment robot learning algorithms, thereby improving training efficiency. The proposed NEURO-LOOP framework provides a structured approach for evaluating various neural augmentation methods, comparing different model granularities, and testing the robustness of the system to noise and specific finetuning schedules.

The work's primary significance lies in its validation of an *offline* approach to fNIRS-guided reinforcement learning. By showing that neural signals can enhance learning without requiring real-time BCI setups, the framework offers a practical and deployable alternative for environments where such real-time systems are impractical, costly, or where only limited prerecorded data is available. This reduces the barriers associated with hardware overhead and calibration, potentially broadening the applicability of brain-computer interfaces in human-robot interaction. The finding that augmenting trajectory priorities and state-action Q-values is particularly effective provides specific guidance for future development in this area. The ablation study confirming that performance gains are directly linked to the neural signal reinforces the foundational premise of the research.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Increased Accessibility in HRI:** The use of implicit, brain-based feedback, particularly in an offline manner, has the potential to make human-robot interaction more accessible to individuals with motor or speech impairments, who may struggle with traditional explicit or implicit feedback methods.
- **Efficiency in Robot Training:** By providing a direct measure of human judgment regarding agent optimality, neural signals can accelerate the learning process, potentially reducing the time and resources required to train autonomous agents.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** NEURO-LOOP serves as a starting point for further exploration into real-time, physically embodied human-robot interaction systems. It lays the groundwork for more complex, adaptive, and personalized robot behaviors guided by human cognitive states.
- **Practical Deployment:** The offline nature of the framework addresses a significant hurdle for deploying BCI-based systems, offering a more robust and less demanding solution for practical applications.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that suggest avenues for future research:
- **Alignment of Labels:** The current agent performance labels are based on objective agent optimality, which may not perfectly reflect the participant’s subjective judgment or preferences. Future work should explore labeling schemes that more directly capture user preference.
- **Credit Assignment Problem:** The current models use fixed 4-second neural feature windows, which is a simplification for handling the temporal delay inherent in the hemodynamic response measured by fNIRS. A more standardized approach to managing this temporal credit assignment problem is warranted.
- **Comparative Analysis:** Further research should compare this fNIRS-based method against other human-in-the-loop (HITL) approaches to elucidate the unique contributions of neural signals beyond what other feedback channels provide.
- **Real-time and Embodied Systems:** Extending these methods to online settings and integrating them with physical robots are identified as natural and critical next steps to transition the framework from simulation to real-world applications.
