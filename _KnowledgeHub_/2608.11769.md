# Policy-Induced Hand Priors in Humanoid Dual-Arm Manipulation: Diagnosing and Mitigating Initial-Pose Dependence

> [!summary] Summary
> This work diagnoses initial-pose dependence in GR00T-based humanoid dual-arm manipulation, naming the initial-condition-dependent early hand preference a policy-induced hand prior and quantifying it with HandPriorScore, residual bias, and target responsiveness. Wrist-camera masking and bidirectional joint swaps localize the bias to two right-arm joints, while pose diversification lifts mean success from 5.8% to 63.3%.

## Problem

- Humanoid and manipulation evaluation protocols use reference-aligned or fixed initial configurations, making measured policy performance conditional on one selected starting pose.
- Robustness benchmarks quantify only aggregate degradation under perturbation, leaving unclear whether initial-state shifts become shortcuts such as hand-selection bias in dual-arm settings.
- Pose shifts change proprioception, self-pose visual cues, and wrist geometry at once, so success drops alone cannot attribute failure to any single modality.

## Method

- **HandPriorScore**: normalized left-right difference of ==state-relative joint actions== over an initial decision window, decomposed into ==residual hand bias== and ==target responsiveness== across apple locations.
- ==Bidirectional joint-level counterfactual intervention== swapping only the most-divergent right-arm joint values between two behaviorally distinct initial poses, with low-divergence joints as control.
- ==Paired wrist-camera masking== (left, right, both) plus ==PCA-guided pose diversification== and ==targeted local augmentation== of the training set around failure-prone initial configurations.

## Results

- Pose diversification from two target-coupled poses to eight raised mean success from **5.8%** to **63.3%** (high camera) and **14.2%** to **72.5%** (wrist-enabled).
- Swapping the two most-divergent joints cut right-hand attempts on left-target episodes from **87.5%** to **0%**, while the control swap stayed at **100%**.
- Targeted augmentation around a weak pose lifted its success from **30%** to **75%**; adding auxiliary real tasks cut overall success from **61.5%** to **32.6%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aggregate success hides pose-specific failure: policies can pick the wrong hand consistently at one initial configuration while averaging well overall.
> - A few localized joint values, not global state, can act as a causal shortcut switch for action-mode selection in dual-arm policies.
> - More auxiliary data hurts when it dilutes target-task exposure or mismatches observation availability; coverage of failure-prone initial states matters more than volume.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards the development of generalist robot policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models capable of learning complex behaviors from multimodal observations and large-scale demonstrations. These models are expected to exhibit robustness across varied task conditions, including changes in the robot's initial configuration. However, current evaluation protocols in humanoid control and robot manipulation often rely on reference-aligned or fixed initializations. While these standardized setups facilitate controlled comparisons, they may inadvertently obscure performance dependencies on the chosen initial configuration.

Previous studies on VLA policy robustness, such as LIBERO-Plus, have demonstrated that even minor alterations in camera viewpoints or robot initial states can lead to significant performance degradation in policies that otherwise achieve high success rates under benchmark conditions. These studies primarily quantify the extent of performance decline but do not fully elucidate the underlying causes, particularly whether failures stem from proprioceptive states, visual self-pose cues, or their interactions. Related research on vision-proprioception policies indicates that policies might favor concise proprioceptive signals during training, potentially underutilizing visual evidence when visual localization is critical. Other analyses have reported policies overfitting to proprioceptive trajectories or progressing actions despite inconsistent visual information.

This body of work highlights a gap in understanding how initial-state perturbations translate into shortcut behaviors within learned policies, especially in complex tasks like humanoid dual-arm manipulation. In such scenarios, the robot's initial configuration can influence both the approach trajectory and the selection of which arm to use. The current paper addresses this gap by investigating whether an initial robot configuration can act as a shortcut for hand selection, an empirical, initial-condition-dependent preference referred to as a "policy-induced hand prior." It aims to characterize this phenomenon, diagnose its origins, and explore methods for mitigation, thereby contributing to the development of more robust and generalizable VLA policies for humanoid robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to diagnose and mitigate initial-pose dependence in VLA-based humanoid dual-arm manipulation. The motivation for this work stems from observations that aggregated task success rates in VLA policies can obscure specific failures tied to the robot's initial configuration and lead to inappropriate hand selection. Specifically, the authors observed that in a GR00T-based Unitree G1 setting, identical object configurations could elicit different hand-selection behaviors depending on the robot's initial pose. Certain initial configurations consistently triggered a preference for the right hand, even when the target object was located on the left side, while other nearby configurations in the training dataset led to more object-consistent hand choices. This indicates a policy-induced hand prior—an initial-condition-dependent preference for a specific hand-action mode.

This observation led to three detailed research questions that constitute the core objectives of the study:

1. **Localized Initial Arm Configuration Influence:** To determine if localized changes in the initial arm configuration can alter the observed policy-induced hand prior. This objective investigates the causal relationship between specific physical states of the robot's arm and its early hand-selection behavior.
2. **Wrist-Camera Observation Impact:** To analyze how observations from wrist-mounted cameras influence both hand preference and overall task performance. This aims to understand the role of local visual cues in the policy's decision-making process for hand selection.
3. **Robustness Factors in Training:** To investigate how initial-pose coverage in the training dataset, the availability of camera observations (specifically wrist cameras), and the overall composition of training data (e.g., ratio of simulation to real-world data, auxiliary tasks) affect the policy's robustness across diverse initial configurations. This objective seeks to identify effective strategies for data collection and training to improve generalization.

By addressing these questions, the research aims to provide a mechanistic understanding of initial-pose dependencies, characterize the policy-induced hand prior, and offer strategies for developing more robust VLA policies that are less susceptible to initial-state shortcuts in humanoid dual-arm manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic approach involving problem formalization, a novel metric, controlled interventions, and extensive training data manipulation and evaluation.

**A. Problem Formulation and Hand Prior Quantification:**
The authors defined the "policy-induced hand prior" as the initial-condition-dependent preference of a learned policy for early hand-action modes. To quantify this, they developed the **HandPriorScore (H^(T))**, a continuous, normalized measure of left-right action imbalance during an initial decision window (T).
1. **Relative Joint Actions (Δa):** The difference between the policy's predicted target joint position and the current joint state, representing commanded motion.
2. **Normalization:** Each relative action was normalized by a shared joint-wise standard deviation (σ) estimated from pooled evaluation rollouts to prevent high-variance joints from dominating the score.
3. **Root-Mean-Square Magnitude (M):** Computed for normalized relative actions of each arm and hand group to measure overall commanded motion without sign.
4. **Average Action Activity (A^(T)):** Averaged M values over the initial decision window (T=120 timesteps) for each side, combining arm and hand magnitudes with a weight λ for hand contribution.
5. **Raw HandPriorScore:** The direct difference between right- and left-side activities (A^(T)_R - A^(T)_L).
6. **Normalized HandPriorScore (H^(T)):** Normalized the raw score by the sum of left and right activities, resulting in a value between -1 and +1, where positive indicates right-hand preference and negative indicates left-hand preference.
7. **Residual Hand Bias (H^(T)_prior):** The mean HandPriorScore averaged across left and right target conditions, capturing the overall directional bias irrespective of target location.
8. **Target Responsiveness (H^(T)_target):** The difference between the mean HandPriorScore for right targets and left targets, indicating how strongly the policy adjusts hand preference to target location.

**B. Robot and Task Setup:**
The target task was "PickApple," chosen because it necessitates target-dependent hand selection. The experiments were conducted in a simulation environment using the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, equipped with Dex3-1 hands. Policies were fine-tuned from GR00T-N1.5-3B, a vision-language-action foundation model.

**C. Training Data and Configurations:**
A variety of training datasets were used (Table I & II):
- **P_sim:** Simulated PickApple demonstrations from eight diversified initial robot poses, paired with left/right apple placements. Contained both high and wrist camera observations.
- **P_real:** Real-robot PickApple demonstrations, only high-camera observations.
- **Auxiliary Real Datasets:** `7-task` (seven pick-type tasks, high camera only), `13-common` (13 tasks, high camera only), `13-all` (13 tasks, retaining all available camera observations, thus some with high+wrist).
Training configurations (`A` to `E` groups in Table II) systematically varied:
- Use of high-camera only vs. high+wrist cameras.
- Inclusion and breadth of auxiliary real-robot data.
- Simulation sampling ratio (natural vs. reweighted).

**D. Initial-Pose Sets and Evaluation Protocol:**
Policies were evaluated across 17 distinct initial robot poses (Fig. 1, Fig. 3), including:
- Eight diversified training poses (`pose01-pose08`).
- Mean poses (`pose_mean`, `dataset_mean`, `zero`).
- Specific teleoperation-derived poses (`teleop_default`, `right_sim`, `left_sim`).
- Real-robot derived poses (`left_real`, `right_real`).
- A synthetic out-of-distribution pose (`synthetic_unseen`).
Each policy was rolled out 20 times for each initial pose. Task success was manually assessed based on grasping, transporting to a target plate, releasing, and completing arm-lift motion.

**E. Controlled Interventions:**
1. **Initial-Pose Diversification:** Compared policies trained on a limited two-pose dataset (where pose was coupled with target location) against those trained on the diversified eight-pose dataset (pose uncoupled from target).
2. **Targeted Pose Augmentation:** For a policy exhibiting low success at a specific pose (`right real`), additional training poses were added in the local vicinity (along a principal component direction) to investigate localized robustness improvement.
3. **Wrist-Camera Masking:** For diagnostic failures (e.g., `teleop default`), rollouts were performed with left-wrist, right-wrist, or both-wrist camera observations masked. Input-channel sensitivity was analyzed by measuring cosine distance changes in visual, intermediate-token, and predicted-action representations compared to full-observation baselines.
4. **Joint-Level Interventions:** Counterfactual initial configurations were created by swapping specific joint values between behaviorally distinct poses (e.g., `teleop default` and `right sim`). This aimed to localize the causal joint(s) responsible for the policy's hand preference. Swaps were performed on the most divergent joints and on less divergent control joints. Cross-policy generalization of these interventions was also tested.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings concerning initial-pose dependence, policy-induced hand priors, and training strategies.

**A. Initial-Pose Dependence and Policy Interaction:**
- Evaluations across 17 initial poses and multiple policies revealed substantial variations in task success (Fig. 2a). The same initial pose could produce vastly different success rates across policies (e.g., at `pose_mean`, success ranged from 15% to 95%). Conversely, a single policy exhibited significant performance variation across different poses (e.g., policy A1 ranged from 30% at `right real` to 95% at `pose01`). These results indicate strong initial-pose–policy interactions.

**B. HandPriorScore Reveals Residual Hand Bias and Target Responsiveness:**
- The `HandPriorScore` metrics characterized early hand-selection behavior. The `teleop default` initial pose consistently exhibited a strong residual right-hand bias (`H^(T)_prior`, Fig. 2b) and weak responsiveness to target location (`H^(T)_target`, Fig. 2c). This pattern suggested a pose-conditioned shortcut, where the initial robot state exerted a stronger influence on hand selection than the visual target-side cue.

**C. Pose Diversification and Targeted Augmentation Improve Robustness:**
- **Pose Diversification:** Expanding the training dataset from two target-coupled initial poses to eight diversified poses (Fig. 1) significantly improved mean success rates. For example, policy A1's mean success rate across six evaluation poses increased from 5.8% to 63.3%, and A2's increased from 14.2% to 72.5% (Fig. 4). This demonstrated that broader initial-state coverage in training data reduced pose-dependent failures.
- **Targeted Augmentation:** For policy A1, which performed poorly at `right real` (30% success), adding two specific training poses around this configuration in the initial-state space (Fig. 5a, 5b) increased its success rate at `right real` to 75% (Fig. 5c). This indicated that targeted augmentation in failure-prone regions could improve local robustness.

**D. Effects of Auxiliary Data, Wrist Observations, and Simulation Ratio:**
- **Wrist Observations:** Policies trained with wrist-camera observations (A2 vs. A1) generally showed improved overall success (76.8% vs. 61.5%) and diversified-pose success (81.2% vs. 56.9%). However, at the `teleop default` pose, wrist observations strengthened the right-hand bias, increased wrong-hand attempts, and reduced success, suggesting that while generally beneficial, they did not consistently mitigate pose-conditioned hand bias.
- **Auxiliary Real Data and Simulation Ratio:** Adding real-robot auxiliary data to `P_sim` had varied effects. While adding `P_real` (B1) slightly improved success, incorporating larger auxiliary datasets (`7-task`, `13-common` in B2 and B3) consistently reduced overall and diversified-pose success. This decline correlated with a decrease in the natural simulation ratio and indicated that sufficient exposure to the target task (P_sim) was crucial. Reweighting the simulation ratio to increase `P_sim` exposure (D vs. B2) improved overall robustness and target responsiveness but did not resolve specific diagnostic failures like at `teleop default`.
- **Mixed Wrist Availability:** Policies that utilized wrist camera streams when available (C1, E) significantly outperformed their high-camera-only counterparts (B1, D), demonstrating the benefit of comprehensive observation availability, even when requiring zero-filling for missing streams.

**E. Localization of the `teleop default` Failure:**
- **Wrist-Camera Intervention:** Masking both wrist cameras reduced success to 20%. While right-wrist masking on left-target episodes reduced right-hand attempts (from 97.1% to 40.5%), it did not eliminate them entirely. This indicated that wrist observations contributed to the behavior but were not its sole cause. Cosine distance analysis showed that wrist masking primarily influenced incremental motion (`action-delta chunk`) rather than absolute joint targets.
- **Joint-Level Intervention:** Bidirectional swaps of specific right-arm joint values (R_arm,3 and R_arm,5), which exhibited the largest position differences between `teleop default` and `right sim`, significantly suppressed or induced the asymmetric right-hand preference (Table III). For example, swapping these values from `right sim` into `teleop default` reduced right-hand attempts on left-target episodes from 87.5% to 0%. Control swaps of less divergent joints did not produce this effect. A broader four-joint intervention (`R_arm,3`, `R_arm,5`, `L_arm,0`, `L_arm,6`) showed that the prior-removal effect transferred across policies (B1 and C2), although reverse induction remained policy-dependent (Table IV). These interventions identified specific localized initial arm configurations as a causal handle on the pose-conditioned hand prior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and mitigation of robustness challenges in VLA policies for humanoid dual-arm manipulation, particularly concerning shortcut learning from initial robot states.

**1. Characterization and Quantification of Policy-Induced Hand Priors:** The introduction of the "policy-induced hand prior" concept and the `HandPriorScore` (comprising residual hand bias and target responsiveness) provides a novel framework for diagnosing and quantifying initial-pose-dependent hand preferences. This moves beyond aggregate task success metrics to reveal underlying decision-making patterns, enabling more granular analysis of policy behavior.

**2. Identification of Causal Handles:** The joint-level intervention experiments identified that localized changes in specific arm joint configurations (e.g., R_arm,3 and R_arm,5) can causally induce or suppress asymmetric hand preferences. This finding suggests that certain proprioceptive state cues, rather than solely visual observations, can act as direct shortcuts for action mode selection, providing a more precise understanding of how initial states influence policy decisions.

**3. Practical Implications for Training Data Design:** The study offers actionable insights for improving the robustness of VLA policies:
 - **Initial-Pose Coverage:** Demonstrates the critical importance of diversifying initial robot poses in training datasets and ensuring that each pose is paired with multiple target locations to reduce pose-target coupling.
 - **Targeted Augmentation:** Shows that strategically augmenting training data in specific regions of the initial-state space where a policy exhibits low performance can effectively boost robustness in those critical areas.
 - **Data Composition and Modality:** Highlights the need for careful consideration of training data composition, including the ratio of simulation to real-world data and the consistent availability of observation modalities (e.g., wrist cameras). While wrist observations are generally beneficial, their influence can be complex and context-dependent, sometimes exacerbating existing biases. Maintaining sufficient exposure to target task data is shown to be more beneficial than simply increasing the volume of auxiliary data if it reduces target-task exposure.

**4. Advancements in Robot Learning Robustness:** By dissecting the multimodal nature of initial-pose shifts and localizing the origins of shortcut behaviors, this work contributes to the development of more reliable and generalizable robot policies. It provides a foundation for future research to investigate these findings across different tasks, robot embodiments, and VLA architectures, ultimately paving the way for more robust and adaptable humanoid robot systems in complex, real-world scenarios.
