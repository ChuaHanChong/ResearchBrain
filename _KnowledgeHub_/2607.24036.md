# WARL: Wrench-Augmented Reinforcement Learning for Task-Agnostic Learning in Legged Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Wrench-Augmented Reinforcement Learning (WARL) introduces a framework for legged robots that leverages policy-generated wrenches for efficient spatial exploration, allowing for task-agnostic learning of complex dynamic motions. The approach, which employs a switching curriculum to gradually eliminate wrench dependence, successfully learned six diverse motor and goal-reaching tasks for two quadruped robots, significantly outperforming joint-only control methods.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning for legged robots often suffers from limited exploration capabilities due to the complex, non-linear mapping between joint action space and the robot's task space, leading to slow learning and suboptimal solutions.
- Current RL approaches require extensive heuristic design, including task-specific reward functions and environment curricula, which is costly and limits the versatility of learned policies across different tasks.
- Policies learned with external assistance often cannot be directly deployed on real robots, necessitating a method to transition to joint-only control.

## Method

- **WARL** integrates a direct, policy-generated ==wrench== (force and torque) applied to the robot's torso into the action space to simplify action-to-task space mapping and enhance exploration.
- A "==Switching Curriculum==" alternates between a "Joint+Wrench Learning Phase" (optimizing both joint and wrench policies) and a "Joint-only Adaptation Phase" (training the joint policy to reproduce motions with attenuated, saved wrenches), gradually reducing wrench dependence.
- A ==Teacher-Student framework== is used, where a teacher policy with privileged observations guides a student policy, which relies solely on proprioceptive observations, enabling sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- WARL successfully learned complex motions for **six** diverse tasks (HurdleJump, LedgeJump, GapLeap, IslandTraverse, Backflip, Barrel-Roll) across **two** quadruped robot models (KLEIYN and Unitree Go2) using the same learning parameters and reward functions.
- An ablation study demonstrated that the Switching Curriculum is essential for stable learning and wrench dependence removal, and the wrench penalty facilitates smoother adaptation, with joint-only baselines failing to solve the HurdleJump task.
- Learned policies, after the wrench-attenuation curriculum, were successfully executed in a different simulator (MuJoCo), indicating feasibility for sim-to-real deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct wrench application to the robot's torso dramatically improves spatial exploration efficiency, allowing rapid discovery of complex dynamic motions that are difficult with joint-space control alone.
> - The explicit switching curriculum and a penalty for wrench usage are crucial for stable learning and successfully attenuating wrench dependence, leading to deployable joint-only policies.
> - While wrench-guided exploration is highly effective for discovering motions, the majority of training time is dedicated to the adaptive phase where the joint policy learns to reproduce these motions without external assistance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) has advanced the capabilities of legged robots, enabling dynamic maneuvers across various terrains. However, current RL approaches for these robots typically encounter significant limitations. A primary challenge is the reliance on extensive heuristic design, including task-specific reward functions and environment curricula, which is costly and limits the versatility of learned policies across different tasks. For instance, complex terrains often necessitate curriculum designs that gradually increase environmental difficulty, or detailed reward shaping for specific dynamic motions like jumping.

The underlying issue for this design complexity is hypothesized to be the difficulty of exploration. Traditional RL for legged robots often uses joint-space actions (e.g., joint angles or torques), where the mapping to the robot's task space (e.g., center of mass position, orientation) is highly non-linear and influenced by discrete contact states. This complexity impedes efficient exploration, leading to slow learning and convergence to sub-optimal solutions.

Previous research has attempted to mitigate these challenges by:
- **Modifying environment and reward designs:** While successful for specific tasks, these methods often lack generality.
- **Altering action space parameterization:** Approaches have explored using end-effector positions for robot arms or foot-end positions, hierarchical control combining model-based methods with high-level actions, or introducing prior structures like periodic trajectories for legged robots. While these methods improve learning efficiency, they still face non-linearity between the foot-end space and the robot's body pose.
- **Introducing external forces/wrenches:** Some studies have used external forces to facilitate specific dynamic motions (EFGCL), generated model-based wrenches for reference tracking (ZEST), or employed wrenches as auxiliary assistance (A2CF). However, these methods often involve manual design of forces, do not explicitly use wrenches for broad exploration, or evaluate them mainly in tasks already solvable without wrench assistance, leaving the full potential of wrench-guided exploration underexplored.

This work, WARL, positions itself within this landscape by directly addressing the exploration limitation. It proposes using wrenches (forces and torques) as primary exploratory actions, hypothesizing that this direct manipulation of the robot's base body simplifies the action-to-task space mapping, thereby enhancing exploration efficiency. Crucially, WARL combines this wrench-augmented exploration with a curriculum learning scheme that gradually removes wrench dependence, aiming to eventually acquire a joint-only control policy applicable to real robots without external assistance, and to do so in a task-agnostic manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are rooted in addressing the limitations of current reinforcement learning methods for legged robots:

1. **Overcome Limited Exploration Capability:** The core motivation is the hypothesis that the complex, non-linear relationship between the joint action space and the robot's task space (e.g., torso position and orientation) hinders effective exploration. This complexity leads to slow learning speeds and susceptibility to local optima in traditional joint-space control. By introducing a wrench directly applied to the robot's torso, the goal is to simplify this relationship, making it easier to explore diverse body motions.

2. **Enable Task-Agnostic Learning:** Conventional RL frameworks frequently necessitate significant heuristic design, such as meticulous reward shaping and environment-specific curriculum designs, for each new task (e.g., varying terrain heights, specific dynamic maneuvers). This high design cost limits the versatility and scalability of learned policies. The objective of WARL is to develop a method that can learn robustly across a wide range of terrains and motor tasks without requiring such task-specific reward adjustments or complex curriculum designs.

3. **Acquire Deployable Policies for Real Robots:** While wrenches can facilitate exploration in simulation, they cannot be directly applied to real robots during normal operation. Therefore, a critical objective is to develop a curriculum learning method that gradually attenuates and ultimately eliminates the reliance on wrench assistance, ensuring that the final learned policy relies solely on joint control and can be transferred to physical hardware. This involves learning a joint-only control policy that can reproduce the complex behaviors initially discovered with wrench assistance.

4. **Validate Wrench-Guided Exploration:** Prior work has used wrenches primarily as auxiliary or manually designed components. This study aims to rigorously validate the effectiveness of integrating a wrench as a primary, policy-generated exploratory action, demonstrating its capacity to drive efficient spatial exploration and facilitate the discovery of complex dynamic motions that are difficult to achieve with joint-space control alone.

In summary, the motivation is to simplify the learning process for legged robots by enhancing exploration through direct wrench application, leading to more versatile and autonomously learned behaviors that are eventually expressed through standard joint control, thereby reducing development effort and expanding applicability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Wrench-Augmented Reinforcement Learning (WARL) is a framework designed to leverage wrench-based exploration for legged robots while ultimately yielding a joint-only control policy. It operates within a Teacher-Student framework to stabilize learning, where a "teacher" policy uses privileged observations during training, and a "student" policy (for deployment) relies on proprioceptive observations.

**A. Overview of WARL**
WARL employs a **Switching Curriculum** that alternates between two distinct phases:
1. **Joint+Wrench Learning Phase:** Both a wrench policy ($\pi_w$) and a joint policy ($\pi_j$) are optimized simultaneously. The wrench serves as a primary exploratory action, enabling efficient spatial exploration. Successful wrench sequences are stored.
2. **Joint-only Adaptation Phase:** The joint policy ($\pi_j$) is further trained to reproduce the desired motion under attenuated versions of the previously saved wrenches. The wrench policy ($\pi_w$) is frozen, and the goal is to reduce dependence on external assistance.

This curriculum progressively reduces the influence of the wrench through a decaying coefficient $\alpha$, aiming to converge to a joint-only control policy.

**B. Action Space Design**
The action space for WARL is composed of two parts:
- **Joint Action:** The joint policy ($\pi_j$) outputs target joint angles ($\mathbf{q}_{\text{ref}}$) which are converted to joint torques via PD control. This is a standard joint-space action.
- **Wrench Action:** The wrench policy ($\pi_w$) outputs a 7-dimensional vector: (3D force vector $\mathbf{a}_{\text{force}}$, 3D torque vector $\mathbf{a}_{\text{torque}}$, and a 1D scale factor $\mathbf{a}_{\text{scale}}$).
 - The scale factor $\lambda_{\text{scale}}$ is derived from $\mathbf{a}_{\text{scale}}$ using a sigmoid function.
 - The final force $\mathbf{f}$ and torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ components are calculated by clipping, scaling by fixed factors ($\lambda_{\text{wrench}}, \mathbf{f}_{\text{max}}, \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{max}}$), and modulated by the curriculum coefficient $\alpha$ and $\lambda_{\text{scale}}$.
 - The resulting 6D wrench $\mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{f}, \boldsymbol{\tau})$ is transformed to the global frame using the robot's torso orientation and applied directly to the torso link.
 This design allows the policy to generate both the magnitude and direction of the wrench, facilitating broad exploration.

**C. Switching Curriculum (Detailed)**
The curriculum alternates between the two phases based on the success rate ($\zeta$) of the agent.
- **Joint+Wrench Phase:**
 - Both $\pi_w$ and $\pi_j$ are updated using the scaled wrench $\alpha \mathbf{w}_t$.
 - For successful episodes, the generated wrench sequences $\mathbf{W}_i = \{(\mathbf{f}^i_t, \boldsymbol{\tau}^i_t)\}$ are recorded in a replay buffer.
 - This phase continues until the success rate $\zeta$ exceeds a threshold $\gamma$ (e.g., 0.6) and its change $|\Delta \zeta|$ falls below a threshold $\delta$ (e.g., 2e-6).
- **Joint-only Adaptation Phase:**
 - The wrench policy $\pi_w$ is frozen.
 - The joint policy $\pi_j$ is trained using attenuated saved wrenches ($\sigma \mathbf{W}_{\text{saved}}$), where $\sigma$ is a decay coefficient for the saved wrench magnitude. This encourages the joint policy to reproduce the motion with reduced external assistance.
 - This phase also continues until the success rate criteria ($\zeta > \gamma$ and $|\Delta \zeta| < \delta$) are met.
- **Curriculum Progression:** After completing the adaptation phase, the global curriculum coefficient $\alpha$ is updated by $\alpha \leftarrow \sigma \alpha$. This process repeats, gradually reducing the wrench's influence, until $\alpha$ falls below a minimal threshold $\epsilon$ (e.g., 0.01), at which point a joint-only policy is considered learned.

**D. Saving Wrench Sequences**
To select representative wrench sequences for the adaptation phase, the method stores successful sequences.
- When a predefined number of successful sequences ($\text{N}_{\text{succ}}$) are accumulated in the buffer, a "median" sequence is selected based on the norm of the wrench.
- This median sequence $\mathbf{W}_{i^*}$ is then used to update the saved wrench $\mathbf{W}_{\text{saved}}$ through a weighted average with the previous $\mathbf{W}_{\text{saved}}$, using a coefficient $\eta$ (e.g., 0.9). This weighted update helps to stabilize the adaptation process by preventing extreme or noisy sequences from dominating. A weighting factor $\kappa$ balances force and torque magnitudes in the norm calculation.

**E. Teacher-Student Learning**
To address the challenges of high-dimensional state spaces and sparse rewards in legged locomotion, especially when using only proprioceptive observations, a teacher-student framework is adopted. During training, a "teacher" policy receives privileged observations (e.g., target position error, terrain information) that are not available on a real robot. After training, a "student" policy is distilled from the teacher, which only takes proprioceptive observations as input, making it suitable for real-world deployment.

**F. Reward and Observation Design**
- **Observations:** Both proprioceptive observations ($\mathbf{o}_{\text{prop}}$), available on a real robot (e.g., robot posture, velocity, contact info, joint configurations, base angular velocity), and privileged observations ($\mathbf{o}_{\text{priv}}$), available only in simulation (e.g., position error, target orientation, terrain heights, linear velocity), are defined for each task. A phase variable $\phi$ is introduced for learning stabilization.
- **Rewards:** A common reward structure is used for goal-reaching tasks (HurdleJump, LedgeJump, GapLeap, IslandTraverse) and dynamic tasks (Backflip, Barrel-Roll). Key reward terms include:
 - **Goal-reaching:** Target position proximity, standing pose maintenance, joint configuration (to default), angular velocity minimization, collision penalties, and a penalty for wrench magnitude (to encourage wrench reduction).
 - **Dynamic tasks:** Target angle proximity, maintaining a specific height, joint configuration, angular velocity around the target axis, no-tilt rewards (to prevent unwanted rotations), collision penalties, and wrench magnitude penalty.
 A curriculum weight $w_{\text{cur}}$ is also applied to certain penalty terms to dynamically adjust their influence during learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated WARL using two quadruped robot models, KLEIYN and Unitree Go2, in the Isaac Gym simulation environment, across six diverse tasks: HurdleJump, LedgeJump, GapLeap, IslandTraverse (goal-reaching), and Backflip, Barrel-Roll (dynamic).

**A. Learning Results in Simulation**
- **Versatile Learning:** WARL successfully learned motions for all six tasks for both KLEIYN and Unitree Go2. This was achieved using the same learning parameters and reward functions, with only a minor adjustment for the robot's standing height ($z^*$). This demonstrates the method's ability to achieve versatile learning without extensive task-specific tuning.
- **Curriculum-Driven Adaptation:** The learning curves show an overall improvement in reward, punctuated by temporary decreases when the curriculum-driven wrench decay coefficient ($\alpha$) was updated. These drops were consistently followed by a recovery in reward as the joint policy adapted to the reduced wrench assistance, indicating the effectiveness of the adaptive learning process.
- **Wrench's Role in Motion Generation:** In tasks like HurdleJump, analysis of the learned wrench and joint motions revealed that the wrench largely generated the primary motion (e.g., lifting the robot to clear the hurdle), while the joint control coordinated leg movements (e.g., lifting feet) to complement this wrench-induced action. This highlights the dual role of the wrench in guiding the main movement and enabling the joint policy to learn coordinated sub-motions.
- **Efficiency of Exploration vs. Adaptation:** The success rate quickly reached near 1.0 within approximately 100 iterations during the initial Joint+Wrench phase, suggesting that the wrench effectively facilitated the discovery of the target motion. However, completing the full curriculum and achieving wrench-free performance took around 2000 iterations. This indicates that while wrench-guided exploration rapidly identifies a feasible motion, the majority of the training time is dedicated to the adaptive learning phase, where the joint policy learns to reproduce that motion autonomously without external assistance.
- **Embodiment Utilization Challenges:** In the IslandTraverse task, the robots learned to jump directly over the 1.4 m gap, entirely neglecting the two intermediate 0.2 m square footholds. This suggests that the strong wrench-based exploration encouraged direct, possibly less energy-efficient, solutions over dexterous motions that utilize environmental features or the robot's specific physical embodiment, which might have been more challenging for the wrench to discover or encourage.

**B. Ablation Study**
An ablation study on the HurdleJump task investigated the contribution of WARL's components:
- **WARL (Proposed Method):** Demonstrated the most stable and consistent improvement in success rate throughout the learning process.
- **w/o Switch (Without Switching Curriculum):** Learning was inconsistent, succeeding in only 3 out of 10 trials. The policies learned tended to remain dependent on the wrench, leading to a decrease in success rate during the final stages of the curriculum as wrench influence was reduced. This indicates that the explicit alternation between Joint+Wrench and Joint-only adaptation phases (the Switching Curriculum) is crucial for stable learning and wrench dependence removal.
- **w/o Wrench Reward (Without Wrench Penalty):** Although learning eventually succeeded, the decrease in success rate during phase switches was more pronounced compared to WARL. This suggests that without a penalty for wrench usage, the policy in the Joint+Wrench phase tends to become overly reliant on the wrench, making the subsequent adaptation more challenging. The wrench reward term contributes to a smoother transition by encouraging less wrench dependence from the outset.
- **Baseline (PPO with Joint Control Only):** The success rate remained low throughout learning, demonstrating that the HurdleJump task was difficult to solve with only joint control and without the wrench-guided exploration. This result strongly validates the effectiveness of introducing the wrench for enhancing exploration.

**C. Execution of Policy for Real Robot in MuJoCo Simulator**
The policies learned in Isaac Gym (using the Teacher-Student framework) were successfully transferred and executed on the MuJoCo simulator, demonstrating similar motions for HurdleJump, LedgeJump, and GapLeap. This indicates the feasibility of deploying WARL-trained policies on different simulation environments, a step towards real-robot deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study presents WARL as a novel approach that significantly impacts the field of legged robot control by addressing fundamental challenges in reinforcement learning.

**A. Significance**

1. **Enhanced Exploration Efficiency:** The most immediate significance is the demonstration that introducing a wrench into the action space dramatically improves the spatial exploration capability of legged robots. This accelerates the discovery of complex and dynamic motions, which are often intractable or require extensive manual tuning with conventional joint-space control.
2. **Reduced Heuristic Design Dependence:** WARL reduces the necessity for task-specific reward engineering and environment curricula. By enabling versatile learning across diverse terrains and dynamic tasks with a unified reward function and fixed environmental parameters, it simplifies the design process for new robot behaviors.
3. **Principled Wrench Attenuation:** The proposed Switching Curriculum provides a structured method for gradually eliminating wrench dependence, which is crucial for deploying policies on real robots where external forces cannot be directly applied. The ablation study verifies the necessity of this curriculum and the wrench penalty for stable and effective learning.
4. **Teacher-Student Framework for Real-World Applicability:** The integration of a Teacher-Student framework allows the use of privileged observations during training for stability, while ensuring that the final "student" policy is learnable from proprioceptive inputs, facilitating sim-to-real transfer.

**B. Potential Impact**

1. **Accelerated Robot Development:** By making complex motion learning more efficient and less dependent on manual design, WARL could significantly shorten the development cycle for legged robot applications, enabling faster prototyping and deployment of new functionalities.
2. **Broader Task Capabilities:** The ability to learn a wider range of behaviors in a task-agnostic manner could enable legged robots to operate effectively in highly unstructured and previously inaccessible environments without needing extensive pre-programming for each new scenario. This includes overcoming varied obstacles, performing dynamic maneuvers, and potentially adapting to unforeseen conditions.
3. **Foundation for Advanced Learning Frameworks:**
 - **Improved Embodiment Utilization:** The observation that wrench-guided exploration sometimes leads to motions that do not fully exploit the robot's physical embodiment (e.g., jumping over footholds) opens avenues for future research. This could involve developing hierarchical reinforcement learning approaches where the wrench policy optimizes for the "learnability" of the joint policy, or using wrench-generated motions as imitation targets to guide the joint policy towards more embodiment-consistent behaviors.
 - **Knowledge Transfer Across Robots:** The abstract nature of wrench inputs, being less tied to specific joint configurations, suggests a potential for cross-embodiment knowledge transfer. A wrench-based policy could serve as a common representation for motion primitives, allowing different robots (e.g., bipedal, multi-legged) to share and adapt learned skills, thereby advancing the generalization capabilities of robot learning.
 - **New Design Principles:** The success of WARL contributes to expanding the design principles for reinforcement-learning-based robot control, emphasizing the importance of action space parameterization for exploration efficiency.

In conclusion, WARL represents a significant step towards more autonomous and versatile learning for legged robots by strategically integrating wrench-augmented exploration with a robust curriculum. While challenges remain in fully optimizing for robot embodiment, the framework establishes a foundation for future advancements in efficient, task-agnostic, and deployable robot motor control.
