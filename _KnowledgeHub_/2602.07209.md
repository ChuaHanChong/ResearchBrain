# Continuum Robot Localization using Distributed Time-of-Flight Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Toronto Robotics Institute and Oxford Robotics Institute developed a continuous-time, factor-graph-based estimation framework for full-body localization of deformable continuum robots. This method uses sparse, distributed Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensors and achieved sub-centimeter translational accuracy in simulation, along with centimeter-level accuracy in real-world experiments when navigating known and partially unknown environments.

## Problem

- Miniaturization of continuum robots (CRs) restricts the integration of high-resolution sensing, necessitating sparse, low-resolution sensors for localization.
- Estimating the full-body pose of deformable CRs is complex due to their non-rigid nature, unlike traditional rigid robots.
- Onboard, sparse sensors in unstructured, cluttered environments often yield degenerate measurements, where individual sensors lack sufficient features for robust localization.

## Method

- A continuous-time, **factor-graph-based Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimation framework** fuses sparse ToF measurements with a robot shape prior.
- ==Point-to-plane ToF measurement factors==, ==robust Cauchy loss==, and gyroscope factors are integrated, with strain factors included in simulation.
- The framework employs a sliding-window formulation and ==Gauss-Newton optimization== to achieve stable full-body localization for deformable CRs.

## Results

- Simulations showed an average translation Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of **0.61-0.78 cm** and a rotation MAE of **0.96 degrees**, demonstrating robustness to map deviations.
- Real-world experiments in known environments achieved average position errors of **1.2-2.5 cm** and rotational errors of **3.7-10.3 degrees**.
- Localization error generally increased from the robot's base to its tip, with the tip exhibiting an average translation MAE of **3.6 cm** and rotation MAE of **9.9 degrees** due to error accumulation and susceptibility to local minima.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating sparse, low-resolution ToF measurements with a robot shape prior within a continuous-time estimator can overcome frequent geometric degeneracies in sensor data.
> - Robust cost functions enable localization to function effectively even when the prior environmental map has moderate deviations from the true scene.
> - The combination of diverse sensor modalities (e.g., ToF, gyroscope, and simulated strain) is essential for mitigating spatial drift and local minima in continuum robot localization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Continuum robots (CRs) are recognized for their compliance and potential for miniaturization, which makes them suitable for inspection and manipulation tasks in confined or unstructured environments, such as surgical procedures, aircraft inspection, and search and rescue operations. A prerequisite for autonomous operation in such settings is accurate localization and environmental mapping. However, the miniaturization of CRs introduces significant challenges for integrating high-resolution sensing systems typically used in mobile robotics, such as large lidar units. This size constraint necessitates the use of smaller, lower-resolution sensors.

Existing approaches to CR localization often rely on dense sensing, assumptions of rigid-body mechanics, or external tracking systems. These methods are frequently impractical or inadequate for small, deformable robots operating in cluttered, unstructured environments. Onboard sensors in such contexts are often sparse, low-resolution, and prone to geometrically degenerate measurements, where individual sensors cannot acquire enough features to robustly determine their position.

Previous research has explored various sensing modalities for CRs. Off-robot sensing systems, including ultrasound, electromagnetic trackers, fluoroscopic imaging, and external cameras, have been utilized, but they typically require line-of-sight or specialized equipment, limiting their applicability in many real-world scenarios. Onboard sensing, while more challenging due to size and weight constraints, has shown promise. Camera-based localization has been explored, sometimes employing distributed cameras along the robot body for full-state estimation and configuration. Time-of-flight (ToF) sensors offer advantages such as native depth perception and increased robustness in visually degraded environments.

Regarding ToF sensing in CRs, prior work includes studies using arrays of 40-160 small ToF sensors for rigid snake robots in simulated environments, and investigations into miniaturized ToF sensors for soft robot state estimation. Another work integrated single-beam ToF sensors into CRs but required external motion capture for localization.

This research addresses a gap in the existing literature by focusing on onboard-only localization of *deformable* continuum robots using *sparse, distributed, low-resolution ToF sensors* without external tracking. It specifically tackles scenarios characterized by frequent measurement degeneracies and potential mismatches between the prior map and the actual environment, moving beyond rigid-body assumptions prevalent in some earlier distributed ToF sensing work and the reliance on external localization systems. The work integrates these sparse measurements with a robot shape prior within a continuous-time state estimation framework, aiming to enable robust localization where individual sensor readings would otherwise be insufficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop and validate a localization technique for continuum robots (CRs) that utilizes small, low-resolution Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensors distributed along the robot's structure. This technique is designed to function effectively in unstructured environments, even when individual sensors frequently encounter degenerate measurement scenarios.

The motivation for this research stems from several critical challenges in continuum robotics:
- **Miniaturization and Sensor Constraints:** CRs are often designed for operation in constrained spaces, which necessitates miniaturization. This, in turn, imposes strict size and weight limitations on onboard sensing equipment. Traditional high-resolution ToF sensors (e.g., lidar) are generally too large for practical integration into CRs.
- **Deformable Nature of CRs:** Unlike rigid robots, CRs undergo significant deformation, making their full-body pose estimation more complex than tracking a single rigid body. Existing localization methods often rely on rigid-body assumptions, which are unsuitable for CRs.
- **Unstructured and Cluttered Environments:** CRs are intended for environments that are often unstructured and cluttered. In such settings, onboard sensors, especially sparse and low-resolution ones, may frequently fail to observe enough distinct features to uniquely constrain the robot's position or orientation. This leads to "degenerate measurements" where individual sensors are insufficient.
- **Limitations of Current Localization Methods:** Many current CR localization approaches depend on dense sensing, external reference systems, or simplifying rigid-body assumptions. These dependencies limit their applicability for small, deformable robots that must operate autonomously with only onboard, sparse sensors. Furthermore, partial inaccuracies or mismatches between a robot's prior environmental map and the true environment can lead to estimator failure in these settings.

To address these motivations, the authors established two key contributions:
- **Development of a Continuous-Time, Factor-Graph-Based MAP Estimation Framework:** This framework is designed to fuse sparse ToF measurements with a robot shape prior. Its specific design targets stable full-body localization of deformable CRs under conditions of frequent geometric degeneracies in sensor data and partial mismatches in the prior environmental map.
- **First Real-World Demonstration of Onboard-Only Localization:** The research aims to provide the first empirical validation of onboard-only localization for a soft, extensible continuum robot operating in cluttered environments. This demonstration utilizes distributed, low-resolution ToF and inertial sensing, with validation conducted across both simulated and physical platforms.

In essence, the work seeks to enable CRs to determine their full-body pose relative to an environment using minimal, integrated sensing, thereby facilitating greater autonomy in challenging operational contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a continuous-time, factor-graph-based Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimation framework, building upon prior work in continuous-time state estimation for CRs. This approach formulates state estimation as an optimization problem, minimizing a total cost function composed of weighted squared-error factors, and uses a Gauss-Newton solver for optimization with uncertainties estimated via a Laplace approximation.

**State Representation:**
The robot's state, denoted as **x(s, t)**, is modeled as a continuous function of time (t) and arc length (s). It includes the robot's continuous poses **T_ib(s, t) ∈ SE(3)** (transformation from robot body frame to inertial frame), body-centric generalized strains **ε(s, t) ∈ R^6**, and body-centric generalized velocities **ω(s, t) ∈ R^6**. During optimization, the state is estimated at discrete time steps and arc lengths, with a continuous representation achieved through an interpolation scheme. A sliding-window formulation is utilized for the estimation.

**Measurement Factors:**
The framework integrates different sensor modalities through specific measurement factors:

1. **Time-of-Flight (ToF) Sensor Factor:**
 - ToF measurements are incorporated using a point-to-plane measurement factor, inspired by lidar-based methods.
 - The error function for a single measurement *j* is defined as **e_j(x) = αn_j^T D(p_j - T_ib(t, s)q_b_j)**. Here, **p_j** is a matched point from a local environmental map, **n_j** is the estimated normal at **p_j**, **D** is a projection matrix, and **q_b_j** is the query point in the robot's body frame corresponding to measurement *j*.
 - A heuristic weight **α** is applied to favor planar neighborhoods, derived from the eigenvalues of the covariance obtained via principal component analysis of the local map neighborhood.
 - The query point **q_b_j** is assumed to be corrupted by isotropic Gaussian noise, with its covariance **R_ToF** tuned experimentally. A distance-based standard deviation model (Table I) is used, with linear interpolation for intermediate distances, alongside additional noise to account for unmodeled process errors.
 - To mitigate the effect of outlier measurements, a robust Cauchy loss function is implemented via an iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) scheme.
 - The linearized error for the Gauss-Newton update step is computed using an SE(3)-sensitive approach.

2. **Gyroscope Sensor Factor:**
 - Gyroscope measurements are modeled as described in.
 - Gyroscope biases are approximated as constant over small time scales and determined by averaging an initial stationary bias.

3. **Strain Sensor Factor (Simulation Only):**
 - For simulated data, strain information (bending angle **θ_j** and curvature **κ_j**) is mapped to a measured strain value **ε̃_j**.
 - An error factor is defined as **J_j = 0.5 * (ε_j - ε̃_j)^T R_strain^-1 (ε_j - ε̃_j)**, where **R_strain** is an isotropic strain measurement covariance.

**Prior Map Generation:**
Each localization instance requires a prior map of the environment.
- **Simulated environments:** Maps are generated by Poisson sampling of CAD models.
- **Real-world environments:** Maps are created by scanning the scene with a Creality Raptor 3D scanner, which provides 0.1 mm accuracy and 0.1-2 mm resolution. Reflective markers in the scene are used for coordinate alignment with a motion capture system.
- The map is stored as a hashed voxel map for efficient nearest neighbor queries. Environmental deviations (unmodeled objects or missing features) are introduced by providing an estimator with a prior map that differs from the true environment.

**Scene Reconstruction:**
After state estimation, the robot's estimated trajectory and ToF measurements are utilized to reconstruct a point cloud of the environment. Each ToF measurement is projected into the inertial frame using the estimated robot pose at the time of measurement, along with its associated covariance to account for uncertainty.

**Simulation Details:**
- **Physics Engine:** MuJoCo is used to simulate a CR in a confined environment resembling a jet engine.
- **Robot Model:** A 1.5-meter-long CR is modeled as a series of rigid links (1 cm each) connected by spherical joints, approximating a pseudo-rigid body. It features three tendon-actuated modules.
- **Sensors:** Sensor discs, each with three radially mounted ToF sensors and one gyroscope, are placed every 30 cm along the robot. An additional ToF sensor is at the tip. A strain sensor is simulated along the center, providing bending strain measurements every 3 cm.
- **Environment:** A scaled model jet engine (3.1 m outer diameter) is used. Ten simulated scenes (S0-S9) are created, varying in the presence of unmodeled objects, missing features, or both.
- **Sensor Noise:** ToF sensors simulate VL53L5CX characteristics (8x8 grid, 45° FOV, 4 m max range) with distance-dependent noise. Gyroscope measurements include zero-mean Gaussian noise (0.01 rad/s). Strain measurements include Gaussian noise for curvature (0.01 m⁻¹) and bending angle (0.015 rad).

**Experimental Setup (Real-World):**
- **Environment:** A 70 cm x 70 cm x 60 cm cube-shaped environment is used, populated with various objects.
- **Ground Truth:** Four OptiTrack cameras provide ground truth measurements of the robot's pose.
- **Robot:** A 53 cm long soft, extensible continuum robot with three modules made of TPU 82A flexures.
- **Sensors:** Each module includes a sensorized ring equipped with three VL53L5CX ToF sensors and an ISM330BX IMU (only gyroscope measurements are used). ToF sensors are oriented at 120° apart, with modules axially offset by 60°.
- **Data Collection:** Five environments (R0-R4) are created by progressively adding objects (mustard bottle, cup, cracker box, wet floor sign). A total of 23 trajectories are collected, with the robot actuated by four tendons.
- **Tuning:** The estimator parameters (process and measurement noise) are initially tuned using one hold-out trial in a fully known environment (R4 for real-world, S0 for simulation). The chosen parameters, which minimize average localization error, are then applied across all other trials.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents localization performance across both simulated and real-world experiments, with parameters tuned once on a hold-out trial and applied to all other conditions.

**A. Simulation Results:**
A series of simulation experiments were conducted using 10 scenes (S0-S9) that introduced varying levels of deviation from a prior map (S0, representing a fully known environment). Three robot trajectories were executed in each scene.
- **Localization Accuracy:** The simulation results (Table II) indicated an average translation Mean Absolute Error (MAE) ranging from 0.61 cm to 0.78 cm and an average rotation MAE of 0.96 degrees across all scenes and sensor rings.
- **Robustness to Map Deviations:** The robust cost function (Cauchy loss) implemented for ToF factors appeared effective, as no noticeable degradation in localization performance was observed when the prior map deviated from the true environment due to added or removed geometries.
- **Sensor Contribution:** The study highlighted the importance of the strain sensor in simulation; without it, estimates suffered from "spatial drift." Conversely, ToF sensors alone often converged to local minima due to the environment's radial symmetry. The combination of ToF and strain sensing contributed to the high accuracy observed in simulation.
- **Visualization:** Side-by-side comparisons (Fig. 4) showed that the estimated robot shape and trajectory closely matched the ground truth. Scene reconstructions from ToF measurements (Fig. 5) also aligned well with the true scene, indicating that small anomalies had minimal impact on localization or reconstruction accuracy in the simulated setting.

**B. Real-World Experiments:**
Localization performance was evaluated across various combinations of prior maps and real environments, with environments progressively modified by adding or removing objects.
- **Localization in Known Environments:** When the prior map accurately matched the real environment (Table III, "Localization within known environment"), the estimator demonstrated consistent and accurate performance. Average position errors ranged from 1.2 cm to 2.5 cm, and rotational errors ranged from 3.7 degrees to 10.3 degrees.
- **Impact of Environmental Deviations:**
 - **Object Addition:** When an object was added to the environment (e.g., prior map R0, actual scene R1), localization error increased by an average of 1.7 cm in position and 1.0 degree in rotation across all trials. The largest degradation occurred when a large wet-floor sign was added (R3 – R4), leading to an average position error of 5.1 cm and a rotational error of 6.5 degrees.
 - **Object Removal:** When an object was removed (e.g., prior map R1, actual scene R0), localization error increased by an average of 0.5 cm in position and 1.5 degrees in rotation. The most significant degradation was observed when the large wet-floor sign was removed (R4 – R3), resulting in an average position error of 3.6 cm and a rotational error of 14.6 degrees.
- **Error Distribution Along Robot Body:** Across all experimental conditions, the localization error typically increased from the base to the tip of the robot (Table IV). The tip ring exhibited the worst performance, with an average translation MAE of 3.6 cm and a rotation MAE of 9.9 degrees. This is attributed to error accumulation and the tip's increased susceptibility to local minima, despite direct ToF constraints.
- **Visualization of Performance:** A median-performing trial (Fig. 6) demonstrated the estimator's capability to track the robot's shape reasonably well, achieving an average position error of 1.7 cm and a rotational error of 6.7 degrees at marker locations.
- **Scene Reconstruction Accuracy:** Scene reconstruction accuracy correlated with localization performance (Fig. 7). When the prior map was accurate, reconstruction closely matched the real environment (per-point RMSE of 0.8 cm for R4–R4). However, when large anomalies degraded localization, the reconstructed scene also showed significant deviations (per-point RMSE of 1.4 cm for R3–R4).

**C. Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors identify several limitations and areas for future development:
- **Sensor Degeneracies and Local Minima:** Sparse ToF measurements, even with robustness measures, remain susceptible to local minima, especially with poor initialization, significant map deviations, or sudden robot movements. The current experimental setup (extensible robot, limited FOV, scene symmetries) contributes to this challenge. The simulated strain sensor's "restoring force" highlights the benefit of diverse sensor modalities. Future work could incorporate accelerometer data from the IMU.
- **Estimator Smoothing and Tradeoffs:** The spatiotemporal smoothing inherent in the estimator, while aiding regularization and mitigating noisy measurements, can limit the system's ability to capture rapid changes in robot shape.
- **Prior Map Dependency:** The method's accuracy is closely tied to the accuracy of the prior map. Larger deviations between the map and the real scene lead to degraded performance, limiting applicability to scenarios where a reasonably accurate prior map is available. Future work aims to relax this requirement, potentially through live map updates or enhanced robustness to map inaccuracies.
- **Anomaly Detection:** A preliminary anomaly detection application, comparing reconstructed points against the prior map using Mahalanobis distance, showed better results in simulation than in real-world experiments. Real-world false positives near highly reflective or low incidence angle objects indicate that further improvements in localization accuracy and noise reduction are required for reliable anomaly detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a advancement in the localization capabilities of continuum robots (CRs), particularly for their application in constrained and unstructured environments. The core significance lies in demonstrating that full-body localization of *deformable* CRs is achievable using *sparse, low-resolution Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensors* distributed along the robot's body, without the reliance on external tracking systems.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Enabling Autonomous Operation:** By providing a method for onboard-only localization, this research contributes to a critical prerequisite for truly autonomous operation of CRs in real-world applications such as surgical interventions, internal inspection of complex structures (e.g., aircraft engines), and search and rescue operations in hazardous or confined spaces. This autonomy is crucial for tasks where external tracking is infeasible or impractical.
- **Overcoming Sensor Constraints:** The approach effectively addresses the miniaturization challenge by showing that sparse, low-resolution sensors, which are compatible with the size and weight constraints of CRs, can be utilized collectively to achieve robust localization. This contrasts with methods requiring larger, denser sensing systems.
- **Robustness to Challenging Conditions:** The developed continuous-time, factor-graph-based MAP estimation framework demonstrates a capacity to integrate sensor measurements despite frequent geometric degeneracies (where individual sensors lack sufficient features for unambiguous localization). Its use of a robust cost function allows it to function under moderate deviations between the prior environmental map and the actual scene, enhancing its practicality in dynamic or partially unknown environments.
- **Foundation for Future Sensing and Mapping:** This work establishes a foundation for more advanced simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) capabilities for deformable robots. By accurately localizing the robot within an environment, it also enables environmental reconstruction, which could be extended to dynamic mapping and anomaly detection for inspection tasks.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The identified limitations, particularly concerning susceptibility to local minima in highly deviated scenes and strong dependency on prior map accuracy, provide clear directions for future research. This includes exploring the integration of additional sensor modalities (e.g., accelerometers), developing methods for real-time map updates, and improving robustness against map inaccuracies to expand the applicability of the technique.

In summary, this research offers a pathway toward enhancing the autonomy and operational reach of continuum robots by providing a robust, onboard localization solution tailored to their unique characteristics and the demanding environments in which they are intended to operate.
