# Huxley-Gödel Machine: Human-Level Coding Agent Development by an Approximation of the Optimal Self-Improving Machine

> [!summary] Summary
> The Huxley-Gödel Machine is a tree-search method for self-improving coding agents. It replaces benchmark-score guidance with Clade-Metaproductivity, an estimate aggregating success and failure counts over an agent's whole descendant subtree, selects nodes by Thompson sampling with an annealed exploration schedule, and decouples expansion from evaluation for asynchronous runs. It reaches 61.4% on full SWE-bench Verified and, transferred to GPT-5, matches the best human-engineered agents on SWE-bench Lite.

## Problem

- The original Gödel Machine accepts only provably utility-increasing self-modifications, but its reliance on formal proof search makes it impractical for real coding agents.
- DGM and SICA expand whichever agent scores highest on coding benchmarks, yet high scorers can seed stagnant lineages while weaker agents found more productive ones.
- Prior methods also fuse expansion and evaluation, so each new child monopolizes evaluation budget even when an older, more promising agent deserves further probing.

## Method

- **Clade-Metaproductivity (CMP)** scores an agent by pooled pass/fail counts across its entire descendant subtree; Theorem 1 proves a true CMP oracle suffices to implement the ==Gödel Machine==.
- Expansion selects parents by ==Thompson sampling== over clade success/failure counts, scaled by a monotone scheduler that anneals from exploration early to sharp exploitation as budget depletes.
- A separate selection policy, borrowed from ==UCB-Air== infinite-armed bandits, decides expand-versus-evaluate per step at single agent-task granularity, enabling early stopping and asynchronous parallel execution (**HGM-Async**).

## Results

- CMP estimator correlates **0.778** weighted with empirical clade metaproductivity on a 60-task SWE-bench Verified subset versus **0.444** for SICA and **0.285** for DGM; **0.626** versus **0.274** and **0.383** on Polyglot.
- Best-belief agents reach **56.7%** on SWE-Verified-60 and **30.5%** on Polyglot, beating DGM (**53.3%**, **27.1%**) and SICA (**50.0%**, **25.4%**), using **2.38x** and **6.86x** fewer CPU-hours than DGM.
- Scaled to full SWE-bench Verified, the discovered agent hits **61.4%**; it transfers to SWE-bench Lite at **49.0%** standard, **40.1%** filtered, and **57%** with a GPT-5 backbone.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimize for lineage potential, not node score: aggregating descendant outcomes both tracks long-term promise and pools more samples, so estimates are statistically sturdier.
> - Decoupling candidate generation from candidate evaluation is a cheap win: it lets a search abandon weak branches early instead of spending budget defending every new child.
> - A self-modifying agent optimized on one benchmark and a small backbone kept its edge under simultaneous dataset and model shift, evidence against pure benchmark overfitting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields: foundational AI theory, meta-learning, self-improving systems, and large language model (LLM)-powered agentic software engineering.

Historically, the concept of machine self-improvement has fascinated researchers, with early theoretical work by Good (1966) on "intelligence explosion" and Schmidhuber's own contributions dating back to the late 1980s (Schmidhuber, 1987). The **Gödel Machine (GM)**, proposed by Schmidhuber in 2003, represents a theoretical pinnacle in this quest: a fully self-referential algorithm that provably optimizes its own code to achieve higher expected utility. However, the reliance on formal proofs makes the GM practically challenging to implement.

The advent of powerful **Large Language Models (LLMs)** has provided a new impetus for practical self-improving agents. LLMs demonstrate remarkable capabilities in understanding, generating, and even debugging code, making them ideal candidates for building "coding agents" that can operate within conventional computing environments. Recent works like ChatDev (Qian et al., 2023), SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024), OpenHands (Wang et al., 2024), and MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024) exemplify the growing interest in automating aspects of software engineering using LLMs.

Building upon this, a more recent wave of research focuses on **self-modifying LLM agents**, where agents not only solve tasks but also edit their own underlying code or scaffolding. The Self-Taught Optimizer (Zelikman et al., 2024) and Gödel Agent (Yin et al., 2024) began exploring this. Crucially, the Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM) directly positions itself as an advancement over contemporary self-improving coding agents such as the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) (Zhang et al., 2025a) and Self-Improving Coding Agent (SICA) (Robeyns et al., 2025). These prior methods operationalize self-improvement by iteratively modifying agents and selecting those that show higher immediate performance on software engineering benchmarks.

The authors identify a critical flaw in this approach: the **Metaproductivity–Performance Mismatch (MPM)**. This mismatch highlights that an agent with high immediate benchmark performance might lead to unproductive descendants, while a seemingly lower-performing agent might seed a lineage that achieves far greater long-term gains. HGM directly addresses this limitation by introducing a more sophisticated, lineage-based metric for guiding self-improvement.

Furthermore, the paper situates its tree-search problem within existing **optimization and search literature**, including fixed-budget best-arm identification (BAI), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), and infinite-armed bandits. However, it distinguishes its problem by the unique "explicit expansion actions that create new candidate leaves alongside ordinary evaluations," which are not fully captured by these traditional frameworks. HGM's methodology, particularly its adaptive selection and evaluation policies, draws inspiration from these areas while tailoring them to the specific challenges of self-modifying agents.

In summary, this work provides a significant theoretical and practical bridge: it takes Schmidhuber's theoretical Gödel Machine, identifies a key limitation in its current LLM-based approximations (MPM), and proposes a novel, empirically validated solution inspired by biological evolution (clades) and statistical decision-making (Thompson sampling) to create more robust and truly self-improving coding agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a more effective and theoretically grounded approach for building **self-improving coding agents** that can evolve towards human-level performance in software engineering tasks. This overarching goal is driven by a critical insight into the limitations of existing self-improvement methods.

The central motivation stems from the identified **Metaproductivity–Performance Mismatch (MPM)**. Current self-improving coding agents, exemplified by DGM and SICA, typically guide their self-modifications by favoring agents that demonstrate higher immediate performance on coding benchmarks. The authors argue that this greedy heuristic is fundamentally flawed because it fails to account for an agent's *long-term potential for future self-improvement*. An agent that scores highly on current benchmarks might be a dead end in terms of its ability to spawn even more capable future versions, while a seemingly mediocre agent might possess the genetic "code" to unlock a lineage of profoundly better agents. This mismatch means that solely optimizing for immediate performance can lead to suboptimal search trajectories, trapping the self-improvement process in local optima.

To overcome the MPM, the paper sets forth several key objectives:
- **Define a Reliable Metric for Self-Improvement Potential**: To move beyond immediate benchmark scores, the authors aim to introduce a new metric that accurately reflects an agent's "metaproductivity"—its capacity to improve its own self-improvement skills, leading to better downstream performance of future agents. This led to the concept of **Clade-Metaproductivity (CMP)**, inspired by Huxley's biological notion of clades.
- **Establish a Theoretical Link to Optimal Self-Improvement**: To ground their proposed metric in foundational AI theory, a crucial objective is to demonstrate that access to a CMP oracle is sufficient to simulate the behavior of a Gödel Machine under a specific (and practical) set of assumptions for coding agent development. This objective aims to provide a strong theoretical justification for their approach.
- **Develop a Practical Algorithm to Approximate the Gödel Machine**: Based on the CMP concept and its theoretical backing, the authors aim to design a concrete algorithm, the **Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM)**, that can estimate CMP from partial evaluations and leverage this estimate to guide the self-improvement search effectively.
- **Empirically Validate HGM's Superiority**: A significant objective is to empirically demonstrate that HGM can discover higher-quality optimized agents more efficiently than existing state-of-the-art self-improving methods (DGM and SICA) across various challenging software engineering benchmarks.
- **Achieve Human-Level Performance and Robust Generalization**: The ultimate practical objective is to show that HGM can design coding agents that achieve human-level performance on standard benchmarks like SWE-bench Lite and demonstrate robust transferability across different datasets and underlying LLMs, thereby proving its real-world utility and generalizability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of the Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM) revolves around formalizing self-improvement as an iterative tree-search problem and introducing a novel metric, Clade-Metaproductivity (CMP), to guide this search more effectively than previous methods.

#### 3.1. Formalizing Self-Improvement as Tree Search

The paper models the self-improvement process as a tree search where each node represents an agent. Starting from an initial agent (root), the policy incrementally grows the tree by either selecting an agent to *expand* (producing a self-modified child agent) or selecting an agent for *additional evaluation*. The objective is to discover a final agent that maximizes performance on downstream tasks within a computational budget.

#### 3.2. Addressing the Metaproductivity–Performance Mismatch with CMP

The key insight is the "Metaproductivity–Performance Mismatch" (MPM), where immediate benchmark performance is a poor predictor of an agent's long-term self-improvement potential. To address this, HGM introduces:
- **Clade-Metaproductivity (CMP)**: Inspired by Huxley’s concept of clades (lineages of common ancestry), CMP quantifies an agent's potential for self-improvement by aggregating the success of *all its descendants* (its clade), rather than just its immediate benchmark score. This provides a more robust, long-term view of an agent's true "metaproductivity."
- **Theoretical Foundation (Theorem 1)**: The paper establishes a critical theoretical link: under specific assumptions tailored for self-improving coding agent development (Assumption 1, including repeatable trials, fixed utility function, and unit cost for self-modifications), access to a *true CMP oracle is sufficient to implement the Gödel Machine*. This theorem provides a strong theoretical justification for using CMP as the guiding principle for HGM, positioning it as an approximation of the theoretically optimal self-improving machine.

#### 3.3. The Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM) Algorithm

HGM approximates the Gödel Machine by estimating CMP from clade-aggregated descendant outcomes and leveraging this estimate to make expansion and evaluation decisions. It explicitly decouples expansion from evaluation, treating them as separate, adaptive steps, unlike prior methods (SICA, DGM) that conflate these choices. HGM is defined by three sub-policies:

1. **Expansion Policy**:
 - **CMP Estimation**: HGM estimates CMP for each agent `a` in the tree using the ratio of total successes to total (successes + failures) across *all agents in its clade* `C(a)`: `[CMP(a) = n_C_success(a) / (n_C_success(a) + n_C_failure(a))`. This aggregation provides a more statistically robust estimate of long-term potential.
 - **Node Selection**: To select which parent agent `a*` to expand (i.e., generate a self-modification from), HGM uses **Thompson Sampling**. This Bayesian approach samples `a*` probabilistically based on its estimated CMP, incorporating an exploration-exploitation scheduler `τ`. This ensures a balance between exploiting promising lineages and exploring less certain ones, adapting over time.

2. **Evaluation Policy**:
 - **Adaptive Evaluation**: Rather than evaluating newly created agents immediately on all tasks, HGM adaptively decides which *existing* agent to evaluate further.
 - **Agent Selection**: It uses Thompson Sampling on *individual agent performance* (based on `n_success(a)` and `n_failure(a)`) to select `a*` for an additional task evaluation. This prioritizes agents with higher empirical means, effectively inducing a "soft maximum" selection within the clade, which in turn feeds into the robustness of the CMP estimator.

3. **Selection Policy**:
 - **Decoupling Expansion and Evaluation**: HGM distinguishes itself by making a compound decision at each step: whether to *expand* (create a new child agent) or *evaluate* (test an existing agent on one more task).
 - **Decision Criterion**: This choice is inspired by the UCB-Air strategy from infinite-armed bandit literature. HGM expands the tree (creates a new agent) if the number of evaluations `N^α_t` is greater than or equal to the number of existing agents `|T_t|`, promoting exploration when the tree is sparse and evaluation when it is rich.

#### 3.4. Final Agent Selection Strategy

Upon exhausting its computational budget, HGM selects the final best agent by returning the one with the highest `ϵ` percentile of its utility posterior, formally `argmax_{a∈T_B} I_ϵ(1 + n_success(a), 1 + n_failure(a))`, where `I` is the regularized incomplete beta function.

#### 3.5. Asynchronous Implementation (HGM Async)

To maximize computational efficiency, especially given the lengthy execution times of coding agents, HGM employs an asynchronous execution model. HGM Async concurrently runs iterations on all available CPUs, continuously updating shared data. This parallelization significantly reduces wall-clock time, allowing for more comprehensive self-improvement within practical timeframes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical validation of HGM demonstrates its superiority over existing self-improving coding agent methods and its capacity to achieve human-level performance. The experiments were conducted on SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Lite, and Polyglot datasets, utilizing various LLM backbones.

#### 4.1. Mitigation of Metaproductivity-Performance Mismatch (MPM)

- **Weak Correlation in Baselines**: The paper empirically confirms the MPM, showing that the guidance metrics used by SICA and DGM (based on immediate benchmark performance) exhibit weak Pearson correlations with true empirical Clade-Metaproductivity (CMP). Specifically, SICA showed correlations of 0.444 (SWE-Verified-60) and 0.274 (Polyglot), while DGM showed 0.285 and 0.383, respectively.
- **HGM's Stronger Alignment**: In contrast, HGM's `[CMP` estimator demonstrated substantially stronger weighted correlations of 0.778 (SWE-Verified-60) and 0.626 (Polyglot), and unweighted correlations of 0.512 and 0.873. This provides direct evidence that HGM's lineage-based metric effectively mitigates the MPM, offering a more reliable predictor of long-term self-improvement potential.

#### 4.2. Superior Self-Improving Capability and Efficiency

HGM consistently outperformed DGM and SICA in terms of both the quality of the discovered agents and the computational resources required.
- **SWE-Verified-60 (60 tasks)**:
 - HGM achieved a best-found accuracy of **56.7%**, a **+16.7%** gain over the initial agent, in **517 CPU hours**.
 - DGM reached 53.3% (+13.3%) in 1231 CPU hours.
 - SICA reached 50.0% (+10%) but encountered persistent errors, indicating an "infinite loop."
 - HGM was **2.38 times faster** than DGM while achieving higher accuracy.
- **Polyglot**:
 - HGM achieved **30.5%** accuracy (+10.2% gain) in **347 CPU hours**.
 - DGM reached 27.1% (+6.8%) in 2385 CPU hours.
 - SICA reached 25.4% (+5.1%) in 572 CPU hours.
 - HGM was remarkably **6.86 times faster** than DGM and **1.65 times faster** than SICA, demonstrating superior efficiency.

#### 4.3. Human-Level Coding Agent Design

HGM-discovered agents showed remarkable performance, often matching or exceeding human-engineered counterparts.
- **Optimization on Full SWE-bench Verified**: An agent optimized by HGM on the full 500 tasks of SWE-Verified (with GPT-5-mini) achieved **61.4%** accuracy. This performance surpassed the best human-designed agent based on GPT-5-mini on the SWE-Verified leaderboard, placing HGM among the top-10 overall agents even against systems using much more expensive LLMs.
- **Generalization to SWE-bench Lite**:
 - The best HGM agent discovered on SWE-Verified exhibited strong generalization to SWE-bench Lite (300 tasks), achieving **40.1%** on filtered (completely unseen) tasks and **49.0%** on the standard benchmark.
 - This represents significant gains over its initial ancestor (34.8% filtered, 44.0% standard), confirming that HGM fosters general coding ability, not just overfitting.
 - The HGM agent also outperformed an adapted SWE-agent using GPT-5-mini (39.6% filtered, 47.6% standard), highlighting genuine design improvements.
- **Transfer to Bigger LLMs**:
 - When the HGM-optimized agent (originally with GPT-5-mini) was paired with the more powerful GPT-5 LLM, it maintained strong performance: **47.8%** on filtered SWE-Lite and **57%** on standard SWE-Lite.
 - This performance is comparable to, or even slightly surpasses, the best state-of-the-art human-engineered coding agents reported on the SWE-bench Lite leaderboard (e.g., SWE-agent with Claude 4 Sonnet, which achieved 48.3% filtered and 56.7% standard). This critical result demonstrates that the design principles evolved by HGM are robust and transfer effectively across different LLM backbones, further validating the genuine improvement in agent design.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM) represents a significant advancement in the field of self-improving AI agents, offering both theoretical rigor and compelling empirical results. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Paradigm Shift in Self-Improvement**: HGM introduces a crucial paradigm shift by moving beyond immediate performance metrics towards **clade-based measures of improvement potential (CMP)**. This emphasis on long-term generative capacity, rather than short-term gains, provides a more robust and biologically inspired approach to self-evolution. It corrects the "Metaproductivity–Performance Mismatch" that limits previous self-improving agents, leading to more effective and sustainable improvement trajectories.

- **Bridging Theory and Practice**: The work successfully bridges Schmidhuber's theoretical concept of the **Gödel Machine** with practical, LLM-based agent development. By proving that a CMP oracle is sufficient to approximate the Gödel Machine under reasonable assumptions, the paper provides a strong theoretical grounding for its algorithmic choices. This is a vital step towards realizing the grand vision of optimally self-improving AI.

- **Enhanced Efficiency and Performance**: HGM consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines like DGM and SICA, not only achieving higher accuracy but also doing so with **substantially fewer allocated CPU hours**. This efficiency gain, partly attributed to its asynchronous execution and adaptive evaluation strategies, makes large-scale self-improvement more feasible and cost-effective for complex tasks like software engineering.

- **Automated Human-Level Agent Design**: The ability of HGM to discover coding agents that achieve **human-level performance** on challenging benchmarks like SWE-bench Lite is a landmark achievement. This suggests that HGM can automate significant aspects of agent design, potentially surpassing human-engineered solutions under identical model constraints. This reduces the burden on human developers to hand-craft optimal agent architectures and strategies.

- **Robust Generalization and Transferability**: The demonstration that HGM-optimized agents generalize well across unseen tasks and transfer effectively to different (and more powerful) LLM backbones underscores the robustness of the discovered self-improvement principles. This indicates that HGM is learning genuinely better ways for agents to interact with their environment and solve problems, rather than merely overfitting to specific datasets or models.

- **Potential for Broader Impact**: While demonstrated in the context of coding agents, the core principles of HGM – particularly the use of clade-level metaproductivity and the adaptive decoupling of exploration/evaluation – could be applicable to other domains where self-improving AI agents operate. This includes areas such as robotics, scientific discovery, complex system optimization, and even the design of other AI models themselves.

In conclusion, the Huxley-Gödel Machine represents a significant leap forward in the quest for truly self-improving artificial intelligence. By emphasizing the long-term potential of agent lineages over immediate performance, it offers a more principled, efficient, and ultimately powerful framework for developing autonomous agents capable of continual evolution and human-level achievement. This work underscores the importance of designing systems that not only solve problems but also continuously enhance their own capacity for improvement over time.
