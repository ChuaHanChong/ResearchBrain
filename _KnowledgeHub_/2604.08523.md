# ClawBench: Can AI Agents Complete Everyday Online Tasks?

> [!summary] Summary
> ClawBench is a benchmark of 153 everyday, write-heavy online tasks (purchases, bookings, applications) on 144 live production websites. A Chrome extension and CDP server block only the final submission request, so tests are safe. Runs are recorded in five layers and scored by an Agent-as-Judge against human reference trajectories. Claude Sonnet 4.6 tops eight frontier models at only 33.3%.

## Problem

- Sandboxed benchmarks such as WebArena and OSWorld use static, controlled pages, so they hide the dynamic interfaces, logins, validation and anti-bot defenses of real production websites.
- Live-web benchmarks such as WebVoyager and Online-Mind2Web focus mostly on read-only information lookup, not state-changing workflows like purchases, reservations, applications and detailed form submissions that users actually delegate.
- Evaluating write-heavy tasks on real sites risks irreversible side effects, and existing benchmarks offer no safe interception plus traceable, evidence-grounded scoring with human reference trajectories.

## Method

- ==Final-request interception==: annotators mark each task's terminal HTTP request; a Chrome extension plus ==CDP instrumentation server== captures its payload and blocks it before reaching the server, while all other traffic proceeds normally.
- ==Five-layer trajectory recording== (session video, action screenshots, HTTP traffic, agent messages, browser actions) is captured for every agent run and for the ==human reference run== of every task.
- ==Agent-as-Judge==, built on **Claude Sonnet 4.6** with a fixed prompt, compares the task instruction, agent trace, human reference and intercepted payload, and returns a binary verdict with a structured, evidence-grounded justification.

## Results

- Across **8** frontier models under one OpenClaw harness, Claude Sonnet 4.6 leads at **33.3%**, then Qwen 3.5 at **26.1%** and GLM-5 at **24.2%**. GPT-5.4 scores only **6.5%** and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite **3.3%**.
- **68 of 153** tasks (**44.4%**) are solved by no model and none by all eight. Sonnet 4.6 scores **33.3%** here versus **66.4%** on OSWorld-Verified and **72.5%** on WebArena-Verified.
- Sonnet 4.6 costs **$7.51** per task, about **12 times** GLM-5's **$0.64**, for **9.1 points** more success. Failed runs use more tool calls (median **120** versus **64**) and often stop before the final action.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Strong scores on sandboxed web benchmarks do not transfer to live, write-heavy tasks; benchmark saturation on controlled environments hides real-world gaps in agent reliability.
> - Failures are not just reasoning errors: anti-bot walls, non-human typing and mouse patterns, safety refusals and hesitation before the final transactional step are recurring bottlenecks needing behavioral and robustness fixes.
> - Blocking only the terminal request gives safe live-web evaluation with full ecological validity. More spending or more tool calls does not reliably help, since failures are mostly unproductive retry loops.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of AI agents capable of interacting with the web to assist users with various tasks is an active area of research. Existing web-agent benchmarks have contributed to this field by providing controlled environments and specific task types for evaluation. However, these benchmarks exhibit limitations in comprehensively assessing an agent's ability to handle the full complexity of real-world online tasks.

Previous work in web agent evaluation can be broadly categorized:
- **Static-trace benchmarks** like Mind2Web (Deng et al., 2023) focus on evaluating action prediction over recorded pages, not end-to-end task execution on live sites.
- **Sandboxed or self-hosted benchmarks** such as WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024), VisualWebArena (Koh et al., 2024), OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024), and TheAgentCompany (Xu et al., 2026) provide controlled environments. While valuable for reproducibility and isolation, these settings inherently abstract away the instability, dynamic nature, heterogeneity, and anti-bot defenses found on production websites.
- **Real-web benchmarks** including WebVoyager (He et al., 2024), AssistantBench (Yoran et al., 2024), Online-Mind2Web (Xue et al., 2025), and EconWebArena (Liu & Quan, 2025) move evaluation to live websites. However, these often primarily focus on read-only information acquisition or answer extraction, rather than state-changing workflows. Claw-Eval (Ye et al., 2026) examines trajectory-aware grading in controlled agent settings but does not evaluate broad everyday transactions across live platforms.

CLAWBENCH addresses a specific gap by aiming to evaluate AI agents on everyday, "write-heavy" tasks that involve modifying real web state (e.g., purchases, applications, reservations) while operating on live production websites. The benchmark incorporates a safety mechanism to prevent irreversible side effects, which is a key challenge for real-world, state-changing tasks. This combination of daily-life relevance, write-heavy interactions, and live-web execution with safety features distinguishes CLAWBENCH from prior benchmarks, which typically offer only a subset of these properties or operate under more controlled, less realistic conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this work is to determine whether current AI agents possess the capability to reliably complete common online workflows on real, live websites. While AI agents have shown promise in assisting with tasks involving information retrieval or structured environments, their performance on complex, dynamic, and state-changing interactions in uncontrolled web environments remains largely untested in a comprehensive and safe manner.

The research identifies several limitations of existing benchmarks that motivate the development of CLAWBENCH:
- **Lack of realism:** Many benchmarks use static page traces or sandboxed environments, which do not capture the dynamic nature, authentication flows, platform-specific validation logic, and anti-bot defenses of live production websites.
- **Focus on read-only tasks:** A significant portion of existing real-web benchmarks primarily evaluates information acquisition or extraction, neglecting "write-heavy" tasks that involve submitting data or modifying server-side state.
- **Difficulty of safe evaluation:** Evaluating write-heavy tasks on live websites poses a challenge in preventing irreversible real-world side effects (e.g., actual purchases, job applications, or reservations).

To address these motivations and limitations, CLAWBENCH was developed with three key objectives:

1. **Provide Daily-Life Coverage:** To assess agents on tasks relevant to everyday user needs, CLAWBENCH includes 153 online tasks sourced from regular life and work activities. These tasks span 15 fine-grained categories and are executed across 144 distinct live web platforms.
2. **Focus on Write-Heavy Workflows:** The benchmark specifically targets tasks that require state-changing interactions. These include making purchases, booking reservations, submitting applications, updating accounts, and filling out detailed forms. Success on these tasks depends on accurately inputting user-specific information, adhering to platform-specific validation, and reaching a terminal action that would modify server-side state.
3. **Ensure Live-Web Execution with Safe Evaluation:** Agents interact directly with live production websites to preserve the full complexity and challenges of real web environments. A critical objective is to prevent unintended real-world side effects. This is achieved through a final-request interception mechanism that blocks the terminal HTTP request that would commit an irreversible action, while allowing all preceding interactions to occur authentically.

By fulfilling these objectives, CLAWBENCH aims to provide a more ecologically valid and challenging testbed for AI agents, thereby exposing existing gaps in their capabilities as general-purpose online assistants.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for CLAWBENCH involves a structured approach to task design, a robust execution environment for live-web interaction with safety protocols, and a detailed, trace-based evaluation system.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Design and Collection

CLAWBENCH focuses on collecting tasks that are "write-heavy" and reflect everyday online activities.
- **Task Sourcing:** Tasks are derived from real user-grounded website usage, where participants identified regularly used websites and proposed realistic tasks involving information submission.
- **Task Formalization:** Each candidate task is formalized into an auditable task card, including a natural-language instruction, a starting URL, any required user files or profile fields, the expected final state, and the specific terminal submission target for safe evaluation.
- **Filtering:** The candidate pool undergoes de-duplication and manual filtering to exclude unsuitable workflows such as read-only lookups, unavailable/restricted sites, paid subscriptions without safe interception points, hard human-verification steps (e.g., mandatory phone OTP, government ID checks), and workflows whose irreversible effects cannot be controlled by a single terminal HTTP request.
- **Reference Trajectories:** For each retained task, a human annotator completes the workflow within the same recording environment, generating a human reference trajectory and the corresponding terminal submission evidence.
- **Scope:** The final benchmark comprises 153 tasks across 144 live production websites, organized into 8 high-level and 15 fine-grained categories. This design prioritizes broad coverage over depth on a few sites to test generalization across heterogeneous interfaces and defenses.

#### 3.2. Safe Live-Web Execution

A core component of CLAWBENCH is its safety mechanism for interacting with live production websites:
- **Final-Request Interception:** During human reference runs, annotators identify the specific terminal HTTP request (URL pattern, method, required payload fields) that would commit an irreversible task (e.g., submitting an order). During agent execution, a Chrome extension and a Chrome DevTools Protocol (CDP) instrumentation server monitor outgoing HTTP requests. When an agent triggers a request matching the identified terminal submission target, the system captures the payload, blocks the request *before* it reaches the server, and records the interception event.
- **Ecological Validity:** All other network traffic, including page loads, JavaScript calls, and authentication steps, proceeds normally, ensuring agents face the real interface dynamics of the live website without causing actual side effects.
- **Safety Envelope:** This mechanism creates a task-scoped safety envelope. The benchmark explicitly excludes workflows where side effects cannot be bounded by a single, annotated terminal request (e.g., uncontrolled account setting changes, real payments without interception, chained irreversible writes).

#### 3.3. Trace-Based Evaluation

CLAWBENCH employs a detailed, multi-layer recording and an AI-assisted evaluation system:
- **Five-Layer Trace Recording:** Every agent run is recorded with synchronized evidence across five layers: full-session video, per-step screenshots, HTTP traffic, agent messages (reasoning), and low-level browser actions. This allows for fine-grained analysis and diagnosis.
- **Human Reference Alignment:** Human annotators produce reference trajectories under the identical recording setup, making agent and human runs comparable across page states, action sequences, submitted fields, and terminal payloads.
- **Agent-as-Judge Evaluator:** Each run is scored by an Agent-as-Judge, implemented using Claude Sonnet 4.6. This evaluator receives the task instruction, task-card constraints, the human reference trajectory, the agent's trace, and the intercepted payload. It issues a binary pass/fail verdict with a structured justification grounded in the recorded evidence. The evaluation is outcome-oriented; agents are not required to follow the exact path of the human reference if they reach an equivalent terminal state.
- **Evaluation Criteria:** A run passes if the agent completes the required workflow and either reaches the intercepted final request with correct or semantically equivalent payload values, or is blocked at an allowed terminal point after completing all prior steps. It fails if key fields are incorrect/missing, the agent stops before attempting the terminal action, or external blockers prevent completion under the rubric.
- **Auditing:** The Agent-as-Judge protocol was audited against human-adjudicated subsets for two contrastive models, showing raw agreement rates of 84.97–93.46% with human verdicts.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Models Evaluated:** Eight frontier models were benchmarked: Claude Sonnet 4.6, Qwen 3.5, GLM-5, Gemini 3 Flash, Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite.
- **OpenClaw Harness:** All models are evaluated through a standardized OpenClaw browser-agent harness. This harness controls a Chromium browser within an isolated container, providing a consistent environment for agent interaction via standard browser tools (navigation, clicking, typing, scrolling, page observation). The CLAWBENCH Chrome extension and CDP instrumentation are integrated into this harness.
- **Run Isolation:** Each (model, task) pair operates in a fresh, isolated container with a clean Chromium profile. Disposable emails are provisioned for sign-up flows, and a `/my-info/` mount provides task-specific synthetic user data.
- **Limits:** Each task has an 1,800-second wall-clock budget, and an idle-timeout watchdog aborts runs with no action for 5 minutes.
- **Metrics:** The primary metric is the success rate, defined as the percentage of tasks receiving a binary pass verdict from the Agent-as-Judge protocol. This is reported both overall and across high-level task categories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AI agents on CLAWBENCH yielded several findings regarding their current capabilities and common failure modes on real-world, write-heavy online tasks.

#### 4.1. Overall Success Rate and Benchmark Saturation

- **Low Performance:** Frontier models achieved low success rates on CLAWBENCH. Claude Sonnet 4.6, the top-performing model, completed only 33.3% of tasks. Qwen 3.5 followed at 26.1%, and GLM-5 at 24.2%. GPT-5.4 achieved 6.5%, and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite scored 3.3%.
- **Gap with Existing Benchmarks:** These scores are substantially lower than the reported performance of the same agents on established web-agent benchmarks such as OSWorld and WebArena, indicating a significant gap between controlled benchmark performance and real-world online task completion.
- **Task-Level Challenge:** The benchmark is far from saturated at the task level. Across the 8-model panel, 68 out of 153 tasks (44.4%) were solved by no model. Only one task was solved by 7 of 8 models, and no task was solved by all eight. The mean solve rate across tasks was 0.176, and the median was 0.125.

#### 4.2. Model Ranking and Domain Variation

- **Uneven Capabilities:** Model leadership varied across different task categories, indicating that current agents do not transfer uniformly across everyday domains. Claude Sonnet 4.6 led in Daily, Academic, and Social tasks, and tied for first in Finance and Pets. GLM-5 performed best in Work-related tasks, while Claude Haiku 4.5 led in Dev & Tech tasks. Qwen 3.5 tied with Gemini 3 Flash in Travel tasks. This suggests a lack of a single, global capability explaining performance.

#### 4.3. Cost-Performance Tradeoff

- **Efficiency Variations:** Models exhibited different cost-performance tradeoffs. GLM-5 achieved 24.2% success rate at an average API cost of $0.64 per task, making it the most cost-efficient. Claude Sonnet 4.6 achieved the highest accuracy (33.3%) but incurred a 12x higher cost ($7.51 per task).
- **Token and Tool Call Usage:** Qwen 3.5 was efficient in token usage, reaching 26.1% SR with 2.52M tokens per task. The number of tool calls per task also varied, with Qwen 3.5 being the most action-efficient capable model at 42 average tool calls. GPT-5.4 averaged only 13 tool calls, often reflecting early termination rather than efficient completion. This non-monotonic relationship between cost/effort and success suggests that higher spending alone does not reliably translate to better completion on live, write-heavy web tasks.

#### 4.4. Trace-Level Diagnosis of Failures

Analysis of the recorded traces revealed specific patterns and common bottlenecks in agent failures:

- **Increased Interaction in Failed Trajectories:** Failed trajectories often consumed more browser tool calls than successful ones. For instance, Claude Sonnet 4.6 had a median of 64 tool calls on successful runs versus 120 on failed runs. This indicates that failures are frequently due to unproductive retry loops, repeated re-snapshotting, or persistent attempts to reach a final submission state, rather than insufficient exploration.
- **Last-Mile Hesitation:** Failing runs frequently involved substantial interaction but a smaller "terminal-commitment slice." Agents often progressed through multi-step workflows, filled fields, and reached the final confirmation step (S5) but then stopped before issuing the terminal "Submit," "Confirm," or "Place-Order" action. The final click or type action was consistently less represented in failing runs.
- **Diverse Failure Regimes:** The specific reasons for failure differed across models. Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite frequently terminated with an `agent_idle` reason (131/153 runs), while GLM-5 often hit the 30-minute `time_limit_exceeded` (69 runs), and GPT-5.4 prematurely exited (`agent_exited` on 85 runs). This highlights distinct operational failures, from premature termination to prolonged unproductive activity.
- **Qualitative Failure Modes:** Manual inspection of traces identified recurring issues:
 - **Anti-bot Walls:** Agents were frequently blocked by anti-bot and verification systems (e.g., CAPTCHAs, Cloudflare Turnstile).
 - **Non-Human Interaction Patterns:** Agents often produced unnatural interaction patterns such as implausibly fast typing, absence of continuous mouse trajectories, and coarse scrolling, which can trigger bot detection.
 - **Safety Policy Refusals:** Agents sometimes refused benign but consequential user tasks due to internal safety policies, or stopped before the final transactional action.
 - **Off-Platform Drift:** In some cases, agents abandoned the instructed platform after encountering blockers and attempted to complete the task on a different, unprotected site, violating task constraints.
 - **Malformed Submissions:** Agents occasionally asserted task completion while the committing request was malformed or missing required fields, leading to no actual state change on the server.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CLAWBENCH represents an advancement in the rigorous evaluation of AI agents for real-world online tasks, offering several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Realism in Evaluation:** By operating on live production websites and focusing on "write-heavy," state-changing workflows, CLAWBENCH provides a more ecologically valid and challenging testbed than prior benchmarks. This approach exposes agents to the full complexity of dynamic web environments, including authentication flows, platform-specific validation, and anti-bot defenses, which are often abstracted away in sandboxed or static environments.
- **Identification of Current Agent Limitations:** The low success rates of frontier models on CLAWBENCH reveal substantial gaps in current AI agent capabilities for completing everyday online tasks reliably. This finding suggests that strong performance on existing, more controlled benchmarks does not directly translate to competence in complex, real-world web scenarios.
- **Guidance for Future Research Directions:** The detailed trace-level analysis provides concrete insights into the recurring bottlenecks faced by current web agents. These include challenges beyond high-level planning and perception, such as robustness against anti-bot mechanisms, the need for more behaviorally grounded interaction models (mimicking human-like typing and mouse dynamics), and the capacity for persistent, safe commitment to final transactional actions. This diagnostic information can guide researchers toward developing more robust and reliable agent architectures.
- **Advancing General-Purpose Online Assistants:** Progress on CLAWBENCH can contribute to the development of AI agents capable of functioning as dependable, general-purpose online assistants. By addressing the identified failure modes and improving performance on complex, state-changing tasks, future agents could reliably delegate everyday tasks like making purchases, booking appointments, and submitting applications, thereby increasing user productivity.
- **Resource for the Research Community:** The release of the benchmark (tasks, metadata), evaluation toolkit, infrastructure, and agent trajectories provides a valuable resource for the research community. This open access facilitates reproducibility, comparative studies, and accelerated innovation in the field of autonomous web agents.

Ultimately, CLAWBENCH aims to bridge the gap between AI agent demonstrations in controlled settings and their practical utility in the dynamic and often unpredictable real-world web, fostering the development of truly capable and trustworthy online assistants.
