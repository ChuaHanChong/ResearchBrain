# FuseGrasp: Radar-Camera Fusion for Robotic Grasping of Transparent Objects

> [!summary] Summary
> FuseGrasp, a system from The Chinese University of Hong Kong, integrates millimeter-wave radar and RGB-D camera data for robust robotic grasping of transparent objects. It achieved a 93.8% grasp success rate in real-world experiments, a 45.9% improvement over camera-only methods, and an overall material identification accuracy of 90.0% across varied environmental conditions.

## Problem

- Robotic systems relying on camera-guided computer vision struggle with transparent objects due to light refraction and reflection, leading to inaccurate depth estimation and poor localization.
- Vision-centric solutions degrade significantly in challenging environments such as low-light, cluttered backgrounds, or strong sunlight.
- Raw millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar data is sparse and noisy, and a lack of specific datasets for transparent objects makes multimodal data fusion difficult.

## Method

- Developed **FuseGrasp**, a radar-camera fusion system combining an mmWave radar and RGB-D camera with a ==Robotic Imaging== module (SAR imaging + signal calibration) to generate high-quality radar images delineating transparent object geometry.
- Designed a two-stage deep learning training strategy for depth reconstruction: pre-training on a large RGB-D dataset (TransCG) and fine-tuning a radar encoder with a smaller, self-built RGB-D-Radar dataset.
- Integrated a radar-based material identification module using YOLO v3 to classify objects (e.g., glass, plastic) and inform grip force modulation for the grasping module.

## Results

- Achieved a real-world grasp completion success rate of **93.8%**, representing a **45.9%** improvement over camera-only grasping algorithms.
- Demonstrated superior depth completion performance with an RMSE of 0.058m and MAE of 0.041m, substantially outperforming baselines like ClearGrasp (RMSE 0.220m) and TransCG (RMSE 0.109m).
- Attained **90.0%** overall accuracy for radar-based material identification, significantly surpassing camera-based methods (**56.5%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - mmWave radar provides robust perception of transparent objects, treating them as opaque due to distinct signal scattering, making it highly complementary to visual sensors, especially in poor lighting.
> - High-quality SAR imaging, enabled by precise robotic arm movement and signal calibration, is critical for transforming sparse radar data into useful geometric information.
> - A two-stage deep learning approach effectively overcomes multimodal data scarcity by leveraging existing large-scale vision datasets and then fine-tuning with limited, specialized radar-visual data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of transparent objects by robotic systems presents a persistent challenge within robotics and computer vision. Conventional robotic arms, which largely rely on camera-guided computer vision, encounter difficulties when interacting with transparent materials such as glass and plastic. These materials exhibit unique refractive and reflective properties that invalidate the geometric light path assumptions foundational to standard RGB-D cameras, leading to inaccurate depth estimation and hindering precise localization and grasping.

Prior research has primarily focused on vision-based techniques to address these issues. Methods often involve specialized neural networks to identify features, correct depth perception errors, and reconstruct object geometries from RGB-D imagery. Examples include ClearGrasp and TransCG, which leverage synthetic or real-world RGB-D datasets for transparent object depth completion. However, a significant limitation of these vision-centric solutions is their compromised performance in suboptimal environmental conditions, such as low-light environments, cluttered backgrounds, or strong sunlight, where optical sensors struggle.

To compensate for these limitations, some researchers have explored integrating supplementary sensors with traditional cameras. Visual-tactile fusion approaches have been proposed to enhance localization accuracy, sometimes employing synthetic datasets for training. While these methods offer some improvements, they often introduce their own challenges, such as being cumbersome to deploy across operational planes, difficulty in seamless data integration with visual modalities, and high associated costs.

Millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar technology has emerged as a promising alternative for object detection, capable of providing accurate distance measurements in challenging environments. Notably, mmWave signals possess shorter wavelengths that interact with transparent materials differently than visible light, causing these materials to appear opaque to the radar. This characteristic makes mmWave radar a potential candidate to complement RGB-D cameras for transparent object perception, as it is less affected by adverse lighting conditions.

However, developing a robust radar-camera fusion system for transparent object grasping introduces new challenges. These include the sparse and noisy nature of raw radar echoes, which are insufficient for detailed shape reconstruction; the scarcity of existing radar datasets specifically for transparent objects, making data collection labor-intensive; and the fundamental disparity in resolution and characteristics between visual and radar imagery, necessitating sophisticated fusion techniques.

FuseGrasp positions itself within this landscape by addressing these challenges directly. It proposes the first radar-camera fusion system specifically designed to enhance transparent object manipulation. By exploiting the unique properties of mmWave radar and integrating it with advanced SAR imaging and deep learning-based fusion, FuseGrasp aims to overcome the limitations of current vision-only and alternative sensor-fusion approaches, thereby advancing robotic capabilities in handling transparent items.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable robotic arms to reliably detect, localize, identify, and grasp transparent objects, which are ubiquitous in human environments but pose significant challenges for current robotic systems.

The key motivations driving the development of FuseGrasp are as follows:

1. **Addressing the Limitations of Camera-Centric Approaches**: Existing camera-guided robotic systems, particularly those relying on RGB-D cameras, exhibit inherent limitations when encountering transparent materials. The refractive and reflective properties of glass and plastic interfere with conventional light path assumptions, leading to inaccurate depth perception, missing data points, and unreliable object outlines. This issue is exacerbated in suboptimal lighting conditions (e.g., low light, direct sunlight, or cluttered backgrounds), where the performance of optical sensors degrades significantly, rendering robotic grasping impractical. The research is motivated by the need for a perception system that can function effectively across a spectrum of environmental lighting conditions.

2. **Exploiting the Unique Properties of Millimeter-Wave Radar**: Millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar offers a distinct advantage for perceiving transparent objects. Unlike visible light, mmWave signals have shorter wavelengths that tend to scatter off transparent materials, effectively making them appear opaque to the radar. This property allows mmWave radar to "see" the boundaries and surfaces of transparent objects irrespective of their optical transparency or ambient lighting. The motivation is to leverage this unique physical interaction to provide complementary, robust object information that visual sensors cannot reliably capture.

3. **Overcoming Challenges in Radar Data Acquisition and Processing**: While mmWave radar is promising, its raw data is often sparse and contaminated by multipath interference, making it unsuitable for direct shape reconstruction. Therefore, a primary motivation is to develop methods for generating high-quality radar images that can accurately delineate the geometry of transparent objects. This involves sophisticated signal processing, such as Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) imaging, which necessitates precise control over the radar's movement.

4. **Tackling Data Scarcity for Multimodal Learning**: The integration of radar and camera data requires specialized deep learning models. However, there is a notable absence of large, publicly available datasets containing synchronized RGB-D and mmWave radar images for transparent objects. Collecting such a dataset is labor-intensive. A key motivation is to devise an effective training strategy that can achieve robust model performance despite this data scarcity, potentially by leveraging existing large RGB-D datasets.

5. **Developing an Integrated Fusion Strategy**: The distinct characteristics, resolutions, and data formats of visual and radar imagery pose challenges for effective fusion. The research aims to develop a deep neural network architecture capable of seamlessly integrating features from both modalities, thereby enhancing overall perception accuracy for depth completion and object localization.

6. **Enhancing Robotic Grasping with Material Awareness**: Beyond merely locating and perceiving the shape of transparent objects, the ability to identify their material composition (e.g., glass vs. plastic) is crucial for safe and effective manipulation. Different materials require different grasping forces to prevent breakage or slippage. A motivated objective is to utilize the material-dependent scattering properties of mmWave radar to perform material identification, enabling the robotic arm to modulate its grip force appropriately.

In summary, FuseGrasp aims to build a robust, comprehensive robotic perception system for transparent objects by innovatively fusing mmWave radar and RGB-D camera data, addressing data acquisition, processing, fusion, and material awareness challenges to significantly improve grasping success rates in diverse real-world conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FuseGrasp is structured around four interconnected modules: Robotic Imaging, Depth Reconstruction, Material Identification, and Grasping. This modular design facilitates the sequential processing of sensory data from acquisition to robotic action.

**A. Robotic Imaging Module**
This module is responsible for capturing RGB-D images and generating high-quality Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) images of transparent objects.
1. **Data Streams Synchronization**: The mmWave radar signal and the robotic arm's positional data streams are synchronized using a unified clock reference (C++ Chrono library). This aligns data despite differing sampling rates (robot at 1000 FPS, radar at 80 FPS) by associating radar samples with the nearest robot arm timestamps.
2. **Radar Signals Calibration**: To mitigate phase errors, blurring, and ghost artifacts from hardware imperfections and background noise, a streamlined calibration method is employed.
 - A static point target with high reflectivity is used as a benchmark. Its 3D reflection point coordinates are estimated using a least-squares method based on multiple radar observations and measured distances.
 - Signal offsets, expressed as a residual complex gain factor, are modeled and compensated by estimating the phase error (φ_l) through a frequency estimation problem (Eq. 7).
 - A Savitzky–Golay (S-G) filter is applied to remove DC and high-frequency components.
 - Background cancellation is performed by initially scanning the empty workspace to generate a background radar image, which is then subtracted from subsequent object scans.
3. **SAR Imaging**: The precise movement of the robotic arm, equipped with the mmWave sensor, emulates a large antenna array. The Range Migration Algorithm (RMA) is adopted to synthesize 2D radar images from the received signals. This algorithm processes the signals in the wavenumber domain (Eq. 4, 5) to reconstruct the complex target reflectivity, the magnitude of which forms the radar image pixel values.

**B. Depth Reconstruction Module**
This module enhances radar data processing and fuses it with visual imagery using a deep neural network, specifically addressing the challenge of limited radar-transparent object datasets.
1. **Two-Stage Training Strategy**:
 - **Stage 1 (Pre-training)**: A Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) network is pre-trained on a large, publicly available RGB-D dataset (TransCG, >50,000 images of transparent objects). This stage primarily trains the camera encoder (E_c) and decoder (D_c), enabling the network to extract camera features and generate a rough depth map from RGB-D inputs by estimating the mean (μ) and variance (σ) of the depth distribution.
 - **Stage 2 (Fine-tuning)**: The pre-trained model parameters are largely frozen, and a smaller, self-built RGB-D-Radar dataset (600 data pairs) is used to fine-tune the radar encoder (E_r). Radar features from E_r are concatenated with the camera features in the latent space before being fed into the decoder. This step refines the variance (σ) estimation, leveraging radar data to improve the accuracy of the reconstructed depth map.
2. **Network Architecture**:
 - **Camera Encoder (E_c)**: Composed of stacked 2D convolution layers, batch normalization, and ReLU activations, with increasing channel counts in deeper layers to extract diverse features.
 - **Camera Decoder (D_c)**: Mirrors the encoder structure but with decreasing channels and increasing tensor sizes for reconstruction.
 - **Radar Encoder (E_r)**: Similar to E_c but with fewer layers to prevent overfitting to the small radar dataset. It downsamples radar images to match camera feature dimensions, and its output features are merged with the latent space.
3. **Loss Function**: A composite loss function (L) is defined as L = L_d + αL_KL + βL_sn.
 - **Depth Pixel Loss (L_d)**: The L2 norm of the difference between the estimated (D) and initial (D̂) depth images.
 - **Surface Normal Loss (L_sn)**: Measures the cosine similarity between the surface normal vectors derived from the estimated and ground truth depth maps.
 - **Kulback-Leibler (KL) Loss (L_KL)**: A regularization term that encourages the latent space distribution P(Λ) to approximate a Gaussian distribution.
 - Weight parameters α and β are empirically set to 0.1 and 0.01, respectively. Invalid depth values outside the range [0.3, 0.6] meters are excluded from loss calculations.

**C. Material Identification Module**
This module determines the material composition of detected transparent objects.
1. **Principle**: It leverages the distinct scattering coefficients of different materials to mmWave signals. For instance, glass typically produces a stronger radar return (brighter radar image, higher signal amplitude) than plastic.
2. **Methodology**: A YOLO v3 network is employed as a robust classifier. It maps 2D radar images to specific material categories (e.g., glass, plastic). The training dataset includes visually similar objects made from different materials to prevent the network from merely associating shapes with materials.

**D. Grasping Module**
This module translates the perception data into actionable robotic movements.
1. **Grasping Algorithm**: The GR-ConvNet algorithm, known for generating effective 2D grasp poses, is integrated.
2. **Process**: The enhanced depth image from the Depth Reconstruction Module, resized back to its original dimensions, is fed into GR-ConvNet. The network proposes potential 2D grasp candidates, including their positions and orientations. The robotic arm, equipped with a parallel-jaw gripper, then executes the grasping operation. Material identification results from the preceding module inform the modulation of grip force (e.g., stronger for glass, weaker for plastic).

**Implementations**:
- **Hardware**: A TI IWR6843ISK mmWave radar (61.8 GHz, 3.6 GHz bandwidth) and an Intel Realsense D435i RGB-D camera are mounted on a custom 3D-printed stand. A 7-DoF Franka robotic arm is used for manipulation.
- **Software**: MATLAB is used for signal processing, while Python and C++ are employed for robotic arm control. The Adam optimizer is used for network training, with a learning rate of 10^-3 and a multi-step scheduler. Training involves 100 epochs (batch size 32) for pre-training and 500 epochs (batch size 16) for fine-tuning on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX A6000 GPU.
- **Dataset**: A self-built RGB-D-Radar dataset comprising 600 synchronized data pairs of transparent objects across four distinct background settings and varying lighting conditions (normal and dim groups) was created. Ground-truth depth images were obtained by replicating transparent objects with opaque, black-painted counterparts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FuseGrasp focused on its performance in radar imaging quality, depth completion accuracy, material identification, and real-world robotic grasping success.

**A. Radar Imaging**
1. **Distance Estimation Accuracy**: Experiments with plastic and glass bowls demonstrated that signal calibration significantly improved distance estimation. The median errors for the plastic bowl decreased from 0.99 cm (without calibration) to 0.47 cm (with calibration). For the glass bowl, median errors reduced from 1.41 cm to 0.98 cm. This improvement was consistent with the understanding that the plastic bowl's larger radar cross-section (RCS) resulted in smaller localization errors.
2. **Imaging Quality**: Signal calibration enhanced the definition of object edges in SAR images. Quantitatively, image entropy, a measure of image quality where lower values indicate higher quality, decreased from 6.70 to 5.92 after calibration for a glass bowl, indicating a noteworthy improvement in overall imaging quality.

**B. Depth Completion**
FuseGrasp's depth completion performance was rigorously evaluated against baselines using metrics such as Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and threshold accuracy (δ for 1.05, 1.10, 1.25).
1. **Overall Performance**: FuseGrasp with radar signal calibration achieved superior performance across all metrics on the self-built dataset. It yielded an RMSE of 0.058m and MAE of 0.041m, with threshold accuracies of 45.78% (δ1.05), 77.91% (δ1.10), and 92.40% (δ1.25). These results represent a substantial improvement over ClearGrasp (RMSE 0.220m), TransCG (RMSE 0.109m), and a direct fusion baseline (RMSE 0.119m). Direct fusion without FuseGrasp's specialized approach showed marginal or degraded performance, highlighting the necessity of the proposed fusion strategy.
2. **Impact of Two-stage Training**: The fine-tuning approach of the two-stage training strategy significantly outperformed training the entire network solely on the small self-built dataset (RMSE 0.058m vs. 0.160m), validating its effectiveness for limited data scenarios.
3. **Impact of Radar Images**: A camera-only model (without radar images) showed degraded performance (RMSE 0.173m), underscoring the critical role of radar imagery, especially under challenging dim lighting conditions.
4. **Impact of RGB and Depth Images**: Models trained with only RGB-Radar (RMSE 0.127m) or Depth-Radar (RMSE 0.185m) data performed worse than the full FuseGrasp, confirming the complementary value of all three modalities. The Depth-Radar model outperformed RGB-Radar, suggesting the inherent value of depth sensing even with inaccuracies.
5. **Impact of Target Height**: Increasing target height from 5cm to 15cm led to a median depth distance error reduction of 1.11 cm on average, as taller objects reflect more mmWave signal. Cuboids generally performed better than cylinders due to larger cross-sections.
6. **Impact of Background Texture**: FuseGrasp demonstrated robustness against varying background textures (non-textured, simple-textured, complex-textured), with only modest average fluctuations of 0.87 cm in median depth error, as radar complements camera data.
7. **Impact of Lighting Condition**: The system maintained decent accuracy across strong sunlight, weak sunlight, indoor light, and no light conditions. For a cylinder, median depth errors were 2.09 cm, 0.62 cm, and 1.14 cm, respectively, highlighting radar's compensatory role in varied illumination.
8. **Impact of Capturing Elevations**: Elevating the robotic arm's capture height from 0.4m to 0.8m increased the median depth distance error by 3.12 cm on average. However, the relative depth error increase was modest (from 5.09% to 6.64%), indicating robustness due to RGB-D compensation.
9. **Impact of Material**: Glass cylinders exhibited slightly higher percentile errors but a lower median error (below 0.69 cm) compared to plastic cylinders (below 1.07 cm for 75% error). This was attributed to glass being a stronger reflector, yielding higher quality radar images.
10. **Impact of Robotic Arm Precision**: Decreased robotic arm precision (e.g., from 0.1mm to 1mm) resulted in blurred radar images and increased median depth estimation errors (e.g., 2.82 cm at 0.1mm vs. 7.85 cm at 1mm for a cylinder). The precision of industry-grade robots (e.g., Franka at 0.1mm) was deemed sufficient to minimize this impact in real-world applications.

**C. Material Identification**
The radar-based material identification solution within FuseGrasp achieved an overall accuracy of 90.0%, which significantly outperformed camera-based methods (56.5%). Specifically, it identified plastic material much more accurately (80.0%) than camera-based methods (27.3%), while both methods identified glass effectively. This highlights radar's advantage in distinguishing material properties beyond visual cues.

**D. Real-world Robot Experiments**
Real-world robotic grasping experiments were conducted across 16 scenarios under various lighting conditions (strong/weak sunlight, indoor light, no light) using 9 transparent objects (3 of which were unseen during training).
1. **Grasp Completion Success Rate**: FuseGrasp achieved a grasp completion success rate of 93.8% (45 out of 48 objects), representing a 45.9% improvement over camera-only grasping algorithms (47.9% success rate).
2. **Action Efficiency**: FuseGrasp improved action efficiency from 57.5% (camera-only) to 68.2%, requiring 66 attempts to grasp 45 objects compared to 40 attempts for 23 objects by camera-only.
3. **Robustness to Lighting**: FuseGrasp maintained high performance under varying lighting conditions, including strong sunlight where camera-only solutions severely faltered. In contrast, camera-only performance was closer to FuseGrasp in soft indoor lighting.
These trials confirmed FuseGrasp's ability to effectively manipulate transparent objects of diverse materials, including unseen ones, across a range of real-world environmental conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FuseGrasp system presents a significant advancement in robotic perception and manipulation, particularly for challenging transparent objects. Its potential impact spans several domains:

1. **Enhanced Robotic Autonomy in Unstructured Environments**: By providing a robust solution for detecting, localizing, and grasping transparent objects, FuseGrasp directly addresses a long-standing limitation that has hindered the deployment of robots in diverse real-world settings, including domestic, industrial, and service sectors. The ability to handle commonplace transparent items, such as glassware and plastic containers, improves the versatility and applicability of robotic arms.

2. **Improved Robustness to Environmental Variability**: Unlike traditional camera-only systems, which are highly susceptible to variations in lighting, background clutter, and surface reflections, FuseGrasp demonstrates consistent performance across a wide range of challenging conditions (e.g., strong sunlight, low light, varied textures). This enhanced robustness is critical for reliable operation outside of controlled laboratory environments.

3. **Novel Multimodal Sensor Fusion Paradigm**: FuseGrasp introduces a novel and effective radar-camera fusion framework tailored for transparent objects. This approach highlights the complementary strengths of mmWave radar (perceiving opaque-like transparent objects regardless of light) and RGB-D cameras (providing rich visual context). This methodology could inspire similar multimodal fusion strategies for other challenging perception tasks where individual sensor limitations impede performance.

4. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Deep Learning**: The proposed two-stage training strategy, leveraging a large pre-existing RGB-D dataset for initial feature extraction and then fine-tuning with a smaller, domain-specific RGB-D-Radar dataset, offers a practical blueprint for developing deep learning models in scenarios where specialized multimodal data is scarce and difficult to collect. This approach has implications for accelerating research and development in other data-limited robotic perception tasks.

5. **Introduction of Material-Aware Grasping**: The capability of FuseGrasp to identify material composition (e.g., glass vs. plastic) using mmWave radar is a valuable innovation. This allows for the dynamic adjustment of gripping forces, which can significantly reduce damage to fragile objects and improve the security of the grasp. This material-aware manipulation capability enhances the safety, efficiency, and intelligence of robotic systems.

6. **Foundation for Future Research and Development**: FuseGrasp opens several avenues for future research, including the development of more efficient radar scanning strategies, integration with 3D point cloud-based grasping policies (e.g., 6-DoF pose estimation), and the generation of synthetic radar datasets to further overcome data limitations and accelerate Sim2Real transfer. Its low power consumption and computational requirements also suggest its adaptability for deployment on mobile robotic platforms.

In summary, FuseGrasp represents a step toward more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems, providing a practical, robust, and material-aware solution for a previously intractable manipulation challenge. Its contributions in sensor fusion, training strategies, and real-world performance lay a foundation for broader applications of robotics in complex, human-centric environments.
