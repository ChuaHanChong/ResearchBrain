# Disentangling Visuo-Tactile Foresight: Oracle-Guided Interface Discovery for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The research introduces Oracle Visuo-Tactile Foresight (OVTF) to disentangle the study of how future visuo-tactile information is presented to an action policy. It demonstrates that an Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM) interface, which selectively routes phase-aligned visual context to tactile streams, achieves a 32.0% average success rate in contact-rich manipulation tasks, outperforming a modality-isolated baseline (23.7%).

## Problem

- The specific design of the "future-to-action interface"—how predicted visuo-tactile foresight guides action—remained underexplored and poorly understood.
- Directly studying this interface in existing World Action Models (WAMs) was challenging because its performance was entangled with the accuracy and reliability of the future prediction component itself.
- Confounding factors such as physically invalid future predictions, insufficient policy trust in future information, or mismatched multimodal forecasts obscured the true effectiveness of interface designs.

## Method

- Introduced the **Oracle Visuo-Tactile Foresight (OVTF)** framework, which replaces a learned future predictor with an "oracle" providing accurate, physically executable RGB and tactile streams from successful simulation trajectories.
- Proposed ==Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM)== as an interface design, employing learned memory slots with fixed phase anchors and an ==asymmetric routing pattern== where tactile slots jointly attend to their own modality and visual information.
- Developed ==Modality-Isolated Future Memory (IFM)== as a controlled baseline, using the same memory budget and phase alignment but with strictly modality-separated attention to evaluate the impact of cross-modal routing.

## Results

- AFM Oracle achieved a **32.0%** average success rate across seven contact-rich manipulation tasks, representing a substantial improvement over the UniVTAC-ACT* benchmark baseline (**14.9%**).
- Asymmetric routing (AFM Oracle at 32.0% success) demonstrated an **8.3** percentage point improvement in average task success rates compared to modality-isolated routing (IFM at **23.7%** success).
- Experiments with zeroed-out future slots (AFM Forced-Zero at **19.4%** success) confirmed that the policy genuinely utilizes test-time future content, as performance significantly dropped from the 32.0% of AFM Oracle.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The OVTF framework successfully disentangles the future prediction challenge from the future-to-action interface design, enabling isolated and rigorous study of how foresight is consumed by a robot's policy.
> - Asymmetric, phase-aligned routing of visual context to tactile streams at the interface (AFM) significantly improves the actionability of multimodal future information compared to fully modality-isolated processing (IFM).
> - The policy path within the AFM framework itself demonstrates robustness and improved performance over the baseline, even when test-time future information is absent, suggesting benefits from the architectural design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving physical contact, often faces challenges due to the limitations of visual perception alone. Successful execution of such "contact-rich" tasks frequently depends on subtle physical interaction cues that are not easily discernible from visual observations. This has led to the development of World Action Models (WAMs), which extend predictive control by jointly forecasting future visual and tactile signals. These models aim to guide action generation by providing the robot with a form of "foresight" regarding upcoming sensory states.

Existing research in WAMs and visuo-tactile foresight generally focuses on developing models that predict future observations and generate actions end-to-end. Approaches include generating executable video plans, predicting future observations and actions concurrently, or compressing predictive representations into context for the actor policy. However, a significant challenge in this integrated approach is understanding *how* this future information, once predicted, should be structured and presented to the action policy to be most effective. The "future-to-action interface" is recognized as a bottleneck, yet its independent study is difficult because its performance is entangled with the accuracy and reliability of the future prediction mechanism itself.

This paper addresses this specific challenge by proposing a framework that isolates the study of the future-to-action interface. Instead of developing a new end-to-end WAM, it removes the learned future prediction component and replaces it with an "oracle" provider of accurate future information. This allows the researchers to investigate the optimal architectural design for integrating future visuo-tactile signals into an action policy, assuming the future content itself is correct and executable. The work distinguishes itself from other WAM research by focusing on the "consumer-side" interface rather than the "provider-side" prediction accuracy, thereby contributing to the fundamental understanding of effective multimodal sensory integration for robotic control.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate how future visual and tactile sensory information should be structured and presented to a robot's action expert to maximize its utility for contact-rich manipulation tasks. The motivation stems from the observation that while recent tactile WAMs demonstrate the value of jointly modeling future visual and tactile signals, the specific design of the "future-to-action interface"—the mechanism by which this foresight guides action—remains underexplored and poorly understood.

Directly studying this interface within existing learned WAMs is challenging due to several confounding factors that can obscure the true effectiveness of the interface design:
1. **Physically invalid visual futures:** Learned predictors might generate visually plausible futures that are physically impossible for the robot to execute or violate contact mechanics.
2. **Insufficient trust in the future branch:** If predictions are frequently unstable or inaccurate during training, the action policy may learn to disregard or minimally rely on future information, even when some predictions might be useful.
3. **Incorrect or mismatched tactile futures:** Accurate tactile prediction is complex, requiring precise modeling of contact timing, deformation, and local interaction dynamics. Jointly matching tactile evolution with predicted video streams adds further difficulty, potentially leading to inconsistent forecasts.
4. **Poor future-to-action interface:** Even if a future prediction is accurate and physically valid, its encoding or representation might not be in a format that the action policy can efficiently or effectively interpret and utilize.

These factors are typically entangled in end-to-end WAM evaluations, making it difficult to ascertain whether a suboptimal performance is due to inaccurate future predictions or an inefficient interface for consuming that information.

To address this, the authors introduce the Oracle Visuo-Tactile Foresight (OVTF) framework. OVTF is motivated by the need to create a controlled environment where the quality of the future information provider is guaranteed. By supplying paired RGB and tactile futures from successful, simulation-verified trajectories, OVTF disentangles the interface design from prediction errors. This allows for a cleaner research question: "if the future is successful and physically executable, what representation allows the action expert to absorb its benefit?" This framework enables a focused study on interface design, aiming to identify optimal strategies for routing and integrating multimodal future sensory data into the action generation process. Within OVTF, the paper specifically aims to propose and evaluate an interface, Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM), and compare it against a modality-isolated baseline to determine the efficacy of selective cross-modal information routing.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The research introduces Oracle Visuo-Tactile Foresight (OVTF) as a framework to isolate and study the future-to-action interface. Within OVTF, it proposes Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM) and compares it against Modality-Isolated Future Memory (IFM).

**A. Oracle Future Source and Encoding**
OVTF’s core innovation is its "oracle" future provider. Instead of a learned predictor, the oracle provides RGB and tactile streams sampled from successful demonstration trajectories within the simulation environment. At each policy call, a reference trajectory index is determined, and up to 100 subsequent frames from this successful trajectory are sampled. These sampled frames represent a "privileged future" that is physically executable and successful, eliminating uncertainty regarding prediction accuracy. The future is characterized by a normalized remaining-horizon phase, $\phi_i \in$, which indicates the progress within the remaining segment of the reference trajectory. The oracle provides only reference-trajectory visual images and left/right tactile RGB, explicitly *not* providing future actions, robot joint positions, object poses, simulator state, or success labels.

Each future visual frame and tactile frame is processed by a separate trainable ResNet-18 encoder. These encoders are initialized with weights from the released UniVTAC-ACT* current vision/tactile encoders. After encoding, spatial pooling, and projection, three sequences are obtained: visual (H), left tactile (L), and right tactile (R), each of dimension $N \times 512$, where $N$ is the number of sampled frames. For tasks using both head and wrist cameras, the two visual streams are encoded separately and then fused by a two-layer MLP into a single 512-D H stream. A gated local temporal residual is applied to each sequence, allowing the model to selectively integrate local temporal context within each modality stream.

**B. Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM)**
AFM is designed to serve as the interface between the encoded future sequences and the action policy. It consists of 12 learned 512-D memory slots: four for future visual information, four for future left tactile, and four for future right tactile. These slots utilize fixed phase anchors $C = \{0.125, 0.375, 0.625, 0.875\}$, which correspond to specific points in the normalized future horizon.

For each memory slot, a joint multi-head attention mechanism is employed. The attention score calculation includes a Gaussian term that acts as a soft phase prior, biasing the attention towards future frames temporally aligned with the slot's phase anchor, but still allowing content-based attention over all valid source positions.

The key feature of AFM is its asymmetric routing pattern:
- Visual memory slots attend only to the visual (H) sequence ($H \leftarrow H$).
- Each tactile memory slot (left or right) jointly attends to its own tactile sequence *and* the visual (H) sequence ($L \leftarrow (L, H)$, $R \leftarrow (R, H)$).
- Crucially, cross-tactile access is prohibited ($L \leftrightarrow R$ blocked).
This routing implies that visual context can inform tactile interpretation, but tactile streams are processed independently of each other at this interface stage.

**C. Modality-Isolated Future Memory (IFM)**
IFM serves as a controlled baseline for comparison. It retains the same oracle future provider, phase anchors, memory budget, and optimization settings as AFM. However, its routing pattern is completely modality-isolated:
- Visual memory slots attend only to the visual (H) sequence ($H \leftarrow H$).
- Left tactile memory slots attend only to the left tactile (L) sequence ($L \leftarrow L$).
- Right tactile memory slots attend only to the right tactile (R) sequence ($R \leftarrow R$).
This comparison directly tests whether phase-aligned visual context, routed asymmetrically to tactile streams, enhances the actionability of future tactile information compared to full modality isolation at the interface.

**D. ACT Integration and Objective**
The current visual and tactile observations are processed by frozen UniVTAC-ACT* encoders, and the robot's joint positions (qpos) are processed by a frozen state projection. The outputs from these current observation encoders, along with the gated future memory slots (from AFM or IFM), are all passed into the same ACT (Action Chunk Transformer) encoder memory. This allows the action policy to access both current observations and future foresight through a unified memory structure.

The ACT decoder then uses 50 action queries to output a 50×8 normalized action chunk. During rollout, overlapping chunks are aggregated with exponential weighting. The model's training objective is solely masked action L1 loss, with slot decorrelation detached and non-backpropagating. The future encoders, temporal filters, slot reader, ACT transformer, and action head are trainable, while current observation encoders are frozen.

**E. Experimental Setup and Evaluation Conditions**
The methodology includes evaluation on seven contact-rich manipulation tasks from the UniVTAC simulation benchmark. Four AFM-family conditions are tested:
1. **AFM Oracle:** Trained and evaluated with oracle futures and asymmetric routing.
2. **IFM:** Same as AFM Oracle but with modality-isolated routing.
3. **AFM Forced-Zero:** Uses the Oracle-trained AFM checkpoint but all future-memory slots are explicitly zeroed out at evaluation time. This condition assesses if the model genuinely utilizes test-time future content.
4. **AFM Zero-Train:** Never sees future HDF data during training, always injecting zero-valued future slots, and evaluates with zero future slots. This assesses the performance of the AFM-compatible policy path itself when no future information is ever provided.

All conditions are subjected to identical evaluation protocols, including task splits, reset mappings, pre-processing, normalization, temporal aggregation, and success predicates, ensuring a fair comparison by varying only the future information interface or its availability.

### 4. Main findings and results

The experiments were conducted across seven manipulation tasks from the UniVTAC simulation benchmark to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Oracle Visuo-Tactile Foresight (OVTF) framework and the Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM) interface.

**Overall Performance:**
- **AFM Oracle** achieved an average success rate of **32.0%** (112 successes out of 350 rollouts) across the seven tasks.
- This represents a substantial improvement compared to the **UniVTAC-ACT* benchmark baseline**, which achieved **14.9%** average success (52/350 successes).
- These results indicate that providing accurate, physically executable visuo-tactile future signals can significantly enhance performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks.

**AFM Family Ablation (Interface Comparison):**
The comparative analysis within the AFM family highlights the specific contributions of the interface design:
- **AFM Oracle vs. IFM:** AFM Oracle, with its asymmetric phase-aligned visual-to-tactile routing, achieved **32.0%** average success. In contrast, Modality-Isolated Future Memory (IFM), which processes each future modality independently, achieved **23.7%** average success (83/350 successes). The **8.3 percentage point difference** (32.0% for AFM Oracle vs. 23.7% for IFM) demonstrates that selective, phase-aligned visual-to-tactile routing at the future-to-action interface provides a more actionable and beneficial pathway for future information compared to complete modality isolation. This finding is notable because, even though all modality tokens can subsequently interact within the downstream ACT encoder, the explicit fusion performed by AFM at the interface itself provides a crucial organizational benefit that downstream self-attention does not fully recover from an isolated representation.

- **AFM Oracle vs. AFM Forced-Zero:** When an AFM Oracle-trained checkpoint was evaluated with future memory slots intentionally zeroed out (AFM Forced-Zero), the average success rate dropped to **19.4%** (68/350 successes). This decrease from 32.0% directly indicates that the AFM-compatible policy genuinely learns to utilize and relies on the test-time future content provided by the oracle, rather than merely benefiting from the increased model capacity of the future modules.

- **AFM Zero-Train vs. UniVTAC-ACT*:** The AFM Zero-Train condition, where no future HDF data was ever provided during training or evaluation, still achieved an average success rate of **20.0%** (70/350 successes). This performance is higher than the **14.9%** of the UniVTAC-ACT* baseline. This result suggests that the policy path within the AFM framework itself, even without future information, is robust and effective, surpassing the baseline.

**Task-Level Insights:**
- **Significant Gains:** Tasks like "Put Shelf" and "Pull Out Key" showed particularly clear behavioral value from future content. For "Put Shelf," AFM Oracle achieved 54% success (27/50), compared to 42% for UniVTAC-ACT* and IFM. For "Pull Out Key," AFM Oracle reached 54% (27/50), a substantial increase from UniVTAC-ACT*'s 22%. "Lift Can" also saw a notable improvement from 0% (UniVTAC-ACT*) to 30% (AFM Oracle).
- **Policy Path Robustness:** In "Insert Tube," AFM Oracle and AFM Forced-Zero both achieved 42% success (21/50), indicating that for this specific task, a strong policy gain persisted even when test-time future information was absent in the Forced-Zero condition. This supports the general effectiveness of the AFM-compatible policy path.
- **Task Variability:** "Insert Hole" was the only task where IFM (8%) slightly outperformed AFM Oracle (4%), though all conditions remained at a low success rate for this challenging task. This highlights task-specific variations in the utility of different interface designs.

In summary, the results establish that OVTF successfully makes the quality of the future-to-action interface measurable. AFM's asymmetric phase-aligned visual-to-tactile routing demonstrably provides a more effective mechanism for integrating multimodal future information than modality-isolated approaches, leading to higher task success rates in contact-rich manipulation.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes several significant contributions with potential impacts on the development of robot manipulation systems, particularly those operating in contact-rich environments.

The primary significance lies in the introduction of **Oracle Visuo-Tactile Foresight (OVTF)**. By disentangling the "future provider" from the "future-to-action interface," OVTF addresses a fundamental limitation in studying predictive control systems. Previously, errors or inefficiencies in predicting future sensory states were confounded with how those predictions were utilized by the action policy. OVTF provides a controlled framework that allows researchers to isolate and rigorously evaluate interface designs, asking a cleaner question about how future information should be presented if it is known to be accurate and executable. This disentanglement is a methodological advancement that can accelerate research in robot foresight and multimodal integration.

The work's key empirical finding regarding **Asymmetric Phase-Local Future Memory (AFM)** is also highly significant. The demonstration that phase-aligned visual context, when selectively routed to tactile memory streams, leads to a substantial improvement in task success compared to modality-isolated memory (AFM Oracle 32.0% vs. IFM 23.7%) provides concrete architectural guidance for future WAMs. This suggests that simply concatenating multimodal information or relying on a downstream transformer to learn cross-modal relationships might be less effective than explicitly designing fusion mechanisms at the interface itself. The finding underscores the importance of intelligent, structured integration of sensory data, where visual information can provide crucial context for interpreting subtle tactile cues.

The results indicating that the AFM-compatible policy path can perform strongly even without future inputs (AFM Zero-Train 20.0% vs. UniVTAC-ACT* 14.9%) is also noteworthy. This suggests that the proposed architecture for processing current and potential future information is robust and offers benefits even when foresight is unavailable. It implies that improvements to the core policy structure, compatible with foresight integration, can yield general performance gains.

The potential impact of this research is multifaceted:
- **Improved Robot Autonomy:** By identifying more actionable future-to-action interfaces, this work can lead to the development of more robust and reliable robot control policies for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks. This is crucial for applications in manufacturing, logistics, domestic assistance, and hazardous environments where precise physical interaction is required.
- **Guidance for WAM Design:** The findings offer specific architectural insights for designing future World Action Models. Instead of solely focusing on predictive accuracy, developers can now prioritize the design of multimodal fusion interfaces, potentially leading to more efficient learning and more effective deployment of predictive control.
- **Advancing Multimodal Integration:** The research contributes to a deeper understanding of how visual and tactile sensory modalities should be integrated for effective decision-making in robotics. It highlights that intelligent fusion, rather than simple concatenation, is critical for maximizing the utility of diverse sensory streams.
- **New Benchmarking and Research Directions:** OVTF provides a novel framework for benchmarking future interface designs. This could spur further research into alternative interface architectures, phase alignment strategies, and cross-modal routing patterns, pushing the boundaries of how robots learn from and act upon predictive information.

In conclusion, this paper provides both a valuable methodological tool (OVTF) and a critical empirical finding (AFM) that collectively advance the state of the art in robot manipulation. Its focus on the often-overlooked "future-to-action interface" holds the promise of developing more intelligent, adaptable, and capable robotic systems that can better leverage foresight for successful task execution.
