# One Hand to Rule Them All: Canonical Representations for Unified Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A parameterized canonical representation unifies diverse dexterous robotic hand architectures into a shared parameter space and canonical URDF format, enabling a single policy to generalize across different hand morphologies and kinematics. This framework allows a unified grasp-generation model to achieve zero-shot generalization to unseen hand variants, demonstrating high success rates in both simulation and real-world robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Dexterous manipulation policies are typically embodiment-specific, lacking generalization across varied robotic hand designs (morphology, DoF, kinematics).
- This embodiment-specific nature leads to fragmented progress, increased data collection costs, and prevents effective data reuse across platforms.
- Existing hand representation formats like URDFs are heterogeneous and unsuitable for direct neural network input in learning-based pipelines.

## Method

- Developed a ==parameterized canonical representation== that unifies diverse dexterous hand architectures into a ==shared parameter space== and a ==canonical URDF== format.
- Designed a canonical URDF supporting up to five fingers and 22 degrees of freedom, employing ==capsule primitives== for links and standardizing joint axes and coordinate conventions.
- Created an automated framework to convert existing hand URDFs into this canonical parameterization and vice-versa, establishing a ==unified 22-DoF action space==.

## Results

- A VAE learned a structured **16-dimensional** latent space, allowing smooth morphological transitions between distinct hand designs during interpolation.
- Policies trained on canonical URDFs for in-hand reorientation achieved performance comparable to original URDFs, demonstrating fidelity in dynamic tasks (Table II).
- A single unified grasp model, trained on canonical representations, demonstrated robust zero-shot generalization to unseen LEAP Hand variants with up to **71%** real-world success on novel morphologies, outperforming hand-specific baseline methods and achieving high inference efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A structured and continuous latent embedding of hand morphology can be learned from the canonical parameter space, allowing for physically meaningful interpolations between diverse hand designs.
> - The canonical URDF preserves essential kinematic and control properties, ensuring that policies trained with it perform comparably to those trained with original hand models.
> - Unified policy training across multiple hand embodiments using the canonical representation enables effective knowledge sharing and improved generalization over embodiment-specific models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation in robotics has advanced through both learning-based and model-based methods, achieving notable progress in tasks such as object grasping, in-hand manipulation, and tool use. However, a significant limitation within this field is the embodiment-specific nature of most developed policies. Robotic hand designs vary substantially in morphology, degrees of freedom (DoF), and kinematic layouts, meaning policies trained for one specific hand often lack generalization capabilities to other designs. This specialization leads to fragmented progress, increased costs for data collection, and prevents the reuse of valuable datasets across different robotic platforms.

Prior research has begun to address cross-embodiment policy learning, particularly in grasping. Approaches have included using intermediate grasp representations that abstract away hand-specific kinematics, such as interaction-centric fields or contact patterns. While these methods facilitate grasp transfer, they are often task-specific and do not readily extend to sequential manipulation skills. Other efforts, like DexUMI, leverage human hands as a universal manipulation prior, but typically rely on human-like kinematics and specialized teleoperation setups, which can limit scalability. Particle-based dynamics learning methods have also been explored to model hands and objects as particle systems, enabling transfer for deformable manipulation, yet they encounter difficulties with structurally distinct hands and a broader range of manipulation skills.

Despite these advancements, a fundamental challenge remains: establishing a unified representation and action space that allows learning policies to generalize effectively across a diverse range of dexterous hand embodiments and manipulation tasks. Existing Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) files, while comprehensive, are heterogeneous, tree-structured, and hand-crafted, rendering them unsuitable for direct use as input to neural networks in learning-based pipelines. This paper addresses this gap by proposing a parameterized canonical representation designed to unify diverse hand architectures into a consistent, learning-friendly format.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the observation that current dexterous manipulation policies are typically designed and optimized for specific robotic hand models. This embodiment-specific approach severely restricts the generalization and reusability of learned skills when faced with different hand morphologies, DoFs, or kinematic structures. The heterogeneity of existing hand designs necessitates retraining or significant adaptation for each new hand, leading to substantial computational costs, extensive data collection requirements, and a bottleneck in the development of truly versatile robotic manipulators.

The authors identify two fundamental challenges that need to be addressed to overcome this limitation:
1. **Compact and Expressive Hand Representation**: There is a need for a representation of hand morphology that is both concise enough to serve as an efficient input for learning-based models and expressive enough to capture the essential kinematic and geometric variations across a broad spectrum of dexterous hands.
2. **Unified Action Space**: A consistent action space is required that enables learned policies to function seamlessly across hands with different numbers of degrees of freedom and varying kinematic structures, standardizing how a policy interacts with different embodiments.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of this research are:
- **Develop a Canonical Hand Representation**: Introduce a parameterized canonical representation that unifies diverse dexterous hand architectures into a shared parameter space and a canonical URDF format. This representation aims to encode hand structure in a learning-friendly manner while standardizing the action space.
- **Enable Cross-Embodiment Policy Learning**: Demonstrate that this canonical representation facilitates joint policy training across diverse hand embodiments, bridging their structural and dimensional discrepancies.
- **Validate Representational Quality**: Show that the proposed parameter space captures essential morphological and kinematic variations, allowing a structured latent manifold to be learned where interpolations between embodiments yield smooth and physically meaningful morphology transitions.
- **Ensure Functional Fidelity**: Confirm that the canonical URDF preserves the dynamic and functional properties of the original hand URDFs, ensuring efficient and reliable cross-embodiment policy learning without significant performance degradation.
- **Demonstrate Zero-Shot Generalization**: Establish that policies conditioned on this canonical representation can generalize effectively, even in a zero-shot manner, to unseen hand morphologies in both simulated and real-world environments.

Ultimately, the work aims to provide a scalable foundation for cross-hand learning, paving the way for unified, large-scale, and morphology-aware learning toward universal dexterous manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on developing and validating a parameterized canonical representation for dexterous robotic hands. This involves designing a unified kinematic structure, defining a compact parameter set, creating an automated conversion pipeline, and applying this representation to various manipulation tasks.

**Canonical Hand Representation Design:**
1. **Canonical URDF Design (Section III-B)**: The authors analyzed a representative set of dexterous hands (Table I) to identify common human-inspired kinematic patterns and recurring motion schemes. This analysis guided the design of a canonical URDF structure that supports up to five fingers and 22 degrees of freedom (DoF). To abstract geometric complexity, all links are represented as capsule primitives, which approximate the cylindrical shapes common in robotic hand collision meshes. A unified coordinate convention is enforced, aligning the palm normal with the +x axis, the thumb (for a right hand) with +y, and other fingers with +z. Local joint axes are standardized: +x for abduction/adduction, +y for flexion/extension, and +z for axial rotation, ensuring consistent kinematics.
2. **Parameter Definition (Section III-C, Appendix A)**: A compact set of 82 parameters is defined to capture the key morphological and kinematic properties of dexterous hands (Table X). These include:
 - **Morphological Parameters**: Palm radius, a shared finger radius for all fingers, and a six-dimensional vector for finger link lengths (three for the thumb, three for non-thumb fingers). Assumptions include a consistent finger diameter and proportional palm thickness.
 - **Kinematic Parameters**: Translational offsets for finger bases (`finger_xyz`), a rotational parameter for the thumb base (`thumb_rpy`), axes for the proximal two thumb joints (`thumb_axes`), and `joint_lowers`/`joint_uppers` for all 22 canonical DoFs.
 - An **Extended Canonical Parameterization** (173 parameters, Table XI) is also introduced in Appendix A.2 to allow for higher fidelity and to model hands with structural deviations not fully captured by the base parameterization, such as per-finger radii and more specific joint origins and axes. This extended set was used for the LEAP Hand zero-shot experiments.
3. **URDF Parsing and Generation (Section III-D, Appendix B)**: An automated framework converts existing hand URDFs into the canonical parameterization and vice-versa. Parsing extracts canonical parameters from arbitrary URDFs by identifying the palm, estimating finger geometry, computing finger base positions, deriving thumb orientation and joint axes, and reading joint limits. This process requires minimal manual input (joint mapping, palm root transform). Canonical URDFs are generated from the parameter set using Jinja2 templating, allowing dynamic inclusion or omission of elements to match varying numbers of fingers or links while adhering to canonical conventions.
4. **Action Space Unification (Section III-E)**: The canonical URDF imposes a fixed 22-DoF structure, providing a unified control and observation space. Hands with fewer active DoFs have corresponding joints set as inactive or links removed. Bidirectional conversion between original and canonical joint vectors is achieved via the parsing process, standardizing joint dimensionality and ordering.

**Canonical Representation Applications:**
The methodology proceeds with four experimental validations:

1. **Learning a Morphology Latent Space (Section IV-A, Appendix C)**: A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is trained on 65,536 synthetic hand configurations generated by sampling canonical parameters. The VAE learns a 16-dimensional latent space, aiming to create a compact, structured embedding of hand morphology. The VAE architecture uses MLP-based encoder/decoder and type-specific reconstruction losses (L2 for continuous, cross-entropy for joint axes, binary cross-entropy for joint presence) with KL regularization.
2. **In-Hand Reorientation (Section IV-B, Appendix D)**: Reinforcement learning agents (PPO with MLP+GRU) are trained in the IsaacGym simulator to rotate an object about a predefined axis using both original hand models (Shadow Hand, LEAP Hand) and their canonical counterparts. This evaluates whether the canonical URDF preserves essential kinematic and control properties. Observations include joint states, object pose/velocities, and PD controller targets, while the reward system encourages angular velocity and penalizes instability or excessive motion.
3. **Cross-Embodiment Dexterous Grasping (Section IV-C)**: A single, unified grasp pose prediction model is trained entirely within the canonical representation. This model employs a two-stage diffusion-based approach:
 - **Stage 1**: A diffusion model predicts the distribution of wrist translations around an object, conditioned on a grasp feature and a specified wrist rotation.
 - **Stage 2**: A lightweight MLP predicts the corresponding hand joint configuration, conditioned on the sampled wrist pose and grasp feature.
 The grasp feature combines object features (extracted by a D(R,O) point-based encoder) with hand features (obtained from the frozen VAE latent embedding). The model is trained on a dataset of 24,764 grasps from Allegro, Barrett, and Shadow Hands, converted to the canonical URDF, using a Smooth-L1 loss.
4. **LEAP Hand Zero-Shot Generalization (Section IV-D, Appendix F)**: To test zero-shot generalization, 256 LEAP Hand variants are constructed by systematically varying finger link presence. Grasp data for 66 viable variants (sum of finger links ≥ 8) are generated using Lightning Grasp and filtered. The grasping policy is trained using the canonical morphology parameters as a direct hand condition. Zero-shot evaluation is performed on variants with fewer links (sum of finger links < 8) and other variants explicitly excluded from the training data. The extended canonical URDF is used here to better capture the LEAP Hand's specific kinematic structure, minimizing sim-to-real discrepancies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the canonical hand representation across various applications:

**A. VAE Latent Space Visualization**
The VAE trained on the canonical parameter space produced a structured and continuous latent embedding of hand morphology. Visualizations of latent-space interpolations between distinct hand designs (e.g., a three-finger gripper and an anthropomorphic hand) showed smooth and physically meaningful transitions in geometric properties such as palm size, finger count and spacing, thumb placement, and overall DoF configuration (Fig. 5). This finding supports that the latent space effectively captures intrinsic structural relationships, which is crucial for downstream morphology-conditioned learning tasks.

**B. Canonical Hand Fidelity**
1. **In-Hand Reorientation**: In the high-dynamic in-hand object reorientation task, policies trained with the canonical URDFs for Shadow Hand and LEAP Hand achieved performance comparable to those trained with their respective original URDFs. Specifically, the canonical representations showed similar "Steps-to-Fall" and "Cumulative Rotation" metrics (Table II) and exhibited analogous learning progress and convergence patterns during training (Fig. 7). These results indicate that the canonical parameterization preserves the essential manipulation dynamics and functional characteristics necessary for complex control tasks.
2. **Grasp Policy Transfer via Action Mapping**: Experiments involving replay of grasp policies between canonical and original URDF spaces demonstrated robust action semantics. Policies trained in the canonical space, when mapped to original URDFs, achieved success rates closely matching those of policies trained directly on original URDFs, and vice versa (Table III). A minor performance discrepancy was noted with the Allegro Hand, attributed to the canonical URDF's omission of its specific axial-rotation joint. Overall, this confirmed the reliability of the bidirectional mapping and the coherence of the canonical action space for policy execution across diverse hand designs.

**C. Cross-Embodiment Dexterous Grasping Performance**
A single unified grasp-generation model trained on the canonical URDF representation across Allegro, Barrett, and Shadow Hands demonstrated strong performance:
1. **Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods**: The proposed lightweight model achieved grasp success rates comparable to or exceeding several state-of-the-art methods such as DFC, GenDexGrasp, and D(R,O) Grasp (Table IV). Notably, it accomplished this with significantly higher inference efficiency, requiring only 0.13 seconds per grasp generation. This indicates that the canonical parameterization is expressive and coherent enough to produce high-quality grasps across diverse hand designs without requiring complex optimization or hand-specific architectures.
2. **Unified Training vs. Embodiment-Specific Training**: The unified model, trained jointly across multiple hand embodiments, consistently outperformed models trained independently for each specific hand (Table V). This result suggests that the shared action space provided by the canonical URDF enables effective policy sharing, allowing hands with distinct kinematics to benefit from a larger, aggregated dataset, thereby enhancing generalization.

**D. Zero-Shot Grasping Performance**
1. **Simulation on LEAP Hand Variants**: The policy, conditioned on hand morphology parameters, demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization capabilities to unseen LEAP Hand variants. Models trained with data excluding specific variants (e.g., leap\_3033, leap\_3303, leap\_3330) still achieved comparable success rates on these unseen hands relative to models trained with their data included (Table VII). When evaluated on more challenging variants with significantly fewer links (x+y+z+w < 8), zero-shot models often surpassed variant-specific models, particularly where specific data was sparse (Table VIII). Performance was slightly lower for highly simplified, two-finger "gripper-like" variants (e.g., leap\_0303, leap\_3030), possibly due to their distinct grasp patterns and larger volume of specific training data. The importance of correct hand conditioning was further highlighted, as applying incorrect hand parameters substantially reduced grasp success rates (Table IX). Gradient visualization also indicated that the model learned to dynamically focus on functional fingers based on the given morphology (Fig. 9).

**E. Real-World Experiment**
The LEAP Hand grasping policies, trained in simulation using the canonical representation, were successfully deployed on a Franka Research 3 robotic arm with an Intel RealSense L515 depth camera.
Both trained and zero-shot models achieved high grasp success rates on a set of 10 diverse real-world objects (Fig. 8a, Table VI). Trained models for specific LEAP Hand variants demonstrated high reliability (e.g., 83% for leap\_3333). Crucially, the zero-shot models, which had never encountered the target hand variants during training, exhibited success rates close to those of the trained models (e.g., 71% for zero-shot leap\_3033 compared to 75% for trained leap\_3033). These results confirm strong sim-to-real transfer and robust generalization to previously unseen morphologies in physical hardware. Multi-panel illustrations (Fig. 8b) provided visual evidence of stable and robust grasps across diverse and unusual LEAP Hand variants.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of a canonical representation for dexterous hands marks a significant step toward addressing the long-standing challenge of embodiment-specific policy learning in robotics. By unifying heterogeneous hand designs into a shared parameter and action space, this work provides a foundational framework that enables scalable cross-embodiment learning.

The key contributions and their potential impact include:
- **Scalable Cross-Embodiment Learning**: The canonical representation allows for consistent and learning-friendly structural encoding across diverse hand morphologies and kinematic structures. This standardization facilitates joint policy training, meaning data and learned policies can be shared and leveraged across different robotic platforms. This could drastically reduce the need for hand-specific data collection and retraining, accelerating research and development in dexterous manipulation.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization**: The ability to generalize policies to unseen hand morphologies in a zero-shot manner, as demonstrated in both simulation and real-world experiments, signifies a move towards truly versatile robotic systems. This capability is critical for deploying robots in novel environments or with new hardware without extensive recalibration or retraining.
- **Interpretable and Interpolable Morphology Space**: The learned structured latent manifold for hand geometry and kinematics allows for smooth and physically meaningful interpolations between hand designs. This not only aids in understanding the space of possible hand morphologies but also opens avenues for designing new robotic hands that are optimized for specific tasks or capable of continuous morphological adaptation.
- **Foundation for Large-Scale, Morphology-Aware Learning**: This work establishes an interpretable, interpolable, and scalable representation that is suitable for modern machine learning architectures. It paves the way for developing large-scale "foundation models" for robotic manipulation that are inherently aware of and adaptable to variations in robot morphology, moving beyond fixed-body assumptions.
- **Broader Applicability**: While demonstrated on dexterous hands, the principles of creating a canonical representation for diverse embodiments could extend to other complex robotic systems, such as humanoid robots or mobile manipulators. This approach has the potential to benefit the broader embodied AI and robotics communities by fostering more generalizable and transferable robotic skills across various manipulation tasks.

In conclusion, this research provides a practical and effective solution to a fundamental limitation in dexterous manipulation, laying a groundwork for more adaptable, general-purpose robotic systems capable of operating across a wide array of physical embodiments.
