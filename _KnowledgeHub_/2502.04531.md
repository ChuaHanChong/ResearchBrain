# AnyPlace: Learning Generalized Object Placement for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> AnyPlace introduces a two-stage method for robot object placement, combining a Vision-Language Model for coarse location proposals with a diffusion model for precise local pose prediction. The system achieves successful zero-shot transfer to real robots, demonstrating high success rates and coverage across diverse and multimodal placement tasks after being trained entirely on synthetic data.

## Problem

- Generalizing robot object placement to novel objects and diverse configurations remains challenging.
- Existing methods often struggle to predict multimodal placement solutions (i.e., multiple valid poses for a single task).
- High reliance on extensive real-world demonstrations or re-training limits scalability and deployment of robotic manipulation systems.

## Method

- A two-stage framework that first uses a ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)== for high-level coarse location proposals based on natural language, then a ==transformer-based diffusion model== for low-level fine-grained precise pose prediction within these local regions.
- A fully synthetic dataset generation pipeline in Blender and NVIDIA IsaacSim, procedurally creating diverse objects and stable placement poses (stacking, inserting, hanging) to train the model.
- The low-level diffusion model iteratively refines object poses, trained with a composite loss function including ==L1 distance== for translation, ==geodesic distance== for rotation, and ==Chamfer loss== for point cloud alignment.

## Results

- **AnyPlace** significantly outperformed baselines in real-world zero-shot evaluations, achieving **80%** success for vial insertion and **67-80%** for other precise multi-modal placement tasks with novel objects.
- In simulation, **AnyPlace** demonstrated superior precision and coverage for multi-modal tasks, achieving up to **92.74%** success for single-task vial insertion and **75.25%** for multi-task, significantly surpassing baseline performances.
- The system successfully generalized to diverse, unseen objects and complex configurations, including rigid and deformable items, fine-grained tasks, and language-conditioned scenarios, solely from synthetic training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing the placement problem into coarse location identification and fine-grained pose prediction, guided by a VLM, enhances generalization and precision by focusing the model on relevant local geometries.
> - Programmatic synthetic data generation can effectively train generalizable manipulation models that exhibit robust zero-shot transfer capabilities to real-world environments.
> - A diffusion-based approach, when conditioned on precise local point clouds, can effectively predict multimodal and highly accurate relative transformations for diverse placement tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot manipulation, particularly object placement, presents challenges due to the variety of object geometries and placement configurations, coupled with the difficulty of generalizing to novel objects and predicting multimodal placement solutions. Traditional approaches often fall into two categories:
- **Task-specific methods:** These typically rely on large numbers of demonstrations for a single placement task, limiting their ability to generalize to new objects or scenarios. Examples include methods focused on specific tasks like hanging objects on racks.
- **Few-shot approaches:** These aim to learn object placement with limited demonstrations, enabling the robot to replicate an operation across similar objects or setups. However, they frequently struggle with broad generalization and scalability across diverse tasks.

A related area is object rearrangement, where models predict the relative transformation between an object's initial and final poses. Works like Neural Descriptor Fields (NDF) and TaxPose learn representations or correspondences to estimate transformations, often operating in a few-shot setting and predicting a single placement pose. However, many real-world tasks involve multiple feasible placement poses (multimodal outputs), which these methods may not fully capture. RPDiff, a diffusion-based approach, attempts to address multimodality but has shown incomplete coverage and struggles with varying object sizes due to fixed-size cropping.

Another approach is direct end-effector pose prediction, where models predict the robot's gripper pose. While methods like M2T2, Pick2Place, and RVT utilize various techniques, including transformers and scene-based affordance maps, they often focus on planar placement or lack the fine-grained spatial reasoning required for precise manipulation tasks like peg-in-hole insertion. Contemporary Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) have also been noted for lacking robust spatial reasoning, which limits their application in precise manipulation.

The AnyPlace work aims to bridge these gaps by reframing the placement problem as pairwise shape mating, unifying multiple tasks. It seeks to develop a generalizable object placement method that is robust to different objects, capable of predicting diverse and precise placement poses across various tasks (e.g., placing in open areas, inserting, hanging), and can be trained entirely on synthetic data for zero-shot real-world transfer. This positions AnyPlace as an effort to enhance generalization, precision, and multimodal output capabilities in robot manipulation by combining VLM reasoning with local pose prediction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generalizable and robust robotic object placement system, named AnyPlace, that can predict a wide range of feasible placement poses for diverse real-world tasks. The system is designed to be trained entirely on synthetic data while demonstrating zero-shot transfer capabilities to physical robots.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in robotic object placement:
- **Diversity of object geometries and placement configurations:** Robots must be able to handle a vast array of objects and understand how they can be placed in different contexts (e.g., stacking, inserting, hanging). Existing methods often specialize in one type of task or object.
- **Generalization to unseen objects and configurations:** Many current robotic manipulation systems require extensive real-world demonstrations or re-training for new objects or environments, which is resource-intensive and limits scalability. The goal is to reduce this dependency.
- **Prediction of multimodal placement outputs:** For many tasks, there isn't a single "correct" placement pose but rather a continuous distribution or several distinct valid configurations. Capturing this multimodality is crucial for flexibility and robustness in robot operations.
- **Precision requirements:** Some placement tasks, such as inserting a peg into a hole, demand high precision, which is challenging to achieve given sensor noise and geometric variations.
- **Efficiency of training:** Relying on large real-world datasets for training is often impractical. Developing a method that can be effectively trained on synthetic data is desirable for rapid development and deployment.

The key insight proposed by the authors is to decompose the placement problem into two stages:
1. **High-level coarse location proposal:** Leveraging a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to identify approximate placement locations based on natural language descriptions. This addresses the multimodal nature of placement by suggesting multiple potential regions.
2. **Low-level precise pose prediction:** Focusing a dedicated model on these relevant local regions to predict fine-grained placement poses. This simplification, by providing a constrained region of interest, is hypothesized to enable the model to better generalize across objects, learn their geometry, and capture diverse placement configurations with higher precision.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to significantly advance the capabilities of robots in performing complex and varied object placement tasks with greater autonomy and adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The AnyPlace system is a two-stage method for generalizable object placement, designed to be trained on synthetic data and applied in real-world scenarios.

#### 3.1. High-level Coarse Placement Location Prediction

The first stage addresses the identification of potential placement regions. Given a language description of the placement task (D) and an RGBD image of the scene (I), the system performs the following steps:
1. **Object Extraction:** A segmentation model (SAM-2) is used to obtain segmentation masks of the target object, guided by an anchor point predicted by a VLM (Molmo) from a language prompt (e.g., "points to the bottle"). This allows the extraction of the target object's point cloud, P_c.
2. **Location Proposal:** A spatial VLM (Molmo) is queried with language prompts to detect all potential placement locations as 2D keypoints within the image. Examples include "all positions where a vial can be inserted into a vial plate."
3. **Region Cropping:** For each identified placement point, a local region of the base object's point cloud, P_b_crop, is extracted. This cropping is guided by the 3D bounding box of the target object, ensuring the low-level model focuses on the relevant geometric context.
This VLM-guided approach explicitly identifies multiple placement modes, simplifying the task for the subsequent fine-grained pose prediction model by providing a focused region rather than requiring it to explore placements across an entire object.

#### 3.2. Low-level Fine-grained Placement Pose Prediction

The second stage, conditioned on the point clouds P_c (target object) and P_b_crop (local base object region) from the high-level module, focuses on predicting the precise relative transformation (T_n) required for placement.
1. **Diffusion Model Architecture:** A transformer-based diffusion model is employed. The target object point cloud P_c is initially transformed by a random pose (R is sampled from SO(3), t is sampled within P_b_crop's bounding box).
2. **Encoder:** Both P_c (in its current state, P^(t)_c) and P_b_crop are downsampled (to 1024 points using Farthest Point Sampling), normalized, and fed into a transformer encoder. The encoder extracts latent features using self-attention layers and combines them via cross-attention and pooling, creating a unified feature representation of the two objects.
3. **Decoder:** A diffusion decoder, conditioned on this latent embedding and the diffusion timestep, predicts an incremental relative transformation T^(t)_n (rotation R and translation t). This transformation refines the object's pose, moving it closer to the goal.
4. **Iterative Denoising:** This process is iterative. At each step, the predicted transformation T^(t)_n is applied to the object point cloud (P^(t-1)_c = T^(t)_n * P^(t)_c). The final transformation T_n is the product of all incremental transformations across diffusion steps (Q_(t_max)^(t=1) T^(t)_n). During training, 5 denoising steps are used, with ground truth transformations generated by linearly interpolating translation and using spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) for rotation. At inference, a larger number of denoising steps (50) is used by repeating the last step.
5. **Loss Function:** The model is trained using a composite loss function including L1 distance for translation error, geodesic distance for rotation error, and Chamfer loss between point clouds transformed by predicted and ground truth poses.

#### 3.3. Synthetic Dataset Generation

To train the AnyPlace model, a fully synthetic dataset was developed:
1. **Object Generation:** 3D objects (e.g., pegs, holes, cups, racks, beakers, vials) are procedurally generated in Blender. Object parameters (height, width, length, shapes, edges, number of poles/holes, scaling) are randomized to maximize diversity. This programmatic generation aims to provide "free" placement poses.
2. **Placement Pose Generation:** NVIDIA IsaacSim is used to determine stable placement poses for three configurations: stacking, inserting, and hanging. Ideal placement locations are known from the object generation process (e.g., center of a hole). Object placement rotations are randomly sampled along their axis of symmetry.
3. **Data Collection:** Four cameras capture dense object point clouds and RGBD images. The dataset comprises 1489 objects across 13 categories, resulting in 5370 placement poses. The emphasis is on capturing a wide range of *local* placement arrangements, aligning with the low-level model's input requirements.

#### 3.4. Robot Pick and Place Execution

For experimental evaluation, a pick-and-place pipeline is implemented:
1. **Grasp Detection:** AnyGrasp is used to identify optimal grasp candidates for the target object from its point cloud, sorted by confidence.
2. **Motion Planning:** cuRobo is employed as a motion planner to generate collision-free robot trajectories. It plans movements from an initial pre-grasp pose to the grasp, then from a pre-place pose to the final placement pose predicted by AnyPlace.
3. **Rejection Sampling:** To ensure executable placements, rejection sampling is performed on generated (grasp, placement pose) pairs to identify valid combinations that can be executed by the robot while avoiding collisions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AnyPlace was conducted in both high-fidelity simulation and real-world settings, comparing its performance against baseline models across various placement tasks. The metrics used were success rate, coverage of placement modes, and precision.

#### 4.1. Simulation Evaluation

Three baseline models were used: NSM (Neural Shape Mating, a regression model), RPDiff (Relational Pose Diffusion, a diffusion-based model), and AnyPlace-EBM (an energy-based model variant of AnyPlace, sharing the VLM-guided coarse placement module). Models were trained both on placement-type-specific subsets and on the full multi-task dataset.

- **Placement Success:**
 - **Single-mode tasks (Object Stacking, Peg Insertion):** For stacking, where precision is less critical, all models achieved similar success rates. For peg insertion, which demands high precision, AnyPlace significantly outperformed baselines (30.95% vs. 7.63-8.44% for single-task, and 24.99% vs. 7.69-10.75% for multi-task).
 - **Multi-modal tasks (Cup Hang, Vial Insertion):** In the hanging task, AnyPlace showed a slight advantage. For the more challenging vial insertion, AnyPlace achieved the highest success rate (92.74% for single-task, 75.25% for multi-task), while baseline success rates dropped significantly (e.g., RPDiff at 16.51%, NSM at 18.70% for single-task). This indicated the effectiveness of the VLM for location proposal and the diffusion model for precise, fine-grained placement. AnyPlace-EBM, despite using the same high-level module, showed lower performance than the diffusion-based AnyPlace, suggesting the iterative denoising procedure is more effective for precision.

- **Placement Coverage:**
 - For multi-modal tasks (vial insertion and hanging), AnyPlace demonstrated near-perfect coverage with few samples per mode, attributable to its high success rate and the VLM's ability to identify diverse placement locations (Figure 4). AnyPlace-EBM followed a similar trend but plateaued at a lower coverage due to lower success rates. RPDiff exhibited limited multimodality capture, with coverage below 10% for vial insertion, and saturated at 90% for hanging, indicating generalization limitations. NSM also showed poor coverage.

- **Placement Precision:**
 - Evaluation on insertion tasks (Figure 5) showed that AnyPlace consistently achieved smaller and more uniform distance and rotation errors compared to baselines. This suggests that restricting input regions for the low-level model contributes to improved placement pose precision.

#### 4.2. Real-World Evaluation

AnyPlace was evaluated on 16 real-world placement configurations in a zero-shot setting, meaning models were trained solely on synthetic data. A simplified pick-and-place pipeline was used without rejection sampling or full motion planning.

- **Precise Multi-modal Placement:** Systematic evaluations over 10 trials on three tasks with multiple possible placement locations (vial insertion, ring hanging, battery stacking) showed that AnyPlace significantly outperformed baselines (Table 2). AnyPlace achieved 80% success on the vial insertion task and 67-80% on others, while NSM and RPDiff frequently failed (0-60%). AnyPlace-EBM also performed poorly. This demonstrated the model's generalization and precision from synthetic to real-world scenarios and its robustness to noisy real-world data.

- **Generalizable and Language-conditioned Placement:** AnyPlace successfully performed diverse placements with novel objects, including rigid and deformable items (e.g., stacking a funnel, hanging tools, peg-in-hole). It handled fine-grained tasks, long-horizon sequences (e.g., lid-on-pot, then pot-on-stove), and language-conditioned placements (e.g., bottle on different shelves). Visualizations showed accurate raw pose predictions and multimodal outputs, confirming the combined effectiveness of VLM-based location prediction and local pose refinement for accurate and language-guided placements (Figure 6, A1, A6). The approach was shown to generalize to unseen objects and complex configurations, despite being trained exclusively on synthetic data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AnyPlace research demonstrates advancements in robotic object placement, with several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Generalizable Object Placement:** The core contribution is the development of a method capable of performing a wide range of object placement tasks, from open-area placements to precise insertions and hanging, using a unified learning objective. This is a step towards more versatile and autonomous robotic manipulation systems.
- **Zero-Shot Synthetic-to-Real Transfer:** A key finding is the successful zero-shot transfer of models trained exclusively on a synthetic dataset to real-world robots. This capability significantly reduces the need for extensive and costly real-world data collection and training, accelerating the development and deployment of robotic applications.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Precision through Two-Stage Approach:** The proposed coarse-to-fine architecture, leveraging a Vision-Language Model (VLM) for high-level location proposal and a diffusion model for low-level fine-grained pose prediction, addresses limitations in existing methods. By focusing the precise pose prediction on local regions of interest, the model improves generalization across diverse object geometries and enhances precision, which is critical for complex tasks.
- **Effective Handling of Multimodal Placements:** The VLM-guided approach effectively identifies and leverages multiple potential placement locations, allowing the low-level model to cover a wider range of valid placement modes. This increases the robustness and flexibility of the robot in scenarios with multiple acceptable solutions.
- **Data Generation Pipeline:** The development of a programmatic synthetic dataset generation pipeline in Blender and NVIDIA IsaacSim is a valuable contribution. This pipeline can rapidly create large, diverse datasets covering various local placement arrangements, providing a foundation for training and evaluating manipulation models for current and future research.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge several limitations that indicate areas for future research and highlight the broader challenges in robot manipulation:
- **Full Pick-and-Place Generality:** The current work primarily focuses on pose prediction, with grasp compatibility and motion planning handled by separate modules. Integrating grasp selection with placement requirements more tightly and making rejection sampling more efficient in real-world scenarios remains a challenge.
- **Object Contact Interactions:** The model does not explicitly account for contact interactions during placement, potentially leading to aggressive approaches or failures due to lack of compliance control. Incorporating force feedback or compliance control could lead to more robust and gentle placements.
- **Precision of Point Cloud Data:** The accuracy of placement is still limited by the precision and completeness of input point clouds from real-world sensors. Future work could explore improved depth estimation, higher-quality sensors, or reactive policies based on force/torque feedback to compensate for noisy sensory data.
- **Language Conditioning in Low-Level Model:** The current low-level model does not incorporate language conditioning, limiting its ability to choose between different types of placements at the same location. Adding this capability would further enhance language-guided manipulation.

Overall, AnyPlace represents a step toward more adaptable and capable robots by providing a framework for learning generalizable, precise, and multimodal object placement from synthetic data, with clear pathways for future development to address remaining challenges in real-world deployment.
