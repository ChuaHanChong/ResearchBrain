# WAM-OPD: Sharpening World Action Models via On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> WAM-OPD adapts pretrained world action models to target tasks with on-policy distillation. Frozen task-specific teachers supervise action velocities along denoising paths that the student generates itself. Prefix-weighted trajectory replay reuses a fixed pool of student and teacher rollouts and reweights each history by accumulated denoising-path scores, so no extra environment interaction is needed. On RoboTwin, target success rises from 56.5% to 69.75%, and retention drops only 0.25 points.

## Problem

- Supervised fine-tuning specializes pretrained WAMs on target tasks but degrades pretrained skills, with single-task specialists collapsing on unrelated tasks such as bimanual handover.
- Online RL improves task success but needs repeated environment rollouts with execution, reset and safety costs, while sparse rewards further reduce its sample efficiency.
- Existing on-policy distillation assumes fixed conditioning inputs, or in closed-loop robot settings still needs fresh rollouts because observation histories shift as the student evolves.

## Method

- **WAM-OPD** ==on-policy distillation==: frozen task-specific teachers supply velocity targets at detached low-noise states on denoising paths freshly generated by the current student, supervising only the action branch.
- ==Prefix-weighted trajectory replay== (**PWTR**): a fixed pool of mostly initial-student rollouts plus teacher rollouts supplies stored histories, so distillation needs no further environment interaction.
- ==Prefix proxy importance weights==: stored denoising paths are scored against student-snapshot, initial-student and teacher velocity fields, and accumulated prefix scores give clipped, normalized ==change-of-measure weights== per history.

## Results

- On RoboTwin 2.0 with a Fast-WAM backbone, mean target success rises from **56.50% to 69.75%** while six-task retention stays at **86.92% versus 87.17%** initially.
- Nearly matches online OPD, which uses extra rollouts (**70.25%**), and beats STEAM (**57.50%** target, **76.00%** retention) and πRL, whose retention collapses to **29.42%**.
- Ablations: prefix weighting adds **2.5 points** in the mixed pool, the mixed pool beats student-only and teacher-only pools, and shuffling weights across histories costs **4.25 points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distilling on the student's own denoising paths preserves prior skills far better than SFT or RL, which makes merging several teachers into one generalist viable.
> - In closed-loop policies only the histories need environment interaction, while denoising paths can be regenerated by inference, so reweighted replay of fixed histories cheaply approximates on-policy training.
> - Reweighting cannot create coverage: teacher-only pools gained nothing from the weights, so replay pools must include histories the student itself actually visits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

WAMs are pretrained robot models that reuse learned behaviors across manipulation tasks. A general-purpose manipulation policy may need to become more reliable at particular recurring jobs without sacrificing its other skills. In practice, a failure on a frequently repeated task can mean workflow interruptions and human intervention.

The authors train one student from two sources of advice. A frozen task specialist teaches on action paths the student generates. Meanwhile, the student reuses histories from a fixed set of robot rollouts instead of collecting new ones during each distillation update. The method, WAM-OPD, is reported to produce a better average on two adapted tasks with nearly unchanged average success on six held-out tasks. It uses no additional environment interaction during distillation.

RoboTwin success-rate table. Methods are in rows, with two target-task columns, six retention-task columns, and target and retention averages. The target and retention groups are different task sets.

### 2. Replay histories, refresh the lesson

In on-policy distillation (OPD), a frozen teacher supplies learning targets on samples generated by the current student. The student does more than copy a fixed set of teacher actions.

A WAM produces an action chunk from an interaction history. Executing part of the chunk changes the scene, and therefore the history for the next decision. This makes closed-loop WAMs different from a setup with fixed conditioning inputs.

The paper calls its answer PWTR. Before distillation, the authors collect a fixed pool made primarily of initial-student rollouts and supplemented by task-specific teacher rollouts. For each stored history, the current student generates a fresh denoising path, which is the sequence of intermediate states used to generate an action. The frozen teacher supplies a velocity target at a low-noise point on that path. The environment history stays stored, but the denoising path and the supervision are refreshed. The stored action is not the regression target.

Each history also receives a weight. Stored denoising paths are scored under a periodically refreshed student snapshot and under fixed initial-student and task-teacher references. Scores are accumulated over the sampled decisions before the current decision. Histories whose preceding paths are more compatible with the snapshot, relative to those references, get more weight.

This weight is a path-compatibility proxy inspired by a history-density ratio. It is not an exact action likelihood or an exact online importance ratio. It can rebalance which stored histories are used, but it cannot create histories missing from the pool.

WAM-OPD overview. Left: initial-student and task-teacher rollouts feed a fixed pool. A refreshed student snapshot and fixed references score the stored paths, and prefix sums over earlier sampled decisions produce history weights. The current decision is excluded from its own prefix weight. Right: a stored history conditions the current student, which generates a fresh path. The frozen task teacher gives local velocity supervision at a low-noise state.

### 3. Testing the weighting and the outcome

The mechanism test comes from ablations with 200 cases per task on the two target tasks. In the mixed pool, WAM-OPD averages 69.75% across Stapler and Microwave. It averages 65.50% under M-Shuffle, which permutes weights among histories at the same sampled sequence position. The 4.25-percentage-point gap supports the importance of history–weight correspondence. It does not prove that the proxy weights are exact.

Prefix weighting does not help in every pool. In the teacher-only pool, its mean changes from 64.00% to 63.75%. This is consistent with the authors' explanation that weighting cannot compensate for inadequate coverage.

In the main RoboTwin 2.0 evaluation, success rate is the percentage of successful trials per task, measured over 200 trials. Each average weights its tasks equally. Move Stapler Pad and Open Microwave form the target group. Switch, Mug, Laptop, Pillbottle, Dual Bottles, and Handover form the retention group.

The same adapted student is evaluated on both task groups. Its target mean rises from 56.50% to 69.75%. Its retention mean is 86.92%, versus 87.17% for the initial student.

The target mean is comparable to online OPD's 70.25%. Online OPD, however, uses extra environment rollouts during distillation, and WAM-OPD does not. That saving has a specific scope. Rollouts for the pool are collected beforehand, and evaluation still involves the environment. Teacher inference and weight refreshes still require computation.

### 4. What replay can and cannot buy

WAM-OPD refreshes the student's denoising-path lesson while keeping the robot's stored histories fixed. It uses prefix weighting to make those histories more relevant as the student changes. The result shows task adaptation and near-initial average performance on the six tested retention tasks. It does not demonstrate retention of every pretrained skill. The method's performance depends on pool coverage and the accuracy of the history-weight approximation.
