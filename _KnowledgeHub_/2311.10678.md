# Distilling and Retrieving Generalizable Knowledge for Robot Manipulation via Language Corrections

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University and Google Deepmind developed DROC, a framework that allows robots to interpret arbitrary language corrections, distill generalizable knowledge from these interactions, and retrieve it using both semantic and visual cues for novel manipulation tasks. The system significantly reduced human interventions, requiring less than half the corrections of baseline methods in skill-level tasks and demonstrating superior generalization for plan-level constraints like user preferences and common-sense reasoning.

## Problem

- Current robot policies exhibit subpar generalization to novel environments and tasks, often necessitating frequent human interventions.
- Existing LLM-driven robotics systems struggle to flexibly interpret arbitrary human feedback that spans both high-level task planning and low-level skill execution.
- Valuable knowledge from past human corrections is frequently lost due to the limited context windows of LLMs, preventing long-term learning and knowledge retention across interactions.

## Method

- Developed **DROC** (Distillation and Retrieval of Online Corrections), an LLM-based framework that classifies and responds to arbitrary high-level or low-level language feedback via a ==Correction Handler==.
- Implemented a ==Knowledge Distillation== module that extracts generalizable plan-level (e.g., task constraints, user preferences) and skill-level (e.g., gripper poses, object parameters) knowledge from the full interaction history.
- Designed a ==Knowledge Retrieval== module that indexes and retrieves relevant past experiences from a knowledge base using both semantic similarity between tasks and visual features of objects, guiding future task planning and skill execution.

## Results

- DROC required **less than half** the number of online corrections compared to the Code as Policies baseline for skill-level tasks, demonstrating more effective immediate adaptation to new task variations.
- The system effectively transferred distilled knowledge, reducing corrections for novel plan-level tasks by abstracting high-level constraints (e.g., user preferences reduced from **1 to 0.5** corrections).
- Visual retrieval was critical for tasks requiring nuanced distinctions in object states or orientations, enabling accurate policy selection and outperforming methods relying solely on semantic similarity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robots can flexibly adapt task plans and skill parameters during execution by classifying and contextualizing arbitrary human language corrections (high-level vs. low-level).
> - Distilling transient online corrections into generalizable knowledge, such as user preferences or object-specific information, enables long-term learning beyond the limitations of LLM context windows.
> - Combining semantic and visual feature-based retrieval allows robots to accurately recall and apply relevant knowledge from past experiences to novel tasks and object instances, especially when language alone is ambiguous.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a significant challenge in robotics: enabling robot policies to generalize effectively to novel environments and situations, particularly through human corrective feedback. It situates itself within the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM)-powered robotics, building upon and extending prior advancements while aiming to overcome their limitations.

**LLM-powered Robotics:** Recent research has demonstrated LLMs' capabilities in robotics, ranging from generating high-level task plans and directly producing robot code to adapting based on human feedback. Works like InnerMonologue and ReAct have shown LLMs modifying task-level plans, while other approaches leverage LLMs to adjust reward functions or ground fine-grained language commands (e.g., "move a bit to the right") using Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and various representations. However, existing methods often adopt a rigid approach to human feedback, struggling with arbitrary forms of corrections that span both high-level task planning and low-level skill execution. Furthermore, feedback is frequently handled in a temporary fashion, limited by LLM context lengths, leading to the loss of valuable information across interactions.

**Knowledge Bases for LLMs:** The concept of knowledge bases has proven effective in augmenting LLMs by providing relevant examples for few-shot prompting. Traditional retrieval-augmentation often relies on similarity metrics between input questions and stored examples. However, this paper identifies a gap for robotics tasks: synthesizing large amounts of feedback requires not only similarity-based retrieval but also the ability to summarize and abstract knowledge. This work integrates these elements, combining the summarization of feedback with a mechanism to partition it into high-level or low-level categories, thereby enabling broader generalization from language feedback.

**Learning from Language Corrections:** Prior literature outside LLM-based frameworks has explored incorporating language corrections to adapt policies, often through offline policy updates or learning cost maps from post-hoc feedback. More recent techniques have enabled responses to real-time language corrections, but these often impose restrictive assumptions, such as requiring specific grounding mechanisms or extensive training data and demonstrations. In contrast, this research focuses on online human corrections that modify robot behavior during execution, without requiring specific training data for correction interpretation, leveraging LLMs to directly generate robot code given the feedback.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a unified system capable of interpreting and responding to arbitrary forms of online language feedback—from high-level planning errors to low-level skill adjustments—and critically, by distilling and retrieving generalizable knowledge from these interactions for long-term improvement across novel tasks and environments. It addresses the twin challenges of feedback flexibility and knowledge retention, which are significant bottlenecks in current LLM-driven embodied AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core problem addressed by this research is the subpar performance of current robot policies in generalizing to novel environments and tasks. While human corrective feedback is recognized as a critical mechanism for guiding robots towards generalization, integrating this feedback effectively presents several non-trivial challenges.

The key objectives and motivations driving the development of DROC are:

1. **Responding to Arbitrary Language Feedback:** Current LLM-driven robot policies often exhibit rigidness in how they can receive and interpret human feedback. Human corrections can vary significantly, ranging from high-level guidance (e.g., "You should open the drawer first") pertaining to task plans or preferences, to fine-grained, low-level adjustments (e.g., "Move a little bit to the right") concerning skill parameters or relative movements. A primary objective is to enable robots to flexibly interpret and respond to this arbitrary spectrum of online language corrections, identifying whether feedback relates to task plans or skill primitives, and leveraging prior context for sequential corrections.

2. **Distilling Generalizable Knowledge from Corrections:** A significant limitation of existing methods is the temporary nature of feedback processing. Corrections are often handled in an ad-hoc manner within the limited context window of an LLM, meaning valuable lessons from past interventions are frequently lost. The motivation here is to develop a mechanism that can intelligently "distill" or summarize generalizable knowledge from these transient online corrections. This distilled knowledge should capture reusable insights, such as task constraints, robot capabilities, user preferences, and object-specific information.

3. **Retrieving Relevant Past Experiences for Future Tasks:** Building upon the distillation process, a further objective is to enable robots to "remember" and "retrieve" this generalizable knowledge to improve performance in novel settings. This involves intelligently indexing the distilled knowledge in a knowledge base and selectively retrieving relevant information—based on both textual and visual similarities—when facing new tasks or object instances. The aim is to proactively leverage past learning to reduce the need for repeated human interventions.

4. **Reducing Amortized Corrections and Improving Generalization:** Ultimately, the overarching motivation is to create a robot system that continuously learns and improves from human interaction. By effectively responding to feedback, distilling knowledge, and retrieving it for future use, the system aims to significantly reduce the total number of human corrections required over time across a sequence of tasks (i.e., lower the amortized intervention rate). This directly translates to improved generalization capabilities for LLM-based robot policies in diverse and unseen scenarios, making robots more adaptable and autonomous.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to bridge the gap between human intuitive guidance and robot operational capabilities, transforming ephemeral human corrections into persistent, reusable knowledge that enhances robot adaptability and reduces dependence on continuous human oversight.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system, Distillation and Retrieval of Online Corrections (DROC), is an LLM-based framework designed to respond to arbitrary language feedback, distill generalizable knowledge, and retrieve relevant past experiences for novel tasks. DROC operates through three primary reasoning modules: a Correction Handler (C), a Knowledge Extractor (E), and a Knowledge Retriever (R).

**Problem Formulation:** The system considers a manipulation task defined by a natural language instruction (L). A task planner (T) decomposes L into a sequence of low-level skills (ℓ₁, ℓ₂..., ℓM), and a skill composer (S) maps each skill to a control policy (p), represented as a code snippet. During execution, a human provides online language corrections (cᵢⱼ), which result in a solution (sᵢⱼ) that either triggers a re-plan from T or a new skill execution from S. The interaction history (Hᵢⱼ) accumulates these interactions. The objective is to minimize the amortized number of corrections across multiple tasks.

**DROC Framework:**
1. **Task Planning (T):** The task planner generates high-level plans by decomposing the instruction L. It is prompted with scene information, few-shot examples, and initial rule constraints (e.g., hierarchical abstraction, handling ambiguous references). The LLM is also tasked with updating object states after each task execution or correction. Constraints can be modified during knowledge distillation.
2. **Skill Composing (S):** For each skill primitive, the skill composer generates corresponding robot code. It is provided with function APIs and few-shot examples, similar to prior "Code-as-Policies" approaches. Perception APIs are used to call a VLM-based perceiver (Segment-Anything for masks, CLIP for labeling) to detect task-related objects. S also utilizes task-related knowledge retrieved by DROC.

**Core DROC Modules:**

- **Correction Handler (C):**
 - **Error Level Classification:** Given a language correction cᵢⱼ, DROC prompts an LLM to classify it as either "high-level" (pertaining to task constraints, user preferences) or "low-level" (primitive parameters, object information).
 - **High-Level Correction Response:** If high-level, the correction, current skill, and original plan are passed to the LLM to trigger C_T, which guides the task planner T to generate a modified plan with added constraints.
 - **Low-Level Correction Response:** If low-level, the Knowledge Extractor (E) first retrieves relevant context from a *short-term* interaction history. This short-term history retrieval is limited to three categories, as inferred by the LLM based on cᵢⱼ: (a) Last interaction, (b) Initial interaction, or (c) No dependence. Then, this context is passed to the LLM for C_S to generate a new code policy using S.
 - **Contextual Grounding:** For fine-grained corrections, C_S reasons about the reference frame (absolute vs. object-centric), empirically observing that humans tend to use object-centric frames. Object's geometric properties (e.g., normal vector) are provided to define the object's frame. Vague distance expressions (e.g., "a little bit") are grounded to the size of the related object for adaptability.

- **Knowledge Distillation (E):**
 - **Process:** At the completion of each skill or plan, E processes the skill description (ℓᵢ) and the full interaction history (H). It uses chain-of-thought prompting to identify and extract generalizable knowledge.
 - **Knowledge Types:**
 - **Plan-level:** Task constraints (e.g., object placement rules), robot constraints (e.g., single-arm manipulation), user preferences (e.g., preferred items).
 - **Skill-level:** Task parameters (e.g., gripper pose, pull distance), object information (e.g., visual features, labels).
 - **Storage:** The distilled knowledge is saved in a dictionary format in a knowledge base (B), keyed by task name. Object states are updated, and labels for ambiguous references are remembered as task constraints.

- **Knowledge Retrieval (R):**
 - **Relevancy Metrics:** For a new task or skill description, R retrieves relevant knowledge from B using two metrics: (a) task semantics and (b) visual features of the task-related object. Some knowledge (e.g., robot/user constraints) is always shared and retrieved.
 - **Retrieval Process:**
 1. R is prompted in a zero-shot manner to identify all tasks in the knowledge base that are semantically similar to the current query.
 2. For these semantically similar tasks, the queried object's visual features are compared to the visual features of objects from those tasks. The experience with the highest visual similarity is selected.
 - **Visual Feature Use:** Visual features are crucial when language alone is insufficient (e.g., distinguishing between different types of drawer handles or cup orientations that require different manipulation policies). DINO-V2 features are used for visual-visual retrieval, and CLIP features for visual-semantic retrieval. This retrieved knowledge then guides T or S.

**Implementation Details:** GPT-4 is utilized for all LLM modules within DROC. Experiments are conducted on a real-world tabletop environment with a Franka Emika Panda Robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DROC was conducted on a real-world tabletop environment using a Franka Emika Panda Robot, with GPT-4 powering the LLM modules. The experiments aimed to validate DROC's capabilities in responding to online corrections, distilling generalizable knowledge, and retrieving relevant information for novel tasks.

**Skill-Level Corrections:**
The system was tested on five table-top manipulation tasks, iterating each task three times with varying object locations and instances. The performance was measured by the amortized number of corrections needed across learning iterations (Fig. 3).
- **Effective Correction Response:** DROC demonstrated superior performance compared to the Code as Policies (CaP) baseline, requiring less than half the number of corrections in the initial round. This indicates that DROC's correction handling mechanism, which interprets and responds to immediate feedback, is more effective than directly generating robot code via LLMs without DROC's integrated architecture.
- **Intra-Task Generalization via Distillation:** Comparison with "Ours–H" (DROC with no initial history) showed that distilled knowledge significantly aids in adapting to different variations of the same task. The initial corrections required by DROC were lower, suggesting that knowledge extracted from prior iterations of the *same* task (but with different configurations) was effectively reused.
- **Accuracy through Visual Retrieval:** For tasks like "Hang cup on Rack," where object orientation (e.g., upright vs. flipped cup) dictates different policies, "Ours–V" (DROC without visual retrieval) performed less effectively. This highlights the importance of visual features, alongside semantic similarity, in accurately retrieving the correct skill-level knowledge when language alone might be ambiguous or insufficient to distinguish object states requiring distinct actions.
- **Cross-Object Transfer:** In "pick up object" and "put tape in drawer" tasks, "Ours+Transfer" (reusing history from other objects or similar tasks, e.g., "put scissors in top white drawer" for "put tape in drawer") further reduced the number of online corrections. This result indicates that DROC can distill generalizable skill parameters that are transferable across different object instances or task categories, enhancing its adaptability.
- **Learning Curve:** Across all skill-level tasks, the number of corrections consistently declined with increasing iterations, demonstrating DROC's capacity to learn, generalize, and adapt to unseen new settings over time.

**Plan-Level Corrections:**
Seven long-horizon tasks were designed to evaluate DROC's ability to handle plan-level corrections and generalize knowledge across different types of high-level constraints: user preferences, feasibility of plans, common-sense reasoning, and scene information (Table I). Each task scenario involved a "train" task followed by a "test" task.
- **Superior Generalization:** DROC consistently required fewer corrections on the test tasks compared to "Ours–R" (a naive baseline that retrieves saved plans without distillation). Table II shows that DROC significantly reduced corrections for all tested knowledge types:
 - **User Preferences:** 0.5 corrections (DROC) vs. 1 (Ours–R).
 - **Feasibility of Plans:** 0.67 corrections (DROC) vs. 1.33 (Ours–R).
 - **Common-sense Reasoning:** 0 corrections (DROC) vs. 1.5 (Ours–R).
 - **Scene Information:** 0.5 corrections (DROC) vs. 1.5 (Ours–R).
- These results demonstrate that DROC's knowledge distillation and retrieval mechanisms are effective at abstracting high-level constraints, preferences, and common-sense rules from corrections during training tasks and applying them to related but novel test tasks, leading to more robust task planning. Visual illustrations (Fig. 4) further demonstrate DROC's ability to identify plan-level errors, replan based on distilled constraints, and leverage both semantic and visual retrieval for ambiguous instructions, validating the system's integrated approach.

In summary, DROC consistently outperformed baselines across both skill-level and plan-level tasks. Its ability to effectively respond to diverse online corrections, distill meaningful knowledge, and retrieve it using both textual and visual cues, led to a substantial reduction in required human interventions and improved generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DROC framework represents a notable advancement in robot learning from human interaction, particularly in the context of LLM-powered embodied agents. Its significance and potential impact span several critical areas:

1. **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** DROC directly addresses the fundamental challenge of generalization in robot manipulation. By enabling robots to distill reusable knowledge from online human corrections and retrieve it for novel tasks, DROC facilitates adaptation to new environments, objects, and configurations. This capability is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic, real-world settings where encountering unforeseen variations is common.

2. **Reduced Human Intervention and Learning Efficiency:** A key finding is the significant reduction in the number of human corrections required over time. This translates to increased operational efficiency and a less burdensome experience for human operators. By learning from past mistakes and applying that knowledge, robots can become more autonomous, requiring fewer "teaching moments" for similar future scenarios, which is vital for scalable robot deployment.

3. **Flexible and Comprehensive Feedback Integration:** DROC's ability to interpret and respond to arbitrary forms of language feedback—spanning high-level task planning and low-level skill execution—is a substantial improvement over prior rigid approaches. This flexibility makes the human-robot interaction more natural and intuitive, as users are not constrained to specific types or formats of commands. This comprehensive handling of feedback makes robots more robust to varied user instructions.

4. **Long-Term Knowledge Retention for LLMs in Robotics:** The framework provides an effective solution to the "context window problem" in LLMs, where valuable information from past interactions can be lost. By distilling generalizable knowledge into a persistent knowledge base, DROC enables robots to accumulate learning over extended periods, moving beyond ephemeral in-context learning to a more durable form of knowledge acquisition.

5. **Multimodal Knowledge Retrieval:** The integration of visual similarity alongside semantic similarity for knowledge retrieval is a significant methodological contribution. This multimodal approach allows robots to make more nuanced distinctions between objects or situations that might appear semantically similar but require different manipulation strategies, thereby improving the accuracy and robustness of retrieved knowledge.

6. **Modular and Extensible Architecture:** The DROC system, with its distinct correction handler, knowledge extractor, and knowledge retriever modules, offers a modular and extensible architecture. This design allows for potential improvements or substitutions of individual components (e.g., using different LLMs, VLMs, or knowledge representation schemes) without redesigning the entire system, fostering future research and development.

In essence, DROC moves robot learning closer to a continuous, adaptive paradigm where robots can progressively refine their behaviors and plans through natural language interaction, ultimately making them more capable, autonomous, and seamlessly integrated into human environments. This research lays foundational groundwork for developing intelligent robotic systems that can truly learn and evolve from their experiences and human guidance.
