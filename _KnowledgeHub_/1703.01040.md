# Learning Robot Activities from First-Person Human Videos Using Convolutional Future Regression

> [!summary] Summary
> A robot learns collaborative activities from unlabeled first-person human videos. An SSD hand detector is extended with a convolutional autoencoder to compress each frame into a scene representation; a fully convolutional regression network predicts that representation one second ahead, decoded back into future hand boxes. A fully connected manipulation network maps predicted hand pixels plus joint angles to motor commands, running in real time on a Baxter robot.

## Problem

- Deep visuomotor policies need huge amounts of robot-collected motor data, limiting them to simple grasping and pushing rather than new collaborative activities.
- Prior video-based imitation learned grammars over pre-defined atomic actions, requiring labeled supervision and hand-coded motion transfer, so it was not end-to-end trainable.
- Future-frame prediction work in vision never drove real manipulation, lacking components that convert predicted representations into executable motor commands.

## Method

- **Hand representation network**: SSD extended with a five-layer convolutional, five-layer deconvolutional autoencoder, splitting into feature extractor g and box estimator h over a compressed feature map.
- ==Convolutional future regression== network r: seven 5x5 256-filter layers plus 13x13 and 1x1 layers, trained by Euclidean loss to map current feature maps to those one second later, optionally concatenating K past frames.
- **Manipulation network**: seven fully connected layers (32-32-32-16-16-16-7) mapping current joint angles, current hand pixels, and predicted future hand pixels to future joint torques, trained on unlabeled robot logs.

## Results

- Future hand prediction reached F-measure **30.90** with K=10 versus **13.23** for the best modified SSD baseline, **4.87** for hands-only, and **0.20** for hand-crafted features.
- Mean pixel error over all hands fell to **46.66** (**36.78** for the right hand) against **58.58** for SSD with future annotations and **143.85** for hand-crafted representation on 1280x720 frames.
- In a **12-participant** user study on two collaboration tasks, the full system scored **3.29** out of 5 versus **2.29**, **1.92**, and **1.72** for ablated perception or control, at roughly **100 ms** per frame.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Regressing a compressed intermediate representation forward in time, then reusing the frozen decoder head, yields semantic future predictions without any future-frame labels.
> - Feeding a stack of K past frames steadily improves anticipation, indicating that motion context, not single-frame appearance, carries the activity's temporal structure.
> - Egocentric human video approximates a robot's own camera view closely enough to transfer perception, but ablations show the control network matters as much as the predictor.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper contributes to the fields of robot learning, particularly robot learning from demonstration (LfD), deep learning for robotics, and egocentric (first-person) vision.

Previous efforts in robot learning from demonstration have often focused on learning motor control policies from direct robot control sequences or simulated data. These approaches typically require extensive robot-specific data, which can be challenging and resource-intensive to acquire, especially for complex or novel activities. Furthermore, many traditional LfD methods relied on pre-defined sets of primitive actions or required significant human annotation for supervised learning of activity grammar representations. This limited a robot's ability to learn new activities from scratch or generalize to novel scenarios without explicit instruction for each atomic action.

The emergence of deep learning has shown promise in robotics, enabling robots to learn motor control policies directly from visual inputs. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been successful in tasks like object grasping and pushing. However, these methods typically demand large volumes of 'robot' training data, which again poses a significant barrier to teaching robots a wide array of new activities.

Concurrently, research in computer vision has explored video prediction, aiming to forecast future frames or intermediate visual representations. While these techniques have advanced the understanding of visual dynamics, their direct application to robotic manipulation for generating actionable control commands has been limited. Existing work on visual prediction for robot control often necessitated substantial training data acquired from actual physical robots.

First-person (egocentric) videos, captured from an actor's own viewpoint, have garnered attention in computer vision for activity recognition. However, the use of such videos to learn executable representations for robots or to transfer learned human activities to robotic systems has been relatively underexplored.

This paper positions its work as an advancement that addresses several of these limitations. It seeks to leverage the abundance of human video data, specifically first-person videos, to train robots without requiring large robot-specific datasets or extensive manual annotations. By employing an unsupervised approach to learn the temporal structure of activities from human videos, and then transferring this model to a robot, the authors aim to facilitate more scalable and flexible robot learning for collaborative tasks. The novelty lies in its entirely CNN-based method for learning human-robot interactions from human-human first-person videos, explicitly predicting future human hand locations to inform robot motor control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel approach that enables robots to learn new activities and their associated motor controls directly from unlabeled human example videos, specifically those recorded from a first-person perspective.

This overarching objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Addressing Data Scarcity in Deep Learning for Robotics:** Current deep learning approaches for robot manipulation often require millions of parameters to be trained, necessitating a large quantity of 'robot' data. Acquiring such data, which typically involves numerous demonstrations of humans or robots directly controlling a physical robot, is a significant bottleneck. This limitation restricts the robot's ability to learn new, previously unseen activities efficiently.
- **Leveraging Abundant Human Video Data:** Human activity videos are an attractive and readily available resource for training. Unlike robot-specific data, they do not require specialized hardware or software for collection. The paper specifically targets *first-person* human videos, motivated by the premise that the visual input from a human's viewpoint during an activity is inherently similar to what a humanoid robot would observe from its own camera during the same activity. This similarity is hypothesized to allow the robot to learn "visual memory" of itself performing activities, directly inferring expected visual observations and their temporal changes.
- **Enabling Unsupervised Activity Learning:** Many prior approaches to robot activity learning from visual data relied on supervised learning, requiring users to define and label atomic actions within human activity videos. This process prevented robots from learning activities from scratch and required new definitions and hand-coding for each new activity or atomic action. The authors aim for an unsupervised learning paradigm for the core temporal structure, eliminating the need for activity or object labels in the training videos.
- **Facilitating End-to-End Learning:** Traditional methods often involved separate modules for perception (e.g., recognizing atomic actions) and execution (e.g., hand-coding robot motions). This research seeks an end-to-end deep learning solution where visual input directly informs motor control, promoting more natural and integrated learning.
- **Predicting Future States for Proactive Control:** A core idea is that humans learn not only how to execute an activity but also to visually predict the future consequences of motion. By enabling the robot to predict future visual representations, specifically future hand and object locations, the system can infer a proactive motor control plan, allowing the robot to anticipate and respond to evolving interaction scenarios.
- **Enabling Real-Time Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ultimate goal is to enable robots to execute learned collaborative activities in real-time, directly based on live camera input, which is crucial for practical human-robot interaction scenarios.

In summary, the motivation is to develop a data-efficient, flexible, and scalable deep learning framework that allows robots to autonomously learn diverse human-like activities from readily available, unlabeled first-person human videos, thereby overcoming significant challenges in robot data acquisition and task generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach integrates a perception component and a manipulation component, both built upon deep convolutional neural networks, to enable robots to learn activities from first-person human videos and execute corresponding motor controls.

#### 3.1. System Overview

The system operates by taking a sequence of current video frames as input. Its goal is twofold: first, to predict the future locations of hands and interactive objects in the robot's field of view; second, to generate robot control commands to move the robot's hands towards these predicted future locations. The core concept relies on training the perception component using human first-person activity videos, assuming a similar viewpoint to the robot's camera, allowing the robot to predict its ideal future hand positions during an activity.

#### 3.2. Perception Component

The perception component is responsible for processing visual input and predicting future hand locations. It consists of two fully convolutional neural networks:

1. **Hand Representation Network:**
 - This network is an extension of the Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD), a state-of-the-art object detection framework.
 - Its purpose is to generate a hand-based scene representation and estimate bounding boxes for hands and objects in the *current* video frame.
 - A key modification involves inserting a fully convolutional auto-encoder within the SSD architecture. This auto-encoder comprises five convolutional layers followed by five deconvolutional layers. It uses 5x5 kernels, with filter counts decreasing (512, 256, 128, 64, 256 for convolution) and then symmetrically increasing for deconvolution. Stride 2 is used in the last convolutional layer for dimensionality reduction, without pooling layers.
 - This auto-encoder abstracts the image scene (including hands and objects) into a lower-dimensional intermediate visual representation or feature map, denoted `F_t`.
 - The network, `f = h(g(X_t))`, where `g` is the feature extractor (encoder) and `h` is the box estimator (decoder), is trained to predict current hand locations `Y_t`.

2. **Future Regression Network:**
 - This network's objective is to predict *future* intermediate scene representations (`F_t+Δ`) from the *current* representation (`F_t`).
 - It is a fully convolutional network comprising seven convolutional layers (256 5x5 kernels), followed by a layer with 1024 13x13 kernels, and a final layer with 256 1x1 kernels.
 - The network, `r_w(F_t)`, is trained *unsupervisedly* using unlabeled first-person human activity videos. The training objective is to minimize the L2 loss between the predicted future feature map `r_w(g(X_t))` and the actual future feature map `F_t+Δ` (obtained by `g(X_t+Δ)`).
 - The model can optionally take advantage of previous `K` frames by concatenating their feature maps `[g(X_t), ..., g(X_t-(K-1))]` as input for `r`.
 - Once `F_t+Δ` is predicted, it is fed into the box estimator (`h`) of the hand representation network to obtain the final predicted future hand locations `Y_t+Δ`.
 - This two-step process (`Y_t+Δ = h(r(g(X_t)))`) allows the system to predict explicit future hand locations while implicitly considering activity and object context, without explicit object detection.

#### 3.3. Manipulation Component

The manipulation component translates the predicted 2-D future hand locations into actual robot motor control commands.

1. **Regression Network for Motor Control:**
 - This component is a regression network consisting of seven fully connected layers with hidden unit counts: 32, 32, 32, 16, 16, 16, 7.
 - It predicts future robot joint states (`Z_t+Δ`) based on the current robot joint states (`Z_t`), current robot hand locations (`Y_Rt`), and the *predicted future robot hand locations* (`Y_Rt+Δ`) from the perception component. The assumption `Y_Rt ≈ Y_t` implies that the robot's view and target behavior are analogous to the human demonstration.
 - The network is trained on unlabeled robot activity log files, which contain sequences of robot hand positions (u,v in image plane) and corresponding joint angles. This training minimizes the L2 loss between the predicted and actual future joint states (`m_θ(Z_t, Y_Rt, Y_Rt+Δ)`) and `Z_t+Δ`.
 - This neural network-based approach for manipulation is claimed to generate more natural arm movements compared to standard Inverse Kinematics, as it considers both desired end-effector locations and observed joint configuration sequences from the log files.

#### 3.4. Datasets for Training

- **EgoHands:** A public dataset of first-person videos with ground-truth hand labels, extended with additional frames, used to train the *Hand Representation Network*.
- **Unlabeled Human-Human Interaction Videos:** A custom dataset of 47 first-person videos of collaborative activities (e.g., clearing a table, pushing a trivet). This dataset is central for training the *Future Regression Network* in an unsupervised manner, by learning changes in hand-based representations over time.
- **Unlabeled Robot Activity Log Files:** Fifty log files recorded from a Baxter research robot, containing robot hand positions (projected 3-D grippers to 2-D image plane) and corresponding 7-DOF joint angles, collected by a human operator moving the robot arms. This dataset is used to train the *Manipulation Network*.

#### 3.5. Real-time Operation

A key design consideration and stated capability of the entire system is its ability to operate in real-time, taking raw video frames and generating motor control commands for direct robot activity execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation focused on two main aspects: the performance of the perception component in predicting future hand locations and the success level of real-time human-robot collaboration.

#### 4.1. Evaluation of Future Hand Prediction (Perception Component)

The perception component's ability to predict future human hand bounding boxes and precise pixel locations was quantitatively assessed against several baselines using a set of human-human interaction videos. The prediction horizon (Δ) was set to 30 frames (1 second).

- **Metrics for Bounding Box Prediction:** Precision, Recall, and F-measure were used, with an Intersection Over Union (IoU) ratio greater than 0.5 for true positives.
 - The proposed "Deep Regressor" model consistently outperformed all baselines.
 - Increasing the number of input frames (`K`) for the regression network improved performance. With `K=10` (using 10 previous frames), the Deep Regressor achieved the highest F-measure of 30.90 (Precision 36.58, Recall 28.78), significantly surpassing the best baseline, "SSD with future annotations1 " (F-measure 13.23).
 - Baselines like "Hand-crafted representation" and "Hands only" performed substantially lower, indicating the benefit of learning rich visual representations and temporal dynamics.

- **Metrics for Hand Position Accuracy:** Mean Pixel Distance between ground truth and predicted hand positions was measured on a 1280x720 image plane.
 - Across all four types of hands (my left, my right, opponent's left, opponent's right), the Deep Regressor (K=10) exhibited the lowest mean pixel distance error at 46.66 pixels, outperforming the best baseline "SSD with future annotations1 " at 58.58 pixels.
 - Focusing specifically on "my right hand" (relevant for the robot's primary manipulator in the test scenarios), the Deep Regressor (K=10) maintained its superior performance with a mean error of 36.78 pixels, compared to the best baseline "SSD with future annotations1 " at 48.63 pixels.
 - The mean pixel distances, while appearing somewhat high, were deemed sufficient for generating the required robot motion in practical scenarios.

Visual examples provided in the paper illustrated the system's capacity to predict future hand locations (represented by colored bounding boxes) overlaid on actual future frames, demonstrating its ability to anticipate human actions in dynamic interaction scenarios like clearing a table or pushing a trivet.

#### 4.2. Real-time Robot Experiments (Human-Robot Collaboration)

A user study was conducted with 12 participants to evaluate the robot's performance in two collaborative tasks: clearing a table and preparing a trivet. Participants rated the robot's behaviors on a 1-5 scale.

- **Comparison with Baselines:** Four configurations were tested:
 1. "Base SSD + Base control" (baseline perception and manipulation networks).
 2. "Base SSD + Our control" (baseline perception, proposed manipulation).
 3. "Our perception + Base control" (proposed perception, baseline manipulation).
 4. "Ours" (proposed perception + proposed manipulation).
- **User Study Results:** The complete proposed approach ("Ours") achieved the highest average user satisfaction score of 3.29 across both tasks (Task 1: 3.17, Task 2: 3.42).
 - This score was notably higher than all baseline combinations, which ranged from 1.72 ("Base SSD + Base control") to 2.29 ("Our perception + Base control").
 - The results suggest that both the proposed perception component (for future hand prediction) and the manipulation component (for generating natural robot movements) contribute to the perceived success of the robot's collaborative activities.
- **Real-time Performance:** The system demonstrated real-time operation, processing approximately 10 frames per second (~100 ms per frame) using a single Nvidia Pascal Titan X GPU, which was adequate for conducting interactive human-robot collaboration experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses several challenges in contemporary robot learning and human-robot interaction, offering several areas of significance and potential impact.

Firstly, the most notable significance lies in **enabling robot activity learning from unlabeled human first-person videos**. This approach mitigates the critical issue of data scarcity that often plagues deep learning-based robotics, where collecting vast quantities of robot-specific data is time-consuming and expensive. By leveraging readily available human video demonstrations, the method significantly reduces the overhead for teaching robots new activities.

Secondly, the introduction of a **convolutional future regression network** allows the robot to learn the temporal structure of activities in an unsupervised manner. This means the robot can acquire a foundational understanding of how an activity unfolds over time without requiring explicit activity labels or hand/object annotations in the training videos. This capability moves towards "learning from scratch," which is a departure from previous methods that often relied on pre-defined atomic actions or heavily supervised segmentation of activities.

Thirdly, the system's ability to **predict semantic-level future states**, specifically future hand locations, directly informs the robot's motor control. Unlike some prior video prediction works that focused solely on feature-level predictions, this research bridges the gap to actionable robot behaviors. This proactive predictive capability allows the robot to anticipate the dynamics of an interaction and plan its movements accordingly, which is crucial for fluid and effective human-robot collaboration.

Fourthly, the **demonstration of real-time human-robot collaboration** based on the learned models validates the practical utility of the proposed architecture. The system’s ability to take raw camera input and generate motor commands with acceptable latency highlights its readiness for deployment in dynamic environments. This capability is vital for applications requiring direct human interaction.

The methodology's independence from specific robot hardware for initial perception learning means that the learned temporal models from human videos could potentially be transferred to various robotic platforms, provided a suitable manipulation component is developed or calibrated. This enhances the generalizability of the activity learning process.

Potential impacts of this work include:

- **Accelerated Robot Deployment:** By simplifying the process of teaching robots new tasks, the framework could accelerate the deployment of robots in diverse settings, including manufacturing, logistics, and service industries, where robots need to adapt to changing tasks or environments.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** Robots capable of understanding and anticipating human actions from visual cues could become more intuitive and safer partners in collaborative tasks, leading to more efficient and natural human-robot teams.
- **Assistive Robotics:** In assistive roles, robots could learn to assist individuals with daily tasks by observing human demonstrations, making them more adaptable to user-specific needs and preferences without extensive programming.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By decreasing the reliance on expert human operators for programming or extensive data collection, the approach could lower the development and operational costs associated with robotic systems.

While the current work focuses on location-based hand movements, its foundation suggests a pathway for future research into more complex manipulations involving dynamic hand postures or finer object interactions, further broadening the scope of activities robots can learn.
