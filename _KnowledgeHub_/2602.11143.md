# APEX: Learning Adaptive High-Platform Traversal for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed APEX, a system enabling humanoid robots to adaptively traverse platforms up to 114% of their leg length through learned full-body coordination. The system demonstrates robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, integrating diverse climbing and locomotion skills into a unified perceptive policy.

## Problem

- Existing DRL-based humanoid locomotion methods, primarily feet-based or limited jumping, struggle to safely and reliably overcome platforms significantly exceeding the robot's leg length due to actuator limits and high-impact forces.
- Learning contact-rich, goal-reaching full-body maneuvers (like climbing) is challenging for DRL due to a lack of dense, phase-invariant supervision and conflicts between task rewards and safety regularization.
- Integrating a diverse set of full-body maneuvers (climb-up, climb-down) with continuous locomotion into a unified policy presents a long-horizon decision-making problem, compounded by the sim-to-real perception gap.

## Method

- A two-stage DRL learning pipeline was implemented, first training a library of individual full-body and locomotion skills, then distilling them into a single, unified policy.
- A "generalized ratchet progress reward" was introduced for contact-rich, goal-reaching maneuvers, providing dense, velocity-free, and history-dependent supervision that facilitates safe and stable exploration.
- Policy distillation integrates six heterogeneous skills using a "divide-and-conquer" data sampling strategy, alongside a robust perception pipeline that models simulation artifacts and uses real-world post-processing for LiDAR-based elevation maps.

## Results

- The APEX system demonstrated robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 humanoid, continuously traversing a 0.8m platform (**114%** of leg length) and adapting to varied platform heights, approach angles, and external perturbations.
- In simulation, the unified policy achieved a **95.4%** success rate over 1,000 trials on challenging continuous traversal courses, while individual maneuver policies maintained high success (e.g., **98.8%** for climb-up) within safe force limits.
- Comparative studies showed the ratchet progress reward enabled stable whole-body coordination, while ablation studies confirmed the necessity of the perception robustness pipeline, with policies failing (**0%** success) on hardware without it, but achieving **100%** with the full system.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The ratchet progress reward successfully guides the learning of complex, contact-rich whole-body maneuvers, enabling sustained coordination without conflicting with safety constraints or succumbing to local optima.
> - Robust sim-to-real transfer for dynamic and contact-rich humanoid tasks requires addressing the perception gap through both comprehensive simulation artifact modeling during training and real-time sensor data post-processing in hardware.
> - Multi-teacher policy distillation, coupled with strategic data sampling that covers crucial skill transitions, effectively integrates diverse full-body and locomotion behaviors into a single, context-aware policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robot locomotion has progressed significantly with the application of deep reinforcement learning (DRL), enabling robots to traverse uneven terrain using feet-based methods. Prior research has also explored whole-body jumping for overcoming elevated obstacles. However, these jumping-based solutions typically achieve limited heights (often less than 63% of leg length) and involve high-impact dynamics and large impulsive torques. Such characteristics can exceed actuator limits and pose safety risks, rendering them unsuitable for substantially higher platforms (e.g., exceeding 100% of leg length) in real-world deployment.

For navigating extreme heights, full-body climbing, which coordinates multiple contact points using arms, torso, and legs, offers a more controlled and reliable alternative. Complete traversal of high platforms using this approach necessitates a repertoire of diverse behaviors, including climb-up and climb-down at edges, walking or crawling on the platform surface, and posture reconfigurations such as stand-up and lie-down. Learning and integrating these behaviors within a unified DRL framework present several challenges.

First, contact-rich, goal-reaching maneuvers, unlike cyclic locomotion, lack dense, phase-invariant supervision. Success for these behaviors is defined by achieving terminal conditions through staged contact transitions and whole-body reconfigurations, making traditional velocity-tracking or periodic-contact rewards difficult to apply. Second, achieving complete high-platform traversal is a long-horizon sequential problem demanding autonomous skill selection and seamless transitions. A unified policy must acquire varied skills, infer appropriate behaviors from local terrain and commands, and smoothly switch between them while maintaining stability.

Existing literature on humanoid locomotion largely focuses on feet-only contacts, limiting traversable height. Work on individual full-body maneuvers often requires multi-stage pipelines or extensive task-specific engineering due to conflicts between task rewards and safety regularization. Motion-tracking approaches, while enabling some full-body behaviors, rely on pre-recorded trajectories, which restrict adaptability to novel terrains or initial states. Motion generation models often lack physics-feasibility or real-world validation. Furthermore, policy distillation for legged robots has primarily concentrated on quadrupeds, where skills share similar contact modes and transitions occur near nominal postures. Humanoid multi-skill integration, particularly across heterogeneous behaviors and significantly different postures, poses greater challenges for both reinforcement learning and distillation, requiring compatible terminal-state distributions and context-aware action generation. This work aims to address these identified limitations by developing a comprehensive system for high-platform traversal.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable humanoid robots to robustly and adaptively traverse extremely high platforms in real-world environments, specifically demonstrating capabilities for platforms exceeding 100% of the robot's leg length.

The motivation for this work arises from several limitations in the current state of humanoid robot locomotion:
- **Insufficient Height Traversal Capabilities:** Existing DRL-based locomotion methods, primarily focused on feet-based movements or limited whole-body jumping, are generally unable to safely and reliably overcome platforms that significantly exceed the robot's leg length. Jumping solutions are constrained by actuator limits and generate high-impact forces, making them unsuitable for deployment in environments requiring controlled and safe interaction.
- **Need for Full-Body Coordination:** To overcome extreme heights, a more distributed and controlled approach involving the coordination of the robot's entire body (arms, torso, legs) for multi-contact climbing is necessary. This contrasts with simpler feet-only locomotion.
- **Challenges in Learning Complex Maneuvers:** The required full-body maneuvers (e.g., climb-up, climb-down, stand-up, lie-down) are contact-rich and goal-reaching, making them difficult to learn using traditional DRL reward structures. These maneuvers involve complex, staged contact transitions and whole-body reconfigurations where success is defined by reaching terminal conditions rather than maintaining a constant velocity or periodic pattern. Existing reward designs often fail to provide the dense, phase-invariant supervision needed for efficient learning under safety constraints.
- **Integration of Diverse Skills:** A complete high-platform traversal requires a system that can autonomously select and seamlessly transition between a diverse set of skills—including both goal-reaching full-body maneuvers and continuous locomotion (walking, crawling)—based on local terrain observations and high-level commands. This presents a long-horizon decision-making problem that couples perception, high-level reasoning, and low-level control.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer Challenges:** Real-world deployment of learned policies is often hindered by the sim-to-real gap, particularly concerning perception degradation (e.g., LiDAR artifacts, localization drift) during dynamic and contact-rich maneuvers.

To address these motivations, the APEX system proposes a two-stage learning framework that integrates perceptive, climbing-based behaviors, introduces a novel reward mechanism for complex maneuvers, and employs strategies to bridge the sim-to-real perception gap and facilitate multi-skill integration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The APEX system employs a two-stage learning pipeline to enable adaptive high-platform traversal. This framework comprises training a library of individual skills using Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), followed by distilling these skills into a unified policy.

**A. Ratchet Progress Reward for Humanoid Maneuvers**
The system introduces a "generalized ratchet progress reward" designed for contact-rich, goal-reaching humanoid maneuvers (climb-up, climb-down, stand-up, lie-down).
- **Task Definition:** Each maneuver is defined as a goal-reaching task, where success is achieved when a specific task state `x_t = ϕ(s_t)` surpasses a target task state `x_g`. The task state `x_t` extracts minimal variables (e.g., center of mass position, head position, lower body position, balance margin) to evaluate task completion. For instance, climb-up success is defined by the lower body and center of mass exceeding specific height and horizontal thresholds relative to the platform edge.
- **Ratchet Progress Reward Mechanism:** The core of this reward is a self-updating "best-so-far" task state, `x*_t = max(x_0, x_1..., x_t-1)`. This `x*_t` tracks the maximum progress achieved by the agent up to time `t-1`. The binary reward `r_t` is `0` if the current task state `x_t` strictly surpasses `x*_t`, and `-1` otherwise. This structure provides:
 - **Dense task-aligned supervision:** Penalizes any step that does not advance beyond the historical best, guiding exploration towards task completion.
 - **Velocity-free progress:** Does not incentivize rapid movement, supporting patient, contact-aware exploration and allowing effective regularization of safety constraints (e.g., impact, torque) without conflicting with task objectives.
 - **History dependence:** Prevents "retracing" exploits where the agent oscillates to accumulate reward by making incremental but non-genuine progress.

**B. Learning Perceptive Full-Body Maneuvers**
The framework for learning individual full-body maneuver skills (climb-up, climb-down, stand-up, lie-down) utilizes a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with specific observation, action, and reward components.
- **RL Training Environment:**
 - **State, Observation, and Action:** Observations include robot proprioception (gravity vector, base angular velocity, joint positions/velocities) and the previous action, both provided as a 5-step history. The current task state `x_t` is also an input. For climbing skills (climb-up, climb-down), a LiDAR-based local elevation map (`m_t`, 1x1 m² area at 0.05m resolution) is additionally provided. The actor network outputs target joint positions, tracked by a low-level PD controller. The critic also receives `x*_t` as input to account for history dependence.
 - **Simulation Environment:** Training is conducted in a simulation environment with extensive randomization of terrain configurations (platform height [0.55m, 0.85m]), initial robot pose (distance to platform, yaw angle), and comprehensive domain randomization techniques from prior work to enhance sim-to-real transfer. Symmetric augmentation is applied to reduce handedness bias.
 - **Initial Posture Sampling:** To facilitate seamless skill transitions, initial joint angles for each skill are sampled by perturbing predefined nominal postures (standing or prone) that are compatible with the terminal states of preceding skills. A terminal-pose reward (described below) is used to shape the terminal behavior of full-body maneuvers towards these nominal postures. If necessary, initial-state distributions of successor skills are augmented.
- **Reward Design:** The total reward `r` is a sum of five components:
 - `r_alive`: Constant survival bonus.
 - `r_reg`: Standard regularization penalties for smooth and energy-efficient motions.
 - `r_force`: A contact-force penalty that increases exponentially if contact forces (`F_t`) exceed a safety threshold (`F_limit`), with `F_limit=0` for the head link to prevent perception degradation.
 - `r_task`: The generalized ratchet progress reward.
 - `r_tp`: A terminal-pose reward activated only after the goal is reached and near the end of an episode, encouraging the robot to adopt a desired terminal joint configuration (e.g., standing or prone) for smooth transitions.
- **Robust Perception via Elevation Mapping:** A LiDAR-based elevation mapping pipeline is used. To mitigate the sim-to-real perception gap caused by dynamic maneuvers, a dual strategy is employed:
 - **Simulation Artifact Modeling:** Gaussian noise, spatial offsets (localization drift), and synthetic outlier clusters are injected into elevation maps during training to improve robustness to real-world perceptual degradations.
 - **Real-World Post-Processing:** Spatial outlier filtering and an inpainting algorithm are applied to raw LiDAR maps at deployment to reconstruct missing regions and suppress noise, providing a coherent terrain representation to the policy.

**C. Policy Distillation for Skill Integration**
All six individual policies (four full-body maneuvers and two cyclic locomotion skills) are integrated into a single, unified policy through a distillation pipeline.
- **Pipeline:** Behavior Cloning (BC) is used for initial student policy pretraining, followed by DAgger-style data aggregation for refinement, optimizing the policy via Mean Squared Error (MSE) between student and teacher actions.
- **Construction of Data Distribution:** A "divide-and-conquer" data sampling strategy is adopted. Instead of sequential rollouts, training environments are dedicated to either a single core skill or combinations of two consecutive skills (e.g., "Walk + Climb-up"). This ensures a balanced dataset across all teachers and covers crucial skill transitions, preventing data imbalance issues.
- **Data Augmentation Strategy:** The student policy is trained with the same randomization suite used for teacher training, including domain randomization, physical perturbations, and perceptual artifacts. Initial states for skills are sampled from broader distributions to maximize state-space coverage, and action noise and symmetry augmentation are applied to further improve robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both qualitative and quantitative evaluations of the APEX system, demonstrating its performance in simulation and real-world deployment on a 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

**A. Performance of Context-Aware Continuous Traversal**
- **Simulation Performance:** The system was evaluated on three challenging simulation courses (Continuous Traversing, Ascending, Descending) requiring long-horizon, multi-skill execution without resets. Despite environmental perturbations and LiDAR degradation, the policy achieved a 95.4% success rate over 1,000 trials, demonstrating stable and context-aware traversal.
- **Zero-shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The learned policy successfully transferred to hardware without additional real-world training. The robot autonomously executed a continuous full-loop traversal of a 0.8 m platform (approximately 114% of its leg length), coordinating walking, climb-up, stand-up, lie-down, and climb-down skills. The policy exhibited adaptive motor strategies, such as selecting different lead legs based on the approach angle to the platform.
- **Robust Skill Transitions under Perturbations:** The robot demonstrated robustness to external disturbances; when heavily kicked while approaching a platform, it adapted by adjusting its gait and switching its pivoting leg to stabilize and initiate climbing, indicating the policy's ability to execute skill transitions from near-failure states.

**B. Robustness and Adaptability to Environmental Variations**
- **Individual Maneuver Robustness (Simulation):** Specialized teacher policies for climb-up, climb-down, stand-up, and lie-down achieved high success rates in simulation (e.g., 98.8% for climb-up, 100% for lie-down) over 1,000 trials, while maintaining maximum contact forces within safe operational limits.
- **Generalization Across Real-World Configurations (Hardware):**
 - The climb-up policy consistently achieved high success rates on hardware across varied platform heights (0.6 m to 0.8 m) and approach angles (-45° to 45°). It also generalized to extreme, out-of-distribution approach angles of up to ±65°, adapting its whole-body strategy (e.g., reorienting torso, leveraging full-body motion) rather than executing a fixed movement.
 - Other full-body maneuvers (climb-down, stand-up, lie-down) also achieved a 5/5 success rate in hardware tests.
- **Robustness to Varying Contact Properties:** The climbing policy successfully navigated a soft vinyl-foam mat on the target platform, demonstrating stability despite unseen compliance and friction properties. This resilience is attributed to the policy's quasi-static, contact-rich motion patterns that distribute loads across multiple contact points.
- **Symmetry Augmentation:** The use of symmetry augmentation resulted in a balanced strategy, allowing the policy to dynamically select the lead leg during climb-up based on the robot’s relative heading.

**C. Skill Acquisition via Progress Rewards**
A comparative study evaluating the climb-up skill acquisition with the proposed ratchet progress reward against several baseline reward formulations demonstrated the efficacy of APEX's approach:
- **Baseline Failures:**
 - **Velocity-tracking:** Policies failed to complete climb-up, becoming stuck at the platform edge due to over-constraining motions to a fixed forward speed.
 - **Random Network Distillation (RND):** Policies failed due to a lack of structured guidance for multi-stage coordination, exploring irrelevant states without directed progress.
 - **Distance-minimization:** Policies either exhibited strong velocity biases that conflicted with safety regularization or, with reduced regularization, resulted in aggressive "full-body jumping" with impulsive contacts and excessive joint torques, deemed unsafe for real-world deployment.
 - **Direction/Increment-based rewards:** Policies converged to local optima at the platform edge, exhibiting repetitive "back-and-forth" motions without committing to the full maneuver, as they could accumulate reward by cyclically resetting position.
- **APEX Success:** The ratchet progress reward facilitated the learning of sustained whole-body coordination, enabling the robot to modulate contact locations and force distribution. This resulted in stable climb-up behaviors with significantly reduced peak forces suitable for hardware deployment. Hardware measurements showed that the policy learned to incorporate deliberate pauses (plateaus in torso horizontal displacement) for kinematic feasibility and stability (e.g., securely placing a lead leg before resuming forward progression).

**D. Effectiveness of Multi-Teacher Distillation**
The distilled student policy achieved comparable success rates and statistically similar maximum contact forces to the individual specialized teacher policies across all four contact-rich full-body maneuvers in simulation. This indicates that the unified student policy successfully captured the diverse behaviors of the experts while maintaining performance near teacher levels.

**E. Robustness to Perception Artifacts**
- The full system demonstrated 100% success for climb-up in real-world environments even with severe LiDAR artifacts (e.g., "ghost points" forming fake obstacles).
- Ablation studies showed that disabling real-time filtering and inpainting in the real world reduced success rates to 60%. Critically, policies trained *without* simulated sensor drift and outlier corruption failed entirely (0% success) on hardware, underscoring the necessity of simulation artifact modeling and real-time map reconstruction for robust real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The APEX system presents several significant contributions and has potential impacts on humanoid robotics:

- **Expanded Traversal Capabilities:** This work extends the terrain traversability of humanoid robots to extreme heights (up to 114% of leg length), a capability previously limited by the safety and mechanical constraints of feet-based locomotion and jumping solutions. This significantly broadens the operational envelope for humanoids in complex, elevated, and unstructured environments.

- **Novel Reward Formulation for Complex Maneuvers:** The introduction of the generalized ratchet progress reward provides a method for learning complex, contact-rich, and goal-reaching behaviors with DRL. Its velocity-free and history-dependent nature offers dense, task-aligned supervision that facilitates efficient exploration while allowing for strong safety regularization, avoiding common failure modes associated with instantaneous or distance-based rewards. This reward mechanism may be applicable to a broader range of robotic tasks requiring multi-stage, contact-rich interactions.

- **Integrated, Context-Aware Multi-Skill System:** APEX develops a comprehensive framework that integrates a diverse library of six terrain-conditioned skills—four complex full-body maneuvers (climb-up, climb-down, stand-up, lie-down) and two cyclic locomotion skills (walking, crawling)—into a single, perceptive policy. This unified controller autonomously selects behaviors and transitions based on LiDAR-based elevation maps and commands, representing a step towards more versatile and autonomous humanoid operation.

- **Robust Real-World Deployment:** The successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a 29-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid robot, along with demonstrated robustness to variations in platform height, approach angle, external perturbations, and unseen contact properties, highlights the practical utility and reliability of the APEX system. The dual strategy for bridging the sim-to-real perception gap (simulation artifact modeling and real-world post-processing) is also a key enabler for this robust deployment.

- **Advancements in Humanoid Control and Learning:** By addressing the fundamental challenges of learning contact-rich maneuvers and integrating heterogeneous skills, APEX contributes to the development of safer, more agile, and adaptable humanoid robot controllers. The methodology has the potential to influence future research in learning complex manipulation, locomotion, and interaction behaviors for humanoid and other complex robotic systems.
