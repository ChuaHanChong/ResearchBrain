# Sample Complexity of Robust Reinforcement Learning with a Generative Model

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the Robust Empirical Value Iteration (REVI) algorithm for learning robust policies in Robust Markov Decision Processes (RMDPs) when the underlying model is unknown. It provides the first finite-sample guarantees for REVI, characterizing sample complexity for Total Variation, Chi-square, and Kullback-Leibler uncertainty sets, and analytically demonstrates that non-robust policies can perform substantially worse than optimal robust policies by a factor of {93} (1/(1-{94})).

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulators often perform poorly in real-world systems due to the "simulation-to-reality gap" caused by model mismatches.
- The Robust Markov Decision Process (RMDP) framework offers robustness to uncertainties but typically assumes complete knowledge of the system model, which is often not available.
- Existing robust reinforcement learning algorithms that learn robust policies without explicit model knowledge largely lack provable, non-asymptotic finite-sample performance guarantees.

## Method

- The Robust Empirical Value Iteration (**REVI**) algorithm is proposed, which uses a generative model to obtain N samples for each state-action pair to estimate the nominal transition model.
- An empirical uncertainty set is constructed based on this estimated model, and robust value iteration is performed using this empirical model.
- The algorithm outputs an approximately optimal robust policy derived after K iterations of the empirical robust value iteration.

## Results

- REVI achieves finite-sample bounds for learning an {94}-optimal robust policy with sample complexities of O({93}|S|^2 |A|/((1-{94})^4 {94}^2)) for TV, O({93}c_r |S|^2 |A|/((1-{94})^4 {94}^2)) for Chi-square, and O({93}|S|^2 |A| exp(1/(1-{94}))/(c_r^2 (1-{94})^4 {94}^2)) for KL uncertainty sets.
- Empirical evaluations on the Gambler's Problem and FrozenLake8x8 show REVI policies converge to the optimal robust value and consistently exhibit superior robustness to environmental parameter deviations compared to non-robust policies.
- The derived sample complexity bounds are higher than those for non-robust RL (e.g., O({93}|S||A|/((1-{94})^3 {94}^2))), specifically with an additional exponential factor for the KL uncertainty set.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Finite-sample guarantees for robust reinforcement learning can be derived across various uncertainty sets (TV, Chi-square, KL divergence), quantifying the data required to learn robust policies.
> - Analytical techniques involving covering numbers and specific bounds for the non-linear robust Bellman operator are essential to establish uniform concentration for robust value functions.
> - A quantitative "robustness gap" of {93} (1/(1-{94})) formally demonstrates that non-robust policies can have arbitrarily poor worst-case performance compared to optimal robust policies, highlighting the necessity of robust design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) typically necessitates extensive data collection for policy training, a process that can be costly and potentially hazardous when conducted directly on real-world systems. A common practice to mitigate this is to train RL algorithms on simulators and subsequently transfer the learned policies to physical systems. However, this approach frequently encounters the "simulation-to-reality gap," where discrepancies arise between the simulator model and the real-world environment due to factors such as approximation errors, parameter drift, or adversarial disturbances. Such mismatches can significantly impair the real-world performance of simulator-trained policies.

The Robust Markov Decision Process (RMDP) framework was developed to address this challenge by enabling the computation of control policies that are robust to parameter uncertainties. Initiated by Iyengar (2005) and Nilim and El Ghaoui (2005), the RMDP literature has extensively explored various uncertainty sets and efficient computational algorithms. However, these prior works primarily focus on the *planning problem*, which assumes complete knowledge of the system model.

Robust RL algorithms aim to learn optimal robust policies when the underlying system model is unknown. Existing robust RL approaches, such as those by Roy et al. (2017) and Panaganti and Kalathil (2021), have largely provided only *asymptotic convergence guarantees*. Concurrently, several deep RL methods have explored robust RL empirically (e.g., Pinto et al., 2017; Derman et al., 2018, 2020; Mankowitz et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020a), but these typically lack formal theoretical guarantees for the learned policies. The broader landscape therefore indicates a gap in robust RL research, specifically concerning algorithms with *provable, non-asymptotic finite-sample performance guarantees*.

This work positions itself to address this identified gap. It extends the theoretical understanding of robust RL by providing finite-sample guarantees, a aspect that has received limited attention. While a concurrent work (Yang et al., 2021) also investigates similar bounds, this paper differentiates itself through its distinct proof techniques and the provision of open-source experimental results. Furthermore, while Zhou et al. (2021) analyzed sample complexity for KL uncertainty sets, this work expands the analysis to Total Variation (TV) and Chi-square uncertainty sets, and provides a more explicit characterization of the exponential dependence on the discount factor for KL uncertainty. The authors also distinguish their RMDP framework from classical robust control theory and related areas like risk-sensitive or adversarial RL, noting fundamental differences in approach.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical challenges encountered when deploying reinforcement learning agents in real-world environments. The inevitable "simulation-to-reality gap," characterized by model mismatches between simulators and physical systems, can severely degrade the performance of policies trained in simulation. The Robust Markov Decision Process (RMDP) framework offers a theoretical foundation for designing policies that are resilient to such uncertainties. However, traditional RMDP formulations assume full knowledge of the system model, which is often unavailable in practical scenarios. Existing robust reinforcement learning methods, which learn robust policies without explicit model knowledge, typically lack formal guarantees regarding their finite-sample performance. This absence of provable non-asymptotic bounds makes it difficult to ascertain the reliability and data efficiency of such algorithms.

Given this context, the key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Develop a Model-Based Robust RL Algorithm with Finite-Sample Guarantees:** The central objective is to propose a novel model-based reinforcement learning algorithm capable of learning an approximately optimal robust policy. A critical aspect is to provide provable, non-asymptotic finite-sample performance guarantees, specifically expressed in terms of sample complexity within a Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) framework. This addresses the limitation of previous works that offer only asymptotic convergence results or lack theoretical guarantees altogether.

2. **Characterize Sample Complexity for Diverse Uncertainty Sets:** The research aims to provide a precise characterization of the sample complexity for the proposed algorithm across three distinct and commonly used forms of uncertainty sets. These include uncertainty sets defined by the Total Variation (TV) distance, the Chi-square divergence, and the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. The motivation here is to understand how the sample requirements for learning a robust policy vary depending on the mathematical structure of the uncertainty about the transition dynamics. The algorithm assumes access to a generative model that can provide samples from a nominal simulator model, a standard assumption in theoretical RL.

3. **Provide a Formal Justification for Robust Policies:** Beyond algorithmic development and theoretical guarantees, a significant objective is to offer an analytical argument for the necessity of employing robust policies. The paper aims to formally demonstrate that the worst-case performance of a non-robust policy (i.e., one optimized solely for a nominal model) can be arbitrarily poor, potentially matching the performance of a random policy, when compared to the performance of an optimal robust policy. This objective seeks to provide a quantitative answer to the qualitative understanding that robust policies are beneficial in uncertain environments.

4. **Empirically Validate Algorithm Performance:** The final objective is to demonstrate the practical performance and theoretical properties of the proposed algorithm through numerical experiments. This involves illustrating its convergence behavior with respect to both the number of iterations and the number of samples, and showcasing its robustness against changes in environment parameters on benchmark problems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on developing a model-based robust reinforcement learning algorithm and rigorously analyzing its finite-sample performance.

**Problem Formulation:**
The work considers a finite Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple (S, A, r, P, γ), where S is the finite state space, A is the finite action space, r is the bounded reward function (assumed in), P is the transition probability function, and γ ∈ (0,1) is the discount factor. The core distinction from standard MDPs lies in the Robust Markov Decision Process (RMDP) framework, which considers an *uncertainty set* P of possible transition models. This uncertainty set is typically defined as a collection of transition probabilities P_{s,a} for each state-action pair (s,a) that lie within a certain distance c_r from a known *nominal model* P^o_{s,a}. Three types of distance metrics D(·, ·) are investigated for defining P_{s,a}:
1. **Total Variation (TV) distance:** $D_{tv}(P_{s,a}, P^o_{s,a}) = (1/2)||P_{s,a} - P^o_{s,a}||_1 \le c_r$.
2. **Chi-square distance:** $D_c(P_{s,a}, P^o_{s,a}) = \sum_{s'} (P_{s,a}(s') - P^o_{s,a}(s'))^2 / P^o_{s,a}(s') \le c_r$.
3. **Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence:** $D_{kl}(P_{s,a}, P^o_{s,a}) = \sum_{s'} P_{s,a}(s') \log(P_{s,a}(s') / P^o_{s,a}(s')) \le c_r$.

The objective in RMDP is to find an optimal robust policy $\pi^*$ that maximizes the value function under the *worst-case* model within the uncertainty set, i.e., $V^* = \sup_\pi \inf_{P \in \mathcal{P}} V_{\pi,P}$. This is solved through a robust value iteration based on the robust Bellman operator $T(V)(s) = \max_a (r(s,a) + \gamma \sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}(V))$, where $\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}(v) = \inf\{u^T v: u \in \mathcal{B}\}$ for a set $\mathcal{B}$. The operator $T$ is a contraction mapping, ensuring convergence to $V^*$.

**Robust Empirical Value Iteration (REVI) Algorithm:**
The central contribution is the REVI algorithm, designed for scenarios where the nominal model $P^o$ is unknown. The algorithm assumes access to a *generative model* that can sample next states according to $P^o_{s,a}$ for any given $(s,a)$ pair. The steps are:
1. **Model Estimation:** For each $(s,a)$ pair, $N$ samples of the next state are drawn from the generative model. A maximum likelihood estimate $\hat{P}^o_{s,a}(s') = N(s,a,s')/N$ is computed, where $N(s,a,s')$ is the count of transitions to $s'$.
2. **Empirical Uncertainty Set Construction:** An empirical uncertainty set $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$ is formed using $\hat{P}^o$ and the given robustness parameter $c_r$, following the same structure as the true uncertainty set (8).
3. **Empirical Robust Value Iteration:** The algorithm then performs a robust value iteration using the empirical model $\hat{\mathcal{P}}$. Specifically, starting with $Q^0 = 0$, it iteratively computes $Q^{k+1}(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \sigma_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{s,a}}(V^k)$, where $V^k(s) = \max_a Q^k(s,a)$.
4. **Policy Output:** After $K$ iterations, the algorithm outputs the policy $\pi_K(s) = \arg\max_a Q_K(s,a)$.

**Sample Complexity Analysis:**
The core of the theoretical analysis lies in establishing finite-sample guarantees for REVI. The main challenge is bounding the error $\lVert V^* - V_{\pi_K} \rVert$. This error is decomposed into three terms: $\lVert V^* - \hat{V}^* \rVert + \lVert \hat{V}^* - \hat{V}_{\pi_K} \rVert + \lVert \hat{V}_{\pi_K} - V_{\pi_K} \rVert$.
- The term $\lVert \hat{V}^* - \hat{V}_{\pi_K} \rVert$ (error due to finite iterations on the empirical model) is bounded using the contraction property of the robust Bellman operator, similar to standard RL analysis.
- The terms $\lVert V^* - \hat{V}^* \rVert$ (error between true and empirical optimal robust values) and $\lVert \hat{V}_{\pi_K} - V_{\pi_K} \rVert$ (error between empirical and true value of the learned policy) are more complex due to the non-linear nature of the $\sigma(\cdot)$ operator. Unlike linear expectations, $\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{\hat{P}}(V)] \ne \sigma_P(V)$, making standard concentration inequalities difficult to apply directly.
 - **Solution Strategy:** The authors develop specific upper bounds for $|\sigma_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{s,a}}(V) - \sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}(V)|$ for each uncertainty set.
 - For **TV uncertainty**, the error is bounded by a term involving $\max_{\mu} |\hat{P}_{s,a}\mu - P^o_{s,a}\mu|$.
 - For **Chi-square uncertainty**, the error involves differences in means and square roots of variances.
 - For **KL uncertainty**, the error is bounded by $\max_\lambda |\lambda \log(\hat{P}_{s,a} \exp(-V/\lambda) / P^o_{s,a} \exp(-V/\lambda))|$, leveraging results from (Zhou et al., 2021) to manage the non-linearity.
 - **Uniform Concentration:** To bound the maximum over various functions (e.g., $\mu$ or $\lambda$), a *covering number argument* is employed (Lemma 3). This technique allows for uniform concentration bounds over function classes but introduces an additional $|\mathcal{S}|$ factor in the sample complexity compared to non-robust settings. For Chi-square, a self-bounding variance inequality (Lemma 6) is used.

**Analytical Argument for Robust Policies:**
To formally justify the need for robust policies, the authors construct a specific two-state, two-action MDP with a binary uncertainty set (Theorem 4). By analyzing the performance of a non-robust optimal policy versus an optimal robust policy in this construction, they analytically demonstrate a "robustness gap" of $\Omega(1/(1-\gamma))$, quantifying the potential performance degradation of non-robust policies.

**Experimental Evaluation:**
The algorithm's performance is demonstrated on two benchmark problems: the Gambler's Problem and the FrozenLake8x8 environment from OpenAI Gym. The experiments evaluate:
1. **Convergence rate:** How quickly the policy value approaches the optimal robust value as the number of iterations (k) and samples (N) increases.
2. **Robustness:** The performance of the learned REVI policy under varying environment parameters, comparing it against the optimal robust policy and a non-robust policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents a model-based robust reinforcement learning algorithm, Robust Empirical Value Iteration (REVI), and provides theoretical and empirical analyses of its performance.

**Sample Complexity Characterization:**
The primary theoretical contributions are the precise characterizations of the sample complexity required for REVI to learn an $\epsilon$-optimal robust policy with high probability (at least $1-2\delta$). These bounds are derived for three distinct uncertainty sets:
- **Total Variation (TV) Uncertainty Set (Theorem 1):** The total number of samples required is of order $O(\frac{|S|^2 |A|}{(1-\gamma)^4 \epsilon^2})$.
- **Chi-square Uncertainty Set (Theorem 2):** The total number of samples required is of order $O(\frac{c_r |S|^2 |A|}{(1-\gamma)^4 \epsilon^2})$, where $c_r$ is the robustness level parameter.
- **Kullback-Leibler (KL) Uncertainty Set (Theorem 3):** The total number of samples required is of order $O(\frac{|S|^2 |A| \exp(1/(1-\gamma))}{c_r^2 (1-\gamma)^4 \epsilon^2})$. This work explicitly clarifies the exponential dependence on $1/(1-\gamma)$, a factor previously subsumed within a constant in related literature (Zhou et al., 2021).

**Comparison with Non-Robust RL:**
A comparison with the best-known sample complexity results for non-robust RL (e.g., $O(\frac{|S||A|}{(1-\gamma)^3 \epsilon^2})$) reveals that the REVI algorithm's sample complexity is higher by factors of $|S|$ and $1/(1-\gamma)$. For the KL uncertainty set, an additional exponential factor of $\exp(1/(1-\gamma))$ is observed. The authors suggest that the additional $|S|$ factor arises from the covering number argument used in the proof for uniform concentration, acknowledging that it is not yet clear if this is a fundamental limitation inherent to robust RL with TV uncertainty.

**Analytical Justification for Robust Policies ("Robustness Gap"):**
Theorem 4 provides a formal analytical argument demonstrating the quantitative advantage of robust policies. It shows that for certain RMDPs, the worst-case performance of a non-robust policy (one optimized for a specific nominal model) can be substantially lower than that of an optimal robust policy. Specifically, the gap in value can be $\Omega(1/(1-\gamma))$. Given that the maximum possible value function is $1/(1-\gamma)$, this implies that a non-robust policy's worst-case performance can be as poor as that of an arbitrary, non-optimal policy in an order-of-magnitude sense. This result provides a theoretical foundation for the practical need for robust policy design.

**Empirical Performance:**
Experiments conducted on the Gambler's Problem and FrozenLake8x8 environments support the theoretical findings:
- **Convergence:** The REVI algorithm demonstrates fast convergence with respect to the number of iterations, consistent with the contraction property of the robust Bellman operator. As the number of samples (N) increases, the value function of the learned policy converges to the optimal robust value function, confirming the finite-sample guarantees.
- **Robustness:** Policies learned by REVI consistently exhibit superior robustness when environment parameters deviate from the nominal model. For instance, in the Gambler's Problem, the REVI-learned policy maintains a higher winning ratio under varying coin biases compared to a non-robust policy. Similarly, in FrozenLake8x8, it performs more reliably against action perturbations. The performance of the REVI-learned policy closely approximates that of the optimal robust policy for sufficiently large sample sizes.
- **Hyperparameter Exploration:** The study also briefly explores the impact of the robustness parameter $c_r$, showing that an optimal value can be determined to maximize performance across a range of environmental disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the theoretical foundations of robust reinforcement learning by providing the first (or among the first) provable finite-sample performance guarantees for learning robust policies. This moves beyond prior work that largely offered asymptotic convergence results or empirical demonstrations without strong theoretical underpinnings.

The **theoretical significance** lies in:
- **Formalizing Data Requirements:** The derived sample complexity bounds for TV, Chi-square, and KL uncertainty sets provide a quantitative understanding of the data needed to learn approximately optimal robust policies. This is crucial for practitioners and researchers to assess the feasibility and cost of deploying robust RL in real-world applications.
- **Advancing RL Theory:** The analytical techniques developed to handle the non-linearity of the robust Bellman operator and to derive uniform concentration bounds using covering arguments represent an advancement in the theoretical toolkit for analyzing complex RL algorithms.
- **Justifying Robustness:** The formal proof of the "robustness gap" offers a rigorous analytical justification for the qualitative observation that robust policies are necessary in uncertain environments. This provides a fundamental theoretical argument for the value of robust design in decision-making under uncertainty.

The **potential practical impact** of this work is substantial, particularly in domains where model mismatch is a critical concern:
- **Reliable Autonomous Systems:** In areas such as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and critical infrastructure control, where discrepancies between simulation models and reality are inevitable and potentially dangerous, REVI offers a principled approach to learn policies that maintain performance guarantees under these uncertainties. This could lead to more trustworthy and deployable RL agents.
- **Informed Algorithm Design:** The quantitative insights from the sample complexity bounds can guide the development of more efficient robust RL algorithms. Understanding how sample requirements scale with state/action space size, discount factor, and the desired level of robustness and accuracy can help engineers optimize resource allocation during policy training.
- **Foundation for Extensions:** By establishing fundamental theoretical guarantees for finite state and action spaces, this work provides a solid basis for future research. The authors identify extensions to robust RL with linear and nonlinear function approximation, and to deep RL architectures, as important next steps. These extensions are critical for scaling robust RL to real-world problems with large or continuous state/action spaces.

While acknowledging that the sample complexity bounds obtained are higher than those for non-robust RL, the work opens avenues for further investigation into potential refinements of these bounds and the fundamental limits of robust RL. Overall, this research significantly enhances the theoretical understanding of robust reinforcement learning, laying groundwork for more robust and reliable real-world RL applications.
