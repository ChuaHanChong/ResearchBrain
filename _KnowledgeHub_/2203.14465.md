# STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner) algorithm enables large language models to iteratively improve their reasoning capabilities by generating and filtering their own rationales, overcoming the need for extensive human-annotated datasets. This method, which includes a novel 'rationalization' step, allowed a 6B-parameter GPT-J model to achieve performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and math word problems comparable to or surpassing much larger state-of-the-art models.

## Problem

- Acquiring large, high-quality datasets of human-annotated rationales is prohibitively expensive and often infeasible for training LLMs in step-by-step reasoning.
- Existing methods like few-shot in-context learning for rationale generation often lead to suboptimal performance compared to fine-tuning on extensive rationale datasets.
- LLMs typically receive no direct training signal for problems they fail to solve, limiting their ability to learn from mistakes in complex reasoning tasks.

## Method

- **STaR** (Self-Taught Reasoner) is an ==iterative bootstrapping algorithm== where a pre-trained LLM generates rationales for a problem set, and only those leading to correct answers are used to fine-tune the model.
- The algorithm incorporates ==rationalization==, which involves prompting the model with failed problems and their correct answers as hints, encouraging it to generate rationales that justify the correct solution.
- The model is repeatedly fine-tuned on the combined dataset of self-generated correct rationales and rationalized correct answers, leading to continuous improvement in rationale generation and task performance.

## Results

- On arithmetic tasks, STaR achieved **89.5%** accuracy, significantly outperforming a baseline fine-tuned directly on answers (**76.3%**) and showing improved generalization to unseen digit lengths.
- For CommonsenseQA, STaR with GPT-J achieved **72.5%** accuracy, comparable to a **30x** larger GPT-3 model (**73.0%**) and outperforming a 137B LaMDA model prompted with few-shot CoT (**55.6%**).
- STaR generated rationales were perceived as higher quality than few-shot prompts and some human-generated rationales, indicating an improved coherence in reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs can iteratively improve their reasoning capabilities by generating and filtering their own rationales, effectively creating a self-improving loop.
> - Providing the correct answer as a 'hint' (rationalization) for initially failed problems significantly boosts the learning process, allowing the model to 'reason backward' and expand its training data.
> - The approach enables smaller LLMs (e.g., GPT-J 6B) to achieve reasoning performance comparable to or exceeding much larger models (e.g., GPT-3, LaMDA) that lack this self-improvement mechanism.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

#### 1.1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of large language models, explicit reasoning (often termed "Chain-of-Thought" or CoT), and self-improvement algorithms. Recent advancements have highlighted that LLMs generate explicit intermediate reasoning steps (rationales or "scratchpads") before providing a final answer can dramatically boost performance on complex reasoning tasks, ranging from mathematics to commonsense question-answering. This approach, exemplified by "Chain-of-Thought prompting" and "scratchpads", suggests that modeling human-like step-by-step thinking improves LLM capabilities and generalization.

However, existing methods for inducing such rationale generation face significant challenges. One common approach involves collecting vast datasets of human-annotated rationales, which is prohibitively expensive and often infeasible for new problem domains. Alternatively, automatically generated rationales through hand-crafted templates are limited to scenarios where general solutions are already known. A third method, few-shot in-context learning, allows LLMs to generate rationales by including a few examples in the prompt. While this improves accuracy compared to direct prompting, it generally underperforms models fine-tuned on large datasets that directly predict answers. STaR aims to bridge this gap by enabling models to *generate* their own large rationale datasets from a small initial set of examples.

The paper also draws parallels to "iterated learning" and "self-play" algorithms, such as Expert Iteration (ExIt), where a model iteratively improves by generating solutions and learning from successful outcomes. STaR's mechanism of filtering correct rationales and fine-tuning on them can be seen as an approximation of a policy gradient objective from reinforcement learning, where the model is implicitly rewarded for generating rationales that lead to correct answers. While previous work has explored curriculum learning or modular networks for self-improvement, STaR distinguishes itself by focusing on bootstrapping reasoning *within* the LLM's inherent language modeling capacity, without requiring external theorem provers or separate value functions.

#### 1.2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind STaR is to overcome the critical bottleneck of acquiring large, high-quality rationale datasets, which are essential for training LLMs to perform complex, step-by-step reasoning. The authors observe that current methods for inducing rationale generation are either costly (manual annotation) or yield suboptimal performance (few-shot prompting).

The key objectives of this research are:
1. **Bootstrapping Rationale Generation:** To develop a scalable and iterative technique that allows an LLM to generate its own high-quality rationales, starting from a minimal set of rationale examples. This aims to automatically expand a small initial rationale prompt set into a comprehensive fine-tuning dataset.
2. **Enhancing Reasoning Performance:** To demonstrate that this self-generated rationale dataset can significantly improve the LLM's performance on diverse reasoning tasks, including symbolic reasoning (arithmetic), natural language commonsense reasoning (CommonsenseQA), and mathematical word problems (GSM8K).
3. **Efficiency and Accessibility:** To enable smaller, more accessible LLMs (like GPT-J) to achieve performance comparable to or surpassing much larger, state-of-the-art models on these reasoning tasks, thereby democratizing access to high-quality reasoning capabilities in LLMs.
4. **Addressing Training Signal Limitations:** To introduce "rationalization" – a novel component designed to provide a training signal for problems that the model initially fails to solve, allowing it to "reason backward" from the correct answer and learn from its mistakes.

In essence, STaR seeks to create a synergistic loop where improved rationale generation leads to better training data, which in turn further enhances the model's ability to reason, allowing the LLM to iteratively "teach itself" increasingly complex reasoning skills.

### 2. Methodology and Approach

#### 2.1. The STaR Algorithm

The Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) is an iterative bootstrapping method designed to enable large language models (LLMs) to learn to generate high-quality rationales for complex problems. The core of the STaR algorithm operates through a simple, repetitive loop:

1. **Initialization:** The process begins with a pre-trained LLM, denoted as \(M\), and a dataset of problems \(D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^D\), where \(x_i\) is a problem and \(y_i\) is its ground-truth answer. A small initial "prompt set" \(P = \{(x_p, r_p, y_p)\}_{p=1}^P\) consisting of a few examples with intermediate rationales is also required.

2. **Rationale Generation:** In each outer loop iteration \(n\), the current model \(M_{n-1}\) (initially the pre-trained model \(M_0 = M\)) is prompted with the few-shot examples from \(P\). For every problem \(x_i\) in the dataset \(D\), the model attempts to generate a rationale \(\hat{r}_i\) followed by a final answer \(\hat{y}_i\).

3. **Filtering Correct Solutions:** The key principle is to assume that rationales leading to correct answers are of higher quality. Therefore, only those generated rationales \(\hat{r}_i\) for which the predicted answer \(\hat{y}_i\) matches the ground-truth answer \(y_i\) are retained. These form a new dataset \(D_n = \{(x_i, \hat{r}_i, y_i) \mid \hat{y}_i = y_i\}\).

4. **Fine-tuning:** The original pre-trained LLM \(M\) (not \(M_{n-1}\)) is then fine-tuned on this newly constructed dataset \(D_n\). This fine-tuning step produces the improved model \(M_n\), which will be used in the next iteration for rationale generation. The authors explicitly state that they retrain from the original pre-trained model each time, rather than continually training the same model, to mitigate overfitting.

5. **Iteration:** Steps 2-4 are repeated for a set number of iterations or until performance plateaus. This creates a synergistic cycle: as the model improves its rationale generation, the quality and quantity of the filtered training data increase, leading to further model improvements.

#### 2.2. The Role of Rationalization

The basic STaR algorithm, while effective, has a limitation: it only learns from problems the model *correctly* solves. If the model fails to solve a problem, it receives no direct training signal for that instance. To address this, the authors introduce a crucial technique called "rationalization."

1. **Hint-based Rationale Generation:** For problems that the model failed to answer correctly during the initial rationale generation step (\(\hat{y}_i \neq y_i\)), the model is prompted again. This time, however, it is provided with the correct answer \(y_i\) as a "hint" within the prompt. The model is then tasked with generating a rationale \(\hat{r}^{\text{rat}}_i\) that justifies this *given* correct answer. This "reasoning backward" from the answer often makes it easier for the model to produce a coherent and useful rationale.

2. **Filtering Rationalized Solutions:** Similar to the initial rationale generation, only those rationalized rationales \(\hat{r}^{\text{rat}}_i\) that successfully lead to the correct answer \(y_i\) (even if hinted) are included. This forms a supplementary dataset \(D^{\text{rat}}_n = \{(x_i, \hat{r}^{\text{rat}}_i, y_i) \mid \hat{y}_i \neq y_i \land \hat{y}^{\text{rat}}_i = y_i\}\). It's important to note that when these rationalized examples are used for fine-tuning, the explicit hint is removed, so the model learns to generate the rationale as if it had arrived at the answer independently.

3. **Combined Fine-tuning:** In the fine-tuning step, the model is trained on the union of the rationales generated from correct answers (\(D_n\)) and the rationalized correct answers (\(D^{\text{rat}}_n\)). This exposes the model to a wider array of problems, including those it initially struggled with, allowing it to "think outside the box" and learn from its failures.

The addition of rationalization provides a robust mechanism to generate training signals for difficult problems that would otherwise be ignored, increasing the dataset size and accelerating the bootstrapping process. The authors draw a connection to RL, suggesting that rationale generation explores \(p(r|x)\) while rationalization leverages \(p(r|x,y)\), providing a potentially superior search space for rationales.

#### 2.3. Experimental Setup

The authors evaluated STaR on three distinct reasoning domains:
- **Arithmetic:** Summation of n-digit integers, using a scratchpad format inspired by. A dataset of 50,000 randomly sampled questions was generated, with 10,000 problems sampled per iteration. Few-shot prompts for 1 to 5 digits were used.
- **CommonsenseQA (CQA):** A multiple-choice commonsense reasoning task, which requires inferring relationships from general knowledge. The training set of 9,741 questions was used. Few-shot prompts were adapted from.
- **Grade School Math (GSM8K):** A dataset of natural language math word problems requiring multiple calculation steps. It includes 7,473 train examples.

**Base Model:** GPT-J, a 6B-parameter decoder-only transformer model, was chosen due to its public availability for checkpoints and fine-tuning.
**Hyperparameters:** A batch size of 8 sequences (length 1024) with packing was used. A learning rate of \(10^{-6}\) with a 100-step warmup was found to be optimal using the Adam optimizer. For the first outer loop, 40 fine-tuning steps were performed, increasing by 20% in subsequent iterations. Few-shot prompts were generally included during sampling in later iterations to prevent rationale "drift" and maintain performance. Experiments were conducted on a single TPU-v3 node.

### 3. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that STaR significantly improves the reasoning capabilities of large language models across diverse tasks, often allowing a smaller model to achieve performance comparable to much larger, state-of-the-art systems.

#### 3.1. Performance on Arithmetic

On the arithmetic task (summing n-digit integers), STaR showed dramatic improvements:
- **Overall Accuracy:** After 16 iterations with rationalization, STaR achieved 89.5% accuracy.
- **Baseline Comparison:** This significantly outperforms a baseline model fine-tuned directly on 10,000 examples without rationales, which only attained 76.3% accuracy.
- **Few-Shot Limitations:** Initially, few-shot accuracy on arithmetic problems was very low (e.g., less than 1% for 2-digit addition and near zero for more digits), highlighting the need for a fine-tuning approach.
- **Impact of Rationalization:** Rationalization enabled much faster and more comprehensive improvement. Without rationalization, performance improved in a stepwise manner (e.g., needing good (n-1)-digit sum performance before excelling at n-digit sums). With rationalization, the model could learn multiple lengths simultaneously.
- **Generalization:** When additional 9- and 10-digit examples (never seen during training) were introduced during later iterations, the model successfully solved many of these out-of-distribution problems, showcasing its improved generalization abilities.

#### 3.2. Performance on Commonsense Question Answering (CQA)

On the CommonsenseQA dataset, STaR with rationalization demonstrated remarkable gains:
- **STaR Performance:** STaR with rationalization achieved 72.5% accuracy on the CQA dev set.
- **Baseline Outperformance:** This substantially surpasses a GPT-J model fine-tuned to directly predict answers (60.0%) and the few-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) GPT-J baseline (36.6%).
- **Comparison to Larger Models:** Critically, STaR's 72.5% accuracy with a 6B-parameter GPT-J model is comparable to the 73.0% achieved by a 30x larger GPT-3 model fine-tuned directly on the answers. It also significantly outperforms a 137B-parameter LaMDA model prompted with few-shot CoT (55.6%). This indicates STaR's ability to extract more value from a smaller model through iterative self-improvement.
- **Rationale Quality:** Qualitative analysis and a human evaluation study (using crowdworkers) suggested that STaR-generated rationales were perceived as higher quality than those generated by few-shot prompting, and surprisingly, even preferred over some human-generated rationales (though the authors acknowledge limitations in human data quality). This suggests STaR can refine and improve the coherence of reasoning.
- **Failure Cases:** The analysis of failure cases revealed common logical fallacies and reasoning quirks, such as the model making assertions that merely restated the question, begging the question, making overly specific "world state assertions," or introducing red herring statements.

#### 3.3. Performance on Grade School Math (GSM8K)

For the more complex natural language mathematical word problems in GSM8K:
- **STaR Performance:** STaR with rationalization achieved 10.7% accuracy on the test set.
- **Baseline Improvements:** This is a significant improvement over few-shot Direct GPT-J (3.0%), few-shot CoT GPT-J (3.1%), and GPT-J fine-tuned directly (5.8%).
- **Rationalization Impact:** While beneficial, rationalization's impact was less pronounced on GSM8K compared to arithmetic or CQA. The model used a smaller percentage of rationalized data (0.5% vs. 8.5% for CQA).
- **Efficiency in Reasoning:** Interestingly, the model often found simpler or more direct solutions than the human-provided ground truth, sometimes using fewer calculation steps while still arriving at the correct answer.

#### 3.4. Impact of Rationalization and Other Factors

- **Rationalization's Efficacy:** Rationalization consistently demonstrated its value by allowing the model to learn from problems it initially failed, accelerating the bootstrapping process and boosting overall performance, particularly for tasks where the initial success rate was lower. It acts as a mechanism to "reverse-engineer" solutions and expand the effective training data.
- **Temperature Considerations:** The authors found that using higher sampling temperatures to generate more diverse rationales was counterproductive. It often led to unhelpful or incorrect reasoning, hindering generalization, especially in structured tasks like arithmetic. STaR's low-temperature, correct-answer filtering approach was superior.
- **Few-shot Prompting During Training:** Maintaining few-shot prompts during fine-tuning iterations proved beneficial, significantly reducing "drift" in rationale style and generally improving performance. This suggests that while STaR generates new data, the initial style guide remains valuable for guiding coherent reasoning.

### 4. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 4.1. Contributions

STaR represents a significant step forward in enabling large language models to acquire and refine complex reasoning abilities. The key contributions of this work are:

1. **Iterative Self-Improvement of Reasoning:** STaR proposes what the authors believe is the first technique allowing a pre-trained large language model to iteratively use its own language modeling capacity to improve its reasoning. By bootstrapping its ability to generate rationales, the model becomes a "self-taught reasoner," moving beyond static fine-tuning or in-context learning.
2. **Scalable Rationale Dataset Generation:** The method provides a powerful solution to the bottleneck of acquiring large, high-quality rationale datasets. Instead of costly human annotation or limited template-based generation, STaR automatically translates a small number of initial examples into a comprehensive dataset for fine-tuning.
3. **Introduction of Rationalization:** The concept of "rationalization" is a novel and crucial complement to rationale generation. By providing the correct answer as a hint for failed problems, it allows the model to "reason backward," generating useful rationales and providing a training signal for challenging instances that would otherwise yield no learning. This mechanism significantly accelerates and enhances the bootstrapping process.
4. **Demonstrated Performance Gains:** The empirical results across arithmetic, commonsense question answering, and grade school math problems clearly demonstrate STaR's effectiveness. It enables a relatively smaller model (GPT-J, 6B parameters) to achieve performance comparable to or better than much larger state-of-the-art models (like GPT-3 or LaMDA) that lack this self-improvement mechanism, highlighting its efficiency and impact.

#### 4.2. Implications and Future Directions

The development of STaR has several important implications for the future of LLM research and application:

- **Democratizing Advanced Reasoning:** By enabling smaller models to achieve high-level reasoning performance, STaR can make advanced AI capabilities more accessible to researchers and practitioners without requiring access to the largest, most computationally intensive models.
- **Towards More Autonomous AI:** The iterative self-improvement loop of STaR pushes LLMs towards greater autonomy in skill acquisition. This principle of "learning from one's own generated reasoning" could be extended to other complex tasks, fostering more adaptive and generalizable AI systems.
- **Bridging In-Context Learning and Fine-tuning:** STaR effectively bridges the gap between the flexibility of few-shot in-context learning and the performance gains of fine-tuning, by using the former to *create* the data for the latter.
- **Foundation for Sophisticated Techniques:** STaR's general approach of bootstrapping reasoning from examples without explicit rationales provides a strong foundation for more sophisticated techniques, potentially integrating with formal verification or more nuanced feedback mechanisms beyond simple correctness checks.
- **Broader Applicability:** The method's success across symbolic and natural language reasoning tasks suggests its potential applicability to a wide array of domains requiring step-by-step problem-solving.

Future research could further explore the theoretical connections between STaR and reinforcement learning, investigate different "hinting" strategies for rationalization, and develop robust methods for filtering out "bad reasoning" in scenarios with high chance performance, especially where ground-truth rationales are ambiguous or unavailable. Additionally, understanding the interaction between dataset biases and STaR's amplification of reasoning patterns is crucial for responsible deployment.

#### 4.3. Limitations and Challenges

Despite its significant contributions, STaR, as presented, has certain limitations:

- **Initial Reasoning Capability Requirement:** The method relies on the base LLM possessing *some* initial reasoning capabilities above chance performance, via few-shot prompting. As noted, smaller models like GPT-2 were unable to bootstrap effectively. This implies a minimum scale for the base model.
- **Filtering Bad Reasoning:** In tasks with high levels of chance performance (e.g., binary decisions), simply filtering for correct answers might lead to the inclusion of "poor rationales" that incidentally result in a correct answer. Identifying and filtering truly "bad reasoning" remains an open problem.
- **Hinting Mechanism Specificity:** The method of providing the "hint" (e.g., `add_hint(x_i, y_i)`) might be task-specific and not immediately generalizable across all problem formats. Further exploration of universal hinting techniques is warranted.
- **Faithfulness of Rationales:** While STaR improves the quality of *generated* rationales and their correlation with correct answers, it does not guarantee that these rationales faithfully represent the model's *internal* reasoning process. The model could still be implicitly choosing an answer and then generating a post-hoc justification, a common challenge in interpretable AI.
- **Computational Cost:** While more efficient than extensive high-temperature sampling, the iterative nature of generating large datasets and fine-tuning still requires significant computational resources, especially for very large base models.

Overall, STaR presents an innovative and highly effective approach to scaling LLM reasoning abilities by enabling models to teach themselves. It lays groundwork for future research into autonomous and robust AI reasoning.
