# ModPack: An Extensible Teleoperation Interface for Bimanual Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University researchers developed ModPack, an extensible and wearable teleoperation interface designed for bimanual mobile manipulation. This system integrates joint-level control, mobile base navigation, active perception, and haptic feedback, demonstrating improved data collection efficiency for training robot policies, achieving up to 88% task success on cloth placement and 60% on box transfer across two distinct robot platforms.

## Problem

- Existing teleoperation interfaces are often purpose-built for specific robot hardware and tasks, leading to fragmentation and limiting scalability, adaptability, and reusability for robot learning research.
- Integrating diverse capabilities like bimanual joint control, mobile navigation, active perception, and haptic feedback into a single, cohesive, and easily extensible system presents significant engineering challenges.
- The lack of generalizable teleoperation tools creates a bottleneck for collecting high-quality, multimodal demonstration data necessary for advancing generalist robotic learning and control.

## Method

- **ModPack** is a self-contained, 3D-printed wearable backpack that provides onboard computation, power, and a common software interface, acting as a modular core for various robot embodiments.
- The system utilizes extensible modules for leader arms (with ==gravity compensation== and ==haptic feedback==), mobile base motion tracking (via an iPhone), and active perception (via a VR headset streaming head pose and egocentric views), allowing for 'plug-and-play' customization.
- A lightweight, bidirectional '==bridge==' API based on a message queue server decouples the core software from robot-specific implementations, ensuring compatibility and low-latency communication with diverse target robots.

## Results

- On a customized mobile robot, a policy trained with head camera observations ([Head Cam]) achieved **88%** success for a cloth placement task, outperforming policies using wrist cameras ([Wrist Cam], **12%**) or all cameras ([All Cam], **80%**).
- For the RB-Y1m robot, a policy trained with all cameras plus joint torque information ([All Cam + Torque]) achieved **60%** success for a box transfer task, surpassing vision-only policies ([Head Cam], **55%**; [All Cam], **30%**).
- Qualitative analysis of policy attention revealed that incorporating joint torques improved the model's focus on critical regions (e.g., box edges) and increased reliance on torque information during contact-critical phases of the task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A modular and wearable design paradigm can effectively integrate complex teleoperation functionalities, including bimanual control, mobile manipulation, active perception, and haptic feedback, into a unified and extensible system.
> - Active gravity compensation on leader arms and integrated haptic feedback from robot force/torque sensors significantly enhance operator comfort and task precision, particularly in contact-rich manipulation tasks.
> - Careful disentanglement of operator motions, such as compensating for mobile base movement when commanding head pose for active perception, is crucial for intuitive and unambiguous multi-modal control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic teleoperation is integral to developing and training autonomous robot systems, particularly for tasks requiring human supervision, data collection, and demonstration for imitation learning. Historically, teleoperation interfaces have often been purpose-built, rigidly tailored to specific robot hardware configurations and task domains. This specialization, while effective for a narrow scope, has resulted in a fragmented ecosystem where systems lack scalability, adaptability, and reusability. Extending these bespoke systems to new capabilities or different robot platforms typically necessitates significant redesigns of both hardware and software, creating a bottleneck for advancing generalist robotic learning and control.

Existing teleoperation systems have explored various facets of robot control. Kinesthetic teaching, while intuitive, is physically demanding. Systems using input devices like spacemouses or VR controllers offer decoupled control but may lack the fidelity of direct joint manipulation. Kinematic replica systems, such as GELLO, provide one-to-one joint mapping for improved control granularity. For mobile manipulation, approaches like Mobile ALOHA enable bimanual arm and base control, though often with some degree of decoupling. Other exoskeleton-based systems, like AirExo and AirExo-2, offer high-fidelity arm control and cross-robot adaptability but typically do not integrate mobile base control. Full-body teleoperation systems, such as CHILD and PAPRLE, manage arm and leg control but often without simultaneous operation of all modalities. Supersuit allows whole-body teleoperation with base and torso tracking but does not incorporate active perception or haptic feedback.

Within the domain of haptic feedback, a crucial element for contact-rich tasks, a trade-off exists between feedback fidelity and hardware portability. High-fidelity bilateral systems are often stationary, while lightweight wearable solutions may compromise kinesthetic resistance and transparency. Similarly, active perception, which allows robots to dynamically adjust their viewpoint to gather task-relevant visual information, has primarily been explored in stationary teleoperation settings. Integrating active perception into mobile teleoperation introduces the challenge of distinguishing between viewpoint changes caused by operator head motion and those stemming from body or base movement.

ModPack positions itself within this landscape by explicitly addressing the need for a unified, modular, and extensible teleoperation system. Unlike prior work that often focuses on a subset of capabilities or suffers from fragmentation, ModPack integrates bimanual joint-level control, mobile manipulation, active perception, and haptic feedback into a single, cohesive framework. Its modular design aims to overcome the limitations of task- and robot-specific specialization, enabling researchers to flexibly combine components and adapt the system to diverse requirements without extensive re-engineering. This approach seeks to accelerate data collection for robot learning by providing a versatile and robust interface that generalizes across various robotic embodiments and complex tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop ModPack, a teleoperation system designed to overcome the limitations of existing solutions that are often specific to certain robot hardware and task domains. The core motivation stems from the observation that such specialized systems inhibit scalability, adaptability, and efficient iteration in robot learning research. This fragmentation makes it challenging to build unified data collection and control systems that can generalize across a variety of tasks and robot platforms.

To address these limitations, the authors established several key objectives:

- **Achieve Modularity and Extensibility**: The system was designed around a self-contained, wearable "backpack" unit that provides integrated power, onboard computation, and data storage. This core unit serves as a common, shared infrastructure, intended to decouple fundamental system functionalities from specific task or robot requirements. This modular foundation allows for a "plug-and-play" interface, enabling operators to flexibly add, remove, or replace capability modules based on the demands of a given task.
- **Support Cross-Robot Joint-Level Control**: A specific goal was to enable the system to adapt to diverse robot arm kinematics. This is achieved through swappable leader-arm modules, each kinematically matched to a particular follower robot arm, facilitating precise, one-to-one joint-level control across different robot platforms.
- **Enable Mobile Manipulation**: The system was designed to track the operator's base motion, allowing for intuitive control of the robot's mobile base. This capability is crucial for tasks that require the robot to cover large workspaces, moving beyond static tabletop operations.
- **Integrate Active Perception**: A key objective was to allow operators to control the robot's head camera pose while simultaneously streaming real-time robot observations. This active perception module, enabled through a wireless VR headset, aims to facilitate demonstrations of behaviors such as object search, occlusion handling, and optimal viewpoint selection.
- **Provide Haptic Feedback**: The motivation for including haptic feedback was to enhance operator situational awareness and facilitate safer, more precise teleoperation, particularly for contact-rich tasks. This is achieved by leveraging force feedback from the robot and actively actuating the leader arms.
- **Overcome Integration Challenges**: The design also sought to address practical difficulties inherent in integrating multiple complex capabilities:
 - **Disentangling Control Inputs**: When simultaneously controlling arms, mobile base, and head, operator motions can become ambiguous. A specific objective was to develop mechanisms to disentangle operator movements into distinct commands for each robot component.
 - **Handling High-Rate Heterogeneous Data**: The system needed to process various data streams—proprioception, visual observations, force/torque signals—with low latency, necessitating efficient communication protocols.
 - **Ensuring Operator Comfort and Unobtrusiveness**: To reduce operator fatigue during extended operation, gravity compensation for the exoskeleton leader arms was a design priority, alongside packaging the entire system into a compact, wearable form factor.
 - **Balancing Hardware Constraints**: The design aimed to integrate power, computation, sensing, and communication hardware into a self-contained backpack while managing constraints related to weight, thermal management, and overall usability.
- **Facilitate Future Research**: A final objective was to open-source the complete hardware design and software stack, providing a flexible and reusable framework for other researchers for data collection and policy learning, thereby supporting and accelerating future developments in robot learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ModPack is designed as a self-contained, 3D-printed wearable backpack system that provides onboard computation, power, and data storage for mobile teleoperation. Its primary architectural principle is extensibility, enabling users to modify or replace hardware and software modules to support various robot embodiments with minimal configuration.

**ModPack Core System:**
The core of ModPack is a portable backpack serving as a shared platform for modular hardware and a common software interface.
- **Hardware:** The backpack is constructed from 3D-printed components, facilitating accessibility and rapid modification. It features front and rear panels secured by toggle latches for internal access. Two top-mounted slots on the front panel accommodate support bars for leader-arm modules. Internally, five removable shelves house the mini PC, power banks, and other accessories. Components are vertically distributed along the operator's spine to reduce carrying strain and improve ergonomics. Weight and thermal management are addressed through material removal from structural panels and shelves.
- **Software:** An onboard mini PC acts as the standalone computational unit, managing module activation, data logging, and target-robot startup/shutdown. Communication with target robots is established via a lightweight bidirectional "bridge" API, implemented using a message queue server for low-latency communication. This abstraction layer decouples the core backpack software stack from robot-specific implementations, ensuring compatibility with diverse robots and modular extensions. The software stack facilitates data logging in a zarr format using robologger.

**Extensible Modules:**
The core system is augmented by various modular components, each comprising a hardware unit and its corresponding software interface, allowing modules to be swapped across different target robots while maintaining a unified API.

- **Leader Arms:**
 - **Design:** Two variants of 3D-printed leader arms were developed: 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) arms for a customized mobile manipulation platform (using ARX5 arms) and 7-DoF arms for the RB-Y1m robot. These arms are kinematically equivalent to their respective follower arms, following the GELLO framework for direct joint-space teleoperation. Dynamixel servo motors are used for actuation.
 - **Gravity Compensation:** To mitigate operator fatigue, an active gravity compensation mechanism is implemented. Gravity torque is computed using the Orocos Kinematics and Dynamics Library (KDL) and the leader arm’s URDF model. For stability, a joint-space damping term is optionally added. The augmented torque is then smoothed using two cascaded exponential moving average (EMA) filters before being scaled and commanded to the leader arm motors, subject to predefined current constraints.
 - **Haptic Feedback:** For robots equipped with force/torque (F/T) sensors (e.g., RB-Y1m), haptic feedback is provided. The net wrench at the robot's end-effector is mapped to joint-space torques via the Jacobian transpose. The final control torque commanded to the leader motors is a superposition of the gravity compensation torque and this haptic feedback component. A current budgeting strategy limits peak torque commands, preserving hardware and facilitating safe operation within amperage constraints.

- **Mobile Base:**
 - **Operator Motion Tracking:** The operator's planar SE(2) pose is tracked using an iPhone mounted on the backpack, which runs a modified WebXR session.
 - **Egocentric Mapping:** The system implements an egocentric mapping procedure, directly coupling the robot's base translation to the operator's displacement in their local frame, enabling intuitive forward/backward and turning movements.
 - **Pose Recovery and Synchronization:** Raw WebXR pose data is transformed to align with the robot’s coordinate system. An essential step involves compensating for the physical offset between the iPhone's position on the backpack and the operator's actual center of rotation. The operator's tracked pose is then mapped from the WebXR session frame into the robot’s world frame using initial session and robot global poses. The resulting world-relative target planar x, y coordinates and yaw heading are issued as absolute setpoints to the robot base controller.

- **Active Perception:**
 - **Hardware and Streaming:** The active perception module utilizes an Apple Vision Pro running a custom visionOS app. This app streams the operator's head pose as commands for the robot's neck, while simultaneously providing a low-latency egocentric view (either monocular RGB video or 3D point clouds).
 - **Head Motion Mapping:** The operator's head motion is tracked as a device pose relative to the initial session pose. This relative transformation is converted into the robot head coordinate convention using a fixed basis transformation, yielding a robot-frame head command. This command is then transformed into the robot world frame using an initial tracking reference to establish a global target head pose.
 - **Decoupling Head and Base Motion:** To prevent redundant commands when the robot is simultaneously navigating and moving its head, the system compensates for the current mobile base pose. The target head pose is re-expressed in the local robot base frame, isolating only the residual neck motion intended by the operator.

**Policy Learning:**
The authors employed a standard transformer-based Diffusion Policy model for policy learning from collected demonstrations.
- **Inputs:** For the customized mobile robot, inputs included RGB-D observations from the head camera and RGB from left and right wrist cameras. For the RB-Y1m robot, inputs consisted of RGB from two head cameras, left and right wrist cameras, and optionally joint torques. Visual inputs were encoded using a CLIP-pretrained ViT (with depth images repeated across channels), while torque observations were encoded via sequential CausalConv layers.
- **Action Space:** The action space was multi-modal, including mobile-base commands (x, y, θ), head/neck end-effector poses, and bimanual arm joint positions with gripper states. Base commands were predicted relative to the current state, while head, arm, and gripper commands were absolute targets.
- **Inference:** During inference, the model used DDIM with 16 steps to denoise an action chunk of length 16. The first 8 actions were executed for cloth placement, and the full 16-action chunk for box transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy and adaptability of ModPack were evaluated through real-world bimanual mobile manipulation experiments conducted on two distinct robot platforms. These experiments involved collecting teleoperation demonstrations and subsequently training behavior cloning policies, whose deployment success rates were then assessed.

**Platform 1: Customized Mobile Robot with ARX5 Arms**
This robot featured bimanual 6-DoF ARX5 arms, a 6-DoF ARX5 neck for active perception, and a holonomic mobile base from Tidybot++. It was equipped with an iPhone for depth and RGB images from the neck, and Logitech webcams on the wrists for RGB images.

**Task: Cloth Placement with Active Perception**
- **Description:** The robot was required to grasp a cloth from a counter, reverse its position, locate a basket (randomized left or right of the robot) using active perception, navigate towards it, and place the cloth inside. Task success was defined by the cloth being dropped into the basket.
- **Data Collection:** 125 demonstrations were collected, covering variations in cloth color (blue, pink) and basket location (left, right).
- **Policy Evaluation:** Three policy variants were evaluated based on their visual observation modalities:
 - **[Head Cam]:** Used only head camera observations.
 - **[Wrist Cam]:** Used only wrist camera observations.
 - **[All Cam]:** Used head camera and both wrist cameras.
 Each policy underwent 25 rollouts with randomized cloth initial positions.
- **Results:**
 - **[Head Cam]** achieved the highest success rate, succeeding in 22 out of 25 rollouts (88%). The hypothesis presented is that the head camera's broader egocentric view and depth information were advantageous for grasping and basket localization, while the cloth's varied grasps reduced the critical need for close-up wrist observations. Additionally, this approach avoided potential out-of-distribution wrist camera observations due to variations in wrist pose or cloth deformation.
 - **[All Cam]** succeeded in 20 out of 25 rollouts (80%).
 - **[Wrist Cam]** had the lowest success rate, succeeding in only 3 out of 25 rollouts (12%). This policy often failed due to placement errors, dropping the cloth on the edge or outside the basket, indicating insufficient contextual information about the basket's location.

**Platform 2: RB-Y1m Robot**
This robot featured a bimanual setup with 7-DoF arms, a 6-DoF actuated torso on a holonomic mobile base, a 2-DoF active neck, fin-ray grippers, two wide-angle FLIR cameras, and a FLIR camera on each wrist.

**Task: Box Transfer with Haptic Feedback**
- **Description:** The robot was tasked with picking up a box from a cabinet, then rotating to observe an existing box on a rack to infer the unoccupied target shelf (either top or bottom), and finally placing the new box stably on the unoccupied shelf.
- **Data Collection:** 102 demonstrations were collected, split between scenarios where the top or bottom shelf was initially occupied.
- **Policy Evaluation:** Three policy variants were compared:
 - **[All Cam]:** Used RGB camera views from two head cameras and both wrist cameras.
 - **[Head Cam]:** Used only RGB observations from the two head cameras.
 - **[All Cam + Torque]:** Used all camera views (as in [All Cam]) plus joint torque information for both arms.
 Each policy was evaluated over 20 rollouts across two scenarios, varying the cart location and robot starting positions.
- **Results:**
 - **[All Cam + Torque]** achieved the highest success rate, with 12 out of 20 rollouts succeeding (60%). The authors hypothesize that incorporating projected joint torques improved performance by providing a coarse alignment signal for grasp timing and depth, mitigating issues such as imprecise grasp poses observed in other policies.
 - **[Head Cam]** succeeded in 11 out of 20 rollouts (55%).
 - **[All Cam]** had the lowest success rate, succeeding in 6 out of 20 rollouts (30%). A common failure mode for this policy was identified as poor grasp poses.
- **Attention Analysis:** Qualitative analysis of the model's attention patterns revealed that the [All Cam + Torque] policy exhibited stronger attention to task-relevant regions, such as the edges of the box crucial for grasping, compared to the vision-only [All Cam] policy. Additionally, the aggregated attention weights assigned to the torque tokens increased as the robot approached and initiated grasping, indicating that the policy increasingly relied on torque information during contact-critical phases of the task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ModPack system represents a notable advancement in teleoperation for robot learning by directly addressing the pervasive issue of system fragmentation and lack of adaptability in existing interfaces. Its significance lies in several key areas:

Firstly, **Modularity and Extensibility**: By providing a self-contained, wearable backpack core that supports plug-and-play capability modules, ModPack establishes a unified framework for diverse robot embodiments and task requirements. This modularity minimizes the need for extensive redesigns when integrating new capabilities or adapting to different robot platforms, thereby accelerating the iteration cycles in robot learning research. This approach contrasts with the prior landscape of rigid, task- and robot-specific systems.

Secondly, **Comprehensive Capability Integration**: ModPack successfully integrates a range of advanced teleoperation capabilities—bimanual joint-level control with cross-robot adaptability, mobile manipulation, active perception, and haptic feedback—into a single system. This comprehensive integration enables the collection of demonstrations for more complex, real-world tasks that require coordinated control across multiple modalities. This capability is particularly impactful for imitation learning, where high-quality, diverse demonstrations are crucial for training robust robot policies.

Thirdly, **Enhanced Data Collection for Robot Learning**: The system's features contribute to collecting higher-quality and more versatile data. Haptic feedback is shown to improve performance in contact-rich tasks, offering operators better situational awareness and promoting more precise interactions. Active perception facilitates data collection for tasks involving object search and dynamic viewpoint selection, allowing robots to learn adaptive visual behaviors. Gravity compensation on leader arms reduces operator fatigue, potentially enabling longer and more consistent data collection sessions. The successful training of Diffusion Policy models on ModPack-collected data, demonstrating strong deployment performance, validates its practical utility as a robust data collection interface.

Fourthly, **Cross-Robot Generalization**: The demonstration of ModPack's flexibility across two distinct robot platforms (a customized bimanual robot and an RB-Y1m robot) highlights its potential to facilitate the development of more generalizable robot policies. Researchers can use ModPack to collect data for a variety of robots and tasks, fostering policies that are less tied to specific hardware.

Finally, **Open-Source Contribution**: The commitment to open-sourcing the complete hardware design and software stack is a substantial contribution to the robotics community. This action lowers the barrier to entry for other researchers and practitioners, enabling them to replicate, modify, and extend ModPack's capabilities. This open approach is likely to foster collaborative development and accelerate advancements in teleoperation systems and robot learning paradigms.

In summary, ModPack addresses a critical need for a flexible and reusable teleoperation system, offering a practical tool for data collection and policy learning that can significantly impact the development of more capable and general-purpose robots.
