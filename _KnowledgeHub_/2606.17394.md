# Damage Adaptation in Seconds for Architected Materials

> [!summary] Summary
> Northwestern University researchers developed Latent Ensemble Adaptive Proprioception (LEAP), a method for rapid, real-time proprioceptive adaptation to damage and physical repairs in soft-actuated architected materials. The approach reduced force/torque sensing errors by an average of 87% for single actuators and recovered task performance in a 6-DoF soft wrist following damage.

## Problem

- Soft robots suffer from limited operating lives due to material failure (creep, fatigue) and lack robust mechanisms for adaptation to damage and subsequent repairs.
- Traditional control and sensing methods for soft robots assume nominal conditions, leading to performance degradation or failure when components inevitably change or are damaged.
- Existing damage adaptation methods are often sample-intensive and struggle with the high-dimensional or continuous damage models typically associated with continuum soft systems.

## Method

- Introduces **Latent Ensemble Adaptive Proprioception (LEAP)**, which combines discrete damage parameterization with a ==variational latent model== and ==ensemble learning==.
- Damage to Handed Shearing Auxetics (HSAs) is represented in a low-dimensional, discrete coordinate system, which is then mapped to a 3D continuous latent space via a ==β-VAE==.
- Adapts to unseen damage in real-time by linearly weighting an ensemble of pre-computed latent damage states using ==least-squares regression== over collected data, taking approximately 37 seconds.

## Results

- LEAP reduced force/torque sensing errors by an average of **87%** in single HSAs compared to their damaged, unadapted state.
- Applied to a 6-DoF soft wrist, LEAP achieved mean error magnitudes of **1.5N** in force and **0.074Nm** in torque, demonstrating a **49%** error reduction over unadapted models across different configurations.
- The 6-DoF wrist, after damage and LEAP adaptation, fully recovered task-level performance in a tactile contour tracing task, maintaining contact for **85%** of the trajectory, identical to its pre-damage performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Architected materials, with their lattice-based structures, enable low-dimensional, discrete parameterization of damage, drastically simplifying damage characterization compared to continuum soft robots.
> - This discrete parameterization allows the sample complexity for learning damage representations to collapse from exponential to linear, making comprehensive modeling of all local damage states feasible.
> - The method demonstrates that adaptation can successfully account for the variability introduced by manual physical repairs, integrating maintenance into the operational lifecycle of soft robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of soft robotics has demonstrated capabilities for complex manipulation and interaction in human-centric environments. However, a significant challenge for their widespread adoption outside of laboratory settings is the reliability and durability of their compliant components. Soft actuators often experience material failure through creep and fatigue, leading to operating lives significantly shorter than those of rigid counterparts. Furthermore, modeling and control of soft robots are complex due to high dimensionality, hysteretic material properties, configuration-dependent behavior, temperature sensitivity, and manufacturing variability. Existing control and sensing methods typically assume "nominal" conditions, leading to performance degradation as components inevitably change with use or damage.

The broader research landscape addresses damage adaptation in robotics through two main categories: self-modeling and control-adaptation. Control-adaptation methods modify robot policies directly based on dynamics changes, proving effective for well-defined tasks but not directly modeling the system, making them unsuitable for proprioception. Self-modeling methods, an extension of classical system identification, involve the robot building and maintaining a representation of its own dynamics. These methods are typically sample-intensive but can handle significant dynamics changes and enable general-purpose control and sensing. This paper contributes to the self-modeling approach, specifically focusing on proprioceptive adaptation in soft-actuated systems.

This work positions architected materials, characterized by microscale geometric designs dictating macroscale properties, as particularly advantageous for damage adaptation. Unlike continuum soft mechanisms or rigid-linked systems that require continuous or high-dimensional damage parameterizations, architected materials with lattice- or unit cell-based structures offer a discrete means to parameterize damage. Previous work on rigid quadruped adaptation, for instance, necessitated modeling thousands of damage states. Architected materials, by contrast, exhibit degeneracies where multiple damages within a lattice member are equivalent, and redundancies ensure functionality despite severed links. Furthermore, their failure modes are often non-acute, allowing continued operation with altered properties before catastrophic failure. This paper explores how these inherent properties of architected materials can be leveraged for efficient and rapid adaptation to damage.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work is to overcome a significant barrier to the long-term autonomy and practical deployment of soft robots: their susceptibility to damage and the lack of robust mechanisms for adaptation to such damage and subsequent repairs. Current soft robots often exhibit limited operating lives and are vulnerable to hazards in real-world environments, such as cuts, wear, or melting. When damage occurs, traditional controllers, designed for nominal conditions, become out-of-distribution, leading to performance degradation or failure.

The researchers posit that while damage adaptation is challenging, architected materials possess unique characteristics that make them well-suited for this problem. Specifically, actuator failure in architected materials tends to occur gradually, and damage can often be described in a low-dimensional, discrete coordinate space. This contrasts with rigid or continuum soft systems, which typically present higher-dimensional or continuous damage models that demand extensive data for adaptation. Furthermore, the concept of "repair" in soft systems is often overlooked; this work aims to integrate repair as an intermediate state requiring adaptation, acknowledging the variability introduced by manual repair processes.

The key objectives of this research are:
1. **To develop a method for rapid, real-time proprioceptive adaptation to catastrophic damage and physical repairs in soft-actuated systems.** This method, termed Latent Ensemble Adaptive Proprioception (LEAP), aims to achieve adaptation in seconds, without requiring simulation, addressing the need for practical deployment of soft robots.
2. **To leverage the inherent properties of architected materials for damage adaptation.** The work seeks to demonstrate that representing damage in a low-dimensional, discrete coordinate space and using latent damage representations combined with ensemble methods can enable robust adaptation to unseen damage.
3. **To identify and articulate the unique advantages of architected materials for adaptation.** This includes demonstrating how exponential sample complexity for learned representations can collapse to linear sample complexity, offering a concrete advantage over rigid components or continuum soft mechanisms.
4. **To design and evaluate a novel 6-DoF soft wrist capable of proprioceptive force/torque sensing and rapid adaptation.** This robotic platform, based on Handed Shearing Auxetic (HSA) actuators, serves as a testbed for the LEAP method in a multi-actuator, complex system.
5. **To empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of LEAP in recovering performance for tasks.** Specifically, the work aims to show that LEAP can adapt to various damage types (cuts, burns) and repairs, enabling the recovery of task-level performance (e.g., contour tracing) using only proprioception.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Latent Ensemble Adaptive Proprioception (LEAP), integrates a latent damage representation with an ensemble learning approach to achieve rapid adaptation. The methodology consists of three main components: representing damage in discrete coordinates, modeling damage via a latent space, and adapting to damage using HSA ensembles.

**A. Representing Damage in Discrete Coordinates:**
The core of LEAP relies on the structural properties of Handed Shearing Auxetics (HSAs), a type of architected material. HSAs possess a lattice-based structure, allowing damage to be parameterized in a discrete coordinate system. For the HSAs used, a "cut" refers to severing a vertical or horizontal auxetic link, defined by its position along a vertical helix (A, B, C) and its height (0-7 for vertical, 0-6 for horizontal). "Burns" (melting links) are segmented into three vertical coordinates (0-2) due to their imprecise nature. This system results in 54 possible discrete damage locations. The damage state of an HSA is represented as a sparse binary vector $\mathbf{x} \in \{0, 1\}^{54}$, with a constraint that $\left\Vert \mathbf{x} \right\Vert_0 \le 3$, assuming non-functionality beyond three damages. This discrete parameterization aims to produce an encoded representation where the distribution of latent variables enables dynamically feasible force/torque predictions.

**B. Modeling Damage via a Latent Space:**
A variational latent model is employed to transform the discrete damage representation into a continuous, low-dimensional latent space. A neural network, denoted as $D$, learns the mapping from the 54-dimensional binary damage vector to a 3-dimensional latent vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, such that $\mathbf{z} = D(\mathbf{x})$. The final layer of $D$ is parameterized as a multivariate Gaussian, sampling $\mathbf{z}$ using the re-parameterization trick ($\mathbf{z} = \mu(\mathbf{x}) + \sigma(\mathbf{x}) \odot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim N(0, I)$).
The latent model is trained using a $\beta$-VAE formulation of the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) objective, which balances reconstruction loss (mean-squared error for force/torque prediction) with the Kullback-Leibler Divergence between the learned latent distribution and a prior.
A separate neural network, the "wrench predictor" $W$, learns to predict 6-dimensional forces and torques ($\hat{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^6$) at the actuator base, given the latent state $\mathbf{z}$ and the measured displacement of the actuator in SE(3) ($\mathbf{P}$). The orientation component of $\mathbf{P}$ is processed using a Gram-Schmidt-like process to ensure continuity of rotation, resulting in a 12-dimensional input to $W$ alongside $\mathbf{z}$.
Training data for the latent model and wrench predictor includes data from undamaged HSAs, as well as HSAs with one cut, two cuts, one burn, and combinations of 2+ cuts/burns, totaling 371 minutes of data from 89 HSAs. Ground-truth data is collected using an OptiTrack fiducial tracker and a Robotous RFT60-HA01 six-axis force/torque sensor.

**C. Adapting to Damage with HSA Ensembles:**
For real-time adaptation to unseen damage, LEAP utilizes an ensemble method without requiring re-training or fine-tuning of $D$ or $W$.
1. **Ensemble Generation:** A large number of sampled encoder outputs (latent vectors from various discrete damage states) are clustered (K-Means) to produce an ensemble of $K$ latent coordinates, representing centroids of possible damage states. These centroids $E = \{\mathbf{z}_1, \dots, \mathbf{z}_K\}$ approximate maximally differing HSA damages.
2. **Ensemble Prediction:** For each member $\mathbf{z}_i$ in the ensemble, a force/torque prediction $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(i)} = W(\mathbf{P} | \mathbf{z}_i)$ is generated across the adaptation window.
3. **Weighting:** Given either a ground-truth force/torque signal or a reference weight, component-wise linear weightings $\mathbf{A}$ for the ensemble members are calculated using a least-squares regression: $\mathbf{A} = \arg \min_{\mathbf{A}} \left\Vert \hat{\mathbf{Y}} \mathbf{A} - \mathbf{y} \right\Vert_2^2$, where $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}$ is a matrix of ensemble predictions.
4. **Inference:** After obtaining $\mathbf{A}$, the adaptive force/torque prediction is given by $\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{P}) = \sum_{i=1}^{K} a_i W(\mathbf{P} | \mathbf{z}_i)$. This process is computationally lightweight, scaling linearly with the number of actuators, and can be completed in approximately 37 seconds (30s data collection, 5s pre-processing, 2s regression).
The method assumes the transform between HSA ends is known at runtime, achieved via an internal camera and AprilTag for the 6-DoF wrist, or OptiTrack for single HSAs.

**D. Extension to a 6-DoF Soft Wrist:**
LEAP is extended to hybrid soft robots where actuators connect rigid elements whose relative positions are known, and individual actuator models can be developed. For the 6-DoF soft wrist, driven by six HSAs, a single set of 30 latent clusters is initialized, and different weightings are determined for each constituent actuator. The wrenches generated by each ensemble element are transformed to a common frame, weighted, and summed. The adaptation process remains a linear least-squares regression over these weights. The wrist includes an internal camera and AprilTag for proprioceptive pose sensing and is actuated by servomotors. Kinematic relationships are defined in an XACRO file, and servo rotation feedback augments the camera-based pose for LEAP inputs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to assess LEAP's adaptation capabilities for single HSAs, its scalability to multi-HSA systems, its generalization to unseen damages and repairs, and its impact on task-level performance.

**A. Single HSA Damage Adaptation:**
Experiments on single HSAs subjected to vertical cuts at various coordinates demonstrated that without adaptation, damaged HSAs exhibited force/torque sensing errors as high as 0.25 (N², N²cm²) log-MSE, or 1.78 (N², N²cm²) mean-squared error (MSE) averaged per component. Following LEAP adaptation, an average reduction of 87% in sensing error was observed compared to the damaged, unadapted state. The largest raw improvements were noted in Tₓ, Tᵧ, F𝓩, and T𝓩, which align with the HSA's primary force/torque transmission axes and beam bending behavior.

**B. Adaptation on the 6-DoF Soft Wrist:**
LEAP was applied to a 6-DoF soft wrist with five different configurations, progressively introducing cuts, burns, and repairs. Adaptation models were generated using 60 seconds of randomly selected data, with computation taking 4.5 seconds on an RTX3080 laptop GPU.
The adapted models demonstrated the ability to model all five robot configurations, achieving mean error magnitudes of 1.5N in force and 0.074Nm in torque. Adapted models yielded an average error reduction of 49% compared to unadapted models trained on a different robot configuration. The study also observed that models trained on an undamaged system showed the largest drop in performance when deployed on partially damaged datasets, suggesting that additional stiffness from an undamaged arrangement can lead to a less generalizable model.

**C. Adaptation to Repaired HSAs:**
The repair of architected materials introduces variability. Measurements of the Z-axis spring constant for five HSAs showed that after a vertical cut and subsequent glue repair, the spring constants differed from their default stiffness by an average of 9%, with both increases and decreases observed. Despite repairs, the weighted latent coordinates for healthy and repaired HSAs were distinct, indicating a change in HSA dynamics. LEAP performed similarly in MSE for force/torque prediction for repaired HSAs as for other damage types, demonstrating its ability to adapt to these altered states even when repaired HSAs were not explicitly included in the training data for out-of-distribution evaluation.

**D. Task Demonstration:**
A tactile contour tracing task was performed with the 6-DoF soft wrist mounted on a robot arm, using only the wrist's proprioception. Before damage, the wrist maintained contact with the contour for 85% of the trajectory. After one HSA was burned and another cut, the damaged and unadapted wrist catastrophically failed the task, maintaining contact only briefly. With LEAP adaptation, the system recovered performance, maintaining contact for an identical 85% of the total path, demonstrating task-level recovery.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a method, LEAP, for rapid and robust damage adaptation in architected materials, offering several significant implications for soft robotics and broader robotic design.

A key contribution is the **demonstration of rapid adaptation in seconds for proprioception in soft-actuated systems**, a crucial step towards long-term robot autonomy outside of controlled laboratory environments. By enabling adaptation in under a minute, LEAP addresses the practical constraints of computational resources and hardware degradation during the adaptation process itself. The simulation-free, real-time nature of the adaptation is particularly impactful for deployment in unpredictable real-world scenarios.

The work highlights the **unique advantages of architected materials** for damage adaptation. The researchers argue that the discrete, lattice-based structure of these materials allows for damage to be parameterized in a low-dimensional, coordinate-based system. This contrasts with rigid or continuum soft robots, which face higher-dimensional or continuous damage modeling challenges. This discrete parameterization, coupled with the inherent degeneracies (where specific local damages have equivalent functional impacts) and redundancies (maintaining functionality despite localized failures) in architected materials, simplifies damage characterization. The paper specifically identifies conditions under which the exponential sample complexity typically associated with modeling numerous damage states collapses to linear sample complexity for learned representations in architected materials. This finding suggests that a finite and countable number of experiments can sufficiently model the system's response to all local damages, making comprehensive characterization feasible.

The methodology's ability to **adapt to physical repairs** is also a significant aspect. By treating repairs as states requiring adaptation rather than distinct nominal conditions, LEAP provides a framework for integrating real-world maintenance into the operational lifecycle of soft robots. This acknowledges the imprecise nature of manual repairs and demonstrates that the system can account for the resulting material property changes.

The development of the **6-DoF soft wrist capable of proprioceptive force/torque sensing and rapid adaptation** serves as a concrete example of applying LEAP to a complex, multi-actuator system. The successful recovery of performance in a contact-rich tracing task validates the practical utility of the method for restoring functionality after damage.

The work proposes a **new perspective on robot design**, suggesting that architected materials, due to their specific in-distribution guarantees for statistical learning, possess inherent advantages over rigid and continuum materials for mechanisms intended to learn and adapt. The empirical observation that damage (e.g., cuts removing non-linearities) can sometimes improve proprioceptive prediction accuracy by making the material behave more like a linear spring or beam also opens avenues for future investigation into the design tradeoff between force transmission and algorithmic observability.

While limitations include the need for a reasonable amount of initial actuator characterization and potential for optimizing ensemble selection or data collection strategies, this research establishes a foundational approach for creating resilient soft robots that can autonomously adapt to unforeseen damages and repairs, pushing closer to widespread applicability.
