# Natural Functional Gradients for Smooth Trajectory Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Korea University introduced Natural Functional Gradients (NFG), a function-space trajectory optimization framework that generates intrinsically smooth and collision-free robotic motions. The method achieves 100% success rates in challenging synthetic and simulated manipulation tasks, producing smoother trajectories in constrained environments compared to baselines, and operates effectively with black-box objective evaluations.

## Problem

- Existing robotic motion planning methods often face a trade-off between ensuring geometric feasibility (collision avoidance) and temporal regularity (smoothness) of trajectories.
- Traditional gradient-based optimization struggles with non-differentiable cost functions, such as collision detection, which are common in real-world robotic scenarios.
- Many trajectory optimization techniques rely on time discretization, making intrinsic control over trajectory regularity difficult and dependent on parameter tuning.

## Method

- The framework models trajectories as continuous functions in a ==Hilbert space==, enabling a resolution-independent and intrinsic approach to trajectory regularity.
- It employs ==Gaussian smoothing== on an exponentially transformed objective functional, allowing for ==zeroth-order optimization== with black-box objective evaluations (e.g., from collision checkers) and making non-smooth landscapes tractable.
- **Natural Functional Gradients (NFG)** are derived and estimated via ==Monte-Carlo sampling==, providing update rules that inherently preserve trajectory smoothness and offer a more global perspective on the optimization landscape.

## Results

- NFG achieved a **100% success rate** in a synthetic narrow-passage task and various simulated cabinet insertion tasks, outperforming STOMP, CHOMP, and MPPI in feasibility and producing smoother motions than cuRobo in constrained scenarios.
- The method successfully demonstrated real-world robotic manipulation, performing a stable and smooth object transfer into a narrow compartment on a cluttered bookshelf.
- Theoretical guarantees were established, including the first global convergence-rate result of **O(d_eff * epsilon⁻⁴)** for trajectory optimization formulated directly in function space.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing directly in function space, rather than discretized waypoints, allows for intrinsic control over trajectory smoothness, making it an inherent property of the optimization process.
> - Gaussian smoothing coupled with an exponential objective transformation effectively regularizes non-smooth, non-convex cost landscapes, enabling robust optimization even when only black-box access to costs is available.
> - Natural functional gradients leverage the geometry of the function space to generate inherently smooth and effective update directions, helping to avoid local minima in highly constrained environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Context and Challenges

Robotic motion generation, particularly in manipulation tasks, frequently encounters the challenge of producing collision-free and smooth trajectories. This challenge is amplified in cluttered environments or narrow passages where the feasible configuration space is highly constrained and fragmented. A critical aspect of successful robotic manipulation is ensuring both the geometric feasibility (collision avoidance, kinematic limits) and the temporal regularity (smoothness) of trajectories, as the latter directly impacts tracking accuracy and robustness during real-world execution.

#### 1.2. Existing Approaches and Limitations

Current approaches to motion planning and trajectory optimization generally present a trade-off between feasibility and smoothness:
- **Sampling-based planners** (e.g., Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM), Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT)) are effective at exploring high-dimensional, non-convex configuration spaces to find collision-free paths. However, their outputs are typically piecewise-linear paths that necessitate additional post-processing for smoothing before execution.
- **Gradient-based trajectory optimization methods** (e.g., Covariant Hamiltonian Optimization for Motion Planning (CHOMP), TrajOpt) aim to generate smooth motions directly by optimizing local cost information. These methods often depend on analytic or approximated gradients and can be susceptible to local minima or sensitive to initialization in complex, fragmented feasible regions. Furthermore, they struggle when collision checking or contact-rich dynamics introduce non-differentiabilities.
- **Stochastic and gradient-free optimization methods** (e.g., Stochastic Trajectory Optimization for Motion Planning (STOMP), Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), evolutionary algorithms) relax differentiability requirements by estimating updates from sampled perturbations. While flexible with black-box objectives, most operate on finite-dimensional trajectory parameterizations and enforce smoothness indirectly, often through discretization-dependent penalties.
- **Inference-based or Gaussian Process (GP) planning approaches** integrate smoothness priors using GP trajectory models or Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS). These methods typically rely on model-dependent or variational/analytic gradients.

A persistent difficulty across these methods is the often-separate handling of feasibility and trajectory regularity, leading to solutions that might be collision-free but jerky, or smooth but prone to local collisions. The absence of reliable analytic gradients in many real-world robotic scenarios further complicates gradient-based approaches.

#### 1.3. Contribution to the Landscape

This work positions itself at the intersection of function-space trajectory optimization, Gaussian smoothing, and gradient-free robotic motion generation. By adopting a function-space perspective and employing natural functional gradients induced by Gaussian smoothing, the proposed method seeks to address the limitations of existing techniques. It aims to intrinsically control trajectory regularity through the geometry of function space, independently of time discretization, while accommodating black-box objective evaluations that characterize many real-world robotic tasks. This approach endeavors to overcome the reliance on analytic gradients and discretization-dependent smoothness penalties inherent in many prior smooth trajectory optimization methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Primary Goal

The primary objective of this research is to develop a trajectory optimization framework capable of reliably generating both collision-free and smooth motions for robotic manipulation, particularly in challenging environments characterized by cluttered spaces and narrow geometric clearances.

#### 2.2. Challenges in Robotic Motion Planning

The motivation for this work stems from several fundamental challenges observed in current robotic motion planning paradigms:
1. **Feasibility-Smoothness Trade-off**: Existing methods often struggle to simultaneously satisfy collision avoidance (feasibility) and temporal regularity (smoothness) constraints. Sampling-based planners prioritize feasibility but yield unsmooth paths, while gradient-based optimizers produce smooth paths but are prone to local minima and require differentiable cost functions.
2. **Sensitivity to Non-differentiable Costs**: In practical robotics, costs related to collision detection, contact dynamics, and hard feasibility checks are frequently non-differentiable or only accessible via black-box simulators. This renders traditional gradient-based optimization methods ineffective or unreliable.
3. **Discretization Dependence**: Many trajectory optimization methods discretize trajectories into finite-dimensional vectors, leading to smoothness properties that are dependent on the chosen time discretization. This can make controlling intrinsic trajectory regularity difficult and require tuning of regularization parameters.
4. **Local Optima**: In highly constrained and fragmented feasible regions, local optimization methods can easily get stuck in sub-optimal solutions or fail to find any feasible path.

#### 2.3. Specific Motivations for the Proposed Approach

To address these challenges, the authors were motivated to:
1. **Adopt a Function-Space Perspective**: Model trajectories as continuous functions within a Hilbert space rather than finite-dimensional waypoint sequences. This allows for a resolution-independent notion of regularity.
2. **Incorporate Intrinsic Smoothness**: Control trajectory regularity through the induced geometry of the function space (specifically, Gaussian kernels), ensuring that smooth perturbations are generated inherently, independent of a particular time discretization.
3. **Enable Zeroth-Order Optimization**: Develop a method that requires only black-box evaluations of the objective functional, thereby remaining applicable when analytic gradients are unavailable, unreliable, or computationally expensive due to collision checking and complex simulations.
4. **Leverage Gaussian Smoothing**: Utilize Gaussian smoothing to regularize the optimization landscape, effectively averaging over local perturbations to make non-smooth or non-convex objectives more tractable.
5. **Derive Natural Functional Gradients**: Formulate update rules based on natural functional gradients within the Cameron-Martin space, which ensures geometry-aware updates that align with the covariance structure of the perturbations.
6. **Provide Theoretical Guarantees**: Establish convergence properties for the proposed function-space, zeroth-order optimization framework to provide a rigorous theoretical foundation.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to deliver a trajectory optimization framework that offers enhanced feasibility, intrinsic smoothness, and robustness in complex robotic manipulation scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a function-space trajectory optimization framework based on natural functional gradients and Gaussian smoothing. This approach operates directly in a continuous function space, enabling intrinsic control over trajectory regularity and compatibility with zeroth-order objective evaluations.

#### 3.1. Trajectory Representation in Hilbert Space

The method models continuous-time trajectories, denoted ξ : [0, T] → R^d, as elements of a separable real Hilbert space H. This abstract representation is resolution-independent, meaning time discretization is used solely for numerical approximation rather than defining the inherent optimization geometry. The choice of H (e.g., Sobolev spaces or Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHSs)) encodes a specific notion of trajectory regularity, such as smoothness.

#### 3.2. Zeroth-Order Objective Functional

The core objective is defined by a functional f : H → R ∪ {-∞}, which aggregates task objectives, control effort, collision penalties, and environment-specific costs. A key assumption is that f is not necessarily differentiable, nor are analytic gradients required. Instead, the method assumes only "zeroth-order access," meaning f(ξ) can be evaluated for any given trajectory ξ, typically through collision checking or simulation rollouts. Feasibility constraints are incorporated by assigning f(ξ) = -∞ for any trajectory ξ falling outside the feasible set S.

#### 3.3. Gaussian Smoothing and Surrogate Objective

To handle non-smooth and non-convex objectives, the method employs Gaussian smoothing.
1. **Exponential Transformation**: An exponentially transformed functional, g_Npow(ξ) := exp(N_pow * f(ξ)) with N_pow > 0, is introduced. This transformation amplifies relative differences between trajectories and enforces feasibility through an indicator function, inducing a homotopy-like effect where increasing N_pow concentrates the objective around high-cost trajectories.
2. **Gaussian Perturbations**: Gaussian perturbations ε ∼ N(0, σ²Σ) are applied, where Σ is a self-adjoint, positive, trace-class, and injective covariance operator in H, and σ > 0 controls the perturbation magnitude. The covariance operator Σ shapes the temporal and dimensional variations of perturbations, thereby influencing the smoothness of updates.
3. **Smoothed Surrogate Objective**: The Gaussian-smoothed surrogate objective is defined as F_Npow(μ) := E[g_Npow(μ + ε)], where μ represents the current mean trajectory. This objective regularizes the optimization landscape by averaging over local perturbations.

#### 3.4. Natural Functional Gradient (NFG)

The NFG is derived within the Cameron-Martin space HΣ, which is the range of Σ^(1/2). This space provides the intrinsic geometry along which Gaussian measures can be differentiated.
1. **Directional Derivative**: The directional derivative of F_Npow(μ) is shown to exist along directions h ∈ HΣ.
2. **Closed-Form Gradient**: A unique natural functional gradient ∇_HΣ F_Npow(μ) ∈ HΣ is established, given by the closed-form expression: ∇_HΣ F_Npow(μ) = σ⁻² E[g_Npow(μ + ε) ε]. This expression demonstrates that the gradient is an expectation of the perturbations weighted by their exponentiated objective values, allowing for zeroth-order estimation.

#### 3.5. Monte-Carlo Estimation and Update Rule

In practical implementation, the expectation in the NFG formula is approximated using Monte-Carlo sampling.
1. **Estimator**: B i.i.d. samples ε^(1)..., ε^(B) are drawn from N(0, σ²Σ). The unbiased estimator for the NFG is computed as b∇_HΣ F_Npow(μ) = (1 / (Bσ²)) Σ_b=1^B g_Npow(μ + ε^(b)) ε^(b).
2. **Gradient Ascent**: Trajectory updates are performed via an explicit natural-gradient ascent step: μ_{k+1} = μ_k + η_k * b∇_HΣ F_Npow(μ_k), where η_k is the step size. Since the update direction resides in HΣ, trajectory smoothness is inherently preserved by the method's construction.

#### 3.6. Numerical Discretization and RKHS Implementation

For practical application, trajectories are discretized on a finite time grid {t_i}_m_i=1.
1. **RKHS Kernel**: The covariance operator Σ is implemented using a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) kernel k: [0, T] × [0, T] → R, generating a kernel matrix K. A regularized kernel matrix K_λ = K + λI is used for numerical stability.
2. **Perturbation Sampling**: Gaussian perturbations are sampled in this discretized space as ε^(s) = σ L z^(s), where L is the Cholesky factor of K_λ, and z^(s) ~ N(0, I_m). This generates perturbations consistent with N(0, σ²K_λ).
3. **Resolution-Agnostic**: Although numerically discretized, the method's theoretical foundation remains in continuous Hilbert space, ensuring that changes in discretization do not alter the underlying optimization problem's geometry.

#### 3.7. Theoretical Framework and Convergence Guarantees

The paper provides theoretical guarantees for the Gaussian-smoothed natural functional gradient ascent:
1. **Directional Ascent (Theorem 1)**: For sufficiently large N_pow, the natural functional gradient of the surrogate objective is directionally aligned with the global optimum ξ*, ensuring strict ascent towards ξ* outside an ϵ-neighborhood. This implies the absence of spurious stationary points far from the optimum.
2. **Lipschitz Continuity (Lemma 4)**: The natural functional gradient is shown to be Lipschitz continuous along Cameron-Martin directions, with a constant dependent on the boundedness of g_Npow and σ.
3. **Monte-Carlo Estimator Properties (Lemma 5)**: The projected Monte-Carlo natural gradient estimator is unbiased, and its second moment is bounded, scaling with the effective dimension d_eff.
4. **Iteration Complexity (Theorem 2)**: The method achieves a global iteration complexity bound of O(d_eff * ε⁻⁴) for reaching ε-stationarity, which aligns with standard rates for zeroth-order Gaussian smoothing methods. This is presented as the first global convergence-rate result for trajectory optimization directly formulated in function space.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed Natural Functional Gradient (NFG) trajectory optimizer was evaluated across a range of progressively constrained robotic manipulation tasks, from synthetic environments to real-world demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Synthetic Narrow-Passage Experiment

- **Environment**: A one-dimensional trajectory optimization problem requiring navigation through a narrow, time-fragmented collision-free corridor. The objective combined collision avoidance with a bonus for low trajectory jerk.
- **Feasibility (Success Rate)**: NFG achieved a 100% success rate, significantly outperforming baselines: STOMP (20%), CHOMP (20%), and MPPI (60%). This demonstrated NFG's ability to consistently find feasible paths in challenging, fragmented feasible regions.
- **Smoothness (Average Jerk)**: NFG produced an average jerk of 90.91 ± 33.43 m/s³ on successful trials. While STOMP and CHOMP had lower average jerk on their few successful trials, their low success rates made a direct comparison across all runs less representative. MPPI, despite a higher success rate than STOMP/CHOMP, yielded significantly higher jerk (621.22 ± 86.07 m/s³). NFG maintained a balance between feasibility and regularity.
- **Optimization Time**: NFG's average runtime was 0.73 ± 0.03 seconds, comparable to STOMP (0.63s) and MPPI (0.37s), but slower than CHOMP (0.02s).

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments in Cabinet Environments

- **Environment Setup**: A Franka Research 3 manipulator performing a cylindrical object insertion task into cabinets with varying geometric constraints: free space, fully open, quarter-closed, and half-closed. The objective combined collision avoidance (based on simulation penetration depth) with joint-space path length regularization.
- **Unified Evaluation Protocol**: To ensure fair comparison, all planner outputs were converted into a unified time-parameterized joint-space trajectory and re-evaluated in a MuJoCo simulator, performing collision checks and jerk computations at 100 Hz. This addressed discrepancies arising from different internal representations and collision models of baseline planners.
- **Feasibility (Success Rate)**:
 - NFG achieved a 100% success rate across all four environments, including the most constrained "half-closed" setting, demonstrating its robustness in maintaining feasibility under tight clearances.
 - BiTRRT, STOMP, and CHOMP showed decreasing success rates as constraints increased. STOMP failed in all constrained environments (0% success for fully open, quarter-closed, half-closed). CHOMP also failed in quarter-closed and half-closed environments.
 - cuRobo also maintained a 100% success rate across all environments.
- **Smoothness (Average Jerk)**:
 - For the constrained environments, NFG generally produced lower average jerk compared to cuRobo. For the "half-closed" environment, NFG's average jerk was 840.40 ± 370.27 rad/s³, while cuRobo's was 1177.27 ± 79.62 rad/s³.
 - STOMP and CHOMP, when successful in easier environments, sometimes exhibited lower jerk, but their high failure rates in constrained scenarios limited their overall utility.
 - NFG's joint trajectories consistently exhibited gradual configuration changes, avoiding abrupt transitions near constrained regions.
- **Optimization Time**: cuRobo demonstrated significantly faster optimization times (e.g., 0.02s for fully open) compared to NFG (e.g., 4.10s for fully open, escalating to 166.10s for half-closed). NFG's computation time increased with environment complexity due to Monte-Carlo estimation in challenging landscapes.

#### 4.3. Real-World Robotic Demonstration

- **Task**: A Doosan Robotics A0912 manipulator transferred a cylindrical object into a narrow compartment on a cluttered bookshelf.
- **Outcome**: The robot successfully completed the transfer and insertion task, exhibiting stable and smooth motion even when operating close to constrained shelf geometry. This validated the practical applicability and robust execution behavior of the NFG framework in a physical system.

#### 4.4. Theoretical Contributions

The paper formally established theoretical guarantees for the proposed method:
- Demonstrated that exponential smoothing induces an optimization landscape oriented towards global optimality, eliminating spurious stationary points (Theorem 1).
- Derived a global iteration-complexity bound of O(d_eff * ε⁻⁴) for achieving ε-stationarity (Theorem 2), which is consistent with standard rates for zeroth-order Gaussian smoothing methods. This represents a significant theoretical contribution as the first global convergence-rate result for trajectory optimization formulated directly in function space.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Addressing Core Challenges

The research offers a principled solution to the long-standing challenge of generating both smooth and collision-free motions for robotic manipulators in complex, cluttered, and geometrically constrained environments. By formulating trajectory optimization directly in function space and employing natural functional gradients, the method intrinsically handles trajectory regularity, addressing the limitations of approaches that rely on post-processing for smoothness or struggle with local minima.

#### 5.2. Methodological Advancements

The proposed framework introduces several methodological advancements:
1. **Function-Space Optimization with Intrinsic Smoothness**: Optimizing directly in a Hilbert space with a kernel-induced geometry allows trajectory regularity to be an inherent property of the updates, rather than a parameter-tuned regularization term or a post-processing step. This provides a more fundamental approach to controlling smoothness.
2. **Zeroth-Order Capability for Black-Box Costs**: The method's ability to operate effectively with only black-box objective evaluations (e.g., from collision checkers or high-fidelity simulators) is a significant advantage. This bypasses the critical limitation of gradient-based methods that require differentiable or reliably approximated gradients, expanding applicability to a wider range of real-world, non-ideal robotic systems and tasks involving contact.
3. **Robustness in Constrained Environments**: The use of Gaussian smoothing and natural functional gradients enables the optimization to navigate fragmented and narrow feasible regions more effectively, as demonstrated by the high success rates in challenging synthetic and simulated cabinet environments. The framework aggregates information from sampled perturbations, offering a more global perspective than purely local gradient approaches.

#### 5.3. Empirical Performance

The experimental results consistently show that the proposed method improves trajectory feasibility and produces smoother motions compared to representative planning and trajectory optimization baselines in environments with narrow geometric clearances. The high success rates in constrained simulation tasks and the successful real-world robotic demonstration highlight the practical utility and reliability of the NFG approach for complex manipulation scenarios.

#### 5.4. Theoretical Rigor

The establishment of global convergence guarantees in function space, along with an iteration-complexity bound, provides a robust theoretical foundation for the method. This contributes to the broader understanding of function-space optimization and its application to robotic control.

#### 5.5. Future Outlook and Limitations

While demonstrating significant potential, the paper also identifies areas for future work and inherent limitations:
1. **Computational Cost**: The primary limitation is the computational expense associated with Monte-Carlo estimation of the natural functional gradient, especially in high-dimensional or time-critical applications. Future work could focus on improving sample efficiency, potentially by adapting sampling distributions or leveraging massively parallel computation (e.g., GPU acceleration) to enhance practical throughput.
2. **Covariance Operator Design**: The performance of the method depends on the chosen covariance operator (kernel) for generating perturbations. Designing adaptive or task-dependent covariance operators that can better reflect specific environmental geometry, contact dynamics, or task constraints remains an open research direction.

In conclusion, this research provides a robust and principled framework for robotic trajectory optimization. By harmonizing feasibility and intrinsic smoothness through function-space natural functional gradients and zeroth-order optimization, it contributes to the development of more capable, reliable, and adaptable robotic systems for complex real-world tasks.
