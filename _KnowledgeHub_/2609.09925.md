# Time-Frequency Geometric Cross-Attention for Chunked Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> TFGCA is a drop-in module inserted between a chunked VLA's transformer output and its action head. It projects hidden action tokens to control space, decomposes each dimension with a per-dimension learnable stationary wavelet transform, and lets time tokens cross-attend to the resulting time-frequency tokens via a learnable blend of dot-product and wedge-product scores. A zero-initialized residual reproduces the base at attachment; gains reach +28.5 average under RoboTwin domain randomization.

## Problem

- Chunked VLA policies emit short multivariate trajectories but represent them as generic per-timestep hidden tokens decoded by one linear head, leaving motion structure implicit.
- A single hidden token entangles slow transport motion with fast contact corrections, so prior policies get no explicit handle on trend versus correction.
- Dot-product attention scores only alignment and is least sensitive near orthogonality, so cross-phase relations between reach, contact and settling must be relearned indirectly.

## Method

- **TFGCA** applies action-space projection plus a per-dimension learnable ==stationary wavelet transform== (db2 init, DC removal, optional finite differences) turning each control dimension into length-preserving multi-resolution frequency tokens.
- ==Geometric cross-attention==: time tokens query the chunk's time-frequency tokens, blending softmax(dot) and softmax(==wedge-product magnitude==) with a learnable scalar beta initialized at 0.5.
- ==Zero-initialized output projection== gives identity at attachment, plus an auxiliary stop-gradient ==alignment loss== supervising projected tokens toward ground-truth actions; 4.23M added params, 0.10% of the backbone.

## Results

- LIBERO four-suite average rises **96.7** to **98.2** (**+1.5**) over the reproduced base, with the hardest Long suite going **94.6** to **97.0**.
- LIBERO-Plus Total improves **66.7** to **73.0** (**+6.3**), winning **six of seven** perturbation families; removing the wedge channel costs most OOD, dropping to **69.7**.
- RoboTwin randomized six-task mean jumps **14.2** to **42.7** (**+28.5**), clean **+3.7**; real AgiBot A2 success rises **50.00%** to **61.67%** over **60 trials**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Wedge-product magnitude is a cheap second attention channel: it scores near-orthogonality in closed form, where a dot product needs a quadratic feature lift.
> - Component importance flips with distribution shift: the alignment loss matters most in-distribution, the geometry channel matters most under out-of-distribution perturbation.
> - Frequency decomposition needs horizon: at T=10 with one wavelet level the branch degenerates to a trend/detail split and contributes little.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision–language–action (VLA) models connect visual observations and language instructions to robot control actions. Recent systems increasingly predict action chunks rather than individual control commands. A chunk contains several future actions, typically covering approximately one or two seconds, and the robot executes part of that prediction before replanning. This design is used by both generative policies, such as diffusion and flow-matching models, and transformer-based action-chunking methods such as ACT.

Action chunking provides temporal consistency and enables short-horizon coordination, but it also creates a representation problem. The output is a multivariate trajectory with temporal, frequency, and cross-dimensional structure, whereas many VLA models represent the chunk as one generic hidden token per timestep and use a linear action head to decode those tokens. The paper argues that this representation does not explicitly expose two properties of manipulation trajectories: multiple temporal scales and relationships between temporally separated phases that may occupy different directions in representation space.

The proposed method relates to two existing research areas. First, time-series forecasting has used Fourier, seasonal–trend, and wavelet decompositions to separate temporal scales. FAST, for example, applies a discrete cosine transform to action chunks for compact tokenization, but its purpose is compression and discrete autoregressive modeling rather than continuous cross-attention in a flow-matching policy. Second, geometric attention methods use geometric quantities in addition to ordinary inner products. TFGCA adopts only the magnitude of the wedge product as a scalar attention signal, rather than implementing a full geometric-algebra or Clifford-valued network.

The work therefore occupies an intermediate position: it preserves the standard VLA architecture and training objective while adding an explicit trajectory-processing module immediately before the action head.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to improve the representation and prediction of chunked robot actions by making two types of structure directly available to the policy.

#### 2.1. Representing multi-scale motion

A manipulation trajectory may include slow, global motion alongside rapid corrective movements. For example, a robot may transport an end effector toward an object while making faster adjustments during contact, grasping, or placement. In a conventional action-token representation, these components are mixed within the same timestep-level hidden vectors.

The paper uses a stationary wavelet transform (SWT) to produce time-localized frequency representations. Unlike a decimated wavelet transform, an SWT preserves the original sequence length in every frequency band. This allows each time–frequency position to remain aligned with the action chunk and to function directly as an attention key or value.

#### 2.2. Representing cross-phase geometric relationships

Manipulation often proceeds through ordered phases such as reaching, contacting, grasping, adjusting, and settling. These phases may involve different control directions. The authors argue that some phase relationships are approximately orthogonal rather than aligned in the relevant representation space.

Standard dot-product attention is designed to assign high scores to aligned query and key vectors. It produces a low score for orthogonal vectors, even though orthogonal directions may represent complementary roles in a task. The proposed solution adds the magnitude of the wedge product, which is zero for parallel vectors and largest for orthogonal vectors of fixed norm.

The paper introduces the concept of Temporal Orthogonal Division-of-Labor (TO-DoL) to describe sequentially occurring phases that occupy distinct, approximately orthogonal subspaces. Analysis of RoboTwin demonstrations suggests that strong same-instant multi-phase orthogonality is uncommon, while sequential phase structure is more evident. However, the authors characterize this as a motivation and inductive hypothesis rather than definitive causal evidence that the wedge channel is responsible for the observed performance gains.

#### 2.3. Safe integration with pretrained VLAs

A further objective is to make the method attachable to an existing pretrained VLA without changing its initial behavior. TFGCA uses a zero-initialized output projection in a residual branch. At initialization, the module therefore reduces exactly to the base model. The paper also ensures that the value path is nonzero so that the output projection receives a gradient and can move away from the identity during fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Base policy and insertion point

The experiments use a flow-matching VLA based on π0.5. Given images, language, proprioceptive state, and a noisy interpolant of the target action chunk, the model predicts a velocity field. The final \(T\) hidden states of the decoder transformer form an action-token matrix \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}\). TFGCA transforms these tokens before the action head produces the predicted velocity.

The method is applied during both training and inference denoising steps.

#### 3.2. Projection into action space

Because hidden dimensions do not necessarily correspond to physical control variables, TFGCA first applies a learned linear projection:

\[
Z^{act} = \text{ActionProj}(H) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times D}.
\]

Here, \(D\) is the number of action dimensions. These dimensions represent Cartesian end-effector increments and gripper state in LIBERO, and joint-space actions in RoboTwin and AgiBot A2.

The projection is trained with an auxiliary alignment loss against the ground-truth action chunk:

\[
L_{align} = \|Z^{act} - \text{sg}(a)\|^2,
\]

where the stop-gradient operator prevents gradients from passing through the target. The complete training objective combines the flow-matching loss with this alignment term. The alignment coefficient is 0.01 for LIBERO and 0.1 for RoboTwin.

#### 3.3. Stationary wavelet tokenization

Each action dimension is processed with a learnable SWT initialized using Daubechies-2 filters. With \(J\) levels, the transform produces \(J\) detail bands and one final approximation band. The approximation band captures slower trends, while detail bands capture progressively finer temporal variations.

The transform includes several design choices:

- The filters are learned separately for each action dimension.
- The temporal mean is removed from each dimension before decomposition.
- The standard deviation is not removed, preserving amplitude differences between active and stationary portions.
- Finite differences can be applied before the SWT. This is used for RoboTwin, where joint trajectories have substantial low-frequency drift.
- At each time and frequency-band position, the full \(D\)-dimensional action vector is embedded into the model dimension \(d\), together with band-type and temporal-position embeddings.

The resulting frequency-token matrix has \(T(J+1)\) tokens. In LIBERO, the chunk length is 10 and one SWT level is used. In RoboTwin, the chunk length is 50, two levels are used, and second-order differences precede the transform.

#### 3.4. Dot-plus-wedge cross-attention

The original time tokens act as queries, while the wavelet-derived frequency tokens provide keys and values. For query \(q_i\) and key \(k_j\), TFGCA computes two scores:

\[
S^{dot}_{ij} = \frac{q_i^\top k_j}{\sqrt{d_h}},
\]

and

\[
S^{wed}_{ij} =
\frac{\sqrt{\|q_i\|^2\|k_j\|^2-(q_i^\top k_j)^2}}{\sqrt{d_h}}.
\]

The second expression is the wedge-product magnitude, equivalent to the area spanned by the two vectors. Separate softmax operations produce dot-product and wedge-based attention distributions. They are combined as

\[
A=(1-\beta)\operatorname{softmax}(S^{dot})
+\beta\operatorname{softmax}(S^{wed}),
\]

where \(\beta\) is a learned scalar initialized at 0.5.

The attended values are projected and added to the original time tokens through a residual connection. The output projection is zero-initialized, so the initial model function is unchanged. The module adds approximately 4.23 million parameters, or about 0.10% of the roughly four-billion-parameter π0.5 backbone.

#### 3.5. Theoretical argument

The paper shows that, after normalization, the dot and wedge quantities correspond to cosine and absolute sine of the angle between query and key vectors. Consequently, they provide complementary information. A dot-product channel cannot prefer a less-aligned key over a more-aligned key when both have positive dot products, whereas the wedge channel can. A learnable blend can therefore represent rankings unavailable to dot-product attention alone at a fixed representation.

The authors explicitly limit this claim. A sufficiently powerful dot-product model could learn a feature transformation that converts an orthogonal relationship into an aligned one, but this may require additional representational capacity or nonlinear feature lifting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. LIBERO

On the four LIBERO suites, the reproduced π0.5 base achieves an average success rate of 96.7%. Full TFGCA reaches 98.2%, a gain of 1.5 percentage points. The largest suite-level improvement is on LIBERO-Long, from 94.6% to 97.0%.

The ablations show lower average performance when individual components are removed:

- Without SWT: 97.7%.
- Without the wedge geometry channel: 97.8%.
- Without alignment loss: 97.1%.

Because LIBERO is already close to saturation, these results demonstrate modest in-distribution gains but do not by themselves isolate the contribution of each mechanism.

#### 4.2. LIBERO-Plus

LIBERO-Plus evaluates models trained on clean LIBERO data under unseen changes in camera viewpoint, robot initialization, language, lighting, background, sensor noise, and object layout. The reproduced base obtains 66.7% overall, while TFGCA reaches 73.0%, an improvement of 6.3 points.

The gains are concentrated in difficult perturbations. Averaged across suites, TFGCA improves performance by 9.4 points for camera changes, 10.5 points for robot initialization, and 13.9 points for sensor noise. Performance is nearly unchanged under lighting and decreases slightly under background perturbations. Removing the geometry channel produces the largest ablation decrease, reducing the total to 69.7%, compared with 72.1% without SWT and 71.9% without alignment. This pattern supports, but does not prove, a stronger role for geometric attention under distribution shift.

#### 4.3. RoboTwin 2.0

RoboTwin policies are trained only on clean demonstrations and tested in both clean and randomized environments. Across six tasks, the base averages 61.3% on clean conditions and 14.2% under randomization. TFGCA reaches 65.0% and 42.7%, corresponding to gains of 3.7 and 28.5 points.

The randomized improvements are especially large for contact- and multi-stage tasks. For example, click_bell increases from 6% to 86%, and stack_bowls_three increases from 6% to 59%. However, not every task improves: handover_block decreases from 13% to 11% under randomization, and several clean-task regressions occur. Given 100 evaluations per task, some smaller differences may reflect sampling variation.

#### 4.4. AgiBot A2 real-robot evaluation

On three real-robot tasks, each evaluated 20 times, the base succeeds in 30 of 60 trials, or 50.0%. TFGCA succeeds in 37 of 60 trials, or 61.67%, for an improvement of 11.67 points. The task-specific gains are 10 points for placing soap, 10 points for placing plush toys, and 15 points for pulling a tissue.

The evaluation provides evidence that the method can transfer beyond simulation, although the sample is limited and the real-robot data and collection procedure are not publicly released.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TFGCA offers a way to incorporate trajectory-specific inductive biases into existing chunked VLAs without redesigning the backbone or changing the flow-matching objective. Its low parameter overhead and identity-at-initialization design could make it practical for fine-tuning pretrained policies, particularly where out-of-distribution robustness is more important than small gains on saturated benchmarks.

The results suggest that explicit time–frequency information and complementary geometric scoring may be useful for manipulation tasks involving contact, sequential phases, and environmental variation. The method is also conceptually compatible with diffusion policies, regression policies, and other continuous chunk predictors, although the experiments focus on a π0.5-based flow-matching implementation.

The evidence remains bounded by several limitations. The strongest improvements occur in selected simulation tasks and relatively small real-robot evaluations. The wavelet interpretation is less compelling for short ten-step LIBERO chunks, where only a single trend/detail split is used. The TO-DoL analysis finds that inter-phase orthogonality is partly explained by disjoint joint-coordinate support, and its temporal coupling appears to reflect population-level action scripts rather than demonstrated real-time feedback between arms. Finally, the paper does not yet provide direct attention-routing evidence showing that the wedge channel attends to the hypothesized cross-phase pairs. Further studies with larger and more diverse real-robot evaluations, broader hyperparameter testing, and causal routing analyses would be needed to establish when each TFGCA component is beneficial.
