# Know Your Self-supervised Learning: A Survey on Image-based Generative and Discriminative Training

> [!summary] Summary
> This comprehensive survey outlines the evolution and current landscape of image-based self-supervised learning, dissecting its methodologies into generative and discriminative paradigms. It elucidates core technical concepts and evaluates modern SSL frameworks, highlighting their ability to learn powerful representations competitive with supervised methods, often using Vision Transformers.

## Problem

- Traditional supervised learning requires vast amounts of manually labeled data, which is often expensive and time-consuming to obtain, limiting scalability and application in data-scarce domains.
- Supervised learning approaches, despite strong performance on benchmarks, are approaching performance plateaus and face inherent limitations due to their dependence on explicit labels.
- The challenge of effectively leveraging abundant unlabeled data for learning robust and generalizable visual representations.

## Method

- The survey examines self-supervised learning as a paradigm that generates supervisory signals from unlabeled data itself, allowing models to learn meaningful representations.
- It categorizes existing image-based SSL approaches into two main groups: ==discriminative SSL== (e.g., contrastive learning, non-contrastive methods, clustering) and ==generative SSL== (e.g., masked image modeling, denoising).
- It details the technical concepts and architectural components common across SSL frameworks, such as ==Siamese networks, stop-gradient operations, momentum encoders==, and various loss functions.

## Results

- Both discriminative and generative SSL methods have achieved performance levels **comparable to or exceeding** supervised learning benchmarks on various computer vision tasks.
- Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as the dominant architecture, particularly for generative SSL methods like Masked Image Modeling, demonstrating improved performance with increased model size.
- Discriminative SSL methods often yield strong results with **linear evaluation** on frozen features, while generative methods typically show superior performance when fine-tuned on downstream tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised learning (SSL) has evolved from diverse pretext tasks (e.g., colorization, inpainting) to sophisticated frameworks like contrastive learning and masked image modeling.
> - SSL methods can be broadly categorized into discriminative approaches, which learn to distinguish instances, and generative approaches, which focus on reconstructing or generating data.
> - Key technical concepts such as Siamese networks, stop-gradient, momentum encoders, and projection heads are fundamental to modern SSL framework design, particularly for preventing representation collapse and improving feature quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm in computer vision, enabling models to learn meaningful representations from unlabeled data. As the field of machine learning continues to advance, researchers have increasingly turned to SSL as a way to overcome the limitations of traditional supervised learning, which requires large amounts of manually labeled data.

As shown in Figure 1, there has been a remarkable increase in interest in self-supervised learning in recent years, with a sharp upward trend beginning around 2018. While supervised learning approaches have shown impressive results on benchmarks like ImageNet (as indicated by the rising top-1 accuracy over the years), they are reaching a performance plateau and face inherent limitations due to their dependence on labeled data.

This comprehensive survey examines the landscape of image-based self-supervised learning, covering both generative and discriminative approaches. The authors, led by Utku Ozbulak from Ghent University, provide a historical perspective on the evolution of SSL techniques, analyze the technical concepts underlying different SSL frameworks, and discuss evaluation methodologies and future research directions.

### 2. Evolution and Growth of Self-supervised Learning

Self-supervised learning has roots in both unsupervised learning and supervised learning. It leverages the inherent structure of unlabeled data to generate supervisory signals, effectively allowing the data to supervise itself. The evolution of SSL in computer vision has been significantly influenced by its success in Natural Language Processing (NLP), where techniques like word embeddings and language models demonstrated the power of learning from unlabeled text.

Early SSL approaches in computer vision focused on designing pretext tasks that could generate supervision signals without requiring manual labels. These pretext tasks included:

As shown in Figure 2, various pretext tasks have been developed for self-supervised learning, including:

1. **Colorization**: Training networks to predict color for grayscale images
2. **Inpainting**: Learning to fill in missing or corrupted parts of images
3. **Geometric transformations**: Predicting how images have been rotated or transformed
4. **Puzzle solving**: Reconstructing images from shuffled patches
5. **Instance discrimination**: Learning to distinguish between different instances and their augmentations
6. **Context prediction**: Predicting the spatial relationships between parts of an image
7. **Masked image modeling**: Reconstructing masked portions of images
8. **Corrupted image modeling**: Recovering original images from corrupted versions
9. **Masked feature prediction**: Predicting masked features rather than raw pixels

The field has evolved from these early approaches to more sophisticated frameworks that can learn powerful representations competitive with those learned through supervised methods.

### 3. Generative vs. Discriminative Self-supervised Learning

The survey categorizes SSL methods into two main paradigms:

#### 3.1. Discriminative SSL

Discriminative SSL methods focus on learning representations that can distinguish between different instances or their transformations. The most prominent approach in this category is instance discrimination, where models learn to recognize that differently augmented views of the same image should have similar representations, while views of different images should have dissimilar representations.

Key discriminative SSL frameworks include:

1. **Contrastive learning methods** (e.g., SimCLR, MoCo): These use positive and negative pairs to learn representations. Positive pairs are different augmentations of the same image, while negative pairs are from different images.

2. **Non-contrastive methods** (e.g., BYOL, SimSiam): These eliminate the need for negative samples by using architectural components like stop-gradient operations or momentum encoders.

3. **Clustering-based methods** (e.g., SwAV, DeepCluster): These assign pseudo-labels to images based on clustering and then train models to predict these pseudo-labels.

#### 3.2. Generative SSL

Generative SSL methods focus on reconstructing or generating images or parts of images. The survey highlights the recent rise of Masked Image Modeling (MIM) as a powerful generative SSL approach, inspired by masked language modeling in NLP.

In MIM, portions of an image are masked, and the model is trained to reconstruct these masked regions. This approach has proven particularly effective with Vision Transformers (ViTs), which can capture long-range dependencies in images.

Key generative SSL frameworks include:

1. **Masked Image Modeling** (e.g., MAE, BEiT): These mask random patches of images and train models to reconstruct the missing content.

2. **Denoising approaches** (e.g., DINO): These train models to recover clean images from noisy or corrupted inputs.

3. **Auto-regressive models**: These predict image content sequentially, similar to how language models predict text.

### 4. Technical Concepts in Self-supervised Learning

The survey explains several key technical concepts that underpin modern SSL frameworks:

1. **Siamese Networks**: Architectures with shared weights that process different views of the same image to produce consistent representations.

2. **Stop-Gradient**: A technique that prevents gradient flow through part of the network, which helps prevent representation collapse in non-contrastive methods.

3. **Delayed Weight Updates**: Using a momentum encoder whose weights are updated as a moving average of the main encoder, helping maintain representation consistency.

4. **Projection and Prediction Heads**: Multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) that transform encoded representations before applying loss functions, allowing the backbone to learn more general features.

5. **Loss Functions**: Various loss functions like contrastive loss, cosine similarity, and KL divergence are used to optimize SSL models.

```python
# Example of a simple contrastive loss implementation
def contrastive_loss(z_i, z_j, temperature=0.5):
    """
    z_i, z_j: normalized embeddings of augmented views of the same image
    temperature: temperature parameter to scale the similarity scores
    """
    batch_size = z_i.shape[0]
    # Compute similarity matrix
    sim_matrix = torch.exp(torch.mm(z_i, z_j.t()) / temperature)
    # Compute loss
    loss = -torch.log(sim_matrix.diag() / sim_matrix.sum(dim=1))
    return loss.mean()
```

### 5. Evaluation Methods

The survey discusses the most common methods for evaluating self-supervised learning models:

1. **Linear Evaluation**: A linear classifier is trained on frozen features extracted by the SSL model, testing how linearly separable the learned representations are.

2. **Fine-tuning**: The entire pre-trained SSL model is fine-tuned on a downstream task, allowing all weights to be adjusted.

3. **KNN Evaluation**: The representation quality is assessed by using a k-nearest neighbors classifier on the learned embeddings.

4. **Few-shot Learning**: Evaluates how well SSL representations transfer when only a small number of labeled examples are available.

The authors note that discriminative SSL methods are typically evaluated using linear evaluation, while generative SSL methods often prefer fine-tuning. This difference reflects the distinct characteristics of these approaches: discriminative methods focus on learning directly useful features, while generative methods may encode information differently that becomes more useful after fine-tuning.

### 7. Technical Implementations

Two key implementation examples from the paper highlight the differences between discriminative and generative approaches:

For generative approaches like Masked Image Modeling, the Vision Transformer (ViT) has become the architecture of choice. In this framework:
1. Images are divided into patches and processed through a ViT
2. A percentage of patches are masked (typically 75%)
3. The model is trained to reconstruct the masked patches from the visible ones
4. A specialized decoding head transforms the embeddings back into patch predictions

For discriminative approaches, a common architecture involves:
1. Two branches (Siamese network) processing different augmentations of the same image
2. Shared backbone networks extract features from both augmentations
3. Projection heads transform these features into a space where the contrastive loss is applied
4. The loss encourages similar representations for augmentations of the same image and dissimilar representations for different images

### 8. Research Directions and Open Problems

The survey identifies several promising research directions in self-supervised learning:

1. **Combining Generative and Discriminative Approaches**: Leveraging the strengths of both paradigms could lead to more robust representations.

2. **Multimodal Self-supervised Learning**: Extending SSL to multiple modalities (text, image, audio) to learn more comprehensive representations.

3. **Efficient Self-supervised Learning**: Developing SSL methods that require less computation and memory, making them accessible for broader applications.

4. **Self-supervised Learning for Specific Domains**: Adapting SSL techniques for specialized domains like medical imaging, where labeled data is particularly scarce.

5. **Theoretical Understanding**: Developing a better theoretical foundation for why certain SSL methods work effectively.

6. **Novel Architectures**: Exploring architectures beyond CNNs and ViTs that might be particularly well-suited for SSL.

### 9. Conclusion

Self-supervised learning has rapidly evolved from a promising research direction to a powerful paradigm that can match or exceed supervised learning in many computer vision tasks. The survey provides a comprehensive overview of the field, categorizing methods into generative and discriminative approaches and explaining the technical concepts that underlie modern SSL frameworks.

The trends identified in the survey suggest that SSL will continue to play a crucial role in advancing computer vision, particularly as researchers develop methods that can learn more effectively from limited data and transfer to a wider range of downstream tasks. The combination of powerful architectures like Vision Transformers with innovative SSL techniques has pushed the boundaries of what's possible without labeled data.

As self-supervised learning continues to mature, it's likely to become an increasingly important component of machine learning workflows, reducing dependence on labeled datasets and enabling applications in domains where labeled data is scarce or expensive to obtain.
