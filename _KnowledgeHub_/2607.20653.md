# PhysCoRe: Physics-Corrected Residual World Models for Material-Aware Deformable Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysCoRe presents a hybrid dynamics model for deformable objects, integrating a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator with neural networks. This approach infers material properties and their confidence from visual observations in real-time, and learns to correct simulation discrepancies, leading to improved prediction accuracy and generalization to novel elastic and elastoplastic objects.

## Problem

- Accurate prediction of deformable object dynamics is challenging for robotic manipulation, requiring models that can generalize to novel materials.
- Physics-based models need precise, difficult-to-measure material parameters and are slow for new objects, while data-driven models lack physical consistency and robust generalization.
- Latent material properties are not directly observable from visual data, and existing simulators often suffer from a "sim-to-real gap" with real-world dynamics.

## Method

- Develops **PhysCoRe**, a hybrid model integrating a differentiable ==MLS-MPM simulator== with a ==Material from Motion (MfM)== module and a ==Residual from Dynamics (RfD)== module.
- MfM infers per-particle material properties (elasticity, plasticity) and confidence from short-window RGB-D observations using a ==graph U-Net== and temporal aggregation.
- RfD applies a learned ==velocity residual== within the MPM's grid update cycle, using a ==sparse 3D U-Net== to correct discrepancies between simulated and real dynamics.

## Results

- PhysCoRe demonstrated superior future prediction accuracy, reducing Chamfer Distance by **43.7%** for elastic and **30.5%** for elastoplastic objects compared to PhysTwin.
- Material inference achieved a runtime of **11.4 seconds**, significantly faster than baseline methods, enabling rapid online adaptation to new objects.
- The Residual from Dynamics (RfD) module improved prediction accuracy, reducing Chamfer Distance by up to **17.8%**, while the Material from Motion (MfM) module's confidence output reliably guided active robotic exploration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a differentiable physics simulator with data-driven modules allows for combining physical consistency with the adaptability and generalization of learned models.
> - Inference of per-particle material properties and associated confidence directly from visual observations enables rapid adaptation to novel objects and provides a signal for active exploration.
> - Learning residual corrections within the simulation loop effectively mitigates the sim-to-real gap, improving dynamic prediction accuracy, especially for complex elastoplastic behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation of deformable objects faces a fundamental challenge in accurately predicting how these objects will deform under interaction. This prediction is crucial for effective robotic planning and control. Current research approaches generally fall into two categories: physics-based modeling and learning-based modeling.

Physics-based methods, such as those employing the Material Point Method (MPM), Finite Element Method (FEM), or spring-mass systems, offer physical consistency and interpretability. However, they typically require precise knowledge of material parameters (e.g., stiffness, plasticity) which are difficult to measure directly from visual observations and often necessitate time-consuming, per-object calibration or optimization processes. This limits their generalization to novel objects and real-time applicability.

Conversely, purely data-driven, learning-based methods, which leverage neural networks (e.g., graph neural networks, vision-based world models), can learn dynamics directly from interaction data. While these methods avoid explicit material parameterization, they are often data-intensive, can struggle with out-of-distribution generalization, and do not inherently guarantee physical consistency, potentially leading to unrealistic predictions. Recent advancements in world models aim to predict object evolution, often using pixel-space or structured 3D representations, but these typically suffice for rigid objects and lack the underlying physical structure needed to account for varying material properties in deformable objects.

This work addresses the limitations of both paradigms by proposing a hybrid approach. It integrates a differentiable physics simulator (MPM) with learned modules. This design seeks to combine the physical plausibility and structural integrity of physics-based models with the generalization capabilities and data-driven adaptability of learning-based methods. Specifically, PhysCoRe aims to overcome the slow, object-specific material identification of physics-based methods and the lack of physical grounding and generalization of purely data-driven ones. It also builds on the concept of residual physics, where learned modules correct systematic discrepancies between idealized physical simulations and real-world dynamics, a technique previously applied to rigid and simpler soft robots, now extended to complex deformable objects within an MPM framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a dynamics model for deformable objects that can accurately predict their behavior under robotic manipulation, exhibiting both physical consistency and broad generalization to novel objects. This model is intended to facilitate robot anticipation and planning in complex manipulation tasks involving deformable materials.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges:
- **Latent Material Properties**: Deformable objects' behavior is governed by underlying material properties (e.g., stiffness, plasticity) that are not directly observable from standard visual (RGB-D) data. An effective dynamics model must infer these latent properties.
- **Generalization to Novel Objects**: Existing physics-based methods often require per-object optimization to fit material parameters, which is computationally slow and prevents generalization to objects not seen during training. The goal is to enable rapid adaptation to new objects without extensive re-calibration.
- **Physical Consistency**: Purely end-to-end learned models often extrapolate poorly and can violate basic physical laws, leading to unrealistic or unstable predictions. The motivation is to retain the benefits of physical consistency inherent in physics simulators.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap**: Even advanced physics simulators use simplified constitutive laws and discretizations, leading to systematic discrepancies (a "sim-to-real gap") between simulated and real-world dynamics. A robust model should account for these unmodeled effects.
- **Need for Confidence Metrics**: When inferring latent properties, knowing the confidence level of the inference can guide further interaction, particularly for online learning and active exploration. The motivation is to provide a reliable measure of this confidence.

In summary, PhysCoRe aims to bridge the gap between accurate, physically plausible simulation and efficient, generalizable material inference. It seeks to create a dynamics model that is accurate, physically consistent, can generalize to unseen objects, and supports online material identification and confidence-guided exploration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PhysCoRe integrates a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) simulator with two feed-forward neural networks: Material from Motion (MfM) and Residual from Dynamics (RfD). The overall framework leverages the strengths of physics-based modeling for physical consistency and learned modules for adaptability and error correction.

#### 3.1. Differentiable MPM Simulator

The core of PhysCoRe is a Moving Least Squares Material Point Method (MLS-MPM) simulator, enhanced with Affine Particle-in-Cell (APIC) transfers. This simulator discretizes the deformable object into Lagrangian particles that interact with a fixed background grid. The dynamics are integrated through discrete time substeps, involving four main stages:
- **Particle to Grid (P2G)**: Particle mass, momentum, and internal force impulses (derived from Cauchy stress and material properties) are scattered to surrounding grid nodes. The Cauchy stress is computed using a Fixed Corotated Elasticity constitutive model, parameterized by per-particle material properties (Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν), which are inferred by MfM.
- **Grid Update**: Grid node velocities are updated by recovering momentum, integrating gravity, applying damping, and enforcing boundary conditions (e.g., gripper contact, ground friction).
- **Residual Correction (RfD)**: Between the grid update and G2P gather, the RfD module injects a learned velocity residual, correcting the analytical grid velocities.
- **Grid to Particle (G2P)**: The updated grid velocities and APIC matrices are gathered back to the particles.
- **Particle Update**: Particle positions are advected, and their deformation gradients are updated, potentially applying a plasticity return map (von Mises Plasticity) based on a per-episode probability inferred by MfM.

#### 3.2. Material from Motion (MfM) Module

MfM is a feed-forward neural network designed to infer per-particle material parameters and associated confidence from visual observations.
- **Input**: The module takes a short window (K=10 frames) of segmented RGB-D observations. Inputs include mean-centered canonical particle positions, per-frame tracked point displacements, tracked point visibility masks, controller positions, controller displacements, and controller visibility masks. These are encoded using Fourier features for continuous values.
- **Architecture**: A graph U-Net processes the spatially encoded features. It incorporates an evidence-weighted message passing mechanism, giving more weight to tracked points and particles near controllers, as these are considered more informative for material inference. A temporal module, combining a depthwise 1D convolution and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) cells, aggregates information across frames to produce a single per-particle latent representation.
- **Output**: A shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) decodes this latent into per-particle material parameters (log E and ν), constrained within sampled physical ranges by a bounded sigmoid. It also predicts a per-particle confidence (c_p) for each parameter. Separately, the per-particle latents are pooled and fed to a smaller MLP to output a per-episode probability (π) of elastic versus plastic behavior.

#### 3.3. Residual from Dynamics (RfD) Module

RfD is a feed-forward neural network that learns to correct the physics simulator's internal dynamics, addressing the sim-to-real gap.
- **Placement**: RfD operates within the MLS-MPM cycle, specifically between the analytical grid update and the G2P gather, at a periodic interval (every H_r = 10 substeps).
- **Input**: For each active grid cell, RfD assembles a feature vector (f_i) that includes the grid velocity, log-transformed mass, symmetric Cauchy stress, P2G-scattered MfM material estimates (log E, ν, confidence), scattered particle velocity, contact indicators, and signed ground distance.
- **Architecture**: A FiLM-conditioned sparse 3D U-Net processes these features. The network uses submanifold sparse convolutions to operate efficiently on the active grid cells. A global context vector (u), summarizing the current simulation regime (e.g., controller velocity statistics, MfM output statistics, substep phase, friction), conditions the U-Net.
- **Output**: The network outputs a grid-velocity residual (∆v_i) for each active cell. This residual is scaled by a maximum magnitude (δ_max) using a tanh function to maintain stability. The output layer is zero-initialized, ensuring RfD starts as an identity correction. The corrected velocity (v'_i = v_i + ∆v_i) is then used in the subsequent G2P and particle update steps, preserving momentum and angular momentum structures of MLS-MPM to first order.

#### 3.4. Material Augmentation

To address the scarcity of supervised spatially varying material data, the researchers generate synthetic training data. This process starts from real PhysTwin episodes, preserving geometry and controller motion. For each episode, spatially correlated material fields (log E and ν) are generated using multi-octave Perlin noise and affinely mapped to per-particle parameters. An MPM simulator rolls out the dynamics with these synthetic materials and the original controller actions. The resulting motion is then re-rendered into per-frame point clouds and particle correspondences, providing ground truth for training.

#### 3.5. Two-Stage Training

The PhysCoRe model is trained in two sequential stages:
1. **MfM Pre-Training**: MfM is pre-trained using the synthetically augmented dataset. The loss function (`L_refine`) combines a log-regularized Smooth-L1 error on normalized predicted materials (weighted by MfM's per-particle confidence) and a class-weighted Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss on the per-episode plasticity probability.
2. **RfD Training**: With MfM frozen, RfD is trained end-to-end on real-world manipulation episodes. The simulator rolls out dynamics in windows of K camera frames. At the start of each window, MfM refreshes its material and confidence predictions. The loss function (`L_corr`) compares the predicted particle configurations to real observations, using a one-sided Chamfer distance on point clouds and a mean L2 error on visible tracked particles. Gradients are truncated at window boundaries to manage the backpropagation graph.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on a dataset of 12 real-world deformable object manipulation episodes, encompassing elastic objects (rope, towel, plush toy) and elastoplastic objects (Play-Doh plasticines), manipulated by human hands.

#### 4.1. Future Prediction

PhysCoRe was evaluated against state-of-the-art baselines: PhysTwin (physics-informed reconstruction and simulation with per-object optimization) and PGND (Particle-Grid Neural Dynamics). Each method identified material properties from the first 50% of an episode (identification window) and then predicted the remainder.
- **Accuracy**: PhysCoRe demonstrated superior overall prediction accuracy. On elastic objects, it reduced Chamfer Distance (CD) by 43.7% and tracking loss by 15.2% compared to PhysTwin. On elastoplastic objects, the improvements were even larger, with a 30.5% reduction in CD and an 8.3% reduction in tracking loss. PhysCoRe maintained competitive visual quality metrics (IoU, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) across object types.
- **Qualitative Results**: Visualizations showed that PhysCoRe's rollouts remained closer to the observed real-world object positions as the prediction horizon lengthened, indicating more accurate future dynamics.
- **Runtime**: PhysCoRe's feed-forward material inference was significantly faster than baseline methods, identifying material in 11.4 seconds compared to 930.0 seconds for PhysTwin and 8280.0 seconds for PGND. This speed makes online adaptation to new objects practical.

#### 4.2. Ablation: Effect of Residual Correction (RfD)

An ablation study isolating the contribution of the RfD module demonstrated its effectiveness:
- Comparing the MfM-only model to the full PhysCoRe (MfM + RfD) model, the addition of RfD further improved prediction accuracy.
- RfD reduced CD by 13.1% on elastic objects and 17.8% on elastoplastic objects. These gains were more pronounced for elastoplastic objects, supporting the hypothesis that RfD effectively corrects residual errors from simplified analytical models, particularly where the sim-to-real gap is larger (e.g., complex contact, friction, irreversible deformation).

#### 4.3. Confidence for Material Identification

MfM outputs a per-particle confidence value for its material estimates.
- **Confidence Evolution**: As more object motion was observed during material identification, MfM's confidence increased, particularly concentrating in regions that had undergone significant deformation.
- **Meaningful Signal**: This self-emerging confidence, generated without explicit supervision beyond a log-regularized material loss, reliably indicated how well the material in specific regions had been identified.

#### 4.4. Confidence-Guided Exploration

The per-particle confidence from MfM was demonstrated as a signal for active exploration in a real-world robotic setup using a KUKA arm.
- **Exploration Guidance**: When the robot probed objects (rope, towel, plush toy), MfM's confidence started low across the entire object. It increased only in the specific regions actively deformed by the arm, remaining low elsewhere. This suggests that the confidence map can directly guide an active exploration policy to areas where further interaction would be most informative for refining material estimates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PhysCoRe presents a significant advancement in the field of deformable object manipulation for robotics. Its key significance lies in successfully integrating differentiable physics simulation with learned modules to achieve:

- **Enhanced Prediction Accuracy**: The framework consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in predicting complex deformable object dynamics, demonstrating a superior match to real-world observations.
- **Physically Grounded Generalization**: By retaining a differentiable MPM simulator, PhysCoRe ensures physical consistency in its predictions. The MfM module enables rapid, feed-forward inference of material properties from visual observations, allowing the model to generalize to novel objects without slow, per-object optimization. This is crucial for real-world robotic applications where objects with unknown properties are frequently encountered.
- **Robustness to Sim-to-Real Discrepancies**: The RfD module directly addresses the inherent limitations of idealized analytical simulators by learning to correct systematic errors within the simulation cycle. This "physics-corrected" approach improves realism while preserving the physical structure of the underlying model.
- **Online Adaptability and Active Learning Potential**: The rapid material inference capability (MfM) and the generation of meaningful per-particle confidence pave the way for real-time online adaptation for robots. The confidence signal offers a novel mechanism for active exploration, enabling robots to intelligently probe objects to improve their understanding of material properties, thus optimizing data collection for learning.

The potential impact of PhysCoRe is substantial for various robotic applications:
- **Robotic Manipulation**: Improved dynamics models lead to more accurate planning and control for tasks involving deformable objects, such as grasping, folding, cutting, or assembly of soft materials.
- **Manufacturing and Logistics**: Robots could handle a wider variety of deformable goods, adapting to different material properties on the fly.
- **Medical Robotics**: Potential applications in surgical robotics, where interacting with soft tissues requires precise understanding of deformation.
- **Research in Embodied AI**: Provides a robust world model for deformable objects, enabling more sophisticated planning and interaction capabilities for intelligent agents.

However, the authors also note limitations. The current implementation focuses on elastic and elastoplastic objects, relying on predefined material behaviors. Extending to more complex dynamics (tearing, cutting, adhesion, fluid-like motion) or achieving zero-shot category-level transfer remains unexplored. The scalability of the residual prediction module to broader dynamics distributions also warrants further investigation.
