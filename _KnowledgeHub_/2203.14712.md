# Assembly101: A Large-Scale Multi-View Video Dataset for Understanding Procedural Activities

> [!summary] Summary
> Assembly101, a large-scale multi-view video dataset, provides 513 hours of synchronized static and egocentric video of unscripted assembly and disassembly tasks, complete with multi-granular action labels, 3D hand poses, and novel mistake annotations. This resource aims to advance research in action understanding and procedural learning for augmented reality applications.

## Problem

- Existing multi-step activity datasets are often domain-specific (e.g., kitchen) or small-scale for assembly tasks, limiting generalization to diverse real-world scenarios.
- Many datasets lack synchronized multi-view recordings (static and egocentric), detailed 3D hand pose annotations, and specific labels for procedural mistakes and corrections, which are crucial for embodied AR systems.
- Instructional videos from online platforms are often 'produced' with mixed viewpoints, fast-forwarding, or unrelated narration, making them unsuitable for continuous, unscripted observation required by smart assistants.

## Method

- Developed Assembly101, a dataset featuring 513 hours of synchronized video from 8 static and 4 egocentric cameras, capturing 53 participants performing unscripted assembly and disassembly of 101 unique toys.
- Provided multi-granular annotations including over 1 million fine-grained action segments, 100,000 coarse action segments, 18 million 3D hand poses, mistake labels ('correct', 'mistake', 'correction'), and participant skill levels.
- Established benchmarks for fine-grained action recognition and anticipation, coarse temporal action segmentation, and a novel mistake detection task using state-of-the-art baselines.

## Results

- Static camera views consistently outperformed egocentric views across all tasks, achieving 16.2% higher overall accuracy for action recognition and 6.5% higher mean frame-wise accuracy for temporal action segmentation.
- Action recognition performance showed a substantial 37% gap between head and tail action classes, with object recognition in tail classes experiencing a 42% drop.
- The novel mistake detection task proved challenging, yielding a recall of 46.6% for 'mistakes' and 29.6% for 'corrections' using video features, with further degradation in early prediction settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A significant domain gap exists between static and egocentric viewpoints, indicating challenges for cross-view transfer learning and requiring robust approaches for AR applications.
> - Procedural activities exhibit a pronounced long-tail distribution of actions, particularly for objects, making recognition and anticipation of less frequent actions difficult.
> - 3D hand pose information demonstrates effectiveness in encoding movement (higher verb accuracy) and less sensitivity to domain gaps from pre-training compared to appearance-based video features, highlighting its complementary value.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of action understanding, encompassing tasks such as action recognition, anticipation, and temporal segmentation, is a significant area of research within computer vision. These tasks are critical for developing intelligent systems, particularly for applications in augmented reality (AR) and smart assistants, where systems need to interpret real-world observations and user intentions.

Prior research has generated various video datasets to support action understanding. These include datasets of short, isolated clips (e.g., Kinetics, Something-Something) and longer sequences derived from movies (e.g., AVA) or scripted activities (e.g., Charades). A substantial portion of existing datasets focuses on the cooking domain (e.g., EPIC-KITCHENS, Breakfast, 50Salads), which involves multi-step activities. Additionally, instructional video datasets curated from online platforms like YouTube (e.g., YouTube Instructional, YoucookII, CrossTask, COIN, HowTo100M) exist, primarily targeting multi-modal learning involving both vision and natural language processing. However, these online videos often feature "produced" content with mixed viewpoints, fast-forwarding, or unrelated narrations, making them less suitable for embodied AR applications that require continuous, unscripted observations.

Furthermore, several limitations persist in the broader landscape of activity understanding datasets:
- **Domain Specificity:** Many multi-step activity datasets are confined to the kitchen domain, limiting generalization to other everyday tasks.
- **Scale and Diversity:** Datasets focusing on goal-oriented, multi-step activities outside the kitchen, such as assembly tasks (e.g., IKEA ASM, Meccano), tend to be small-scale or restricted in their task and sequence diversity, often following strict scripts or recipes.
- **Viewpoint Limitations:** While egocentric (first-person) datasets (e.g., EPIC-KITCHENS, Ego4D) are valuable for wearable computing, they typically lack synchronized third-person views, which could provide privileged contextual information. Conversely, multi-view fixed-camera datasets often do not include egocentric perspectives. The Charades-EGO dataset includes both, but the views are recorded asynchronously.
- **3D Hand Pose Data:** Datasets providing 3D hand pose annotations for fine-grained hand-object interactions (e.g., FPHA, H2O) are generally limited in scale.
- **Mistake and Variation Handling:** Existing datasets do not explicitly focus on detecting mistakes or capturing the natural variations in action ordering and skill levels that occur in real-world procedural tasks. Skill assessment datasets primarily provide overall skill scores rather than specific mistake labels.

Assembly101 addresses these limitations by introducing a large-scale, multi-view video dataset specifically designed for understanding unscripted, procedural assembly and disassembly activities. It integrates synchronized static and egocentric views, extensive multi-granular action annotations, 3D hand poses, and novel annotations for mistakes and skill levels, positioning it as a comprehensive resource for advancing research in action and procedural activity understanding for real-world AR applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the development of Assembly101 stems from the vision of next-generation smart assistants and augmented reality (AR) hardware that can provide embodied guidance and support in everyday tasks. For such intelligent systems to function effectively, they must be capable of jointly considering instructions or goals alongside real-world observations. Current AR applications for tasks like cooking, monitoring worker safety, or learning surgical procedures demonstrate the potential, but require robust action understanding capabilities.

The specific objectives of this research were driven by identified gaps in existing datasets:

1. **To create a large-scale dataset for goal-oriented, free-style procedural activities beyond the kitchen domain:** Existing datasets often feature multi-step activities that follow strictly ordered recipes or scripts. The authors aimed to capture natural, unscripted human behavior during assembly and disassembly, allowing for realistic variations in action ordering, mistakes, and corrections. This focus on "take-apart" toy vehicles introduces a new domain for procedural activity understanding.

2. **To provide synchronized multi-view video recordings:** A significant objective was to record activities simultaneously from both static (third-person) and egocentric (first-person) viewpoints. This unique setup allows for comprehensive spatial and temporal understanding of human-object interactions, facilitates cross-view transfer learning, and enables the investigation of the domain gap between different perspectives, which is crucial for AR applications involving head-mounted displays.

3. **To offer rich, multi-granular annotations:** The dataset aimed to include detailed annotations beyond simple action labels. This involved:
 - **Fine-grained action segments:** Capturing precise hand-object interactions, including pre-contact, interaction, and post-contact phases.
 - **Coarse action segments:** Grouping fine-grained actions into higher-level procedural steps (e.g., attaching a part).
 - **3D hand poses:** Providing detailed 3D pose information for both hands, which is a common output of AR/VR systems and enables research into pose-based action recognition, particularly when visual data is limited or privacy-sensitive.
 - **Mistake labels:** Introducing a novel annotation category to identify when participants make errors during assembly and subsequently correct them. This directly supports the development of AR assistants capable of detecting and intervening during procedural tasks.
 - **Skill levels:** Annotating the skill level of participants to enable research into skill assessment and personalized assistance.

4. **To establish benchmarks for diverse action understanding tasks:** The paper intended to define and benchmark several key tasks on the new dataset:
 - Action Recognition (fine-grained)
 - Action Anticipation (fine-grained, short- and long-horizon)
 - Temporal Action Segmentation (coarse-grained)
 - A newly proposed Mistake Detection task.

5. **To enable investigation of specific research challenges:** The dataset's design was intended to facilitate studies on:
 - Generalization to new, unseen toys and actions (zero-shot learning).
 - Cross-view transfer learning between static and egocentric perspectives.
 - Performance on long-tailed distributions of actions.
 - The relative importance of 3D hand pose versus visual appearance for action understanding.
 - The impact of participant skill levels on activity analysis.

By achieving these objectives, Assembly101 aims to serve as a challenging and comprehensive resource for advancing various activity understanding problems and paving the way for more intelligent, context-aware AR assistants.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors developed **Assembly101**, a multi-view video dataset focused on procedural activity understanding, by meticulously designing the recording environment, participant protocol, and annotation process.

#### 3.1. Recording Rig

A custom desk-based rig was constructed to capture the activities. This setup included:
- **Eight RGB static cameras:** Mounted on a scaffold around the desk, with five overhead and three on the sides. These recorded at 1920 × 1080 resolution.
- **Four monochrome egocentric cameras:** Integrated into a custom-built headset worn by participants, similar to a VR headset. These recorded at 640 × 480 resolution.
All twelve cameras were synchronized using SMPTE timecode and geometrically calibrated to sub-pixel accuracies, ensuring consistent temporal and spatial alignment across views. Participants were recorded either standing or sitting, depending on their height, to ensure visibility of their hands and the toy across all camera views.

#### 3.2. Participants, Toys, and Recording Protocol

- **Participants:** 53 adults (28 males, 25 females) were recruited. Each participant completed an hour-long recording session, typically working with six different "take-apart" toy vehicles.
- **Toys:** The dataset features 101 unique toys from 15 categories, including construction vehicles, emergency vehicles, and other types. These categories offered variations in color, size, and style, with some shared components across categories (e.g., common base components).
- **Protocol:** To capture natural variations in procedural activities, participants were provided only an image of the fully assembled toy as a reference. No explicit instructions or prescribed part ordering were given, fostering free-style assembly and disassembly. Initial recordings revealed that some participants struggled, so the protocol was adjusted: participants first disassembled a pre-assembled toy before proceeding to re-assemble it. This ensured a more efficient recording process while still capturing natural variations.

#### 3.3. Annotations

Extensive, multi-granular annotations were performed for each sequence:
- **Action Labels:** Two levels of granularity were annotated:
 - **Fine-grained actions:** These are atomic hand-object interactions, defined by a single verb (e.g., "unscrew") and an interacting object or toy part (e.g., "bumper with screwdriver"). Each fine-grained action spans three stages: pre-contact (hand approaching object), interaction, and post-contact (object release). A total of 1380 fine-grained action classes were defined from 24 verbs and 90 objects. To enhance semantic richness and manage the long-tail distribution, specific design choices were made, such as enumerating wheel actions (e.g., "position first wheel") and introducing dedicated tool/screw verbs.
 - **Coarse actions:** These aggregate several co-occurring or sequential fine-grained actions into a single higher-level step related to the attaching or detaching of a vehicle part (e.g., "detach bumper"). Coarse actions are contiguous, meaning they do not overlap. There are 202 coarse action classes composed of 11 verbs and 61 objects.
 A custom annotation interface was developed, displaying three static camera views and diagrams of labelled toy parts to assist annotators. Participants were also requested to verbally describe their actions during recording. Annotators underwent four days of training to ensure high-quality and consistent labelling.
- **3D Hand Poses:** 3D hand poses for both hands were estimated from the four monochrome egocentric cameras using a modified version of MegATrack. This process involved fusing features from all views, regressing joint angles and global transformations for each hand, and then obtaining 21 3D keypoint locations per hand (fingertips, joints, palm center) in world coordinates via forward kinematics.
- **Mistake Labels:** For the coarse assembly segments, a parallel set of labels {“correct”, “mistake”, “correction”} was provided. A "mistake" indicates an incorrect procedural step, and a "correction" denotes the action taken to rectify it.
- **Skill Levels:** The skill level of each participant in each video was annotated on a scale from 1 (worst) to 5 (best), based on assembly speed and the number of mistakes observed.

#### 3.4. Dataset Statistics

Assembly101 comprises 362 unique disassembly-assembly sequences, recorded from 12 viewpoints each, resulting in 4321 videos and a total of 513 hours of footage.
- **Duration:** Average sequence duration is 7.1 ± 3.4 minutes.
- **Segments:** Over 1 million fine-grained action instances (average 1.7 ± 2 seconds) and over 100,000 coarse action segments (average 16.5 ± 15.7 seconds).
- **3D Poses:** Approximately 18 million 3D hand poses were estimated.
- **Temporal Dynamics:** The dataset exhibits a repetition score of 0.18 (0.23 for assembly, 0.11 for disassembly) and an order variation score of 0.05, indicating significant temporal complexity.
- **Mistakes:** In assembly sequences, 15.9% of coarse segments are labelled as "mistake" and 6.7% as "correction."
- **Skill Distribution:** The skill levels range from 9% (worst) to 47% (best skilled participants).

#### 3.5. Training, Validation, and Test Splits

The dataset was divided into 60% training, 15% validation, and 25% test splits. The test split ground truths are withheld for online leaderboard evaluations. The splits were structured to facilitate robust evaluation and generalization studies:
- **Seen/Unseen Toys:** 25 of the 101 toys are shared across all splits, while 20 and 16 toy instances in the validation and test sets, respectively, are entirely new to the training set, enabling zero-shot learning evaluation.
- **Head vs. Tail Classes:** Tail classes, defined as action classes accounting for 30% of the training data, comprise 89% (1238 classes) of fine-grained actions and 84% (171 classes) of coarse actions, posing a challenge for models.

#### 3.6. Benchmark Experiments

The authors conducted baseline experiments for four tasks:
- **Action Recognition:** Using pre-trimmed fine-grained action clips, baselines included TSM (video-based) and 2s-AGCN/MS-G3D (pose-based). Metrics: Top-1 accuracy for verb, object, and action.
- **Action Anticipation:** Predicting fine-grained actions 1 second into the future using TempAgg (video-based). Metric: Class-mean Top-5 recall.
- **Temporal Action Segmentation:** Assigning frame-wise coarse action labels to full video sequences using MS-TCN++ and C2F-TCN (video-based, with TSM features as input). Metrics: Mean frame-wise accuracy (MoF), segment-wise edit distance (Edit), and F1 scores at 10%, 25%, and 50% overlap (F1@10, 25, 50).
- **Mistake Detection:** A novel task using TempAgg (video-based, with TSM features) to classify coarse assembly segments as "correct," "mistake," or "correction." Evaluated in "Recognition" (entire segment) and "Early prediction" (half segment) settings. Metrics: Per-class Top-1 precision and recall.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The benchmark experiments on Assembly101 yielded several findings regarding action understanding challenges in procedural activities:

- **Scale and Diversity of the Dataset:** Assembly101 provides 513 hours of multi-view footage across 4321 videos, making it the largest procedural activity dataset to date. It includes over 1 million fine-grained action segments, over 100,000 coarse action segments, and 18 million 3D hand poses. The non-scripted nature of the recordings captured significant natural variations in action ordering, mistakes, and participant skill levels.

- **Action Recognition and Anticipation (Table 5):**
 - **Viewpoint Performance:** Static (fixed) camera viewpoints consistently outperformed egocentric viewpoints. For action recognition, fixed views achieved 16.2% higher overall accuracy, and for anticipation, a 4.9% higher overall recall. This highlights the inherent challenge of egocentric action understanding.
 - **Long-Tail Distribution:** A substantial performance gap of 37% was observed between head and tail action classes for recognition, indicating that models struggled significantly with less frequent actions. The performance drop was more pronounced for objects (42%) than for verbs (18%) in tail classes. Similar trends were noted for anticipation, with head classes showing much higher recall.
 - **Generalization to Unseen Toys:** Performance for "seen" toys was generally higher than for "unseen" toys across both recognition and anticipation tasks, particularly for objects. This suggests that models exhibit difficulty generalizing to novel object instances.

- **Temporal Action Segmentation (Table 7):**
 - **Viewpoint Performance:** Fixed viewpoints again yielded better segmentation performance compared to egocentric views (e.g., 6.5% higher mean frame-wise accuracy, MoF).
 - **Assembly vs. Disassembly Complexity:** Disassembly sequences generally scored higher than assembly sequences in MoF and segment F1 scores. This indicates the higher complexity of assembly tasks, which involve more variations in ordering and potential mistakes.
 - **Long-Tail Challenges:** The MoF for tail classes was significantly lower (7.2%) compared to the overall MoF (39.2%), reinforcing the challenge posed by the long-tailed distribution of actions.

- **Cross-View Transfer (Table 6):**
 - Experiments demonstrated a substantial domain gap between fixed and egocentric views. Models trained solely on one view type showed significantly degraded performance when tested on the other, indicating that features learned from one perspective do not readily transfer.
 - Among static cameras, overhead views (v4, v1) yielded the highest accuracy, while side views (v5, v7) had the lowest. For egocentric cameras, lower-mounted cameras (e3, e4) performed better than upper ones (e1, e2), but still trailed the best static cameras by over 10%.

- **3D Pose-based Action Recognition (Table 8, 9):**
 - **Performance Comparison:** State-of-the-art pose-based models (MS-G3D w/ context) achieved 28.7% action accuracy, which was lower than the video-based egocentric TSM (33.8%).
 - **Modality Strengths:** Pose-based recognition showed higher verb accuracy than video-based recognition (65.7% vs. 59.0%), indicating its effectiveness in encoding movement. However, video-based recognition had higher object accuracy (46.5% vs. 36.3%), as hand poses inherently provide less direct object information.
 - **Potential for Fusion:** Incorporating ground truth object labels with pose-based features significantly boosted action accuracy to 62.0%, suggesting substantial potential for joint modeling of 3D objects and hand poses.
 - **Domain Robustness:** Pose-based models demonstrated less sensitivity to domain gaps from pre-training on different datasets (Kinetics, SSv2, EPIC-KITCHENS) compared to video-based models. Video features pre-trained on other datasets performed significantly worse than those pre-trained on Assembly101, highlighting a considerable domain gap for appearance-based methods.

- **Skill Level (Fig. 5):**
 - Participant skill level showed minimal impact on disassembly sequence segmentation scores.
 - However, for assembly sequences, lower-skilled participants exhibited significantly reduced segmentation scores, likely attributable to increased ordering variations and a higher incidence of mistakes.

- **Mistake Detection (Table 10):**
 - This novel task proved challenging. Even with ground truth coarse action labels as input, recall for "mistake" segments was 62.7% and for "correction" segments 84.9%.
 - Using TSM video features, the recall dropped to 46.6% for mistakes and 29.6% for corrections.
 - An "early prediction" setting, using only half of the segment, further reduced recall (35.0% for mistakes, 26.4% for corrections), indicating the difficulty of anticipating or detecting errors early.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Assembly101 represents a significant contribution to the field of computer vision and activity understanding due to its scale, unique data characteristics, and comprehensive annotations.

1. **Addressing Data Gaps in Procedural Activity Understanding:** The dataset directly addresses several limitations of existing benchmarks. It is currently the largest procedural activity dataset, offering a substantial volume of data (513 hours, over 1 million action segments). Crucially, it moves beyond the predominantly kitchen-centric domain to focus on assembly and disassembly tasks, which are common in everyday life and require a different set of procedural knowledge. The dataset's capture of "free-style" procedures with rich variations in action ordering, mistakes, and corrections provides a more realistic representation of human activity compared to strictly scripted or produced online instructional videos.

2. **Enabling Advanced Research in Multi-View and Multi-Modal Learning:** The synchronized static and egocentric viewpoints are a core feature, offering a unique opportunity to investigate cross-view domain transfer and the fusion of information from different perspectives. This is highly relevant for augmented reality (AR) systems, where head-mounted displays provide egocentric views, but contextual understanding often benefits from third-person perspectives. The inclusion of 3D hand poses further supports multi-modal learning, allowing researchers to explore the interplay between visual appearance and skeletal kinematics, which is valuable for AR/VR systems that often track hand movements. The findings on the domain gap between video and pose modalities, as well as between different camera views, highlight critical areas for future research into robust representation learning.

3. **Advancing Goal-Oriented Sequence Learning and Mistake Detection:** The dataset's detailed annotations, particularly the multi-granular action segments and novel mistake labels, foster research into deeper procedural understanding. The ability to identify "mistakes" and "corrections" is a critical step towards developing intelligent assistants that can actively monitor user performance, identify deviations from a correct procedure, and provide real-time guidance or alerts. This has direct applications in fields such as manufacturing, training, and home assistance. The task of mistake detection, introduced by Assembly101, presents a significant challenge that requires models to learn and retain long-range sequence information and procedural knowledge.

4. **Facilitating Generalization and Robustness Studies:** The dataset's design, including splits for "seen" vs. "unseen" toys and explicit skill level annotations, enables comprehensive evaluation of model generalization capabilities and robustness to new tasks and varying user proficiencies. The long-tailed distribution of action classes also encourages the development of few-shot learning methods and techniques to handle class imbalance, pushing the boundaries of real-world applicability for action recognition systems.

5. **Direct Applications in Smart Assistants and AR:** The envisioned impact is the development of next-generation smart assistants and AR hardware capable of:
 - Accurately recognizing user actions within complex procedural tasks.
 - Anticipating subsequent steps to offer proactive guidance.
 - Detecting non-compliant or erroneous steps and providing timely alerts or corrective instructions.
 - Adapting assistance based on a user's skill level.

In conclusion, Assembly101 provides a rich, challenging, and versatile resource that is expected to stimulate substantial progress in action understanding, procedural learning, and the development of intelligent, context-aware embodied AI systems for AR applications. The presented baseline results indicate that while existing methods show promise, considerable room remains for further research and innovation in tackling the unique challenges posed by this dataset.
