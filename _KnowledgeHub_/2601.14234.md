# Q-learning with Adjoint Matching

> [!summary] Summary
> Q-learning with Adjoint Matching (QAM), developed by researchers at UC Berkeley, introduces a novel method to optimize expressive flow policies in continuous-action reinforcement learning by stably incorporating the critic's action gradient. This approach achieved an aggregated score of 44 on 50 OGBench tasks, with variants reaching up to 46, outperforming all prior methods while maintaining theoretical guarantees for an unbiased optimal policy.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning in continuous-action spaces faces a trade-off between policy expressivity (e.g., multi-step flow policies) and the stability of optimizing these policies using a critic's action gradient.
- Direct backpropagation through the multi-step denoising process of expressive policies often leads to numerical instability, making reliable policy learning difficult.
- Existing workarounds either discard valuable action gradient information, compromise policy expressivity through distillation, or rely on unreliable approximations of gradients at noisy actions, introducing biases and lacking theoretical guarantees.

## Method

- Q-learning with ==Adjoint Matching== (**QAM**) introduces a temporal-difference (TD)-based algorithm that leverages adjoint matching to formulate a stable and unbiased policy optimization objective.
- It directly incorporates the critic's action gradient into expressive flow policies by aligning the policy's velocity field with an optimal state-conditioned velocity field, without unstable backpropagation through the policy's own SDE.
- The method includes two practical variants, QAM-FQL and QAM-EDIT, to relax the behavior constraint and address potential support mismatch with the behavior policy, enhancing performance and exploration.

## Results

- QAM achieved an aggregated score of **44** across **50** challenging OGBench tasks, outperforming all **13** prior methods, with its variants (QAM-FQL, QAM-EDIT) further improving scores to **45** and **46** respectively.
- The specific adjoint matching mechanism proved crucial for performance; direct backpropagation (BAM) performed significantly worse (score of **35** vs. QAM's **44**) even when optimizing a theoretically similar objective.
- QAM-EDIT demonstrated robust online fine-tuning capabilities and strong resilience to data corruptions (noisy/stitched datasets), maintaining performance in scenarios where most baselines failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adjoint matching allows for the conversion of a Q-value maximization problem into a stable, step-wise objective for flow policies, bypassing the numerical instability of direct backpropagation through multi-step denoising processes.
> - The 'lean' adjoint state, computed by stepping backward through a reverse ODE that depends only on a base behavior flow model, effectively incorporates the critic's final action gradient without compounding instability from the learned policy's own gradients.
> - This approach maintains the full expressivity of multi-step flow models and theoretically guarantees convergence to an unbiased, optimal behavior-constrained policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) in continuous-action spaces faces a fundamental challenge regarding the trade-off between policy expressivity and the tractability of optimizing policies with respect to a critic (Q-function). Simple policies, such as single-step Gaussian policies, can effectively leverage the critic's action gradient via reparameterization tricks, but they often lack the expressivity to model complex, multi-modal action distributions. Conversely, highly expressive policy classes like flow or diffusion policies, which generate actions through multi-step denoising processes, are capable of representing intricate distributions. However, directly optimizing these policies using the critic's action gradient via backpropagation through their entire denoising process often leads to numerical instability.

Prior research has addressed this instability through several strategies:
1. **Discarding action gradient information:** Some methods utilize only the critic's value, which can simplify optimization but often results in reduced learning efficiency compared to methods that leverage gradient information.
2. **Approximating or distilling policies:** Other approaches distill expressive multi-step policies into simpler, one-step noise-conditioned approximations. While this bypasses backpropagation instability, it compromises the inherent expressivity of the original flow or diffusion models.
3. **Intermediate fine-tuning/guidance:** A category of methods attempts to use the critic to construct step-wise objectives for the intermediate denoising process. The difficulty here lies in formulating objectives that do not introduce biases or instability. A common intuition, drawing from diffusion classifier guidance, is to apply the critic's action gradient to noisy intermediate actions. However, this relies on the assumption that the critic's gradient at a noisy action is a reliable proxy for its gradient at the denoised action, an assumption that often fails in practice, especially with limited data coverage where the critic is well-trained only on noiseless actions.

This paper positions "Q-learning with Adjoint Matching (QAM)" within the landscape of actor-critic methods, specifically targeting the offline and offline-to-online RL settings. QAM aims to resolve the tension between policy expressivity and optimization tractability by incorporating the critic's action gradient directly into the denoising process of flow policies, while circumventing the instability issues associated with direct backpropagation and avoiding the expressivity compromises or biases of existing approximation methods. It achieves this by leveraging "adjoint matching," a technique recently developed in generative modeling, to construct a step-wise objective for policy optimization. This approach theoretically guarantees that the learned policy at the optimum is unbiased and maintains full expressivity, aligning with an optimal behavior-constrained policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel temporal-difference (TD)-based reinforcement learning algorithm, QAM, designed for continuous-action spaces. This algorithm aims to efficiently optimize highly expressive flow or diffusion policies with respect to a parameterized Q-function, specifically addressing a long-standing challenge in the field.

The motivation for this work stems from the limitations of current approaches to continuous-action RL, particularly when dealing with expressive policies:

- **Instability of Direct Backpropagation:** Expressive policies like flow or diffusion models generate actions through a multi-step denoising process. While these models are capable of representing complex, multi-modal action distributions, directly using the critic's action gradient for policy optimization requires backpropagation through this entire multi-step process. This often leads to numerical instability, making reliable policy learning difficult.
- **Compromises in Existing Workarounds:**
 - **Discarding gradient information:** Some methods avoid instability by completely disregarding the critic's action gradient, relying solely on its value. This sacrifices potentially rich first-order information, leading to less efficient learning and often underperforming methods that utilize gradients.
 - **Sacrificing expressivity:** Other approaches distill expressive multi-step policies into simpler, one-step approximations (e.g., noise-conditioned policies). While this can mitigate backpropagation issues, it inherently limits the policy's ability to represent complex action distributions, thereby compromising expressivity.
 - **Introducing bias or relying on unreliable approximations:** Attempts to guide expressive policies using the critic's gradient at intermediate noisy actions often rely on the assumption that these gradients are good proxies for gradients at noiseless actions. This assumption frequently breaks down, especially in offline settings with limited action coverage, where the critic's gradients might be unreliable for out-of-distribution noisy actions. Such approximations can introduce biases and prevent convergence to the theoretically optimal policy.

QAM is motivated by the need for an RL algorithm that can:
1. **Maintain full expressivity:** Leverage the full capacity of multi-step flow policies to represent complex action distributions.
2. **Incorporate critic's action gradient directly:** Utilize the first-order information from the Q-function for efficient learning.
3. **Ensure optimization stability:** Avoid the numerical instability associated with backpropagation through multi-step denoising.
4. **Provide theoretical guarantees:** Converge to an unbiased, optimal policy (specifically, a behavior-constrained policy), rather than relying on approximations lacking such guarantees.

By leveraging adjoint matching, a technique from generative modeling, QAM seeks to transform the critic's action gradient into a step-wise objective that inherently bypasses unstable backpropagation. This allows the policy's velocity field to align with the optimal state-conditioned velocity field implied by the critic and a behavior prior, without the compounding instability that can arise from direct backpropagation through the policy's own optimization path. The problem is framed within the context of learning a policy that maximizes expected discounted return, primarily focusing on offline and offline-to-online RL settings, where leveraging prior behavioral data is critical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Q-learning with Adjoint Matching (QAM) is a TD-based RL algorithm designed to address the challenges of optimizing expressive flow policies in continuous action spaces. Its core methodology involves leveraging adjoint matching to formulate a stable and unbiased policy optimization objective.

The method operates on a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and is designed for both offline and offline-to-online RL settings, where the goal is to learn a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that maximizes expected discounted return, often under a behavior prior constraint.

#### 3.1. Policy Optimization with Adjoint Matching

1. **Target Policy Formulation:** QAM aims to learn a policy $\pi^{\star}(a|s)$ that maximizes $E_{a \sim \pi(\cdot|s)}[Q(s,a)]$ subject to a KL-divergence constraint $D_{KL}(\pi \| \pi_{\beta}) \le \epsilon(s)$, where $\pi_{\beta}$ is a behavior policy. This problem is known to have an optimal solution of the form $\pi^{\star}(a|s) \propto \pi_{\beta}(a|s)e^{\tau(s)Q_{\phi}(s,a)}$, where $\tau(s)$ is an inverse temperature coefficient and $Q_{\phi}(s,a)$ is the learned Q-function.

2. **Behavior Policy:** A base flow-matching generative model, $f_{\beta}(s, a, t)$, is trained using a standard flow-matching objective ($L_{FM}(\beta)$, Equation 17) to approximate the behavior policy from the offline dataset. This model maps from noise to data (actions) through time $t \in$.

3. **Optimal Policy Parameterization:** The fine-tuned policy is also parameterized as a flow model, $f_{\theta}(s, a, t)$.

4. **Stochastic Optimal Control (SOC) Objective:** The problem of modifying $f_{\beta}$ to generate the desired tilt distribution $\pi^{\star}$ can be formulated as an SOC objective (Equation 18). This objective seeks to minimize the difference between $f_{\theta}$ and $f_{\beta}$ while maximizing the Q-value, and its optimal solution coincides with the target policy $\pi^{\star}$. However, directly solving this SOC equation involves backpropagation through the SDE defining the action trajectory ($a_t$, Equation 19), which can be numerically unstable.

5. **Adjoint Matching Objective ($L_{AM}(\theta)$):** To circumvent the instability, QAM utilizes the "lean" adjoint matching objective derived from Domingo-Enrich et al. (2025) (Equation 14, adapted to Equation 21 for RL).
 - **Lean Adjoint State Calculation:** Instead of backpropagating through the optimized policy $f_{\theta}$, the lean adjoint states $\tilde{g}_t$ are computed by stepping backward through a reverse Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) that depends *only* on the base (behavior) flow model $f_{\beta}$ (Equation 22). The boundary condition for this reverse ODE is $\tilde{g}_1 = -\tau \nabla_{a_1} Q_{\phi}(s, a_1)$, directly incorporating the critic's action gradient at the final (noiseless) action.
 - **Stability Advantage:** This formulation is crucial for stability. In direct backpropagation, any ill-conditioned gradients of $f_{\theta}$ with respect to intermediate actions ($\nabla_a f_{\theta}(s,a,t)$) can compound over the denoising process, destabilizing the entire optimization. In adjoint matching, $f_{\theta}$'s action gradient does not contribute to the overall gradient used for optimizing $\theta$, leading to a more stable process.
 - **Objective:** The $L_{AM}(\theta)$ objective (Equation 21) then measures the squared difference between a term involving $f_{\theta}$, $f_{\beta}$, and the calculated lean adjoint state $\tilde{g}_t$. Minimizing this objective aligns $f_{\theta}$ with the optimal behavior-constrained velocity field.

#### 3.2. Critic Learning

The Q-function, $Q_{\phi}(s,a)$, is learned using a standard temporal-difference (TD) backup objective (Equation 23). The target Q-value for $Q_{\phi}(s,a)$ is calculated using actions $a'$ sampled from the fine-tuned policy $f_{\theta}$ at the next state $s'$.

#### 3.3. Practical Considerations

- **Discretization:** Both the forward SDE (for action sampling) and the reverse ODE (for adjoint state computation) are approximated using discrete steps.
- **Gradient Clipping:** The magnitude of parameter gradients is element-wise clipped for numerical stability.
- **Inverse Temperature ($\tau$):** A state-independent inverse temperature coefficient $\tau$ is used to modulate the strength of the behavior constraint.
- **Pessimistic Value Backup:** An ensemble of $K=10$ critic functions is used, and a pessimistic target value backup strategy (following Fang et al., 2025a), which subtracts a term proportional to the standard deviation of ensemble Q-values, is employed (Equation 26).
- **Joint Training:** The behavior velocity field $f_{\beta}$, the fine-tuned velocity field $f_{\theta}$, and the critic $Q_{\phi}$ are trained concurrently.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Guarantees

The paper states that, based on the work of Domingo-Enrich et al. (2025), QAM inherits theoretical guarantees. Specifically, if the $L_{AM}$ objective is optimized to convergence, the learned policy $f_{\theta}$ is guaranteed to coincide with the optimal behavior-constrained policy $\pi^{\star}(a|s) \propto \pi_{\beta}(a|s) \exp(\tau Q(s,a))$ (Proposition 1 in Appendix G).

#### 3.5. Expanding Constraint Set (QAM Variants)

To address situations where the optimal policy's support may significantly mismatch the behavior policy's support, two practical variants are proposed to relax the behavior constraint:

1. **QAM-FQL (QAM-F):** This variant uses a 1-step noise-conditioned policy $\pi_{\omega}$ (represented by $\mu_{\omega}$) and an objective that penalizes deviation from the QAM-fine-tuned flow policy $\pi_{\theta}$ in terms of a squared Wasserstein distance, while maximizing Q-values (Equation 30). This allows the policy to be "close" to the QAM policy, but potentially explore actions with low probability under $\pi_{\beta}$.
2. **QAM-EDIT (QAM-E):** This variant learns an "edit policy" $\pi_{\omega}$ that predicts an action edit $\Delta_a$ to modify actions output by $\pi_{\theta}$. A constraint $\|\Delta_a\|_{\infty} \le \sigma_a$ (L-infinity distance) is enforced to ensure the policy remains within a certain Wasserstein distance from $\pi_{\theta}$. An entropy term is also included for exploration (Equation 31).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of QAM was evaluated through empirical studies on 10 domains from OGBench, encompassing 50 tasks characterized by long horizons and sparse rewards. These domains include locomotion tasks (antmaze-\*, humanoidmaze-\*) and manipulation tasks (scene, puzzle-\*, cube-\*) that often involve action chunking policies, which generate complex, high-dimensional actions. QAM was compared against 13 representative baselines categorized into Gaussian, Backprop-based, Advantage-weighted, Guidance with critic's action gradient, and Post-processing approaches. Experiments covered both offline RL (1 million training steps) and offline-to-online RL (0.5 million online environment steps).

#### 4.1. Q1: Offline RL Performance

- **Overall Superiority:** QAM achieved an aggregated score of 44 across the 50 tasks, outperforming all prior methods. Its variants, QAM-FQL (QAM-F) and QAM-EDIT (QAM-E), demonstrated further improvements, reaching aggregated scores of 45 and 46, respectively.
- **Importance of Action Gradients and Adjoint Matching Design:**
 - FAWAC, an advantage-weighted method that aims for the same optimal behavior-constrained distribution but does not leverage the critic's action gradient, yielded a significantly lower aggregated score of 8. This highlights the importance of incorporating gradient information.
 - BAM, a direct ablation of QAM that utilizes the SOC objective but optimizes it via backpropagation through the SDE chain (without the "lean" adjoint mechanism), achieved a score of 35. FBRAC, another backprop-based method, performed even worse (score of 11). The performance gap between QAM and BAM/FBRAC suggests that the specific "lean" adjoint matching mechanism is crucial for mitigating backpropagation instability and achieving superior results, even when the underlying objective is theoretically optimal.
- **Comparison to Approximation Methods:** FQL, which addresses BPTT instability by using a 1-step distilled policy, performed similarly to BAM (score of 36). This indicates that while BPTT can be problematic, its sensitivity to implementation details might be variable, and distillation offers a comparable level of performance in some scenarios.
- **Other Baselines:** QSM, an early guidance method, showed competitive performance when augmented with a behavior cloning term. ReBRAC, a Gaussian baseline, performed well on locomotion tasks but generally lagged behind QAM variants and QSM in overall aggregated scores.

#### 4.2. Q2: Offline-to-Online Fine-tuning Performance

- **Robust Online Fine-tuning:** QAM-EDIT, the top-performing variant in offline RL, also demonstrated effective online fine-tuning capabilities. It consistently outperformed all prior methods on tasks such as `cube-triple` and exhibited robustness across various domains.
- **Consistency vs. Inconsistency:** Unlike QAM-EDIT, some other competitive baselines, such as QSM and FQL, showed inconsistent fine-tuning performance. For instance, QSM fine-tuned better on `antmaze-giant` but struggled on other tasks, while FQL showed similar behavior. This suggests QAM-EDIT's approach leads to more reliable adaptation in online settings.

#### 4.3. Q3: Hyperparameter Sensitivity

A sensitivity analysis of QAM-EDIT components (gradient clipping, number of flow steps (T), critic ensemble size (K), and temperature coefficient ($\tau$)) revealed that all contribute to its effectiveness.
- **Temperature ($\tau$):** This parameter, which modulates the influence of the behavior prior, had the most significant impact on performance and requires careful tuning.
- **Gradient Clipping and Critic Ensemble Size:** Enabling gradient clipping and using a large critic ensemble (K=10) were found to be beneficial for stability and performance.
- **Flow Steps (T):** Setting the number of flow steps to T=10 was sufficient, with further increases not yielding substantial performance improvements.

#### 4.4. Additional Results (Data Robustness)

The method, particularly QAM-EDIT, exhibited strong robustness to data corruptions (noisy or stitched datasets from OGBench). For locomotion tasks, performance remained largely consistent. For manipulation tasks, QAM-EDIT showed minimal performance degradation in noisy environments, whereas most baselines (except BAM) failed. This suggests QAM's stability and robust gradient utilization make it resilient to lower-quality data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Q-learning with Adjoint Matching (QAM) as a significant advancement in continuous-action reinforcement learning, specifically for integrating expressive flow policies with Q-learning. The key contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Resolution of a Core Challenge:** QAM directly addresses the long-standing problem of efficiently optimizing expressive multi-step flow or diffusion policies with respect to a Q-function. It circumvents the numerical instability inherent in direct backpropagation through denoising processes, a common limitation in prior work.
- **Theoretical Soundness and Unbiased Policies:** By leveraging adjoint matching, QAM provides a policy optimization objective that, at convergence, is theoretically guaranteed to yield an unbiased, optimal behavior-constrained policy. This offers a stronger foundation compared to many approximation-based methods that lack such guarantees or introduce biases.
- **Enhanced Policy Expressivity:** The method allows for the utilization of the full expressivity of multi-step flow models, enabling the representation of complex and multi-modal action distributions without the need for distillation into simpler, less expressive forms.
- **State-of-the-Art Empirical Performance:** QAM, and particularly its variants QAM-FQL and QAM-EDIT, demonstrate superior performance across a wide array of challenging, sparse-reward, long-horizon tasks in both offline and offline-to-online RL settings. This empirical validation on difficult benchmarks (OGBench) underscores its practical utility.
- **Robustness to Data Quality:** The demonstrated robustness of QAM-EDIT to noisy and fragmented offline datasets indicates its potential for application in real-world scenarios where data quality can be imperfect.
- **Potential for Broader Applications:** While primarily evaluated in offline and offline-to-online RL, the underlying principle of stable gradient utilization for expressive policies could extend to other RL paradigms, such as online RL. The initial success in manipulation domains, particularly with action chunking, suggests promising avenues for real-world robotic applications involving complex motor skills.

**Acknowledged Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge that while QAM effectively leverages critic action gradients, this can be a double-edged sword: an ill-conditioned critic function might still lead to optimization stability issues. They suggest that gradient clipping, as implemented, offers some alleviation, but more principled methods to combine value and gradient information could further improve robustness. Additionally, while the variants address support mismatch with the behavior policy, further research into constraint relaxation and its theoretical implications could be beneficial. Continued exploration of QAM's application in real-world robotic systems, particularly with action chunking policies, is also noted as a promising future direction.
