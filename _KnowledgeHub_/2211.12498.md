# Touch and Go: Learning from Human-Collected Vision and Touch

> [!summary] Summary
> Touch and Go is a human-collected visuo-tactile dataset: people walk through indoor and outdoor scenes probing objects with a GelSight sensor while an egocentric camera records, yielding ~13.9k touches over ~3971 object instances with material labels. It supports contrastive visuo-tactile representation learning, tactile-driven image stylization, and future touch prediction, beating ImageNet and prior tactile datasets on downstream tasks.

## Problem

- Robot-collected tactile datasets stay in one lab space on tabletops, limited to small graspable objects, so each holds under 200 object instances.
- Simulated visuo-tactile data models mostly rigid objects and misses real deformation, squishing, and surface microgeometry like fabric weaves and pores.
- Prior human-collected haptic datasets cover few, mostly flat indoor surfaces, use low-resolution sensors, and were largely unreleased or not used for cross-modal prediction.

## Method

- Two-person in-the-wild capture protocol: one presses a ==GelSight== sensor onto objects, another records approximately ==egocentric== video at ~27 Hz, both streams logged on one machine.
- Annotation pipeline: a ==ResNet-18== binary ==press detector== trims contact frames, and crowd workers assign material categories with five labelers per image plus press-frame correction.
- Three application models: ==Contrastive Multiview Coding== for visuo-tactile features, a ==CUT==-based generator with a ==visuo-tactile discriminator== for tactile-driven stylization, and a ==3D-convolution residual predictor== for future touch.

## Results

- Self-supervised features reach **78.1%** grasp-success accuracy versus **73.0%** for supervised ImageNet, **75.6%** for VisGel, and **69.4%** for ObjectFolder 2.0 pretraining.
- On material understanding the features hit **54.7%** category, **77.3%** hard/soft, and **79.4%** rough/smooth accuracy, all above ImageNet, and improve monotonically as material classes grow from **25%** to **100%**.
- Stylization raises tactile-image CMC similarity to **0.197** from **0.165** unstyled and **0.178** for CycleGAN; adding vision to touch prediction cuts L1 from **0.691** to **0.663** at the **5-frame** horizon.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Humans are a better tactile data-collection platform than robots for diversity: they reach objects robots cannot grasp and record them where they naturally sit.
> - Tactile representation quality scales with material-category diversity, not just touch count, suggesting breadth of surface types is the binding constraint for pretraining.
> - Vision acts as implicit action conditioning for touch forecasting, and its benefit grows with prediction horizon rather than shrinking.
> - Human collection swaps robot bias for human bias: inconsistent pressing force, unrepresentative probe locations, and single-geography coverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The ability to associate touch with sight is crucial for intelligent agents, especially robots, to interact meaningfully with the physical world. However, progress in visuo-tactile AI has been significantly hindered by the lack of diverse, high-quality datasets. This paper directly addresses this critical data bottleneck, positioning itself as a foundational contribution to the field of multimodal perception and robotic learning.

Prior research in visuo-tactile learning has largely fallen into two main categories:
- **Simulated Data:** Efforts like ObjectFolder have attempted to generate large datasets in virtual environments. While scalable, these simulations struggle to capture the full complexity of real-world object properties, such as non-rigid deformation, intricate microgeometry (e.g., fabric weaves, tiny pores), and the nuanced visual appearance variations encountered in natural settings. The authors emphasize that real objects squish, deform, and bend in complex ways that are difficult to accurately model in simulation.
- **Robot-Collected Data:** Datasets like "More Than a Feeling", "The Feeling of Success", and VisGel have been collected using robotic arms equipped with tactile sensors. While providing real-world interactions, these datasets are typically "robot-centric." They are confined to specific lab environments, contain a limited number of object instances (often small objects that robots can easily grasp from tabletops), and suffer from a "chicken-and-egg problem" – the robot must already possess some manipulation capabilities to collect the data. This limits the diversity of objects and interactions.

"Touch and Go" presents a paradigm shift by advocating for **human-collected data in natural environments**. The authors argue that humans can effortlessly access and probe a far wider variety of objects and spaces than robots, capturing interactions "in situ." This approach yields data that more closely resembles real-world encounters and includes complex material properties that are often overlooked in robot-centric or simulated datasets.

By providing a significantly larger and more diverse dataset of paired vision and touch from "in the wild" indoor and outdoor scenes (e.g., classrooms, gyms, streets, hiking trails), "Touch and Go" aims to:
1. **Overcome data scarcity and diversity limitations:** It offers approximately 4x more object instances and 1.15-1.5x more touches than leading existing datasets, spanning a much broader range of materials and environments.
2. **Enable more generalizable visuo-tactile models:** By capturing the nuanced interplay of vision and touch in natural settings, the dataset supports the development of models that can understand and predict physical properties beyond simplified lab scenarios.
3. **Fuel self-supervised learning:** The large volume of paired, unconstrained data is ideal for training models to learn robust visuo-tactile representations without requiring extensive manual labeling.

This work directly builds upon the success of large, human-collected datasets in other multimodal domains (e.g., audio-visual learning) and extends their principles to the challenging domain of haptics. It pushes the boundaries for developing AI systems with a deeper, more human-like understanding of the physical world.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **construct and validate a novel, large-scale dataset of paired visual and tactile data, collected by humans in natural, "in the wild" environments.** This dataset, named "Touch and Go," is designed to address the significant challenges faced by current visuo-tactile learning systems due to the scarcity and limited diversity of available data.

The primary motivations driving this objective are:

- **The Foundational Role of Visuo-Tactile Correlation:** The paper highlights that for humans, the ability to correlate touch with sight is fundamental to understanding the physical properties of objects and interacting with the world. For artificial intelligence and robotics, this multimodal understanding is equally essential for tasks requiring physical interaction.
- **Limitations of Existing Datasets:**
 - **Robot-Centric Bias:** Current robot-collected datasets are typically confined to lab settings, featuring a narrow range of objects (often small, graspable items on tabletops). This leads to a "robot-centric" view of the visuo-tactile world that may not generalize to diverse real-world scenarios. Robots also face a "chicken-and-egg" problem: they need to be capable of interaction to collect data, which limits the scope of what can be learned.
 - **Simulation Limitations:** While simulations offer scalability, they struggle to accurately model the complex physics of real objects, including non-rigid deformation, subtle microgeometry (e.g., the weave of fabrics, tiny pores), and the intricate ways surfaces reflect light or interact with touch. This "reality gap" means models trained solely on simulated data may not perform well in the real world.
- **Leveraging Human Capabilities for Data Collection:** The authors argue that many aspects of the visuo-tactile world are currently best learned by observing human physical interactions. Humans possess an unparalleled ability to access a vast range of environments and objects, probe them intuitively, and adapt to varied geometries and material properties. By capturing data from objects *in situ*, the sensory signals more closely reflect how these objects would be encountered naturally.

Based on this motivation, the paper sets out to demonstrate the utility of the "Touch and Go" dataset across a variety of multimodal learning tasks:
1. **Self-supervised Visuo-Tactile Feature Learning:** To learn robust feature representations by training models to intrinsically associate visual inputs with corresponding tactile signals.
2. **Tactile-Driven Image Stylization:** To explore the potential for synthesizing visual appearance based on tactile information, effectively making an image "feel more like" a given touch signal. This task aims to evaluate the learned cross-modal associations.
3. **Future Touch Prediction:** To investigate whether visual information can enhance the prediction of future tactile signals, given current visuo-tactile inputs, which is crucial for dynamic interaction tasks in robotics.

Ultimately, the overarching goal is to provide a rich resource that can accelerate research in visuo-tactile learning, moving beyond constrained lab settings and simulations to foster the development of more sophisticated and generalizable AI systems capable of understanding and interacting with the complex physical world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the creation and validation of the "Touch and Go" dataset, followed by its application to several challenging multimodal learning tasks.

#### 3.1. Dataset Collection: "Touch and Go" Dataset

The "Touch and Go" dataset was collected with a focus on real-world diversity and human interaction:
- **Human Data Collectors:** The authors themselves acted as data collectors, walking through a wide array of indoor (classrooms, hallways, apartments) and outdoor (hiking trails, playgrounds, streets) environments. This human-centric approach allowed for probing a diverse range of objects *in situ* that would be challenging for robots to access or manipulate.
- **Two-Person Setup:** To ensure high-quality data, two individuals collected data concurrently: one pressing the tactile sensor onto an object, and the other recording an "approximately egocentric" video of the hand and object. This setup facilitated clear, zoomed-in visuals of the interaction.
- **Hardware:**
 - **Tactile Sensor:** A GelSight sensor (a vision-based tactile sensor, ~1.5cm diameter) was used. GelSight works by observing the deformation of an elastomer gel illuminated by colored lights, with embedded markers. The sensor captures changes in reflected illumination, from which surface normal, depth, and shear can be inferred, providing a rich "tactile video."
 - **Visual Sensor:** An ordinary webcam recorded concurrent egocentric video.
 - Both sensors recorded at approximately 27 Hz, synchronized on a single computer.
- **Collection Strategy:** Data collectors aimed for variety in materials (soft/hard, smooth/rough), geometries, and semantics. Each object was typically pressed around 3 times, with an average press duration of 0.7 seconds. Efforts were made to touch parts with complex geometries and to capture different materials on multi-material objects.

#### 3.2. Dataset Annotation

To enhance the dataset's utility for machine learning, two types of annotations were added:
- **Press Detection:** A binary ResNet-18 classifier was trained on 10,000 hand-labeled GelSight frames to automatically identify segments where the tactile sensor was in contact with an object. This detector achieved 97% accuracy on a 2,000-frame test set, with additional human review for quality assurance.
- **Material Labeling:** For all frames within a detected press, online workers assigned one of 20 material categories (e.g., Synthetic Fabric, Concrete, Wood, Rock). Five workers labeled each image to ensure accuracy, following a scheme similar to.

#### 3.3. Applications and Methodological Approaches

The "Touch and Go" dataset was then applied to three distinct multimodal learning tasks:

- **Self-Supervised Visuo-Tactile Feature Learning:**
 - **Goal:** Learn robust, generalizable feature representations for tactile inputs by associating them with corresponding visual information.
 - **Approach:** Adapted Contrastive Multiview Coding (CMC). This method trains two encoders (one for vision, one for tactile) to produce L2-normalized embeddings. The model minimizes a contrastive loss (InfoNCE-like) that pulls embeddings of matched visuo-tactile pairs closer and pushes mismatched pairs apart in the embedding space.
 - **Evaluation:** Features were evaluated on two downstream tasks: robotic grasping success prediction (generalizing to a separate robot-collected dataset) and material property recognition (material category, hard/soft, rough/smooth) within the "Touch and Go" dataset.

- **Tactile-Driven Image Stylization:**
 - **Goal:** Generate an image whose visual appearance ("style") is consistent with a given tactile signal, while preserving the original image's structure.
 - **Approach:** Adapted the audio-driven image stylization framework of Li et al., which is based on Contrastive Unpaired Translation (CUT). The model uses a multi-modal generator (image encoder, tactile encoder, and decoder) and two discriminators:
 - **Visuo-tactile Discriminator:** A PatchGAN discriminator `D` is trained to distinguish between real (matched) visuo-tactile pairs and fake pairs (generated image with its conditioning tactile input, or mismatched real pairs). This adversarial loss (`L_VT`) encourages the generator to produce visually plausible images consistent with the tactile input.
 - **Structure-Preserving Loss:** A contrastive loss (`L_CUT`) based on NCE is applied to the image encoder. This loss ensures that patches from the input image and the generated image at corresponding spatial locations remain close in embedding space, thus preserving the original image's structural integrity.
 - **Evaluation:** Quantitative metrics measured the similarity of the manipulated image's features to the conditioning tactile input (using the learned CMC embeddings) and consistency of material classification. Qualitative results demonstrated the model's ability to alter texture based on tactile input.

- **Multimodal Future Touch Prediction:**
 - **Goal:** Predict future frames of a tactile sensor's recording, given a sequence of preceding visual and tactile inputs.
 - **Approach:** Adapted a video prediction architecture by Geng et al., which uses residual networks with 3D space-time convolutions in an encoder-decoder structure. Separate encoders were used for visual and tactile inputs, whose features were concatenated and fed into a decoder to predict future tactile frames. The model was trained with L1 and perceptual loss. Predictions for multiple future frames were made autoregressively.
 - **Evaluation:** Compared performance of a tactile-only model versus a visuo-tactile model using MAE, SSIM, and LPIPS metrics, with different time horizons for prediction (skipping 3 or 5 frames).

This comprehensive methodological approach ensures that the "Touch and Go" dataset is not only robustly collected and annotated but also immediately demonstrates its value across diverse applications in multimodal learning, representation learning, and generative modeling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using the "Touch and Go" dataset yielded significant results across all three application areas, demonstrating the dataset's efficacy and impact:

#### 4.1. Dataset Analysis and Comparison

- **Scale and Diversity:** The "Touch and Go" dataset contains approximately 13.9k detected touches and around 3971 individual object instances. It is relatively balanced between indoor (52.2%) and outdoor (47.8%) scenes, featuring a wide range of materials (20 categories) from rigid to deformable.
- **Superior to Existing Datasets:**
 - Compared to simulation-based datasets (e.g., ObjectFolder 2.0), "Touch and Go" offers 4x more object instances and captures the complex microgeometry and non-rigid deformation of real objects that simulations often miss.
 - Compared to robot-collected datasets (e.g., VisGel, The Feeling of Success), "Touch and Go" has more touches (1.15x to 1.5x) and significantly greater scene diversity, not being confined to a lab space. It also contains objects larger than those typically graspable by robots.
 - Compared to other human-collected datasets (e.g., Burka et al.), "Touch and Go" is 11x larger in object instances, much more diverse in scenes (indoor/outdoor vs. mostly flat indoor surfaces), and utilizes a richer GelSight sensor.

#### 4.2. Self-Supervised Feature Learning

- **Performance on Robotic Grasping:** The features learned using "Touch and Go" data via Contrastive Multiview Coding (CMC) significantly outperformed supervised ImageNet features and features from other tactile datasets (Object Folder 2.0, VisGel) on an external robotic grasping success prediction task. The "Touch and Go" CMC model achieved 78.1% accuracy, notably higher than ImageNet's 73.0% and VisGel's 75.6%, indicating good generalization to different environments and sensors.
- **Performance on Material Understanding:** On various material recognition tasks within the "Touch and Go" dataset (material categories, hard/soft, rough/smooth), the self-supervised features consistently outstripped ImageNet features and those from other tactile datasets. For instance, it achieved 54.7% for material categories (vs. ImageNet 46.9%), 77.3% for hard/soft (vs. ImageNet 72.3%), and 79.4% for rough/smooth (vs. ImageNet 76.3%).
- **Impact of Data Diversity:** The results also showed that increasing the diversity of material categories in the training data (e.g., 25% vs. 100% classes) directly led to better downstream performance, reinforcing the value of the "Touch and Go" dataset's breadth.

#### 4.3. Tactile-Driven Image Stylization

- **Quantitative Results:** The proposed CUT-based method for tactile-driven image stylization achieved higher CMC similarity (0.197) and material classification consistency (0.142) compared to a CycleGAN-based baseline (CMC similarity 0.178, material consistency 0.129) and the unstylized baseline (0.165, 0.107). This indicates that the model successfully generated images whose features were more consistent with the given tactile input.
- **Qualitative Results:** Visually, the model demonstrated impressive capabilities. It could successfully manipulate the texture of an input image to match the "feel" of a distinct tactile input. Examples included making smooth surfaces appear rougher to match a rock's tactile signal or adding bumps, and even creating "hybrid" materials (e.g., adding grass-like texture to a surface). Crucially, these results were achieved without the model having access to the *original image* corresponding to the tactile example, suggesting a learned understanding of shared physical properties between modalities.

#### 4.4. Multimodal Future Touch Prediction

- **Benefit of Visual Information:** Integrating visual information significantly improved the accuracy of future tactile signal predictions. Across all evaluation metrics (MAE, SSIM, LPIPS) and both time horizons (skipping 3 or 5 frames), the visuo-tactile model consistently outperformed the tactile-only model.
- **Improved Long-Term Prediction:** The performance gap between multimodal and single-modal prediction models was larger for longer time horizons (skipping 5 frames), suggesting that visual information becomes even more critical for anticipating future touch over extended periods, likely by conveying material properties, deformation, or implicit actions.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that "Touch and Go" provides a valuable and effective resource for training multimodal models, leading to superior performance in tactile representation learning, cross-modal synthesis, and future state prediction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Touch and Go" dataset and the accompanying research mark a significant step forward in multimodal artificial intelligence, particularly for systems that need to understand and interact with the physical world.

#### 5.1. Core Significance

- **Addressing Data Scarcity:** The primary significance lies in addressing the long-standing challenge of data scarcity and limited diversity in visuo-tactile learning. By providing a large-scale, human-collected dataset from "in the wild" environments, "Touch and Go" offers a much-needed resource to accelerate research in this domain.
- **Bridging the Reality Gap:** Unlike simulated or highly controlled robot-centric datasets, "Touch and Go" captures the true complexity of real-world materials, geometries, and interactions, including non-rigid deformation and microgeometry. This is crucial for developing AI models that are robust and generalize well to unpredictable real-world scenarios.
- **Human-Centric Data Collection Paradigm:** The work champions a "human-collected" approach to multimodal data, extending a successful paradigm from other sensory modalities (like audio-visual data). This approach bypasses the limitations of robot autonomy and simulation fidelity, allowing for the rapid acquisition of diverse and rich interaction data.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Advancement in Visuo-Tactile Representation Learning:** The dataset's effectiveness in self-supervised feature learning, especially its superior performance on robotic grasping and material understanding, indicates its potential to foster more robust and generalizable tactile representations. This can lead to breakthroughs in robotic manipulation, enabling robots to "feel" and understand objects with greater nuance, improving tasks like grasping, dexterous manipulation, and assembly in unstructured environments.
- **Novel Applications in Content Generation and Synthesis:** The success of tactile-driven image stylization opens avenues for new forms of multimodal content creation. Imagine designers being able to generate visual textures and material appearances by simply providing a tactile "feel" or an inspiration from a physical object. This could revolutionize areas like computer graphics, virtual reality, and material design.
- **Improved Predictive Models for Physical Interaction:** Demonstrating that visual information enhances future touch prediction is vital for dynamic robotic tasks. Anticipating how an object will feel in the next moment allows for more proactive and adaptive control, crucial for tasks requiring continuous physical contact, such as polishing, sanding, or object tracking under deformation.
- **Deeper Material Understanding:** The dataset's rich material annotations and diverse interactions provide a fertile ground for AI to learn a more profound understanding of material properties from their combined visual and tactile signatures. This can lead to advancements in material identification, property estimation (e.g., slipperiness, compliance), and even inverse material design.
- **Foundation for Broader Multimodal AI:** As a pioneering effort in comprehensive human-collected visuo-tactile data, "Touch and Go" lays the groundwork for future research integrating even more sensory modalities (e.g., sound, proprioception, temperature) in real-world contexts, paving the way for truly intelligent agents with embodied understanding.

#### 5.3. Acknowledged Limitations and Future Directions

The authors transparently acknowledge limitations, such as data being collected mainly from one geographic location (University of Michigan campus), which implies a potential lack of global generalizability regarding object types or environmental conditions. Furthermore, human biases in object selection (e.g., probing "unrepresentative parts" or applying inconsistent force) and potential demographic biases of collectors (e.g., skin tone for egocentric views) are noted. These points highlight areas for future work, perhaps involving more distributed data collection efforts, standardized interaction protocols, or diverse collector pools. Despite these limitations, "Touch and Go" represents a monumental step towards equipping AI with a more comprehensive, human-like perception of the physical world.
