# Robot Utility Models: General Policies for Zero-Shot Deployment in New Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Robot Utility Models (RUMs) present a framework for enabling robots to perform common household tasks in new environments without additional training. The system achieved a 90% average success rate across 25 novel real-world settings by combining diverse data collection, multi-modal policy learning, and mLLM-based self-verification.

## Problem

- Current robot policies typically require fine-tuning for each new environment, significantly limiting their practical deployment.
- Collecting sufficient diverse, high-quality robot data across varied environments is expensive and time-consuming.
- Robots struggle to represent the multi-modal nature of human demonstrations and to adapt to diverse hardware configurations.

## Method

- Developed a specialized handheld data collection tool, 'Stick-v2,' to efficiently gather large-scale, diverse robot demonstrations across numerous environments.
- Trained multi-modal behavior generation models (VQ-BeT and ==Diffusion Policy==) capable of representing various valid solutions for the same task.
- Implemented a self-verifying deployment strategy utilizing multimodal large language models (mLLMs) for real-time error detection and autonomous task retries.

## Results

- Achieved a **90%** average success rate across five common household tasks (door opening, drawer opening, object reorientation, tissue pickup, bag pickup) in 25 novel, real-world environments.
- mLLM-guided self-verification improved the overall success rate by an average of **15.6%**, increasing from **74.4%** without retries to **90%** with retries.
- Demonstrated successful zero-shot transferability to different robot arms (Hello Robot Stretch, UFactory xArm 7) and camera sensors (iPhone Pro, Intel RealSense) with comparable performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Data quantity and environmental diversity are more crucial than model architecture sophistication for achieving zero-shot generalization in robot policies.
> - Collecting diverse data from many environments outperforms acquiring more demonstrations from fewer environments for generalization.
> - Multimodal large language models (mLLMs) can effectively detect task failures and enable autonomous retries, significantly improving overall success rates in novel environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction

Robotics research has long pursued the goal of general-purpose robots that can function effectively in diverse, real-world environments. Current approaches typically require fine-tuning robot policies for each new environment—a limitation that significantly restricts practical deployment. The paper "Robot Utility Models: General Policies for Zero-Shot Deployment in New Environments" introduces a framework to overcome this challenge, enabling robots to perform useful tasks in completely novel settings without additional training.

Robot Utility Models (RUMs) represent a significant shift from the current "pretrain-then-finetune" paradigm toward truly zero-shot capable robot systems. This research presents a comprehensive approach spanning data collection tools, model architectures, and deployment strategies that together enable robots to generalize to new environments without environment-specific adaptation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. The Zero-Shot Deployment Challenge

Creating robot policies that work in new environments without additional training faces several significant challenges:

1. **Data Scarcity**: Collecting diverse, high-quality robot data is expensive and time-consuming, particularly when capturing varied environments.

2. **Multi-Modality**: Human demonstrators naturally solve tasks in different ways, leading to multiple valid solutions for the same task that models must learn to represent.

3. **Hardware Standardization**: Robots vary widely in their hardware configurations, cameras, and control interfaces, making cross-platform deployment difficult.

4. **Error Detection and Recovery**: Robots operating in new environments inevitably encounter failures and must be able to detect and recover from them.

Current approaches in robot learning often address these challenges through environment-specific fine-tuning. While this works well for controlled settings, it limits real-world deployment where collecting new data for each environment becomes impractical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Robot Utility Models Framework

The RUMs framework addresses these challenges with a three-pronged approach:

1. **Large-scale, diverse data collection**: Using custom tools to efficiently gather demonstrations across numerous environments.

2. **Multi-modal policy learning**: Training models that can represent multiple valid solutions to the same task.

3. **Self-verifying deployment**: Integrating multimodal large language models (mLLMs) for error detection and recovery.

The authors focus on five common household tasks: door opening, drawer opening, object reorientation, tissue pickup, and bag pickup. These tasks were selected for their practical utility and to demonstrate generalization across different interaction types.

#### 3.2. Specialized Data Collection Tool

A key innovation in this work is the development of the "Stick-v2" data collection tool—a handheld device built around an iPhone Pro with flexible fingers that mimic a robot gripper.

This tool allows for rapid, consistent data collection across environments by:
- Closely mimicking the robot's visual perspective and gripper capabilities
- Providing a cable-driven trigger for gripper actuation
- Enabling portable, one-person operation

For each task, approximately 1,000 demonstrations were collected across 40 diverse environments, creating a dataset that captures substantial variation in objects, lighting conditions, and room configurations.

#### 3.3. Model Architecture and Training

The research evaluated multiple policy architectures:

1. **VQ-BeT (Vector Quantized Behavior Transformer)**: A transformer-based model that uses vector quantization to handle multi-modal behavior distributions.

2. **Diffusion Policy**: A diffusion-based model that generates actions by gradually denoising a random signal.

3. **ACT and MLP-BC**: Baseline models for comparison purposes.

All models were trained with a ResNet34-based vision encoder and a transformer-based policy trunk. The training process emphasized data diversity over model architecture sophistication, with results showing that:

- Data quantity and diversity are more crucial than model architecture for zero-shot generalization
- Both VQ-BeT and Diffusion Policy outperform simpler baselines
- VQ-BeT performs particularly well when trained on larger datasets

#### 3.4. Deployment with mLLM-Based Self-Critique

A standout feature of the RUMs framework is its integration of multimodal large language models (mLLMs) for self-verification during deployment. The system captures images of task execution and sends them to an mLLM with specific prompts to determine success or failure.

When the mLLM detects failure, the robot automatically resets and retries the task. This self-verification mechanism significantly improves success rates, with experiments showing:

- An average of 1.31 attempts needed for successful task completion
- An increase in success rate from 74.4% without retrying to 90% with mLLM-guided retries
- Varying improvement rates across tasks, with door opening benefiting the most (28% improvement)

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Results

The RUMs framework was evaluated through extensive real-world testing in 25 novel environments not seen during training. Results demonstrated impressive zero-shot generalization capabilities:

Key experimental findings include:

1. **High Zero-Shot Success Rate**: 90% average success across all tasks and environments.
2. **Policy Architecture Comparison**: Both VQ-BeT and Diffusion Policy achieved similar performance, with VQ-BeT slightly outperforming in most tasks.

3. **Data Scaling Effects**: Performance improved with increased dataset size, but showed diminishing returns after approximately 60-80% of the full dataset.

4. **Data Diversity Impact**: The research found that diverse data (fewer demonstrations from more environments) outperformed uniform data (more demonstrations from fewer environments), highlighting the importance of environmental diversity.

5. **mLLM Performance**: The mLLM-based self-verification system showed task-dependent improvement rates from 4% to 28%, with an average improvement of 15.6%.

#### 4.2. Hardware Considerations

An important aspect of this research is its focus on deployment across different robot platforms. The RUMs were tested on both the Hello Robot Stretch and the UFactory xArm 7, demonstrating cross-platform transferability.

The experiments showed that:
- RUMs can successfully transfer between different robot arms with minimal performance loss
- The custom end-effector design was crucial for consistent performance across platforms
- Success rates remained comparable despite different robot kinematics and control interfaces

The research also addressed the challenge of deploying with different camera sensors by demonstrating successful transfer between iPhone Pro cameras and Intel RealSense depth cameras through appropriate image transformation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Implications and Future Directions

The Robot Utility Models framework has several important implications for the field of robotics:

1. **Accelerated Deployment**: By eliminating the need for environment-specific fine-tuning, RUMs could significantly accelerate robot deployment in real-world settings.

2. **Democratized Robotics**: The focus on commodity hardware and open-source tools supports wider accessibility of robot technology.

3. **Task-Specific Generalization**: Rather than attempting to build a single "do everything" model, the RUMs approach succeeds by training focused, task-specific models that generalize well.

Future research directions identified in the paper include:
- Expanding the range of tasks beyond the current five demonstrations
- Improving hardware designs for more dexterous manipulation
- Integrating navigation capabilities for full-room operation
- Developing more sophisticated failure recovery mechanisms

#### 5.2. Conclusion

Robot Utility Models represent a significant advance in robot learning, demonstrating that zero-shot deployment in new environments is achievable with the right combination of data collection, model training, and deployment strategies. The research shows that with diverse training data, multi-modal policy learning, and mLLM-based self-verification, robots can successfully perform tasks in completely novel environments without additional training.

The results—achieving 90% success rates across 25 novel environments—demonstrate that RUMs can bridge the gap between laboratory demonstrations and practical real-world deployment. By focusing on task-specific utility rather than general-purpose capabilities, the framework provides a pragmatic path toward more capable and deployable robot systems.

The open-source release of code, data, models, and hardware designs further encourages community development and adoption, potentially accelerating progress toward robots that can be useful in everyday environments without specialized adaptation.
