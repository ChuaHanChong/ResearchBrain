# KoopMotion: Learning Almost Divergence Free Koopman Flow Fields for Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> KoopMotion, developed by researchers at the GRASP Laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania, introduces a motion planning method for robots that learns from demonstrations using Koopman operator theory augmented with "almost divergence-free" and goal convergence loss functions. The approach generates smooth, asymptotically stable flow fields that ensure convergence to a desired goal, demonstrating superior spatiotemporal accuracy (SEA) over baselines and successful real-world application on an autonomous surface vehicle.

## Problem

- Existing Learning from Demonstrations (LfD) methods, including some Koopman-based approaches, often lack inherent guarantees for convergence to desired goal points or robust stability analysis for learned motion plans.
- Traditional Koopman applications for LfD often focus on control-theoretic aspects, not explicitly addressing the generation of reference trajectories that reliably converge to specific goals.
- There is a need for motion planning methods that produce smooth, robust flow fields from limited demonstration data, preventing spurious attractors and ensuring accurate tracking.

## Method

- **KoopMotion** leverages ==Koopman operator theory== to linearize nonlinear system dynamics into a higher-dimensional feature space, learning both the lifting function (using ==Fourier features==) and the linear Koopman operator from demonstration data.
- It introduces a novel ==almost divergence-free loss== term ($L_{FlowDivergence}$) to regularize the learned flow field, minimizing expansion or contraction along the desired trajectory and helping prevent spurious attractors.
- A dedicated ==goal convergence loss== ($L_{Goal}$) is incorporated, forcing the goal position to be a fixed point in the lifted dynamical system, thereby guaranteeing convergence to the specified end point.

## Results

- **KoopMotion** accurately learned complex, multi-modal nonlinear motions from the LASA dataset, ensuring **all** generated trajectories converged to the intended goal without spurious attractors, even from out-of-distribution initial conditions.
- The method achieved comparable **Dynamic Time Warping Distance (DTWD)** and significantly outperformed baselines in **Swept Error Area (SEA)** metrics, indicating superior spatiotemporal accuracy in reproducing demonstrated movements.
- Real-world experiments on a miniature autonomous surface vehicle (mASV) in a non-static fluid environment confirmed successful trajectory tracking, convergence to goals, and robustness against disturbances, with direct sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Augmenting Koopman operator learning with specific loss functions for "almost divergence-free" flow fields and goal fixed points effectively ensures both robust trajectory following and asymptotic convergence in learned motion plans.
> - The combination of a divergence loss and a goal loss is crucial, as the divergence loss acts as a regularizer, preventing spurious attractors that can arise from enforcing only goal convergence.
> - Koopman theory provides a powerful framework for motion planning, offering inherent mechanisms for spectral analysis to confirm asymptotic stability of the learned dynamical systems (all eigenvalue magnitudes less than 1).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of robotics, specifically within the paradigm of Learning from Demonstrations (LfD). LfD is an imitation learning approach where robots acquire tasks by mimicking observed behaviors. This method is particularly valuable for programming complex actions that are difficult to formulate as optimization problems. LfD approaches typically fall into three categories: learning policies, learning cost/rewards, or learning plans. This work focuses on learning policies by modeling dynamical systems from demonstrations, which can then generate reference trajectories for motion planning.

The current landscape for learning dynamical system representations for motion planning primarily employs methods such as Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM), Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs), normalizing flows, Euclidean flows, or Gaussian Process Regression. While Koopman operator theory has shown efficacy in modeling nonlinear dynamical systems by transforming them into a higher-dimensional space where dynamics become linear, its direct application for learning *reference trajectories* that *converge* to desired goals in LfD settings is less explored. Most Koopman-based research leans towards control-theoretic applications, focusing on model-based control rather than trajectory generation with convergence properties. The existing Koopman-based works that do generate reference trajectories typically do not explicitly address convergence to desired trajectories or specific goal points. This paper aims to bridge this gap by leveraging the analytical advantages of Koopman theory, particularly its capacity for stability analysis via spectral decomposition, to develop a motion planning framework that ensures both accurate trajectory following and convergence to a goal.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a novel flow field-based motion planning method, named KoopMotion, that enables a robot to learn from demonstrations and generate smooth, convergent motion plans. Specifically, KoopMotion aims to drive a robot from any initial state to a desired reference trajectory, and subsequently ensure its convergence to the trajectory's end point.

The motivation for this research stems from several limitations in existing LfD and Koopman-based approaches:
- **Lack of inherent convergence in Koopman models for LfD:** While Koopman operator theory is effective for modeling nonlinear dynamical systems, its standard application does not inherently enforce convergence to desired trajectories or specified goals. This convergence property is a critical requirement for successful LfD, especially when a robot needs to precisely track a demonstrated path and reach a particular destination.
- **Need for stability analysis:** Traditional methods like GMMs and NODEs, while capable of learning complex dynamics, often lack straightforward mechanisms for analyzing system stability. Koopman theory, by linearizing dynamics in a lifted space, allows for spectral analysis (e.g., eigenvalue analysis) to readily assess stability, which is a desirable property for robust motion planning.
- **Generation of smooth and robust flow fields:** The goal is to produce motion flow fields that are not only accurate representations of demonstrated movements but also smooth and robust enough to guide a robot from off-trajectory initial conditions towards the desired path without spurious attractors, and maintain tracking even under disturbances.
- **Sample efficiency:** Developing methods that can learn effective motion plans from a limited amount of demonstration data is crucial for practical robotic applications, as data acquisition can be time-consuming and expensive.

To address these motivations, the authors propose to augment Koopman-based learning with specific loss functions. These losses are designed to encourage the learned flow fields to be "almost divergence-free" along the desired trajectory, thereby minimizing tendencies to expand or contract away from the path, and to enforce an attractor at the desired goal position, ensuring convergence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

KoopMotion is a data-driven modeling approach designed to estimate motion planning flow fields from demonstrations. The methodology integrates Koopman operator theory with novel loss functions.

#### 3.1. Koopman Operator Theory

KoopMotion adopts a data-driven Koopman operator theoretic method. This theory linearizes nonlinear dynamical systems by mapping their states into a higher-dimensional feature space, called the lifted space, where the system dynamics evolve linearly. Instead of directly modeling the nonlinear function $x_{k+1} = f(x_k)$, Koopman theory postulates the existence of a linear operator $K$ and a lifting function $\Psi(\cdot)$ such that $\Psi(x_{k+1}) = K\Psi(x_k)$. KoopMotion learns both the lifting function $\Psi(\cdot)$ and the Koopman operator $K$ from observed trajectory data.

#### 3.2. Learning Interpretable Dynamics with Fourier Features

Similar to prior work in Koopman theory, KoopMotion utilizes a predefined set of Fourier features as lifting functions. The lifting function $\hat{\Psi}(X_k)$ includes the original state $X_k$ along with cosine functions of the form $\cos(w_j^T X_k + b_j)$, where $w_j$ and $b_j$ are learnable parameters for each of the $\nu$ Fourier features. This approach removes the need for expert selection of lifting functions, with the dimensionality of the lifted space being the primary user-defined parameter. Higher values of $w$ intuitively contribute to modeling flows with greater curvature or higher frequency dynamics.

#### 3.3. Almost Divergence-Free Flow Fields

To shape the learned vector field and encourage an attractor along the demonstrated trajectories, KoopMotion introduces a loss term based on the divergence of the estimated vector field $\hat{F}$. The vector field $\hat{F}$ is derived from the difference between the initial conditions and their one-step forward propagation using the learned Koopman operator. Since $\hat{F}$ is a composition of differentiable, learnable functions (lifting functions and Koopman operator), its divergence, $\text{div}(\hat{F}) = \nabla \cdot \hat{F} = \sum_i \frac{\delta \hat{F}_i}{\delta x_i}$, can be computed using automatic differentiation. The "almost divergence-free" property, defined by $\nabla \cdot \hat{F} = 0$, is encouraged by a loss term ($L_{FlowDivergence}$) that penalizes non-zero divergence. This term is applied to points overlapping with the training data, aiming to minimize expansion or contraction of the flow along the desired trajectory.

#### 3.4. Convergence to Goal Position

To ensure that the reference trajectories converge to a specific goal position, a novel loss function ($L_{Goal}$) is introduced. This loss operates in the lifted space and mandates that the lifted system state at the final time step of the trajectory (the goal position, $X_{T_{final}}$) remains unchanged under the action of the Koopman operator, i.e., $\hat{\Psi}(X_{T_{final}}) = \hat{K} \hat{\Psi}(X_{T_{final}})$. This effectively makes the goal position a fixed point in the lifted dynamics.

#### 3.5. KoopMotion Loss Functions

The overall optimization objective for KoopMotion combines three loss terms:
$$ \min_{K,w,b} \beta_k L_{Koopman} + \beta_d L_{FlowDivergence} + \beta_g L_{Goal} $$
Where:
- $L_{Koopman} = \|\hat{\Psi}(X_{k+1}) - \hat{K} \hat{\Psi}(X_k)\|^2$: This is the standard Koopman loss, minimizing the prediction error in the lifted space.
- $L_{FlowDivergence} = \sum_i \left(\frac{\delta \hat{F}_i}{\delta x_i}\right)^2$: This term penalizes non-zero divergence, promoting smooth, non-expanding/contracting flow along the trajectory.
- $L_{Goal} = \|\hat{\Psi}(X_{T_{final}}) - \hat{K} \hat{\Psi}(X_{T_{final}})\|^2$: This term ensures convergence to the goal by making it a fixed point.

The weighting coefficients are set to $\beta_k = 1$, $\beta_d = 0.01$, and $\beta_g = 0.01$. An ablation study in the supplementary material indicates that the combination of these losses is necessary for the desired properties, as $L_{Goal}$ alone can introduce spurious attractors, which $L_{FlowDivergence}$ helps regularize. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer. A low-rank approximation of the Koopman operator is used to reduce the number of learnable parameters.

#### 3.6. Training Data

The method is evaluated on two datasets:
- **2D data:** The LASA handwriting dataset, consisting of 30 human-drawn motions with 7 demonstrations per motion, starting from different initial positions but converging to the same endpoint. To demonstrate sample efficiency, only planar position information is used, and the dataset is sub-sampled to approximately 3% of the original data (every 40th sample from 1000-timestep trajectories).
- **3D data:** A dataset of 3D end-effector trajectories generated from the Robocasa simulator, acquired by teleoperating a 7-DOF robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Offline Flow Field Analysis

##### 4.1.1. Goal Position Convergence Evaluations

Qualitative evaluation on the LASA dataset (Figure 2) demonstrates that KoopMotion can capture complex nonlinear motions, including multi-modal examples with multiple starting conditions converging to a single goal. The learned motion plans (black trajectories) and the extrapolated flow fields (gray) for initial conditions outside the training data appear to lack spurious attractors. Quantitative analysis involving 500 randomly selected initial conditions showed that all trajectories generated by KoopMotion converged to the intended end point or a boundary, with inference used to confirm eventual convergence for boundary-hitting cases (Figure 3b).

An ablation study (Figure 3a) illustrates the role of the novel loss terms. Using only $L_{Koopman}$ results in near-convergence but not exact goal attainment. Adding $L_{Goal}$ alone can introduce undesirable spurious attractors. However, the combination of all three losses ($L_{Koopman} + L_{FlowDivergence} + L_{Goal}$) achieves both goal convergence and desired flow properties without spurious attractors, suggesting that $L_{FlowDivergence}$ acts as a regularizer.

##### 4.1.2. Baselines and Metrics

KoopMotion's performance was quantitatively assessed against baselines from prior work, including CDSP, DMP, and CLF-DM. The metrics used were:
- **Dynamic Time Warping Distance (DTWD):** Measures similarity in shape between temporal sequences, irrespective of speed variations. Lower values indicate better performance.
- **Swept Error Area (SEA):** Penalizes both spatial and temporal misalignment between trajectories. Lower values indicate better performance.

The results (Figure 4) indicate that KoopMotion achieves comparable DTWD metrics to the baselines across four representative LASA motions. However, KoopMotion significantly outperforms the baselines in terms of SEA metrics, suggesting a more accurate alignment in both spatial and temporal aspects of the learned trajectories.

##### 4.1.3. Evaluating System Spectral Properties

A significant advantage of Koopman-based approaches is the ability to analyze system stability through spectral properties. For discrete-time Koopman operators, asymptotic stability is guaranteed if the magnitude of all eigenvalues ($|\lambda_i|$) of the learned operator $\hat{K}$ are less than 1. The spectral analysis presented in the bottom row of Figure 5 confirms that the magnitudes of all eigenvalues of the learned Koopman operators for the evaluated shapes are indeed less than 1, indicating asymptotic stability of the learned dynamical systems. Eigenfunctions, which represent regions of similar dynamics, are also visualized in the supplementary material (Figure 6, Figure 7), demonstrating how the space is partitioned by the learned model.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

KoopMotion was experimentally validated on a miniature autonomous surface vehicle (mASV) in a non-static fluid flow tank. The mASV, equipped with a differential drive and an OptiTrack motion capture system, used the KoopMotion-generated vector fields for motion planning. The learned vector field was scaled to the vehicle's velocity limits, and an on-board PID velocity controller executed the commands.

The experiments (Figure 5) demonstrate the following:
- **Trajectory Tracking and Convergence:** The mASV successfully tracked the desired reference trajectories (e.g., "leaf," "z," multi-modal shapes) and converged to the goal positions. Experiments with the robot starting from positions away from the demonstration paths showed successful convergence to the goal, highlighting the global attractive nature of the learned flow fields.
- **Multi-Robot Rendezvous:** For multi-modal trajectories, the robot demonstrated the ability to converge from different starting points to a single goal, implying potential for multi-robot rendezvous applications.
- **Robustness in Non-Static Environments:** The mASV operated in a non-static fluid environment, demonstrating the robustness of KoopMotion's reference trajectories against external disturbances.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The method allowed for direct transfer from simulation (or dataset training) to the real robot with minimal tuning (only vector field scaling), indicating its practical applicability. The smoothness of the learned KoopMotion dynamical system contributed to effective trajectory following by the mASV.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

KoopMotion presents a significant contribution to motion planning in robotics, particularly for systems learning from demonstrations. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Robust and Convergent Motion Planning:** The primary achievement of KoopMotion is its ability to learn smooth, almost divergence-free flow fields that not only mimic desired trajectories but also ensure convergence to specific goal positions from arbitrary initial conditions. This addresses a critical limitation in many existing LfD and Koopman-based methods, which often lack inherent convergence guarantees or desirable flow field properties.
- **Enhanced Stability and Interpretability:** By leveraging Koopman operator theory, the method allows for spectral analysis of the learned dynamical system, providing guarantees of asymptotic stability (eigenvalue magnitudes less than 1). This is a substantial advantage over methods like GMMs or NODEs, where stability analysis is less straightforward. The use of eigenfunctions also offers insights into how the learned dynamics partition the operational space.
- **Sample Efficiency:** The demonstrated efficacy of KoopMotion with sparsely sampled datasets (e.g., 3% of the LASA dataset) highlights its high sample efficiency in both space and time. This can reduce the data collection burden, making LfD more practical for complex robotic tasks.
- **Improved Spatiotemporal Accuracy:** Quantitative comparisons show that KoopMotion achieves comparable shape similarity (DTWD) and significantly superior spatiotemporal alignment (SEA) compared to baseline methods. This indicates a more faithful and accurate reproduction of demonstrated movements.
- **Practical Applicability and Generalization:** The successful validation on a physical autonomous surface vehicle in a non-static environment, with direct transfer from training data without extensive fine-tuning, demonstrates the practical feasibility and robustness of KoopMotion. Its ability to handle multi-modal demonstrations suggests potential for complex tasks and multi-robot coordination, such as rendezvous.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The method provides a foundation for future research in higher-dimensional systems (e.g., aerial vehicles, manipulators), the incorporation of formal guarantees for system properties, and further exploration of task generalization.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
Despite its contributions, the authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Lack of formal guarantees:** While the effectiveness of the loss terms for desired flow properties is empirically demonstrated, formal theoretical guarantees for these system properties are not provided.
- **Potential loss of variation:** The divergence loss, with the chosen weighting, can cause learned trajectories to collapse onto a single attractor, potentially reducing the variation present in the original demonstrations, especially early in the trajectory.
- **Reliance on low-level controller:** For real-world deployment, KoopMotion relies on the availability and performance of a low-level robot controller to execute the generated velocity commands.
- **Robot dynamics not explicitly considered:** The method learns dynamical systems based on demonstrated motions but does not explicitly account for the robot's own internal dynamics during the learning process.

In summary, KoopMotion represents a step towards creating more reliable and robust learning-from-demonstrations systems for motion planning, offering advantages in convergence, stability analysis, and data efficiency for robotic applications.
