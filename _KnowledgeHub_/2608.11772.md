# Diagnosis Before Recovery: Turning Agent Failures into Selective Self-Correction

> [!summary] Summary
> DARC is a diagnosis-guided recovery harness for language agents in domains lacking compiler-like feedback. It profiles development-set failures to fix one dominant failure mode per task family, prunes mismatched interventions from a shared recovery library, then distills and freezes a cost-aware short-circuit fallback policy. It lifts ALFWorld unseen success from 39.55% to 90.30% while cutting environment steps 54%.

## Problem

- Coding agents get typed repair signals from compilers, tests and traces, but broad language-agent benchmarks report only a coarse episode failure, leaving repairs undirected.
- Generic recovery playbooks broaden context on every failure, so interventions chosen without diagnosis often cannot address invalid actions, missing procedures or strict-format violations.
- Irrelevant recovery context actively interferes with the agent, and triggering every signal on every failure inflates environment steps, retrieval budget and tokens regardless of need.

## Method

- Failure diagnosis: run the base agent on a development split, read failure signatures, assign one dominant task-family failure mode, and prune the shared library to admissible interventions before policy search.
- Recovery-policy distillation: build a training-set evidence matrix of per-intervention success and cost, enumerate bounded short-circuit fallback chains, score them with a cost-penalized objective, and freeze the winner before test.
- Instantiated harnesses: a deterministic action-validity rule scorer ranking admissible ALFWorld commands to a top-12 view, an AppWorld chain of auto-knowledge plus local induction plus retrieval fallback, and a Finance retrieval-budget selector.

## Results

- With DeepSeek-V4-Flash, ALFWorld macro success reaches 91.94% versus 52.24% for the strongest reflective baseline, Finance macro accuracy 94.50% versus 80.50%, AppWorld Test-Normal TGC 95.83% versus 54.76%.
- Ablation confirms diagnosis matters: correct policy 94.50% on Finance versus 80.50% generic and 37.75% mismatched; on AppWorld the mismatched set still beats the generic playbook, 64.88% versus 61.90% TGC.
- Cost falls alongside gains: environment steps drop 54.2% to 15.83, invalid actions per episode 92.9% to 0.091, success per 100 steps rises from 1.16 to 6.02.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Restriction and instruction must be co-designed: the pruned action view alone gained 3.73 points, the recovery prompt alone lost 0.75, but together 49 points.
> - Extra context is not neutral. A mismatched intervention can beat a generic playbook, because irrelevant recovery evidence interferes actively rather than merely wasting budget.
> - More evidence is not monotonically better: retrieval accuracy peaked at one or two demonstrations and degraded at sixteen, so budgets should be distilled, not maximized.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the rapidly evolving field of language agent research, specifically addressing the challenge of agent self-correction. A prominent success area for language agents has been code generation, where environments provide rich, compiler-like feedback (e.g., syntax errors, test failures, execution traces) that makes failures actionable and guides specific repairs. However, the broader landscape of language agent tasks, such as embodied household agents, multi-application assistants, or financial extraction agents, often provides only coarse task failure signals (e.g., "episode not solved" or "task missed"). This lack of diagnostic specificity poses a challenge for generic self-correction strategies, which often involve appending reflection prompts, retrieving more examples, or exposing extensive tool manuals uniformly across all failures.

Existing self-correction and reasoning methods, such as ReAct, Reflexion, Chain-of-Thought, and various prompt optimization techniques, often apply recovery mechanisms broadly. While effective in demonstrating the value of self-correction, these methods do not typically differentiate between distinct failure modes. The paper argues that applying a "universal playbook" can lead to intervention mismatch (irrelevant recovery signals), recovery interference (irrelevant procedures degrading performance), and uncontrolled recovery cost (triggering expensive interventions unnecessarily).

Furthermore, prior research has shown that providing irrelevant or excessive context can negatively impact language model performance, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as "Lost in the Middle" or the distraction by irrelevant context. This work addresses this issue by proposing a mechanism that *selectively* applies recovery context, rather than uniformly expanding it. It relates to work on cascades and adaptive computation, which route queries among models or adjust retrieval complexity, but DARC specifically routes among recovery *interventions* within an agent harness, keeping the base model fixed. This paper aims to bridge the gap between the rich diagnostic feedback available to coding agents and the coarse feedback typically encountered in general agent tasks, by leveraging development-set failures to infer task-family-specific diagnostic information.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework for diagnosis-guided agent self-correction that improves agent reliability and efficiency in domains where explicit, compiler-like diagnostic feedback is absent. The authors are motivated by three specific problems associated with generic recovery strategies:

1. **Intervention Mismatch:** Generic recovery signals, chosen without understanding the specific nature of a failure, often cannot address the underlying problem. For instance, an extensive API manual is ineffective for fixing an invalid embodied action or a strict-format violation.
2. **Recovery Interference:** Irrelevant recovery procedures or overly broad contexts can disrupt an agent's progress. A long tool manual might introduce conflicting workflow rules, or demonstrations tailored for one failure mode could inadvertently degrade performance on another.
3. **Uncontrolled Recovery Cost:** Uniformly applying every recovery signal upon every failure leads to increased environment steps, retrieval budget, and inference tokens, regardless of whether the additional evidence is necessary or helpful.

The central insight driving this work is that development-set failures can be analyzed to infer the "missing diagnostic substrate" for a task family. Instead of merely requesting more context, a failure should be treated as evidence about the dominant bottleneck of a particular task family. This insight motivates the construction of a "recovery harness," a frozen policy that links a diagnosed failure mode to a restricted set of admissible recovery interventions.

The framework, named DARC (Diagnosis-guided Agent Recovery and Correction), makes two design choices explicit to address the identified problems:
- First, it **profiles development-set failures** to identify a dominant failure mode and consequently **restricts the candidate recovery library** *before* test evaluation. This ensures that interventions irrelevant to the diagnosed failure are excluded, preventing mismatch and interference.
- Second, within this restricted set of admissible interventions, DARC uses **training-set verifier feedback to distill a short, cost-aware success-cost policy** for deployment. This ensures that more expensive recovery evidence is only deployed when cheaper interventions fail, thus controlling recovery cost.

By freezing this diagnosis at the task-family level, DARC aims for stability and auditability, transforming self-correction from a generic prompt expansion into a targeted recovery-interface design. The work aims to demonstrate that failures can be made actionable by first determining *what kind* of failure can be repaired, and then deciding *how much* recovery evidence to expend.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DARC constructs a recovery harness through a two-stage process: Failure Diagnosis and Recovery-Policy Distillation. The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.

#### 3.1. Candidate Intervention Library

Before any diagnosis or policy distillation, a fixed library of candidate recovery interventions (R) is established. This library contains all potential executable correction signals, such as action guards for action validity tasks, procedural knowledge sources (e.g., Auto-Knowledge, local induction rules) for procedural tasks, and retrieval-demonstration budgets for format precision tasks. DARC does not invent new interventions but selects from and restricts this pre-specified library.

#### 3.2. Failure Diagnosis Stage

1. **Profiling Development-Set Failures:** The base agent is executed on a set of development tasks (D_dev). During these executions, observable failure signatures are measured. Examples include invalid or state-incompatible actions in ALFWorld, missing cross-application procedures in AppWorld, and exact-format violations in XBRL Finance.
2. **Diagnosing Dominant Failure Mode (m) and Admissible Intervention Set (R_m):** Based on the recurring patterns in these failure signatures, a dominant failure mode (m) is identified for the entire task family. This diagnosis is performed at the task-family level and is frozen before test evaluation, making the harness auditable and preventing test-time updates. The diagnosis then dictates which interventions from the full candidate library (R) are *admissible* for this specific failure mode, resulting in a restricted set R_m ⊆ R. For instance, ALFWorld tasks are diagnosed as primarily suffering from "action validity" issues, leading to an action-validity harness with action guards. AppWorld tasks are diagnosed with "procedural breadth" issues, resulting in a procedural-knowledge harness. XBRL Finance tasks are characterized by "format precision" problems, leading to a format-precision harness based on retrieval demonstrations. This restriction is a key differentiator from generic playbooks, as it excludes irrelevant or conflicting recovery context.

#### 3.3. Recovery-Policy Distillation Stage

1. **Intervention Evaluation:** For each task x_i in the training set (D_train) and each intervention r_j in the *admissible* set R_m, the success s(x_i, r_j) (binary outcome from a task verifier) and execution cost c(x_i, r_j) (e.g., environment steps, latency, retrieval budget) are recorded. This forms a "counterfactual evidence matrix."
2. **Policy Definition:** A recovery harness for a failure mode m is defined as H_m = (m, R_m, π_m), where π_m = (r_1..., r_L) is an ordered deployment policy (a sequence of interventions) over the admissible interventions.
3. **Short-Circuit Policy Evaluation:** DARC evaluates candidate policies as "short-circuit policies." The agent attempts interventions in the specified order, stopping immediately upon the first success. The halting index h*_i(π) determines which intervention in the sequence succeeded (or the last one if none succeeded).
4. **Objective Function (J(π)):** The empirical success and cost of a policy are calculated across the training set. The deployed policy π* is selected by maximizing an objective function J(π) = d_succ(π) - λ * max(0, d_cost(π) - τ_free), where d_succ is empirical success, d_cost is empirical cost, λ is a cost-success tradeoff parameter, and τ_free is a cost allowance for a standard attempt. This distillation process ensures the selected policy is cost-aware and improves task success.
5. **Freezing for Deployment:** The selected optimal policy π* is frozen before test evaluation, making it a fixed strategy for the identified task family.

#### 3.4. Structural Properties

The success term in DARC is a coverage objective, which is monotone and submodular, implying that adding interventions generally increases coverage but with diminishing returns. While the full objective is a cost-penalized ordered policy objective, the diagnosis step significantly reduces the candidate policy search space (Σ_K), supporting selection within a fixed finite class and reducing finite-sample search risk.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DARC across three distinct settings (ALFWorld, AppWorld, and XBRL Finance) demonstrates its effectiveness in improving agent self-correction.

- **RQ1 (Performance):** DARC generally improved average performance compared to base agents and generic playbook baselines.
 - **ALFWorld (Action Validity):** DARC substantially improved success rates. For instance, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash, valid-unseen success increased from 39.55% (base agent) and 54.48% (ACE) to 90.30%.
 - **AppWorld (Procedural Breadth):** The procedural recovery policy yielded strong gains on Test-Normal tasks and improved average Task Goal Completion (TGC) for all three base models. On the harder Test-Challenge split, DARC remained competitive with, though not uniformly superior to, ACE.
 - **XBRL Finance (Format Precision):** DARC achieved 94.50% macro accuracy with DeepSeek-V4-Flash, outperforming ACE (80.50%) and MIPROv2 (74.00%) under the reported protocol.
 - Statistical significance was confirmed for AppWorld results, where scenario-cluster bootstrap confidence intervals and paired scenario-level tests indicated significant performance gaps between DARC and baselines.

- **RQ2 (Cross-Task Transferability):** Frozen DARC recovery policies demonstrated transferability across related tasks within the same family.
 - In Finance, a policy distilled on "Formula" tasks transferred to "FiNER tags" with 89.50% accuracy (compared to 90.00% target-tuned), and vice versa (FiNER-to-Formula: 96.00% vs. 99.00% target-tuned).
 - For ALFWorld, the action-validity policy transferred from `valid_seen` to `valid_unseen`, achieving 90.30% success. These results suggest that the task-family level diagnosis captures stable failure modes.

- **RQ3 (Diagnosis Ablation):** A key finding was the importance of selecting the *correct* diagnosis-guided recovery policy. Comparisons against generic playbooks and mismatched intervention sets showed that the correct policy consistently outperformed both.
 - For AppWorld Test-Normal (under a stricter budget), correct diagnosis achieved 70.24% TGC compared to 61.90% (generic) and 64.88% (mismatched). It also achieved the best Scenario Goal Completion (SGC) with fewer environment steps.
 - The penalty for mismatched interventions was particularly large in ALFWorld and Finance, where they actively conflicted with required recovery behaviors, highlighting that "adding context only helps when interventions match the underlying failure mode."

- **RQ4 (Cost-Efficiency):** The distilled DARC policies effectively reduced unnecessary environment steps or retrieval budgets.
 - **ALFWorld:** DARC reduced average environment steps by 54.2% and invalid actions by 92.9% compared to the base agent, significantly increasing success per 100 steps from 1.16 to 6.02. This was achieved by making more episodes solvable at lower budgets.
 - **Finance:** The distilled policy chose smaller retrieval budgets (k=2 for Formula, k=1 for Tags), maintaining top macro-accuracy while reducing the mean retrieval budget to 1.5 demonstrations. This demonstrated that larger retrieval budgets are not always monotonically beneficial.
 - **AppWorld:** The selected DARC policy was shown to lie on the observed high-success, lower-cost frontier, indicating an optimal balance.

- **Matched-Information Fairness (ALFWorld Analysis):** A detailed factorial decomposition of the ALFWorld action-validity harness demonstrated that DARC's gains were not due to privileged information or simple action-space restriction alone. Instead, the significant improvement (an interaction term of +46.27 percentage points) was attributed to the *pairing* of the restricted, guard-ranked action view with a compatible recovery prompt. Granting the base agent the same parsed metadata only marginally improved success, and restricting the action view randomly *decreased* performance. The gain materialized only when the diagnosis-guided ranking of admissible actions was paired with instructions that leveraged this structured interface, leading to a drastic reduction in invalid actions.

- **Weight-Space Training Extension:** Preliminary experiments showed that a DARC-derived two-stage curriculum for fine-tuning improved Qwen3-8B's macro success on ALFWorld by 4.74 percentage points with GRPO, suggesting transferability to weight-space training.

- **Automatic Diagnosis Evaluation:** The LLM-based automatic failure diagnosis achieved 97.22% agreement with a verifier-backed reference on a sample of development traces, indicating its reliability in identifying dominant failure modes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work introduces a novel approach to agent self-correction by re-framing it as a diagnosis-guided process rather than indiscriminate context expansion. This represents a significant shift in agent design, particularly relevant for domains where explicit, structured feedback (akin to compiler errors in coding) is unavailable.

The DARC framework provides a practical and auditable route towards constructing more reliable language agents. By profiling task-family failure modes and restricting recovery interventions accordingly, DARC mitigates the common problems of intervention mismatch, recovery interference, and uncontrolled costs associated with generic recovery strategies. The empirical results across diverse benchmarks (ALFWorld, AppWorld, XBRL Finance) demonstrate improved task success and accuracy, often accompanied by reduced environment steps, invalid actions, or retrieval budget.

The finding that the effectiveness of recovery interventions is critically dependent on their alignment with the diagnosed failure mode has important implications for agent development. It suggests that future agent architectures might benefit from incorporating explicit diagnostic modules that dynamically assess failure types to invoke targeted, efficient, and appropriate recovery strategies. This approach moves beyond simply "making contexts larger" and instead focuses on "making failures actionable."

Potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Agent Robustness:** Agents can better recover from failures by applying relevant, rather than generic, interventions.
- **Improved Efficiency:** Reduced resource consumption (e.g., environment steps, tokens, retrieval calls) due to targeted recovery.
- **Broader Applicability:** Extending reliable self-correction to a wider range of agent tasks that currently lack rich diagnostic feedback.
- **Foundational Shift:** Encouraging a paradigm where agents learn not just from success, but from structured diagnoses of failure types.

Future work outlined by the authors includes extending static task-family diagnosis to dynamic, instance-level routing for mixed-mode failures, developing more sample-efficient policy distillation methods, and broadening full-library cascade comparisons to more diverse domains. These directions could further unlock the potential of diagnosis-guided self-correction in increasingly complex agent systems.
