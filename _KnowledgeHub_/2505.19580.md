# Whole-body Multi-contact Motion Control for Humanoid Robots Based on Distributed Tactile Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from CNRS-AIST JRL and Tokyo University of Science developed a control method for position-controlled humanoid robots, enabling dynamic whole-body multi-contact motions by incorporating distributed tactile sensors on intermediate body parts. This integration allows for online contact region updates, improving the robot's stability against environmental inaccuracies and disturbances in complex environments.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots typically limit multi-contact motion to extremities (hands, feet) due to the lack of adequate sensing on intermediate body parts, hindering robust operation in confined spaces.
- Prior methods for utilizing non-extremity contacts often assume quasi-static motion or employ control paradigms not directly applicable to position-controlled humanoids.
- Challenges exist in maintaining robust balance control when contacts occur at arbitrary body areas without force/torque sensors, leading to over or underestimation of contact regions.

## Method

- An extended multi-contact motion controller integrates distributed tactile sensors into both centroidal and limb motion control modules for position-controlled humanoids.
- Thin, flexible distributed tactile sensors (e-skin) are mounted on intermediate body parts like forearms and thighs, enabling broad-area contact force measurement without altering robot geometry.
- Online contact polygon updates, based on tactile measurements, refine contact region estimation for the ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== of centroidal motion and for impedance-like damping control of limbs.

## Results

- In simulations, tactile feedback expanded the wall height error range for successful walking with elbow contact from [-**0.02** m, **0.04** m] to [-**0.03** m, **0.04** m].
- The robot withstood a wider range of disturbance forces ([-100 N, 70 N] vs. [-90 N, 40 N]) during one-foot, one-knee standing with tactile feedback in simulation.
- A life-sized RHP Kaleido robot successfully performed dynamic stepping forward with forearm support (exerting ~160 N) and maintained stable sitting balance with thigh contacts on a 15 cm narrow seat.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly integrating distributed tactile sensor feedback into the real-time balance control loop of humanoid robots substantially enhances stability and disturbance rejection in multi-contact scenarios.
> - Accurate, online updates of contact polygon vertices using tactile measurements are crucial for effective contact force distribution, preventing instabilities caused by static or inaccurate contact assumptions.
> - Leveraging intermediate body parts for dynamic multi-contact motion through tactile sensing significantly expands a robot's operational robustness and dexterity beyond traditional extremity-only interactions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are developed with the objective of executing various manipulation and locomotion tasks, often with the goal of supporting or replacing humans in complex environments. A significant challenge in this field is enabling robots to operate robustly in confined spaces, which frequently necessitates "multi-contact motion" where the robot uses multiple points of contact with its surroundings. While planning and control for multi-contact motion have been actively studied, much of the prior work has limited these contacts to the robot's extremities, such as hands and feet, which are typically equipped with force/torque sensors. This limitation is primarily due to the challenges associated with whole-body contact sensing and robust balance control when contacts occur at arbitrary body areas.

Previous approaches to utilizing non-extremity contacts have largely been confined to specific scenarios. For instance, some studies have explored scenarios where humanoid robots make contact with areas not equipped with force/torque sensors by assuming quasi-static motion, estimating contact forces based on the robot's mass model. However, these methods are inherently limited to slow movements. Additionally, torque-controlled humanoid robots have demonstrated balance control using knee and elbow contacts, leveraging passivity-based approaches, which represents a different control paradigm compared to position-controlled robots. Within the domain of tactile sensing for humanoids, existing applications typically focus on human-robot interaction, object manipulation, or environmental perception, with few explicitly addressing balance control beyond basic Center of Pressure (CoP) calculations for bipedal walking soles.

This research addresses a gap in the current landscape by developing a control method that enables position-controlled humanoid robots to perform dynamic whole-body multi-contact motions, including contacts at intermediate limb areas like knees, elbows, forearms, and thighs. It integrates distributed tactile sensors directly into the balance control loop, a novel approach for enhancing robustness in dynamic multi-contact scenarios. By extending an existing multi-contact motion control framework, the work aims to bridge the divide between advanced multi-contact planning and the practical implementation of robust control for arbitrary body contacts on physical humanoid platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable humanoid robots to perform robust whole-body multi-contact motion, particularly in confined and dynamic environments. This involves leveraging contact not only at traditional extremities (hands and feet) but also at intermediate areas of the limbs, such as knees, elbows, and thighs.

The specific objectives that underpin this goal are:

- **Development of a Control Method:** To create a control strategy capable of realizing whole-body multi-contact motion, specifically designed to incorporate contacts at intermediate body parts.
- **Integration of Distributed Tactile Sensors:** To employ thin and flexible distributed tactile sensors on the robot's limb surfaces, allowing for the measurement of contact forces across the body without significantly altering the robot's inherent shape or kinematics.
- **Extension of Existing Frameworks:** To extend a previously developed multi-contact motion controller, originally designed for extremity contacts, to effectively process and utilize data from these distributed tactile sensors for maintaining robot balance.
- **Verification of Stability Enhancement:** To demonstrate through simulation and real-world experiments that the developed tactile feedback mechanism improves the stability and robustness of whole-body multi-contact motion when faced with external disturbances and uncertainties in the environment.
- **Real-world Demonstration:** To showcase the developed capabilities on a life-sized humanoid robot, performing various whole-body multi-contact motions in dynamic scenarios.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives stems from several factors:

- **Environmental Robustness:** Humanoid robots are envisioned for tasks in complex, unstructured, and often confined environments. In such settings, relying solely on foot or hand contact is insufficient for maintaining stability or navigating obstacles. The ability to brace against walls with an elbow or knee, for example, significantly enhances a robot's robustness against disturbances and its capacity to maneuver.
- **Human-like Dexterity and Stability:** Humans naturally employ whole-body contacts to maintain balance, exert forces, or navigate tight spaces. Equipping robots with similar capabilities moves them closer to human-like dexterity and resilience in diverse situations.
- **Addressing Sensor Limitations:** Traditional force/torque sensors are typically installed only at the robot's wrists and ankles due to size and integration constraints. Intermediate limb areas, while crucial for whole-body contact, often lack such sensing capabilities. Distributed tactile sensors provide a solution to this limitation by offering broad-area contact force measurement without requiring significant structural modifications.
- **Advancing Control Paradigms:** Current multi-contact control often makes simplifying assumptions (e.g., quasi-static motion) or relies on specific sensor configurations. This work aims to push the boundaries of real-time dynamic balance control for position-controlled humanoids by directly integrating distributed tactile information, thereby enabling more dynamic and responsive whole-body interactions.
- **Overcoming Over/Underestimation of Contact:** Without precise contact area information, controllers can either overestimate contact surfaces, leading to unstable force exertion attempts, or underestimate them, failing to utilize available support. Tactile sensors, coupled with online contact polygon updates, can mitigate these issues, leading to more accurate and stable control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a control method for whole-body multi-contact motion that extends a previously developed framework. This method integrates distributed tactile sensing into both centroidal and limb motion control modules.

**Hardware and Sensing:**
The physical implementation utilizes the RHP Kaleido, a life-sized humanoid robot, on which deformable sheet-shaped distributed tactile sensors (e-skin from intouch-robotics) are mounted. Specifically, patches of e-skin were installed on one forearm and both thighs of the robot. Each e-skin cell is capable of measuring tactile intensity in the normal direction, proximity, and acceleration. These sensors are approximately 5 mm thick, allowing them to conform to the robot's body surface without substantially altering its geometry. The tactile sensor cells are spatially calibrated using their accelerometers and aligned with the robot body model. The conversion from raw tactile intensity to force is established through linear fitting against force gauge measurements. In addition to e-skin, the control system uses joint angles from encoders, contact wrenches from 6-axis force/torque sensors on the feet, and link orientation from an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) on the base link.

**Control System Architecture:**
The control system is structured into two main components: Centroidal Motion Control and Limb Motion Control. The extensions introduced for tactile sensing are primarily integrated into these components. The robot is assumed to be position-controlled. The desired sequence of contact timing, location, and area is either manually provided or determined by a global planner.

**A. Centroidal Motion Control:**
1. **Resultant Wrench Definition:** The centroidal motion is controlled by the resultant wrench (a 6-DoF force and moment) acting on the robot's Center of Mass (CoM). This wrench is defined based on the scale of contact forces, contact polygon vertices (representing the contact geometry), friction pyramid ridge vectors, and the CoM position.
2. **Online Contact Polygon Update:** A key enhancement is the ability to online update the contact polygon vertices. Distributed tactile sensors measure the actual contact region between the robot and the environment. This measurement is then used to update the `p_i,j` parameters in the resultant wrench calculation. For contact surfaces, an axis-aligned rectangular region encompassing all detected contact cells is estimated. This update prevents instability caused by overestimating contact regions (leading to attempts to generate excessive moments) or underestimating them (leading to insufficient balance restoration moments).
3. **MPC for Centroidal Planning:** A Model Predictive Control (MPC) approach, solved using Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP), is employed for centroidal planning. This method calculates the optimal control input (contact force scales `λ`) over a receding horizon to minimize the difference between the robot's predicted centroidal state (CoM position, orientation, velocity, and angular velocity) and a reference state, while ensuring non-negative contact forces. This results in a planned centroidal state and optimal force scales for the current step. For real-time execution on the robot's onboard computer, a preview control method was applied as an alternative to DDP for computational efficiency.
4. **Centroidal Stabilization:** To compensate for model inaccuracies and external disturbances, a Proportional-Derivative (PD) feedback control adjusts the desired resultant wrench. This adjustment is based on the error between the MPC-planned centroidal state and the actual (sensor-measured or estimated) centroidal state.
5. **Centroidal Estimation:** The actual centroidal state, used for stabilization, is estimated. The base link orientation is determined by an Extended Kalman Filter using IMU data. The robot model, with measured joint angles, is then positioned such that an "anchor point" (a weighted average of contact points based on planned forces) aligns with its planned position. From this, the CoM position is calculated.

**B. Limb Motion Control:**
1. **Wrench Distribution:** To achieve the desired resultant wrench, the robot distributes forces and moments to individual contact areas. This is formulated as a quadratic programming problem that minimizes the difference between the distributed and desired resultant wrenches, subject to non-negative contact force scales. This process yields the desired contact wrench `w_d_i` for each contact area `i`. The online update of contact polygon vertices (from Section III-A) allows for the calculation of a `w_d_i` that is actually feasible given the measured contact.
2. **Damping Control:** An impedance-like damping control strategy is applied to each contact area to track its desired contact wrench. This involves updating a "compliance pose" based on PD feedback using the measured contact wrench (`w_a_i`) and the desired contact wrench (`w_d_i`). This control adjusts the position and orientation of the contact area to respond compliantly to contact forces.
3. **Distributed Tactile Sensing for Damping Control:** Distributed tactile sensors directly provide the measured contact wrench (`w_a_i`) for damping control. The tactile intensity from each sensor cell is converted into a force, and these forces are aggregated to form the overall contact wrench. Due to the normal-direction-only sensing capability of the e-skin cells, damping control parameters are adjusted to disable feedback for forces perpendicular to the normal and moments around the normal axis for these specific contact areas. In certain experiments (e.g., sitting posture), proximity sensors within the e-skin cells were utilized to estimate the contact region, which was found to be more stable than tactile intensity for this purpose.

**Software Implementation:**
The control system is implemented in C++ using the real-time robot control framework `mc_rtc`. Kinematics commands (CoM position, base link orientation, contact area poses) are processed by an acceleration-based whole-body inverse kinematics solver, which generates joint angle commands for the robot's low-level joint position PD controllers. The control parameters for MPC, stabilization, and damping control are detailed in tables. The control system and simulation environments were open-sourced for reproducibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of the developed control method was evaluated through both dynamics simulations and real-world experiments.

**A. Simulation Experiments (JVRC1 in MuJoCo):**
Simulations using the virtual humanoid robot JVRC1 in MuJoCo, equipped with a custom distributed tactile sensor plugin, verified the controller's robustness in three whole-body multi-contact motions. The robustness was compared with and without tactile feedback (damping control based on distributed tactile sensors).

1. **Walking with Elbow Contact (Fig. 5A):** The robot was tasked with walking forward while maintaining contact with an inclined wall using its elbow. When an error was introduced to the wall height, the robot demonstrated improved robustness with tactile feedback. The range of wall height errors for successful walking expanded from \[-0.02 m, 0.04 m] without tactile feedback to \[-0.03 m, 0.04 m] with tactile feedback. Tactile feedback also improved the tracking performance of the feet-only Zero Moment Point (ZMP), contributing to more stable foot contacts.
2. **Standing with Knee Contact (Fig. 5B):** The robot balanced on one foot and one knee on a block, subjected to front-back disturbance forces on its base link. With tactile feedback, the robot could withstand a wider range of disturbance forces, specifically from \[-100 N, 70 N] compared to \[-90 N, 40 N] without it. Analysis of the CoP at the knee contact showed that tactile feedback helped maintain the CoP within the contact region, preventing falls.
3. **Sitting with Thigh Contacts (Fig. 5C):** The robot maintained a sitting posture on a seat board that was movable around its pitch axis. Without tactile feedback, or if tactile feedback was enabled but the contact region was not updated (leading to an overestimated region), the robot would fall backward due to slight CoM shifts. By enabling tactile feedback **and** actively updating the contact polygon vertices based on distributed tactile sensor measurements, the robot successfully maintained balance. This indicated that accurate contact region estimation from tactile sensors was crucial for preventing the generation of excessive, unstable moments.

**B. Real-world Experiments (RHP Kaleido):**
Real-world experiments were conducted on the RHP Kaleido humanoid robot equipped with e-skin sensors.

1. **Preliminary: Forearm Interaction (Fig. 9):** A human applied external force to the robot's forearm, with damping control enabled for that limb. The robot's whole body responded by adjusting its posture to counteract the external force and achieve the target contact force (0 N) on the forearm. This demonstrated the proper functioning of the limb motion control for intermediate body parts using tactile feedback.
2. **Whole-body Multi-contact Stepping Forward with Forearm Contact (Fig. 10A):** The robot successfully performed a dynamic motion where it stepped forward while supporting its body with its forearm against a physical block. The robot shifted its CoM by approximately 0.2 m and exerted about 160 N with the right forearm. Despite some errors in contact force and moment attributed to environmental factors and sensor nonlinearity (Fig. 12), the robot maintained stability, with the feet-only ZMP remaining within the support region (Fig. 11). The generation and termination of contact forces aligned with the planned timing, ensuring stable transitions. This motion highlighted the controller's ability to handle dynamic scenarios, which contrasts with prior methods relying on quasi-static assumptions. Preview control was used for centroidal MPC for real-time execution.
3. **Sitting Balance with Thigh Contacts (Fig. 10B):** The robot maintained balance in a sitting posture solely with its thighs in contact with a narrow seat board (15 cm depth). Without tactile feedback, the robot would fall forward. However, by enabling tactile feedback with online contact region updates (using proximity sensors for robust estimation), the robot automatically adjusted its leg joint angles to maintain balance. As shown in Fig. 13, the CoP of the thigh contacts was effectively controlled to stay near the center of the contact region when tactile feedback was activated, despite some steady errors attributed to robot model inaccuracies and seat surface inclination. The robot also successfully withstood forward disturbance forces during this motion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a notable advancement in the field of humanoid robotics, particularly concerning multi-contact motion control and the integration of advanced sensing technologies.

- **Enabling Robust Whole-body Multi-contact Motion:** The primary significance lies in demonstrating a control method that allows position-controlled humanoid robots to perform dynamic multi-contact motions utilizing *intermediate body parts* (such as forearms, knees, and thighs), moving beyond the traditional reliance on hands and feet. This capability is critical for enhancing robot dexterity and stability in complex, confined, and unstructured environments.
- **Enhanced Stability and Disturbance Rejection:** The integration of distributed tactile sensor feedback is shown to significantly improve the robot's stability against environmental errors (e.g., inaccurate obstacle height) and external disturbances. This increased robustness is paramount for deploying humanoid robots in real-world scenarios where precise environmental models may not be available and unexpected interactions are common.
- **Novel Application of Distributed Tactile Sensors:** The work establishes an early precedent for directly integrating distributed tactile sensor data into the real-time balance control loop of a humanoid robot. This represents a progression from using tactile sensors primarily for perception or localized manipulation to employing them as a fundamental component for whole-body stability and force regulation. The online update of contact polygon vertices based on tactile measurements is a key innovation in this regard, preventing instability from inaccurate contact assumptions.
- **Practical Feasibility on Life-sized Humanoids:** The successful demonstration on the RHP Kaleido, a life-sized humanoid robot, underscores the practical feasibility and scalability of the proposed control method and sensor integration. This bridges the gap between theoretical control developments and their application on complex physical systems.
- **Expanded Operational Domains:** By enabling stable interactions using various body parts, humanoid robots can potentially operate more effectively in environments like disaster zones (climbing over debris, bracing against unstable structures), industrial settings (maneuvering through tight spaces), or domestic assistance (leaning to maintain balance).
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies clear and promising avenues for future research, including the development of planning layers for autonomous contact area determination (addressing combinatorial explosion by possibly referencing human motion), and the more comprehensive utilization of raw, high-resolution tactile sensor data. These directions suggest a path toward even more adaptive and nuanced whole-body control strategies, potentially involving learning-based approaches to overcome current limitations of model-based control.

In conclusion, this research makes a substantive contribution by advancing the state of the art in humanoid multi-contact motion control, paving the way for more robust, versatile, and human-like robotic operations in challenging environments.
