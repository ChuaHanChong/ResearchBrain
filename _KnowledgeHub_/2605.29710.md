# PhAIL: A Real-Robot VLA Benchmark and Distributional Methodology

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces PhAIL, an open real-robot benchmark and a distributional evaluation methodology that employs the time-to-success Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) to rigorously compare Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. It demonstrates that this approach offers superior sample efficiency for distinguishing closely performing policies compared to traditional scalar metrics, revealing that the best VLA policies were approximately 7 times slower per operation than human teleoperation.

## Problem

- Current real-world VLA policy evaluation practices rely on binary success rates with small sample sizes, leading to unreliable comparisons and obscured genuine performance differences.
- Prevalent evaluation methods lack robust statistical machinery, such as confidence intervals and paired statistical tests, hindering reliable conclusions for close comparisons.
- Scalar metrics often provide lossy summaries of policy performance and can yield contradictory rankings, failing to capture the full picture of VLA behavior.

## Method

- Introduces the time-to-success Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) as a fundamental evaluation primitive, which jointly captures policy reliability (hard-failure rate) and throughput (time to completion).
- Establishes a methodology that separates scoring (using Human-Relative Throughput with episode-clustered bootstrap CIs) from significance testing (using a macro-averaged Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with pooled-resample bootstrap p-values).
- Provides **PhAIL**, an open real-robot benchmark built on a DROID-style setup, including a dataset, per-rollout artifacts, and reference implementations for transparent and reproducible VLA policy evaluation.

## Results

- The best evaluated VLA policies were approximately 7 times slower per operation than human teleoperation, with no inference model exceeding **19%** Human-Relative Throughput on any single object.
- The macro-averaged KS test achieved an **80%** detection rate for close policy comparisons at N=25-30 rollouts per (model, object) cell, whereas binary-threshold metrics failed to reach this power even at N=30.
- Different principled scalar aggregations of the same CDF data yielded conflicting top-1 rankings, highlighting that scalar choice is a methodological commitment and no single scalar adequately summarizes comparisons when CDFs cross.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The time-to-success CDF offers a richer, more comprehensive characterization of VLA policy performance, enabling the identification of distinct failure profiles and trade-offs missed by single scalar metrics.
> - The macro-averaged Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test exhibits significantly higher sample efficiency for resolving close policy comparisons on real robots compared to traditional binary-threshold metrics, making real-world experimentation more feasible.
> - Anchoring robot performance to a same-fixture human teleoperation reference via Human-Relative Throughput (HRT) provides a standardized, interpretable, and embodiment-agnostic performance metric.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The evaluation of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies in real-world robotic systems currently faces significant methodological limitations. Prevalent practices typically involve reporting binary success rates at a fixed timeout, often with small sample sizes (N ≤ 25 rollouts per condition), and frequently without confidence intervals or paired statistical comparisons. This approach is prone to sampling noise, which can obscure genuine performance differences between policies and render close comparisons unreliable. While throughput metrics are sometimes used alongside success rates, they can yield contradictory rankings, and the choice between them is often arbitrary or undisclosed.

Recent methodology critiques have highlighted these shortcomings, advocating for more rigorous evaluation protocols such as blinded same-session A/B testing with hundreds of rollouts and distributional comparisons. Some existing real-robot evaluation platforms, like RoboArena and RoboChallenge, compare policies through pairwise Elo ratings or scalar success metrics but generally lack robust statistical machinery, such as confidence intervals or paired tests. More rigorous examples, such as the LBM examination, incorporate Bayesian posterior credible regions and paired statistical tests at higher sample sizes. Similarly, competition-style evaluations (e.g., RGMC, OCRTOC, NIST Assembly Task Boards) standardize tasks but often without comprehensive statistical analysis. Simulated benchmarks (e.g., RoboCasa, CALVIN, ManiSkill) offer fast iteration but do not fully capture real-world complexities like perception, latency, and safety. A survey of recent VLA papers (Appendix A) further confirms that modal per-condition sample sizes are typically between 10-20, and statistical rigor regarding confidence intervals or paired tests is largely absent.

PhAIL positions itself as a response to these challenges by providing an open, real-robot benchmark and a reference implementation of a distributional evaluation methodology. This work is complementary to existing rigorous evaluations like LBM by offering a richer evaluation primitive (time-to-success Cumulative Distribution Function, CDF) and anchoring evaluations to a same-fixture human reference, addressing a gap in standardized, statistically robust frameworks for real-world VLA policy comparison.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the identified statistical weaknesses in current real-world VLA policy evaluation, which impede reliable progress in the field. The paper outlines several key objectives to address these issues:

1. **Introduce a Richer Evaluation Primitive:** The central objective is to move beyond scalar metrics like binary success rate or average throughput, which are inherently lossy summaries of policy performance. The authors propose adopting the **time-to-success Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)** as the fundamental evaluation primitive. This is motivated by the understanding that the CDF jointly captures both reliability (the hard-failure rate, reflected by the asymptote below F=1) and throughput (e.g., median or mean time to completion). This primitive is considered strictly richer than any single scalar, providing a more comprehensive view of policy behavior.

2. **Separate Scoring and Significance Testing:** Current evaluation practices often conflate the tasks of ranking policies and determining if statistical differences exist. The paper aims to formalize a clear separation between these two jobs. Scoring involves generating a scalar metric with confidence intervals for ranking purposes, while significance testing is dedicated to statistically determining if two policies' underlying performance distributions are genuinely different. This separation is intended to provide more nuanced and reliable conclusions.

3. **Anchor Evaluations to Human Performance:** To create a standardized, embodiment-agnostic, and interpretable performance metric, the authors aim to ground robot policy evaluation in human-level performance. This involves introducing **Human-Relative Throughput (HRT)**, a dimensionless scalar anchored to same-fixture human teleoperation. This metric normalizes robot performance against a human baseline, helping to mitigate confounds related to specific hardware embodiments or task difficulties.

4. **Improve Sample Efficiency for Resolving Close Comparisons:** A significant challenge in real-robot experimentation is the cost and time associated with collecting large datasets. The paper seeks to introduce a statistical test that is more sample-efficient than current methods, particularly for distinguishing between closely performing policies. This objective is driven by the observation that modal sample sizes in the field are insufficient for reliable comparisons, especially when effect sizes are small.

5. **Provide an Open, Auditable Real-Robot Benchmark:** To facilitate widespread adoption of the proposed methodology and enable transparent, reproducible research, the authors aim to release an open real-robot benchmark called PhAIL. This includes a dataset, per-rollout artifacts (synchronized video, telemetry, event annotations), and a reference implementation, all hosted on a shared hardware platform. The goal is to establish a common infrastructure for statistically robust VLA policy evaluation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PhAIL methodology is structured around three core components: the evaluation primitive, the scoring layer, and the significance layer, instantiated on a physical robot platform with specific operational protocols.

#### 3.1. The Random Variable and its CDF

The fundamental evaluation primitive is the **time-to-success CDF**, F(t) = P(T ≤ t), where T is the wall-clock time required for a policy to complete one operation.
- **Hard Failures as T=∞ Ghost Events**: Operations that result in unrecoverable states (e.g., items lost outside the workspace, safety stops) are treated as "ghost events" with T = ∞. This is represented in the CDF as an asymptote below F = 1, where the gap (1 - F(t)) at large t explicitly quantifies the hard-failure rate.
- **Censored Tails for Timeouts**: Episodes ending due to operator stoppage or timeout, where operations remain unfinished but were not unrecoverably failed, contribute right-censored observations (T_tail, 0). This indicates that T > T_tail, preserving information about potentially slow but recoverable runs.
- **Estimation**: The CDF F(t) is estimated using the **Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimator**. Confidence intervals for derived scalars are computed using 95% **episode-clustered bootstrap**, resampling entire episodes to account for within-episode correlations.

#### 3.2. Scoring: Scalars and Visualizations from the CDF

The scoring layer focuses on deriving interpretable scalar metrics and visualizations from the CDF.
- **Human-Relative Throughput (HRT)**: This is the headline scalar, defined as the ratio of human-reference Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) to model RMST for a given object, macro-averaged across all objects. RMST(τ) is the integral of the survival function (1 - F(t)) up to a time cap τ (set at 240 seconds for this work). HRT is dimensionless, grounded in human performance, and partially controls for object difficulty.
- **P-P Plots**: These plots visualize the relationship between F_Human(t) and F_model(t) parametrically as t sweeps. They show deviations from the diagonal (representing equal speed) and the area under the curve, which corresponds to the pairwise stochastic-dominance probability P(T_model < T_Human) (AUC). The maximum vertical distance from the curve to the diagonal is related to the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic.
- **Scalar Projections**: The paper demonstrates how common scalar metrics (success rate at threshold, median time, UPH, completion fraction, MTBF/A) are all projections of the CDF, emphasizing that choosing a single scalar is a methodological commitment that weights different regions of the CDF.

#### 3.3. Ranking: Distributional Tests on the CDF

This layer addresses whether two policies' underlying distributions statistically differ.
- **Primary Test: Macro-Averaged Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) Test**: The two-sample KS statistic is computed per-object (D_o = sup_t |F_a,o(t) - F_b,o(t)|) and then macro-averaged across objects (D_bar = (1/J) Σ D_o). This approach preserves per-object discrepancies and avoids dilution.
- **Significance Calibration**: p-values are obtained via a **pooled-resample episode-clustered bootstrap**, which empirically calibrates the test to be robust to right-censoring and within-episode correlations.
- **Resolution Outcomes**: The test differentiates between three outcomes: (i) one policy is consistently better (CDFs separate, one dominates), (ii) policies differ but neither is uniformly better (CDFs cross, different scalars yield different rankings), or (iii) policies are indistinguishable at the given sample size.
- **Why KS Matters**: The KS test is consistent against any alternative distribution, meaning it can detect any difference between CDFs given sufficient samples, unlike scalar-based tests which are only consistent against alternatives where the specific scalar differs.

#### 3.4. PhAIL Benchmark Design and Protocol

- **Platform**: A DROID-style setup featuring a Franka Research 3 robot arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 parallel-jaw gripper, equipped with external and wrist-mounted RGB cameras. All human and robot evaluations use the same hardware and software framework.
- **Task**: Bin-to-bin order picking across four diverse objects: wooden spoons, towels, scissors, and batteries. Each rollout involves multiple independent placement operations.
- **Models Evaluated**: Four publicly available VLAs were fine-tuned and evaluated: OpenPI π0.5, GR00T N1.6, ACT, and SmolVLA.
- **Fine-tuning**: All models were fine-tuned on a shared 449-episode demonstration dataset (~13 hours) using their respective repositories' default recipes (Appendix C, D), without per-model hyperparameter optimization to reflect a standardized benchmark setup.
- **Evaluation Protocol**:
 - Each operation had a 30 s/item time cap.
 - Evaluations were conducted blindly, meaning the operator was unaware of which model was running, and interventions were limited to safety stops.
 - Post-rollout, an operator recorded target/source counts and outcomes, with successful placements timestamped from synchronized video (Appendix E).
 - Spatial configurations (camera and tote placement) were logged to account for potential environmental confounds (Appendix G).
- **Cohort Size and Power Budget**: The analysis included ~995 robot episodes, with 396 human reference rollouts. Per-(model, object) cell sample sizes ranged from 26-46 episodes. Power calculations (Appendix H) indicated that field-modal N (10-20) is severely underpowered for reliable binary comparisons, requiring 600-1500 paired rollouts for a 5 pp difference. In contrast, the macro-averaged KS test was predicted to achieve 80% power at N ≤ 30 for two of the three close pairs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of four VLA policies on the PhAIL benchmark yielded several key findings regarding robot performance, statistical power, and methodological implications.

#### 4.1. Headline Ranking and Performance

- **Overall Performance**: The best evaluated VLA was approximately **7 times slower** per operation (based on RMST ratio) than the human teleoperation reference. No inference model exceeded 19% HRT on any single object.
- **Top Performers**: OpenPI π0.5 (HRT 13.8%) and NVIDIA GR00T N1.6 (HRT 13.3%) were identified as the top two policies. However, their 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for HRT (OpenPI: [12.2, 15.7], GR00T: [12.0, 15.2]) overlapped significantly.
- **Statistical Indistinguishability**: Both the HRT-with-CI scoring and the macro-averaged KS test (p=0.12) and pooled logrank test (p=0.54) confirmed that OpenPI and GR00T were statistically indistinguishable at the current sample size (N ≈ 37 per cell).
- **Distinguishable Policies**: ACT (HRT 10.5%) was statistically separable from both GR00T and OpenPI (logrank p-values 1.0x10^-3 and 4.3x10^-3, respectively). SmolVLA (HRT 6.4%) was clearly the worst performer and statistically distinguishable from all other models.
- **Failure Modes**: Analysis of the CDF asymptotes (Appendix F) revealed distinct failure profiles. ACT had the highest drop-out rate among inference models, while SmolVLA had the highest safety-stop rate, indicating qualitatively different types of failures that a single "hard-failure rate" would obscure.

#### 4.2. Sample Efficiency of Distributional Tests

- **KS Superiority**: The macro-averaged KS test demonstrated significantly higher sample efficiency compared to binary-threshold metrics (F(30s), F(60s)) and RMST-as-scalar for distinguishing close policy pairs.
- **Resolution Power**: At N=25 rollouts per (model, object) cell, KS achieved an 80% detection rate for the GR00T vs. ACT comparison. For OpenPI vs. ACT, 80% detection was reached at N=30.
- **Underpowered Binary Metrics**: In contrast, binary-threshold metrics (F(30s), F(60s)) and RMST-as-scalar failed to reach an 80% detection rate on any of the close pairs within the tested range of N ≤ 30. The closest pair (OpenPI vs. GR00T) remained unresolved by all metrics at N=30, with KS reaching a 63% detection rate, and power analysis predicting N≈45 per cell for 80% power.
- **Magnitude of Difference**: The paper concluded that the field's modal N ≤ 25 is an order of magnitude or more below the budget required by binary tests to resolve observed effect sizes, whereas the KS test significantly lowers this requirement.

#### 4.3. Aggregation-Rule Disagreement

- **Conflicting Rankings**: Different principled scalar aggregations of the same underlying CDF data yielded conflicting top-1 rankings, highlighting that scalar choice is a methodological commitment.
 - Integrated RMST/HRT ranked OpenPI/GR00T highest.
 - Macro-AUC (P(T_model < T_Human)) ranked ACT highest (0.134), followed by OpenPI (0.100) and GR00T (0.095).
- **Crossing CDFs**: This disagreement indicated that the CDFs of ACT and OpenPI/GR00T cross. ACT showed an advantage in early-time success (leading to higher AUC against the human reference), while OpenPI/GR00T performed better in the integrated, longer-term region (leading to lower RMST). This exemplifies why no single scalar can adequately summarize comparisons when CDFs have different shapes.

#### 4.4. Distributional Shape and Scalar Trade-offs (Appendix L)

- **Tail-Heavy Slowdown**: Q-Q plots (Figure 8) revealed that robot policies tend to fall behind human performance disproportionately on the slow tail of operations, rather than exhibiting a uniform multiplicative slowdown across the entire distribution.
- **Scalar Trade-offs**: As the episode timeout (τ_episode) varied, throughput-oriented metrics (UPH) and reliability-oriented metrics (MTBF/A) moved in opposite directions (Figures 9, 10). This further illustrated that different choices of the integration cap (τ_episode) could alter policy rankings, emphasizing that scalar choice reflects a specific prioritization of performance aspects.

#### 4.5. Spatial Configuration Sensitivity (Appendix G)

- Environmental factors significantly influenced policy performance. GR00T, for example, exhibited a 22.2 percentage point drop in completion rate when the external camera and outbound tote were on opposite sides of the workspace compared to the same side. This sensitivity highlights the importance of controlling and logging spatial configurations in real-robot evaluations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PhAIL benchmark and its associated distributional methodology introduce several advancements that could significantly impact the field of real-robot VLA evaluation:

- **Richer Performance Characterization**: By adopting the time-to-success CDF as the evaluation primitive, PhAIL provides a more comprehensive and nuanced characterization of VLA policy performance. It jointly captures reliability and throughput, offering insights that single scalar metrics often miss, particularly when policy CDFs cross.
- **Enhanced Statistical Rigor**: The clear separation of scoring (ranking with confidence intervals) and significance testing (determining statistical separability) provides a more robust framework for drawing conclusions. The use of episode-clustered bootstrap for confidence intervals and pooled-resample bootstrap for KS p-values addresses common statistical challenges in real-robot data, such as censoring and within-episode correlations.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency**: The demonstrated superior sample efficiency of the macro-averaged KS test for resolving close comparisons is a critical contribution. This allows researchers to achieve reliable statistical distinctions with significantly fewer real-robot rollouts than traditional binary metrics, making real-world experimentation more feasible and cost-effective.
- **Transparent and Reproducible Research**: PhAIL provides an open benchmark, including a dataset of approximately 990 episodes, reference implementations, and an auditable run-explorer interface with synchronized video and telemetry for every rollout. This commitment to transparency and reproducibility sets a higher standard for experimental validation in robotics.
- **Standardization and Interpretability**: Anchoring robot performance to a same-fixture human teleoperation reference through Human-Relative Throughput (HRT) offers a standardized, dimensionless metric that is more interpretable and less sensitive to embodiment-specific factors or arbitrary task difficulties.
- **Shifting Evaluation Paradigms**: The work makes visible the limitations of current evaluation practices, particularly the underpowered nature of binary-threshold metrics. It advocates for a shift towards distributional analysis, explicit disclosure of methodological commitments (e.g., choice of headline scalar), and the acceptance of "unresolved" findings when statistical power is insufficient.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Scope of Empirical Validation**: The current validation is limited to a single robotic embodiment (Franka FR3) and a single primitive task (bin-to-bin pick-and-place with four objects). While the methodology is designed to be task-agnostic, broader empirical validation across different robots and tasks would further strengthen its generalizability.
- **Illustrative Rankings**: The evaluated sample size (N ≈ 35 per cell) is still relatively modest compared to what some binary metrics would require for robust ranking of very close pairs. Thus, the per-model rankings are presented as illustrative rather than definitive architectural pronouncements.
- **Annotator Bias**: While efforts were made to control for operator blinding during data collection and robustness checks were performed on annotation streams, the manual review process for a portion of the episodes was conducted by a single, non-blinded reviewer. Future work plans to mitigate this by replacing manual review with objective hardware sensing.
- **Environmental Sensitivity**: The observed sensitivity of policy performance to spatial configuration changes (e.g., camera/tote placement) highlights the need for careful experimental design and control of environmental factors.

In conclusion, PhAIL offers a robust and statistically principled framework for real-robot VLA policy evaluation. By addressing the limitations of current practices, it provides tools that can enable more reliable comparisons, accelerate research progress, and foster greater transparency in the development of intelligent robotic systems. The planned expansion to include tasks like insertion and small-part assembly suggests a continued commitment to broadening the scope and impact of this benchmark.
