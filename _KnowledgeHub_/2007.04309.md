# Self-Supervised Policy Adaptation during Deployment

> [!summary] Summary
> A method called Policy Adaptation during Deployment (PAD) enables pre-trained vision-based reinforcement learning policies to adapt to novel, unknown environments without external reward signals. This approach improves policy generalization in 31 out of 36 tested environments and boosts performance in real robotic manipulation by up to 24% by continuously updating visual representations using self-supervised learning.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning policies trained with visual inputs struggle to generalize when deployed in environments that differ from their training setting (the "sim-to-real" gap).
- Existing solutions like domain randomization are limited by the need for human experts to anticipate all possible environmental changes, which can be unscalable or insufficient for truly unknown variations.
- Adapting policies in new environments typically requires extrinsic reward signals, which are often unavailable or impractical to obtain during real-world deployment.

## Method

- The Policy Adaptation during Deployment (PAD) method utilizes a shared feature extractor for both the policy's action head and an additional self-supervised prediction head.
- Two self-supervised tasks, Inverse Dynamics Prediction (IDM) or Rotation Prediction, are employed to generate continuous, reward-free training signals directly from the agent's online observational data (states and actions).
- During deployment, the policy's shared visual feature extractor is continuously updated by optimizing only the self-supervised objective, thereby adapting the policy's internal representations to the new environment without any explicit reward.

## Results

- PAD improved generalization in 31 out of 36 evaluated environments, consistently outperforming domain randomization across DeepMind Control Suite, ViZDoom, and real robotic manipulation tasks.
- The method achieved up to a 24% improvement over baselines in real robotic manipulation tasks (reach and push) under challenging visual conditions like "disco lights" and patterned tablecloths.
- Evaluations demonstrated long-term stability, with PAD continuing to improve generalization over 10x episode lengths, and successful adaptation to non-stationary environments with dynamic video backgrounds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous online adaptation through self-supervision offers a robust, reward-free mechanism for pre-trained policies to generalize to unforeseen visual and subtle dynamic shifts during deployment.
> - The choice of self-supervised task is crucial, with Inverse Dynamics Prediction being more effective for motor control tasks and Rotation Prediction more suitable for tasks requiring rich scene understanding.
> - Adapting internal visual representations in an online fashion can be more effective than pre-training a statically robust policy via domain randomization, especially for environments with unpredictable changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep reinforcement learning (RL), particularly when combined with convolutional neural networks for processing visual inputs, has achieved notable successes in various domains. However, a significant challenge persists concerning the generalization of trained policies when deployed in environments that differ from the training environment. This "sim-to-real" or "train-to-test" gap is exacerbated by high-dimensional visual observations, where even minor discrepancies in lighting, textures, or object appearances can severely degrade policy performance.

Existing approaches to address this generalization problem generally fall into two categories. One prominent class of solutions involves learning robust policies designed to be invariant to anticipated environmental changes. Domain randomization (DR) is a common technique in this category, where policies are trained in simulated environments with randomized parameters (e.g., textures, lighting) to encompass a wide range of potential real-world variations. The intention is that if the test environment falls within the distribution of randomized training environments, the policy will generalize. However, this approach has limitations: it requires human experts to anticipate all possible changes, may lead to increased model and sample complexity if randomization is too broad, and can fail if the actual test environment variations are not adequately covered by the randomization, potentially yielding worse performance than a non-randomized policy.

Another strategy involves fine-tuning a pre-trained policy in the new environment. This often necessitates an extrinsic reward signal, which is frequently impractical or unavailable during real-world deployment. Crafting dense reward functions in complex, unknown environments can require substantial engineering effort, limiting the scalability of this approach.

Within the broader context of machine learning, self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning visual representations from unlabeled data by designing auxiliary prediction tasks. In RL, SSL has been explored to improve sample efficiency or facilitate exploration in novel environments. However, these prior works typically do not focus on adapting a *pre-trained policy* to *unseen environments* without reward signals during deployment. Similarly, test-time adaptation methods in computer vision, while related, usually focus on image recognition tasks rather than continuous online adaptation of an RL policy with visual inputs.

This paper positions itself by addressing a specific gap: adapting a pre-trained vision-based RL policy to an *unknown* environment *during deployment* where *no reward signal* is available. It proposes to leverage self-supervision to generate continuous, reward-free training signals directly from the online observational data, thereby enabling the policy's visual representation to adapt to the new environmental conditions. This shifts the paradigm from anticipating all possible changes before deployment to continuously adapting to the actual encountered changes online.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable pre-trained reinforcement learning policies to adapt and maintain performance in novel, unknown environments *during deployment*, without relying on explicit reward signals. The authors identify a critical limitation of current vision-based RL systems: their inability to generalize from a training environment to a new, potentially significantly different, deployment environment. This lack of generalization often stems from variations in visual input (e.g., lighting, textures, object appearances) that were not encountered or sufficiently represented during initial training.

The motivation for this work arises from several factors:
1. **Bridging the Generalization Gap:** Policies trained in a simulated or controlled environment often perform poorly when deployed in the real world due to domain shift. Existing solutions like domain randomization are limited by the need to anticipate all possible environmental changes, which is often not feasible or scalable for truly unknown test environments.
2. **Addressing the Reward Scarcity Problem:** Fine-tuning policies in new environments typically requires reward signals. However, in real-world deployment scenarios, providing dense and accurate rewards is frequently impractical, expensive, or impossible. This work seeks to remove the dependency on rewards during the adaptation phase.
3. **Enabling Continuous Learning:** The authors argue that instead of designing policies that are statically robust to all possible changes, it is more effective for policies to continuously learn and adapt to their actual operating environment. This paradigm shift supports the development of more versatile and autonomous agents.
4. **Leveraging Self-Supervision:** The research is motivated by the success of self-supervised learning in generating valuable supervisory signals from unlabeled data. By integrating self-supervision, the policy can obtain "free" training signals from its own stream of observations in the new environment, allowing for representation adaptation without external human intervention or explicit reward functions.
5. **Online Adaptation:** The paper aims to demonstrate that adaptation can occur effectively in an online fashion, where the policy continuously updates its internal representations as it interacts with and observes the new environment. This contrasts with offline adaptation methods that might require pre-collected data from the target domain.

In summary, the core motivation is to overcome the generalization challenge in vision-based RL by providing a practical, reward-free mechanism for continuous policy adaptation during real-world deployment, thereby enhancing the robustness and applicability of RL agents in dynamic and unpredictable settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed Policy Adaptation during Deployment (PAD), integrates self-supervised learning with a standard reinforcement learning framework to facilitate continuous adaptation of policies in novel, reward-free environments during deployment.

**Network Architecture:**
The core of the approach involves a policy network π, whose parameters θ are sequentially split into a feature extractor π_e (with parameters θ_e) and an action head π_a (with parameters θ_a). The action head π_a takes the features extracted by π_e as input to output actions, i.e., π(s; θ) = π_a(π_e(s)). Additionally, a self-supervised prediction head π_s (with parameters θ_s) is introduced, which also takes the output of the feature extractor π_e as its input. This shared feature extractor design is crucial, as adapting π_e through self-supervision implicitly adapts the policy's understanding of the visual input.

**Self-Supervised Tasks:**
Two primary self-supervised tasks are explored to generate training signals from raw observational data:
1. **Inverse Dynamics Prediction (IDM):** This task aims to predict the action taken (a_t) given the feature representations of consecutive states (s_t and s_t+1). The self-supervised head π_s takes π_e(s_t) and π_e(s_t+1) as input and predicts a_t. For continuous actions, the loss function `L` is Mean Squared Error (MSE), while for discrete actions, it is cross-entropy loss. The authors state that IDM is preferred over forward dynamics prediction in feature space to avoid trivial solutions where the feature extractor maps all states to a constant vector.
2. **Rotation Prediction:** Inspired by prior work in self-supervised representation learning, this task involves rotating an input image by one of four angles (0, 90, 180, 270 degrees) and training the network to classify the applied rotation. This task is intended to encourage the learning of representations robust to object configuration and scene structure.

**Training and Testing Phases:**

- **Training (Pre-deployment):** Before the policy is deployed, the agent is trained in a source environment. During this phase, both the standard RL objective J(θ) (which optimizes for expected reward) and the self-supervised objective L(θ_s, θ_e) are jointly optimized. The combined objective is expressed as min_θ_a,θ_s,θ_e J(θ_a, θ_e) + αL(θ_s, θ_e), where α is a hyperparameter balancing the two objectives. This ensures that the feature extractor π_e learns representations useful for both controlling the agent and performing the self-supervised task.

- **Deployment (Test-time Adaptation):** Upon deployment in a new, unknown environment, the extrinsic reward signal is assumed to be unavailable, meaning the RL objective J cannot be optimized. However, the self-supervised objective L can still be computed using the raw observational data (s_t, a_t, s_t+1) naturally acquired as the agent interacts with the environment. During deployment, only L(θ_s, θ_e) is optimized. This optimization updates the parameters θ_s of the self-supervised head and, critically, θ_e of the shared feature extractor. By adapting θ_e, the visual representation used by the policy's action head π_a also adapts to the statistics of the new environment, even without any explicit reward. This adaptation occurs online, with θ_e and θ_s being updated iteratively as new observations arrive.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Base RL Algorithms:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is used for continuous control tasks (DeepMind Control Suite, robotic manipulation), and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) for discrete action tasks (CRLMaze).
- **Observations:** Raw pixel inputs are typically stacks of 1 or 3 colored frames, initially 100x100 pixels, then randomly cropped to 84x84. Time-consistent random cropping is applied during training to preserve spatio-temporal patterns.
- **Online Adaptation:** During deployment, the self-supervised objective is optimized for one gradient step per time iteration using a batch of 32 augmented copies of the most recent observation.
- **Baselines:** The method is compared against vanilla SAC/A2C, SAC/A2C with Domain Randomization (+DR), and SAC/A2C with the self-supervised auxiliary task during training but without deployment-time adaptation (+IDM or +Rot).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents empirical evaluations across diverse simulated and real-world environments, demonstrating the effectiveness of the Policy Adaptation during Deployment (PAD) method.

**Overall Generalization Performance:**
- PAD demonstrated improved generalization in 31 out of 36 tested environments across a variety of tasks, including DeepMind Control Suite, ViZDoom, and real robotic manipulation.
- The method consistently outperformed domain randomization (DR) in a majority of environments.

**DeepMind Control Suite (DMControl) Experiments:**
- **Randomized Colors:** PAD enhanced generalization across all 9 tasks evaluated with randomized colors, outperforming DR in 6 of these tasks. Vanilla SAC and DR often showed comparable or even worse performance than PAD.
- **Long-Term Stability:** Evaluations over 10x episode lengths indicated that PAD does not degrade policy performance over extended periods; instead, it continued to improve generalization in 6 out of 7 tasks, suggesting stability and robustness beyond episodic adaptation.
- **Non-Stationary Environments (Video Backgrounds):** PAD successfully adapted to environments with dynamic video backgrounds, outperforming all baselines in 7 out of 8 tasks. DR showed notably worse performance in these settings, suggesting its limitations when image statistics are not covered by its randomized training domain.
- **Scene Content (Distracting Objects):** PAD demonstrated improved robustness to distracting objects in the scene, outperforming baselines in 3 out of 5 tasks. In tasks where PAD did not improve, all methods were already largely unaffected by the distractors.
- **Training Environment Performance:** When evaluated in the training environment, PAD maintained performance without significant degradation, indicating that the adaptation mechanism does not harm performance when environmental changes are absent.
- **Choice of Self-Supervised Task:** An ablation study showed that Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) was more effective for motor control tasks in DMControl compared to rotation prediction or CURL (Contrastive Unsupervised Representations for Reinforcement Learning). This suggests that aligning the self-supervised task with the primary RL task's feature learning requirements is beneficial.
- **Online vs. Offline Learning:** The study found that online learning significantly contributed to PAD's success, with an offline variant showing lesser, though sometimes still positive, generalization improvements.

**CRLMaze (ViZDoom Navigation) Experiments:**
- PAD improved generalization across all tested CRLMaze environments featuring novel textures for walls, floor, ceiling, and varying lighting conditions.
- It significantly outperformed vanilla A2C and DR.
- For navigation, rotation prediction was identified as a more suitable self-supervised task than IDM, as it better facilitated learning representations for scene understanding.

**Robotic Manipulation Tasks (Real Kinova Gen3 Robot):**
- **Reach and Push Tasks:** Policies trained in simulation were transferred and adapted to a real Kinova Gen3 robot in 6 different environments, including continuously changing "disco lights" and a patterned table cloth that introduced visual distractions and altered dynamics.
- PAD improved generalization in all challenging real-world scenarios where baselines showed sub-optimal performance. For the push task, PAD yielded improvements of up to 24% over baselines in the "cloth" environment.
- **Dynamics Changes (Simulated):** Further simulated experiments for the push task, involving changes in object mass, size, friction, arm mount position, and end-effector velocity, showed that PAD also improved generalization to these shifts in physical properties, particularly when multiple dynamics changes were combined.

**Comparison to Reward-based Fine-tuning (Appendix):**
- While naive fine-tuning with rewards can improve generalization, it requires more data (e.g., 10 to 100 episodes) and access to reward signals in the target environment, which PAD bypasses.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that PAD effectively enables policies to adapt to unforeseen visual and subtle dynamic changes in deployment environments, often surpassing the performance of domain randomization and offering a reward-free alternative to traditional fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning, particularly concerning the practical deployment of vision-based policies in real-world, dynamic, and unknown environments. The significance and potential impact of the Policy Adaptation during Deployment (PAD) method are multi-faceted:

1. **Addressing a Critical Deployment Challenge:** PAD directly tackles one of the most substantial hurdles in real-world RL: the generalization gap between training and deployment environments. By offering a mechanism for adaptation without relying on rewards or prior knowledge of environmental changes, it enhances the viability of deploying RL agents in complex, unstructured settings where such information is often unavailable.
2. **Pioneering Reward-Free Online Adaptation:** The core novelty of enabling a pre-trained policy to continue learning *during deployment* using *only self-supervision* is a notable step forward. This alleviates the need for costly and labor-intensive reward engineering or instrumenting real-world environments with sensors for reward signals. It proposes a more autonomous and scalable approach to policy maintenance.
3. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The empirical results demonstrate consistent and substantial improvements in policy robustness and generalization across a wide array of visual variations (colors, textures, backgrounds, distractors) and subtle changes in environmental dynamics. This capability is crucial for systems that need to operate reliably despite inherent variability in their operating conditions.
4. **Superiority over Domain Randomization (in many cases):** By showing that PAD often outperforms domain randomization, the work highlights a limitation of purely anticipatory strategies. While domain randomization aims to make policies invariant to *known* variations, PAD offers a reactive adaptation mechanism for *unknown* variations, making it potentially more powerful in truly novel environments.
5. **Foundation for Continuously Learning Agents:** This work lays a foundation for future embodied agents that are designed to learn continuously throughout their lifespan. The concept of "learning all the time, with the flexibility to learn both with and without rewards, before and during deployment" envisions a paradigm shift toward more adaptive and intelligent autonomous systems.
6. **Insights into Self-Supervised Task Design:** The investigation into the effectiveness of different self-supervised tasks (inverse dynamics vs. rotation prediction) provides valuable insights for practitioners. It suggests that the choice of self-supervised task should be congruent with the type of features most relevant to the primary RL task (e.g., IDM for motor control, rotation prediction for scene understanding), guiding future development in this area.
7. **Practical Applicability in Robotics:** The successful transfer and adaptation to real robotic manipulation tasks underscores the practical utility of PAD. Operating from an uncalibrated camera and adapting to conditions like "disco lights" demonstrates the method's potential for robust real-world robot deployment where precise environmental control is often impossible.

While the current framework still relies on informed selection of self-supervised tasks, this research marks a significant initial step towards creating truly adaptive vision-based policies capable of robustly operating in the complex and unpredictable real world.
