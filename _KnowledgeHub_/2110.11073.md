# RL4RS: A Real-World Dataset for Reinforcement Learning based Recommender System

> [!summary] Summary
> RL4RS is the first open-source real-world dataset suite for reinforcement-learning-based recommendation, built from raw NetEase game logs. It ships two slate-recommendation datasets with pre- and post-RL splits, tuned user-simulation environments, model-free and batch RL baselines, counterfactual policy evaluators, and a Transformer data-understanding tool. A month-long live A/B test shows PPO lifting GMV 23.9% over the supervised strategy.

## Problem

- Prior RL-based recommender work trains on artificial simulators like RecoGym and RecSim, whose synthetic user feedback never comes from real deployed applications.
- Semi-simulated alternatives force item-wise datasets such as MovieLens into RL format through arbitrary assumptions about choice context and slate size, making transformations unreasonable.
- Evaluation relies on short-term ranking metrics or rewards from the same simulator used for training, hiding extrapolation error and offering no unbiased pre-deployment check.

## Method

- Two released datasets, **RL4RS-Slate** and **RL4RS-SeqSlate**, from NetEase game logs, each split into SL and RL portions before and after RL deployment, with logged behavior-policy probabilities for ==counterfactual evaluation==.
- ==Session zero-padding== replaces explicit user-exit modeling, and a Transformer sequence-to-sequence tool compares greedy against beam-search decode scores to test whether a dataset has exploitable long-term impact.
- A four-part evaluation framework covering simulator fidelity, online evaluation on ==DIEN==-based simulators, ==counterfactual estimators== (IS, SWIS, doubly robust, sequential DR), and environments rebuilt from the test set.

## Results

- The data-understanding tool scores greedy decoding at **0.62** of the best sequences on RL4RS, versus **0.99-4.50** on MovieLens, RecSys15, Last.fm, and CIKMCup2016, confirming genuine long-term structure.
- DIEN simulators hit **0.907** item-wise AUC but **36.2** absolute reward error; on them TD3 reaches **178.1** (Slate) and PPO **319.7** (SeqSlate), while batch-RL BCQ trails at **127.4**.
- A one-month live A/B test lifted GMV **23.9%** over greedy DIEN, **8.4%** over A2C, **12.3%** over DQN, with the online ranking matching the simulator ranking.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High click-prediction AUC does not imply accurate reward estimation; validate simulators on the quantity the policy actually optimizes, not on classification metrics.
> - Check that a dataset actually rewards long-horizon decisions before applying RL; on most traditional recommendation logs a greedy policy already matches the best decoded sequence.
> - Training and evaluating in one simulator overstates policy quality; importance-sampling estimators explode without clipping, so cross-check with doubly robust scores and simulators rebuilt from held-out data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) based Recommender Systems (RL-based RS) represent an evolving area of research that frames recommendation as a multi-step decision-making process, aiming to optimize long-term user engagement or satisfaction. The broader landscape of recommender systems has traditionally relied on supervised learning (SL) algorithms that primarily optimize for immediate, short-term user satisfaction, often through item-wise greedy strategies. However, the paper identifies limitations of SL methods in handling complex, modern recommendation scenarios such as slate recommendation, bundle recommendation, and sequential recommendation, where inter-item dependencies and long-term accumulative utilities are crucial. RL is considered a promising paradigm for these challenges due to its inherent suitability for sequential decision-making, direct optimization of long-term rewards, and efficient exploration of vast combination spaces.

Despite the potential of RL in recommender systems, the current research landscape faces a "reality gap." Existing studies commonly utilize either artificial datasets (e.g., RecoGym, Recsim) that lack real user feedback, or semi-simulated datasets derived from traditional RS datasets (e.g., MovieLens) that require substantial and often "unreasonable" data transformations to fit RL formats. This reliance on synthetic or heavily modified data limits the practical applicability and generalizability of research findings. Furthermore, the evaluation methodologies in RL-based RS are often considered insufficient. Traditional RS metrics are short-term, while standard RL metrics (e.g., cumulative rewards) are highly dependent on the accuracy of potentially flawed simulation environments. The problem is compounded by "extrapolation error," where policies trained on offline data may perform poorly when encountering unseen state-action pairs in real-world scenarios.

The RL4RS project positions itself as a direct response to these identified limitations. It aims to bridge the "reality gap" by providing the first open-source, real-world dataset for RL-based RS. By doing so, it seeks to replace the artificial and semi-simulated datasets previously used, offering a more representative benchmark for research. Concurrently, the work introduces a systematic evaluation framework designed to address the challenges of environment simulation accuracy, policy evaluation bias, and extrapolation error. This comprehensive resource is intended to foster more practical, well-validated, and robust research in applied reinforcement learning, specifically within the context of recommender systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper identifies two primary problems within the existing research on RL-based Recommender Systems, which serve as the core motivations for the RL4RS project:

1. **Lack of Real-World Datasets for RL-based RS Problems:** Current research frequently employs artificial datasets (e.g., RecoGym, Recsim) or semi-simulated datasets, which are traditional RS datasets (e.g., MovieLens) transformed to fit RL data formats. The authors contend that artificial datasets do not reflect genuine user feedback from real applications. Semi-simulated datasets, conversely, often involve forced and potentially "unreasonable" data transformations. This absence of realistic, publicly available datasets creates a "reality gap," making it difficult to rigorously validate RL-based RS algorithms before their costly deployment in live systems. While some industrial research deploys RL models online, the raw logged data for offline experimentation and reproducibility is rarely open-sourced.

2. **Absence of Unbiased Evaluation Methods:** The evaluation of RL-based RS policies faces challenges. Traditional recommendation metrics (e.g., Recall Rate, Accuracy) are short-term indicators, which may not align with the long-term optimization goals of RL. Conversely, pure reinforcement learning indicators (e.g., Cumulative Rewards) are highly sensitive to the accuracy of the simulation environment used for evaluation. This reliance introduces bias, as imperfect simulators can lead to misleading performance estimates. An additional source of bias is "extrapolation error," which occurs when policies are evaluated on state-action pairs not well-represented in the training data, leading to potentially unrealistic value estimations. Consequently, a robust and unbiased framework for assessing policy performance before online deployment is lacking.

Driven by these challenges and the inherent suitability of reinforcement learning for multi-step decision-making and long-term optimization in complex recommendation scenarios, the key objectives of the RL4RS project are:

- To introduce the first open-source, real-world dataset for RL-based RS, directly collected from an industrial application, to address the reality gap.
- To provide a comprehensive resource that includes two large-scale raw logged datasets, reproducible simulation environments, data understanding tools, advanced model-free RL baselines, batch RL baselines, and counterfactual policy evaluation algorithms.
- To propose and implement a new systematic evaluation framework that encompasses evaluation of environment simulation, evaluation on simulation environments (online policy evaluation), counterfactual policy evaluation (offline policy evaluation), and evaluation on environments built specifically from test sets to minimize biases.
- To release separated data collected before and after RL deployment for each dataset (e.g., Slate-SL and Slate-RL), enabling robust evaluation of batch RL algorithms and the measurement of extrapolation error.
- To contribute to the broader field of applied reinforcement learning by offering a realistic benchmark for algorithms dealing with real-world, batched offline data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in RL4RS centers on collecting, processing, and releasing real-world logged data, establishing a standardized MDP formulation, developing data understanding tools, and proposing a systematic evaluation framework.

**Data Collection and Anonymization:**
Raw logged data was collected from a popular game application operated by NetEase Games. To ensure user privacy, a three-phase anonymization procedure was implemented: user sampling, value encoding (using MD5 for discrete values and logarithm function for continuous values), and feature description masking. Users were also provided with an option to opt out of data collection.

**Recommendation Scenario and Datasets:**
The recommendation task within the game is characterized by an "unlock" rule, where the next list of three items becomes available only after items in the current list are sold out. Users can refresh a page up to three times daily. Two distinct datasets are provided:
- **RL4RS-Slate:** Focuses on slate recommendation, where a user's interaction with a single page (containing 9 items in 3 lists) is modeled as an MDP.
- **RL4RS-SeqSlate:** Addresses sequential slate recommendation, considering interactions across multiple pages (up to 4 item pages, or 36 steps) within a session to maximize total session reward.
For each dataset, separate logs collected *before* (e.g., Slate-SL) and *after* (e.g., Slate-RL) the deployment of reinforcement learning policies are provided. This allows for specific experiments on batch RL and extrapolation error.

**Logged Data Format:**
The raw logged data includes: Timestamp, Session ID, Sequence ID, Exposed items (a 9-item slate), User feedback (binary 0/1 for each item, multiplied by item utility for reward calculation), User Features (42-dimensional user portrait, 64-dimensional user click history), Item Features (360-dimensional, including item ID, category, embedding, historical CTR), and Behavior Policy ID (network architecture and parameters of the SL-based policy active during logging).

**Data Preprocessing and MDP Formulation:**
Raw logs are transformed into sequential state/action tuples suitable for RL training. This involves defining state-to-observation mapping, action embeddings, action masks, and action probabilities. For sequential scenarios, user exit behavior is handled via session padding, where sessions are zero-padded to a fixed length with zero feedback, simplifying complex state transitions.
The recommendation task is formulated as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process (MDP):
- **States (s_t):** Comprise static user features (portrait, historical preferences) and dynamic context features (selected items, dynamic statistics).
- **Actions (a_t):** Selecting an item from available candidates. Can be modeled as discrete (item ID) or continuous (item embedding).
- **Transition:** The environment (user) provides feedback, and the next state s_{t+1} is deterministically formed by concatenating s_t with the chosen action a_t.
- **Rewards:** The page reward is defined as the sum of discounted product of purchase probability and item utility for all items on a page. The objective is to maximize expected cumulative utilities over episodes.
- **Policy (π):** A stochastic policy that maps states to a probability distribution over actions.

**Data Understanding Tools:**
A Transformer-based sequence-to-sequence model is used as a data understanding tool. It encodes user context and decodes item sequences. By comparing the scores of sequences generated by greedy search (SL-based) with those from beam search (approximating optimal RL policy), the tool helps determine if a dataset exhibits significant long-term dependencies suitable for RL modeling, rather than being adequately addressed by greedy SL strategies.

**Systematic Evaluation Framework:**
A novel, multi-faceted evaluation framework is proposed to address biases in RL-based RS evaluation:
- **Evaluation of Environment Simulation:** Supervised learning baselines (DNN, Wide&Deep, GRU4Rec, DIEN, Adversarial User Model) are trained to predict user responses to item slates. Performance is measured using slate-wise classification accuracy, item-wise classification accuracy/AUC, and item-wise ranking metrics (Precision, Recall, F1, AUC), as well as direct reward prediction error metrics (mean, absolute mean, standard deviation).
- **Policy Evaluation on Simulation Environments (Online Policy Evaluation):** Various model-free RL algorithms are trained on the *learned* simulation environments. This includes discrete-action baselines (Exact-k, PG, DQN, RAINBOW, PA-DQN, A2C, PPO) and continuous-action baselines (PG-conti, A2C-conti, DDPG, TD3, PPO-conti), implemented using RLlib.
- **Batch Reinforcement Learning (Offline Policy Learning):** This involves learning policies directly from the collected offline data (e.g., Slate-SL). Baselines include imitation learning (Behavioral Cloning - BC), model-free batch RL algorithms (BCQ, CQL), and model-based batch RL algorithms (MOPO, COMBO), implemented using d3rlpy. These are evaluated on the learned simulation environments.
- **Counterfactual Policy Evaluation (CPE):** Methods for offline policy evaluation, such as Importance Sampling (IS), Step-Wise Weighted Importance Sampling (SWIS), Doubly-Robust (DR), and Sequential Doubly-Robust (Sequential DR), are applied to score trained RL agents without online deployment.
- **Evaluation on Environments Built from Test Set:** To mitigate overfitting risks, the framework includes evaluating algorithms on simulation environments constructed specifically from separate test data (e.g., using Dataset-RL to build the environment model after training on Dataset-SL), further decoupling training and evaluation environments.

**Live Experiments:**
Select algorithms (DIEN as a greedy baseline, A2C, DQN, PPO) were deployed in an online A/B test on the real application. Key metrics included Individual Page View (IPV), Conversion Rate (CVR), and Gross Merchandise Volume (GMV) over a one-month period.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several findings derived from the various components of its methodology:

**Data Understanding Tool:**
The analysis using the Transformer-based sequence model indicated that the RL4RS dataset exhibits characteristics suitable for RL. For RL4RS-Slate, the greedy recommendation strategy scored 0.62 relative to the best 5% item sequences (obtained via beam search), and sequences of 'hot items' scored 0.01. This substantial performance gap suggests that simple greedy approaches are insufficient for this dataset and that long-term optimization is relevant. In contrast, traditional datasets like RecSys15, MovieLens, Last.fm, and CIKMCup2016 showed greedy strategies performing very closely to or even exceeding the top beam-search sequences (e.g., RecSys15 greedy score was 1.26 relative to top 5%), suggesting these datasets may not necessitate complex RL modeling. Additionally, RL4RS demonstrated lower Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between immediate item rewards and final sequence scores, indicating a stronger long-term impact on overall session utility compared to other datasets.

**Simulation Environment Construction (Tables 4 & 5):**
Several supervised learning models were evaluated for simulating user behavior. GRU4Rec and DIEN generally achieved the highest predictive performance across slate-wise classification, item-wise classification, and item-wise ranking tasks on both RL4RS-Slate and RL4RS-SeqSlate. For instance, GRU4Rec achieved 0.429 accuracy and 0.913 AUC for slate-wise classification on RL4RS-Slate. Despite these strong classification and ranking metrics, the reward estimation error remained substantial. For DIEN, the absolute mean reward prediction error was 36.2 for RL4RS-Slate and 66.2 for RL4RS-SeqSlate. This observation suggests an inconsistency between traditional classification/ranking objectives and accurate reward estimation, indicating a need for more specialized user simulators for RL-based RS.

**Model-free Reinforcement Learning (Table 6):**
Algorithms were benchmarked on the learned DIEN-based simulation environment.
- **Discrete Action Space:** PPO demonstrated the highest reward for RL4RS-SeqSlate (319.7). For RL4RS-Slate, PG, PPO, and A2C showed similar performance around 160. PA-DQN achieved comparable performance to DQN in RL4RS-Slate but notably better performance in RL4RS-SeqSlate (276.3 vs. 226.1 for DQN), indicating advantages in using concatenated state-action pairs. Exact-k, a method tailored for slate recommendations, did not show dominant performance.
- **Continuous Action Space:** TD3 achieved the best performance among continuous-action baselines (178.1 for RL4RS-Slate, 272.9 for RL4RS-SeqSlate). However, most continuous control RL algorithms generally exhibited lower rewards compared to their discrete counterparts, with the exception of TD3.

**Batch Reinforcement Learning (Table 7):**
Batch RL algorithms were evaluated on the learned DIEN-based simulation environment.
- Behavioral Cloning (BC) achieved a reward of 223.9 for RL4RS-SeqSlate, surpassing the average session reward of 164.5 in the dataset, which might be attributed to its selection strategy.
- BCQ and CQL showed better results than BC, affirming the effectiveness of batch RL techniques.
- Overall, batch RL methods yielded lower rewards compared to model-free RL algorithms, which is expected given that model-free methods train online within the simulation environment.
- CQL exhibited a performance disparity between discrete (103.5 reward) and continuous (82.3 reward) action spaces, suggesting increased difficulty in fitting continuous actions from offline batched data.
- Model-based batch RL algorithms (MOPO-conti, COMBO-conti) performed poorly, attributed to the difficulty in training their dynamic models to predict next observations and rewards directly from offline datasets.

**Counterfactual Policy Evaluation (Table 8):**
CPE methods were applied to score trained RL agents offline.
- IS-based methods (IS, SWIS) were found to be unstable, with SWIS often reaching clipping limits, which affected their reliability.
- DR-based methods (DR, Sequential DR) showed comparatively better stability, benefiting from their state value estimation.
- CPE results for batch RL (e.g., CQL DR score of 2.20) and model-free RL (e.g., PPO DR score of 1.24) presented different conclusions than evaluations on the simulation environment, underscoring the complexities of offline evaluation.

**Live Experiments (A/B Test) (Table 9):**
Online A/B testing over one month revealed that the PPO algorithm led to the highest Gross Merchandise Volume (GMV), showing increases of 23.9% over the DIEN baseline, 8.4% over A2C, and 12.3% over DQN. RL methods (A2C, DQN, PPO) generally outperformed the greedy DIEN strategy in GMV, demonstrating their capability to leverage multi-step decision-making for long-term optimization while managing conversion rate trade-offs. The observed online performance ranking (PPO > A2C > DQN > DIEN) was consistent with the ranking found in the simulation environment, suggesting a degree of correlation between offline simulation-based evaluations and online deployment results.

**Evaluation on Another Simulation Environment (Tables 15 & 16 - Preliminary Findings):**
Preliminary experiments with different dataset splits (e.g., training on Dataset-SL, evaluating on environment built from Dataset-RL) showed that the choice of dataset for environment construction significantly impacted reward estimation performance and the performance of batch RL algorithms. Reward estimation errors showed variations (e.g., a ~10% increase in absolute mean error for DIEN) depending on the train/test split of the environment model. For batch RL, the environment construction dataset had a noticeable impact on final results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RL4RS project has several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Addressing the Reality Gap in RL-based RS:** The primary significance lies in providing the first open-source, real-world dataset for RL-based RS. This directly addresses a recognized limitation in the field, where research has largely relied on artificial or semi-simulated data. By offering industrial-scale, real-world logged data, RL4RS enables researchers to develop and test algorithms under more realistic conditions, potentially leading to more practically applicable and robust solutions.

- **Advancing Evaluation Methodologies:** The proposed systematic evaluation framework is a substantial contribution. It moves beyond simplistic evaluation metrics and settings by integrating environment simulation assessment, online policy evaluation on simulators, batch RL, and counterfactual policy evaluation (CPE), alongside evaluation on environments constructed from separate test sets. This comprehensive framework can enhance the rigor and reliability of research by providing multiple perspectives on algorithm performance, crucial for mitigating biases and extrapolation errors before costly online deployments.

- **Fostering New Research Directions:**
 - **Improved User Simulators:** The observed discrepancy between high classification accuracy and substantial reward prediction error in environment simulation highlights a critical research gap. RL4RS data can stimulate efforts to develop more sophisticated user simulators specifically designed to accurately predict long-term rewards and account for prediction variance in RL-based RS.
 - **Offline Reinforcement Learning:** The release of separated data collected before and after RL deployment (e.g., Slate-SL and Slate-RL) directly supports research in batch RL and CPE. These areas are vital for learning effective policies from static, historically logged data without needing active online interaction, thereby addressing "extrapolation error" and reducing reliance on perfect simulators.
 - **Rethinking Applied RL Evaluation:** The project's findings regarding the impact of different data splits and environment construction on evaluation results challenge conventional RL benchmarking practices. This can prompt a re-evaluation of how applied RL systems, particularly in dynamic, real-world contexts like recommender systems, should be rigorously evaluated to ensure generalizability and prevent overfitting.

- **Enhancing Reproducibility and Accessibility:** By providing open-source datasets, comprehensive documentation, and associated code (including baselines and scripts for data processing and model training), RL4RS significantly enhances the reproducibility of research in this domain. This accessibility lowers the barrier to entry for new practitioners and facilitates collaborative research efforts.

- **Broader Impact on Applied Reinforcement Learning:** Beyond recommender systems, RL4RS serves as a valuable benchmark for the broader applied reinforcement learning community. It offers a unique resource for studying challenges related to real-world, batched offline data, sequential decision-making in complex environments, and the intricacies of evaluation in industrial settings.

- **Industry Relevance:** The origin of the dataset from a live game application by NetEase Inc. ensures its strong practical relevance. The commitment to assist researchers with deploying promising algorithms online for accurate evaluation further strengthens its connection to real-world industrial impact.
