# Communicative Efficiency of Single vs. Multi-Axis Robot Neck Motion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers quantitatively characterized the communicative efficiency of single versus multi-axis robot neck motions, identifying a "morphological information bottleneck" where three-degree-of-freedom movements are less informative but more energy-intensive than two-degree-of-freedom motions. The study found that two-degree-of-freedom roll-yaw movements at specific parameters offered an optimal balance of communicative information and energy cost, while single-axis movements provided the clearest human interpretations.

## Problem

- Robot design frameworks often overlook the communicative properties of individual body segments, such as the neck, despite their crucial role in human nonverbal communication.
- There is a need to balance mechanical performance with the capacity for clear and intuitive social communication in robot design.
- The communicative effectiveness and efficiency of multi-axis robot neck movements that depart from human biological architecture were unknown.

## Method

- An ==information-theoretic framework== was developed to quantify communicative information (==Shannon entropy== from pixel changes) and mechanical energy expenditure of robot neck motions.
- A Universal Robots UR3 manipulator with a compliant neck structure was used to systematically generate and measure various single- and multi-axis movements with controlled parameters.
- A human perception study was conducted to validate how observers interpret the communicative meaning and intensity of the generated robot neck motions.

## Results

- Energy consumption increased monotonically with the number of active degrees of freedom, with a **3-DoF** movement at **10°** being approximately **six times** more expensive than a single-axis Roll at **5°**.
- Communicative information (Shannon entropy) peaked at **2-DoF** (e.g., **5.00 bits** for 2-DoF at **3 Hz** frequency) and substantially dropped for 3-DoF motions (e.g., **4.43 bits** for 3-DoF at **1 Hz**).
- Single-axis Pitch motion was interpreted as 'Agreement/Yes' by **71.4%** of participants, and pure Yaw as 'Disagreement/No' by **63.4%**, while 3-DoF motions were highly ambiguous (**37.3%** 'Nothing').

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A "morphological information bottleneck" exists, where increasing mechanical complexity from 2-DoF to 3-DoF unexpectedly reduces communicative information while increasing energy consumption.
> - Optimal communicative efficiency, measured by the Entropic-to-Neuro-mechanical Information Efficiency (ENIE), is achieved with 1-DoF movements, providing the most information per joule.
> - Slower, more deliberate movements (0.5 m/s² acceleration) and larger angular amplitudes (10°) consistently produce higher communicative information and clearer human interpretation than fast or small movements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the expanding field of robotics, particularly focusing on human-robot interaction (HRI) and the design of humanoid robots. The robot market is experiencing significant growth, with humanoid robots projected to be a major segment. This growth intensifies the demand for design guidelines that extend beyond physical capabilities to encompass social communication.

Existing design frameworks for robots primarily address locomotion, manipulation, structural efficiency, and learning. However, the communicative properties of individual body segments, especially the neck, have remained underexplified. Nonverbal communication, through head movement and gaze, is a fundamental channel of social signaling in human interaction, conveying intent, attention, and affective state. Previous research has demonstrated that robot body movements, even when not explicitly designed for communication, transmit information to human observers and influence perception of attention and intention. Studies have explored the role of eyebrow movements, mouth synchrony, and particularly gaze direction and head orientation in HRI, showing their impact on perceived social presence and the complexity of generating contextually appropriate behaviors.

Despite this extensive body of work on nonverbal robot behavior, the communicative properties of neck movement as an independent expressive modality, and its relationship to energy expenditure, have been underexplored. User preferences for robot design often prioritize perceptual cues like eyes and head orientation, underscoring the communicative significance of head and neck motion. This gap is critical for humanoid design, where neck actuation directly affects mechanical properties and the social legibility of head-based nonverbal communication. This paper addresses this specific gap by providing an information-theoretic characterization of robot neck movements, aiming to offer quantitative design guidelines for next-generation humanoid platforms. It extends existing concepts such as using Shannon entropy to quantify the complexity of facial expression signals to analyze signals transmitted by robotic necks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing integration of humanoid robots into human-populated environments, necessitating a deeper understanding of how their physical form and actions influence social perception. Current robot design frameworks often overlook the communicative aspects of individual body segments, especially the neck, despite its crucial role in human nonverbal communication. There is a need to move beyond merely replicating human anatomy to designing robot morphologies that are both physically efficient and socially expressive.

Specifically, the authors identify two fundamental challenges that motivate their work:
1. **Bridging the gap between physical efficiency and socially expressive behavior:** Robot design must balance mechanical performance with the capacity for clear and intuitive social communication. An optimal design would maximize communicative impact while minimizing mechanical cost.
2. **Identifying how robot embodiments that depart from human features can nevertheless support intuitive and socially meaningful human-robot interaction:** While humans typically use single-axis head movements for gestures, robots are not constrained by biological architecture. This opens up possibilities for multi-axis movements, but their communicative effectiveness and efficiency are unknown.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of this study are:
- **To develop an information-theoretic framework:** This framework characterizes robot neck movement as a communication channel, allowing for the quantification of both information transmitted and energy expended across various morphological configurations. This involves a motor analysis of mechanical properties and an information-theoretic analysis using Shannon entropy.
- **To quantify information transmission and energy expenditure:** The research aims to measure the communicative information (via pixel-change signals and Shannon entropy) and the energy cost (via UR3 robot controller data) for different neck motion configurations, including single-axis and multi-axis movements, and variations in amplitude, acceleration, and frequency.
- **To validate communicative interpretations:** A perceptual study is conducted to assess how human observers interpret the communicative meaning and intensity of the generated robot neck motions, establishing a link between physical movement features and human interpretation.
- **To introduce the Motor Information Space (MIS) framework:** This framework maps entropy against energy to expose communicative efficiency across different robot neck morphologies and motion parameters, facilitating the identification of optimal configurations.
- **To challenge existing assumptions in robot design:** The study specifically investigates whether additional mechanical complexity (e.g., more degrees of freedom) automatically leads to improved robotic expressiveness, or if there are diminishing returns.
- **To establish quantitative design guidelines:** The ultimate goal is to provide a principled, quantitative basis for the morphological design of robot necks, particularly for humanoid platforms, that considers both communicative effectiveness and mechanical efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach combining robotic experimentation, information-theoretic analysis, and a human perception study.

#### 3.1. Robot Neck Mechanism

The study utilized a Universal Robots UR3 6-DoF robotic arm to generate neck motions. Instead of a custom-built neck, a compliant lattice neck structure, covered with colored silicone skin, was mounted as an end effector to the UR3. The UR3's wrist joints 1, 2, and 3 were mapped directly to roll, pitch, and yaw motions, respectively. This design choice allowed for precise, repeatable, and independently controllable motion parameters without needing custom sensing or complex engineering.
Motion parameters were systematically varied:
- **Motion types:** Roll (tilt), Pitch (nod), Yaw (shake), and combinations (roll+pitch, roll+yaw, pitch+yaw, roll+pitch+yaw). These were chosen based on their common human communicative interpretations (curiosity, agreement, disagreement).
- **Angular magnitudes (angles):** 5° and 10°.
- **Accelerations:** 0.5 m/s² and 2 m/s².
- **Repetition frequencies:** Once, twice, or thrice.
- **Speed:** Held constant at 0.5 m/s.
This resulted in 84 distinct video stimuli.

#### 3.2. Motion Analyses

Two complementary analyses were performed: motor-based analysis for mechanical efficiency and pixel-based analysis for information transfer.

- **Motor-based Analysis (Mechanical Efficiency):**
 - **Energy Calculation:** Electrical energy consumed by the UR3 robot controller was used as a proxy for mechanical energy expenditure. Joint motor currents ($I_i(k)$) and angular velocities ($\omega_i(k)$) were acquired at 125 Hz via the Real-Time Data Exchange (RTDE) interface.
 - **Torque and Power:** Joint torque ($\tau_i(k)$) was estimated from current using a linear motor model with motor torque constants ($K_{t,i}$). Instantaneous mechanical power ($P_i(k)$) for each joint was calculated as the absolute product of torque and angular velocity ($|\tau_i(k) \cdot \omega_i(k)|$), ensuring regenerative phases contribute positively to energy budget. Total instantaneous power ($P_{total}(k)$) was the sum across all active joints.
 - **Cumulative Energy:** Total energy ($E(k)$) was computed by numerical integration of $P_{total}(k)$ using the trapezoidal rule over the movement duration. Mean power ($\bar{P}$) and peak power ($P_{peak}$) were also derived.
 - **Assumptions:** Joint-level contributions were assumed independent and additive. Electrical energy was considered a valid proxy for mechanical energy.
 - **Parameters:** Motion types (1, 2, 3 DoF), angles (5°, 10°).

- **Pixel-based Analysis (Communicative Information):**
 - **Video Recording:** Video sequences of the robotic head performing neck motions were recorded using a fixed camera against a static background. The face and neck region was extracted.
 - **Pixel-Change Signal:** For each video, a one-dimensional time series ($\delta_t$) was generated by computing the mean absolute pixel difference between consecutive grayscale frames ($|I_t(x, y) - I_{t-1}(x, y)| / (W \cdot H)$). This signal served as a proxy for the perceptual salience and visual change of the movement.
 - **Shannon Entropy:** Shannon entropy ($H$) was computed from the pixel-change signal. A 64-bin histogram of $\delta_t$ values was constructed across the entire video, normalized to a probability distribution ($p_i$), and the standard entropy formula ($H = - \sum p_i \log_2 p_i$) was applied. Higher entropy (measured in bits) indicated greater visual variability and informational richness.
 - **Entropic-to-Neuro-mechanical Information Efficiency (ENIE):** This metric quantified communicative efficiency as the ratio of communicative information ($H$) to mechanical cost ($E$) ($ENIE = H/E$).
 - **Justification:** This method avoids complex kinematic analysis or marker tracking, directly measuring the information content as perceived by an external observer from visual cues, including skin deformation and shadows.

#### 3.3. Assessment of Human Information Reception (Perceptual Study)

A human perception study involving 17 participants (8 female, 9 male, mean age 30.8 years) was conducted. Participants viewed short video clips of the robotic head performing neck movements with a neutral facial expression.
- **Questions:**
 - Q1: "What does this head motion of robot convey to you?" (Options: Agreement/Yes, Disagreement/No, Curiosity, Nothing, Something else).
 - Q2: "From a scale of 0 to 5, what is the intensity of that nonverbal message?" (0 – Null, 3 – Appropriate, 5 – Fully informative).
- **Data Collection:** A total of 714 valid response pairs were collected across the 84 stimuli.
- **Analysis:** Responses were aggregated and analyzed statistically to assess perceived meaning distribution, intensity scores, and the influence of motion parameters (axis, angle, frequency) and participant demographics (gender) on interpretation clarity.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setting

The robot's face was based on the Eva humanoid robot design (face cover only). The compliant neck's core was a trimmed helicoid structure, designed for anisotropic flexibility (bending flexibility, axial stiffness). The UR3 manipulator was covered with a black background. Data acquisition and motion commands were controlled via the ur_rtde Python library.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the mechanical costs, informational content, and human perception of robot neck movements.

#### 4.1. Motor-based Analysis (Energy Consumption)

- **Total Energy Consumption (Figure 3A):** Energy expenditure increased monotonically with the number of active degrees of freedom (DoF), from single-axis to three-axis combinations. The increase was non-linear for 10° movements, with a much steeper rise compared to 5° movements. Yaw motion was consistently less energetically costly than Roll or Pitch at both angles. Roll+Pitch combinations showed a higher energy cost than Roll+Yaw and Pitch+Yaw for 10° movements, suggesting specific mechanical interaction costs. A 3-DoF movement at 10° was approximately six times more expensive than a single-axis Roll at 5°.
- **Peak Power Consumption (Figure 3B):** Peak power mirrored total energy trends but with a larger spread, indicating that peak power grew faster with increasing DoF. Roll+Pitch at 10° again exhibited the highest peak power among 2-DoF combinations. The 5° trendline was flatter, showing less sensitivity to axis combination at smaller angles.
- **Mean Power Consumption (Figure 3C):** Mean power followed similar trends to total energy but at a much smaller scale. The cost difference between 5° and 10° was proportionally consistent across motion types, suggesting that the additional cost of larger angles is primarily impulsive rather than sustained. At 5°, mean power was nearly independent of the number of active axes.
- **Energy per DoF (Figure 3D):** For 5° movements, energy per DoF remained nearly flat across all combinations (0.016–0.020 J/axis), indicating proportional scaling. For 10° movements, the trend was curved and higher, with Pitch being a notable outlier due to disproportionate energy cost, possibly due to gravity. The cost per axis decreased slightly for the 3-DoF combination, suggesting the third axis (yaw) was relatively inexpensive.
- **Delta Energy (10° vs 5° Movements) (Figure 3E):** All 10° movements cost more than 5° movements. The extra energy cost ($\Delta E$) for increasing amplitude from 5° to 10° was largely additive across individual axes for 2-DoF combinations. For the full 3-DoF combination, $\Delta E$ was slightly sub-additive compared to the sum of individual axis $\Delta E$, potentially indicating shared mechanical work.

#### 4.2. Pixel-based Analysis (Communicative Information)

- **Acceleration vs. Entropy (Figure 4A):** Counterintuitively, entropy decreased as acceleration increased. Slower acceleration (0.5 m/s²) produced higher entropy due to smoother, more sustained pixel changes. 2-DoF motions consistently generated the highest entropy, while 3-DoF motions yielded the lowest.
- **DoF vs. Entropy (Figure 4B, 4E, 4H):** Entropy peaked at 2-DoF and then substantially dropped at 3-DoF, a phenomenon termed the "morphological information bottleneck." This pattern held for both 5° and 10° movements. While 1-DoF and 2-DoF had similar median entropy (~4.87 bits), 3-DoF had a lower median (~4.67 bits) and a wider spread of values, indicating greater variability in informational output. The maximum mean entropy (5.00 bits) was observed at 2-DoF and 3 Hz frequency.
- **Angle vs. Entropy (Figure 4C):** Larger movement amplitude (10°) consistently produced higher entropy across all DoF levels, with the effect being additive and not interacting with DoF.
- **DoF vs. Entropy by Acceleration (Figure 4D):** Slow acceleration (0.5 m/s²) consistently produced higher entropy than fast acceleration (2 m/s²) across all DoF levels. The effects of acceleration and DoF were independent and additive.
- **ENIE vs. DoF (Figure 4F):** The Entropic-to-Neuro-mechanical Information Efficiency (ENIE) dropped monotonically as DoF increased (1 DoF = 0.0121 bits/J, 2 DoF = 0.0061 bits/J, 3 DoF = 0.0039 bits/J). This indicated that 1-DoF motions were the most energy-efficient per joule, while total information capacity was highest at 2-DoF.
- **Heatmaps (Figure 4G, 4H):** The highest entropy was achieved with 10° amplitude and 0.5 m/s² acceleration (5.01 bits), suggesting angle has a stronger effect on entropy than acceleration. The global maximum mean entropy (5.00 bits) was found at 2-DoF and 3 Hz frequency, while the minimum was at 3-DoF and 1 Hz (4.43 bits).
- **Optimal Configuration:** The top-ranked individual video in terms of pixel entropy was a roll+yaw (2-DoF) combination at 10°, 0.5 m/s², and 3 Hz (5.258 bits), outperforming any 3-DoF combination.
- **Morphological Information Bottleneck (Figure 5A):** Visualized as the divergence between normalized Shannon entropy (peaking at 2 DoF and dropping at 3 DoF) and normalized total energy (monotonically rising).
- **Motor Information Space (MIS) (Figure 5B, 5C):** This 2D space mapped entropy against energy. The Pareto frontier, representing non-dominated configurations (more information for less energy), included 1-DoF motions (delivering similar entropy to 2-DoF at lower energy). 3-DoF configurations consistently fell off the Pareto frontier, being less informative and more energy-intensive than alternatives.

#### 4.3. Assessment of Human Information Reception (Perceptual Study)

- **Overall Perceptions (Figure 6A):** "Agreement/Yes" was the most frequently perceived meaning (25.8%), followed by "Curiosity" (21.1%) and "Disagreement/No" (18.8%). The mean perceived intensity was 2.37 out of 5.
- **Influence of Axis of Rotation (Figure 6B):** Pure Pitch motion was strongly associated with "Agreement/Yes" (71.4%), and pure Yaw with "Disagreement/No" (63.4%). Pure Roll was predominantly interpreted as "Curiosity" (53.5%).
- **Combined Motions and Ambiguity:** Combined multi-axis motions generally reduced interpretive clarity. Roll+Pitch retained an affirmative leaning (53.8% "Agreement/Yes"), but Roll+Yaw shifted towards "Disagreement/No" (41.9%). Motions combining all three axes (Roll+Pitch+Yaw) were the most ambiguous, with "Nothing" being the plurality response (37.3%). Pitch+Yaw also showed high ambiguity.
- **Perceived Intensity:** Pitch-only motions yielded the highest mean intensity score (2.80), while Roll+Yaw had the lowest (2.18).
- **Neck Angle Effect (Figure 6C):** Larger angles (10°) significantly increased perceived intensity (mean 2.57 vs. 2.16 for 5°) and reduced "Nothing" responses (from 20.0% to 14.8%), suggesting wider movements convey more meaningful signals.
- **Repetition Frequency Effect (Figure 6D):** A weak positive correlation existed between frequency and perceived intensity, with intensity increasing monotonically with more repetitions, although the overall effect was marginal.
- **Gender Differences (Figure 7):** Female participants assigned slightly higher intensity scores (mean 2.46 vs. 2.29) and showed a stronger tendency to interpret movements as "Agreement/Yes" and "Curiosity," while male participants reported "Nothing" more frequently. These differences did not reach statistical significance in this sample.
- **Consensus:** The mean agreement for the most-chosen response was 54.6%, with 67.9% of stimuli achieving majority agreement (≥50%) and 19.0% reaching high consensus (≥75%).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a quantitative foundation for the design of robot necks, moving beyond mimetic replication of human anatomy to optimize for communicative efficiency. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Challenging Design Assumptions:** The central finding of the morphological information bottleneck – that 3-DoF movements produce less communicative information than 2-DoF movements despite higher energy consumption – directly challenges the assumption that increasing mechanical complexity automatically enhances expressiveness. This suggests that for communication, designers should re-evaluate the necessity of full anatomical completeness.
- **Optimal Morphological Configuration:** The identification of a 2-DoF roll-yaw configuration (at large angles, low acceleration, high frequency) as the optimal balance between communicative capacity and energy efficiency, situated on the Pareto frontier of the Motor Information Space, provides a specific design guideline. It implies that Pitch, despite its prominence in human communication, can be mechanically expensive and informationally redundant when combined with other axes in a robotic context.
- **Motion Planning Guidelines:** The inverse relationship between acceleration and entropy indicates that slower, more deliberate movements are more informationally rich than fast, abrupt ones for communication-oriented robots. This offers a direct, low-cost design guideline for motion planning that can significantly improve communicative effectiveness without hardware changes. Similarly, large angles generally lead to higher entropy.
- **Energy Efficiency for Constrained Platforms:** The finding that a single yaw axis provides the best communicative return per joule has direct implications for lightweight, power-constrained robots, such as mobile robots, wearable devices, or drone-mounted heads, where full articulation may be impractical.
- **Generalizability of the Motor Information Space (MIS):** The MIS framework and the Entropic-to-Neuro-mechanical Information Efficiency (ENIE) metric are not limited to neck design. They represent a general methodology applicable to evaluating the communicative efficiency of any robot body part or motion modality (e.g., arms, torsos, wheeled bases). This allows for a principled, quantitative approach to identifying the "minimum sufficient morphology" for a given communicative function across diverse robotic platforms.
- **Implications for Humanoid Robot Design:** For humanoid robots, these results suggest that neck morphology should be strategically chosen based on the deployment context's communicative requirements, rather than simply mimicking human cervical anatomy. A 2-DoF roll-yaw configuration with optimized motion parameters could be more effective for social interaction in care settings, where clear signals of attention are paramount.
- **Insight into Artificial Communication Niches:** The study combines human-like capabilities (multi-DoF) with artificial features, providing insight into designing for effective human-robot synchrony and advancing the development of expressive cyber-physical systems.
- **Perceptual Clarity and Ambiguity:** The human perception study confirms that single-axis movements, particularly Pitch for agreement and Yaw for disagreement, are highly unambiguous. Conversely, multi-axis movements, especially 3-DoF combinations, significantly reduce interpretive clarity, reinforcing the concept of the morphological information bottleneck from a user's perspective.

In summary, this work provides a quantitative framework and empirical evidence that challenge intuitive assumptions in robot design, offering actionable guidelines for creating more efficient and perceptually effective communicative robots.
