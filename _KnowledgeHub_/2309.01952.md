# Deep Imitation Learning for Humanoid Loco-manipulation through Human Teleoperation

> [!summary] Summary
> The TRILL framework facilitates deep imitation learning for humanoid robots to acquire loco-manipulation skills through human teleoperation. This system integrates whole-body control into both data collection and policy learning, enabling a real-world DRACO 3 humanoid to perform complex bimanual manipulation tasks with high success rates, such as tool pick-and-place (8/10 trials) and removing a spray cap (9/10 trials).

## Problem

- Humanoid robots lack autonomy for diverse tasks in human-centric environments, primarily relying on manual programming or human teleoperation which often yields task-specific and computationally intensive solutions.
- Applying imitation learning to humanoid robots is challenging due to their floating-base dynamics, high degrees of freedom, the need to maintain balance during contact-rich tasks, and large action spaces.
- Existing teleoperation interfaces often lack tactile and proprioceptive sensing, making it difficult for human operators to achieve stable, real-time motions and provide high-quality demonstrations for complex humanoid tasks.

## Method

- ==The TRILL== framework employs a hierarchical control pipeline with a high-level visuomotor policy (neural network) to predict high-level task-space commands and a low-level whole-body controller to execute dynamically feasible joint-torque actions.
- A Virtual Reality (VR) teleoperation system enables intuitive human demonstration data collection by abstracting hand poses to Cartesian setpoints and locomotion to predefined gait sequences, simplifying complex control for operators.
- Implicit-hierarchical Whole-body Control (IHWBC) is used as the low-level controller to stabilize the robot's floating base, robustly execute motion commands, and facilitate compliant manipulation through a prioritized optimization scheme.

## Results

- In simulation, TRILL achieved high success rates of **96%** for free-space locomotion, **80%** for manipulation, and **92%** for loco-manipulation tasks, outperforming baseline imitation learning methods by an average of **28%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical importance of stereoscopic vision, joint state feedback, multimodal hand pose setpoints, and the hierarchical locomotion action space for overall task performance.
- Real-world deployment on the DRACO 3 humanoid robot successfully demonstrated complex bimanual manipulation tasks, such as 'Tool pick-and-place' (8/10 trials) and 'Removing a spray cap' (9/10 trials), showcasing robustness against real-world challenges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating whole-body control into both data collection and policy learning effectively bridges the gap between human intuition and complex humanoid control, allowing data-efficient learning for high-DoF floating-base systems.
> - Stereoscopic vision, proprioceptive joint state feedback, and multimodal action modeling (using Gaussian Mixture Models for hand poses) are crucial for capturing the nuances of human demonstrations and achieving robust manipulation skills.
> - A hierarchical decomposition of locomotion actions, separating gait triggering from locomotion type selection, improves data efficiency and learning performance for dynamic walking behaviors, especially when dealing with sparse commands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are increasingly recognized for their potential versatility in human-centric environments due to their human-like embodiment. However, the widespread deployment of these robots is impeded by a lack of autonomy, with most current operational approaches relying on manual programming or human teleoperation. While significant progress has been made in humanoid hardware and control algorithms, these methods often yield task-specific solutions that lack generality or are computationally intensive.

Imitation learning, a data-driven approach, has emerged as a promising method for developing robot controllers from human demonstrations. Deep imitation learning, in particular, has been successfully applied to robots with simpler morphologies, such as tabletop arms and wheeled platforms. However, applying these techniques to humanoid robots presents distinct challenges. Humanoids are floating-base systems that must maintain balance, especially during contact-rich tasks where physical interactions alter dynamics and increase control complexity. Standard teleoperation interfaces often lack tactile and proprioceptive sensing, further complicating these issues. Additionally, the high degree of freedom (DoF) inherent in humanoids leads to large action spaces, demanding substantial data and computational resources for policy learning.

Existing work in humanoid loco-manipulation explores kinodynamic whole-body solutions, but these often lack generality. Human teleoperation aims to mitigate control complexity, with some approaches developing systems for transferring human motions. However, achieving smooth, stable, real-time motions while maintaining balance for highly dynamic actions remains challenging due to computational intensity and numerical ill-conditioning in inverse dynamics approaches. For imitation learning from teleoperation, while effective for simpler robots and scalable data collection, mapping human operator commands to humanoid robots is difficult due to complex floating-base dynamics, stability issues, and state estimation uncertainties.

This work introduces TRILL as a framework specifically designed to address these challenges by integrating whole-body control into both data collection and policy learning. It aims to bridge the gap between human intuition and complex humanoid control by using a high-level action abstraction for imitation learning, supported by a robust low-level controller. By focusing on data-efficient learning for high-DoF floating-base systems and demonstrating capabilities on real-world humanoids, TRILL contributes to overcoming existing limitations in humanoid autonomy and imitation learning applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop humanoid loco-manipulation skills using deep imitation learning, specifically addressing the challenges associated with the high degree of freedom and floating-base dynamics of humanoid robots.

The motivation for this work stems from several key difficulties in current humanoid robot development:
- **Lack of Autonomy:** Despite advancements in hardware and control, humanoid robots largely lack autonomous capabilities for performing diverse tasks in human environments. Current methods predominantly rely on labor-intensive manual programming or human teleoperation, which are often task-specific and require significant human intervention.
- **Challenges of Floating-Base Systems:** Humanoid robots are complex floating-base systems that must continuously maintain balance, particularly during physical interaction with the environment (e.g., contact-rich tasks). These interactions introduce dynamic changes, increase uncertainty, and complicate control. Standard teleoperation interfaces often lack essential proprioceptive and tactile feedback, making it difficult for human operators to stabilize the robot or understand its interaction forces.
- **High Degree of Freedom and Large Action Space:** The numerous joints and limbs of humanoid robots result in a high degree of freedom. This translates to a vast action space for control policies, which significantly increases the data requirements and computational demands for effective policy learning, especially within deep imitation learning paradigms.
- **Difficulty in Data Collection:** Collecting high-quality, task-relevant demonstrations for complex humanoid loco-manipulation tasks is inherently difficult. This is due to the aforementioned control complexities, the need for robust stability, and the challenge of intuitively mapping human movements to robot actions.
- **Limitations of Existing Imitation Learning Approaches:** While deep imitation learning has shown success with simpler robot morphologies, existing methods struggle when directly applied to humanoids due to their complex dynamics, stability requirements, and the need for more sophisticated action abstractions.

To overcome these motivations and challenges, the authors propose incorporating the whole-body control formulation into both the data collection system and the policy learning method. This integration is intended to simplify demonstration provision for human operators through an intuitive Virtual Reality (VR) interface, enable the policy to predict high-level task-space actions, and transform these actions into dynamically feasible joint-level torque commands while stabilizing the robot's dynamics. The overarching goal is to create a data-efficient framework that facilitates the learning of complex sensorimotor skills for humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TRILL (Teleoperation and Imitation Learning for Loco-manipulation) framework is a deep imitation learning approach designed for humanoid robots. It employs a two-level hierarchical control pipeline consisting of a high-level visuomotor policy and a low-level whole-body controller.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The loco-manipulation problem is modeled as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process. The objective is to learn a closed-loop visuomotor policy, $\pi(a_t|s_t)$, that maximizes expected return. The state space ($S$) includes sensory observations (egocentric cameras, proprioceptive sensors), and the action space ($A$) consists of joint-torque commands. To manage complexity, the policy $\pi$ is decomposed into:
- **High-level policy ($\pi_H$):** A neural network that computes target motion commands ($u_t$), specifically hand pose setpoints and body locomotion instructions.
- **Low-level whole-body controller ($\pi_L$):** Calculates robot joint-torque actions ($a_t$) to realize the commands from $\pi_H$, ensuring dynamic feasibility and stability. The overall policy is represented as $\pi(a_t|s_t) = \pi_L(a_t|s_t, u_t)\pi_H(u_t|s_t)$.

**B. Teleoperation System:**
This system facilitates human demonstration data collection using an intuitive Virtual Reality (VR) interface (Meta's Oculus Quest 2).
- **Hand Pose Commands:** Cartesian hand poses in the robot's body frame are defined as setpoints for arm motions. VR hand controller poses (relative to the VR headset) are mapped to these setpoints. This reduces the action space for manipulation and implicitly compensates for the lack of joint sensory feedback during teleoperation. Trajectory interpolation (using Hermite curves) is applied to smooth hand trajectories, preventing aggressive or discontinuous motions and ensuring safe operation.
- **Locomotion Commands:** Locomotion is abstracted into easily executable, predefined gait sequences. These sequences are triggered by buttons on the VR hand controllers. The Divergent Component of Motion (DCM) approach is used for gait planning, which provides simplicity and a larger basin of attraction by indirectly controlling the robot's Center of Mass (COM). The teleoperation system only accepts new locomotion commands after the current gait sequence is completed. Predefined locomotion types include forward, backward, left/right turn, and left/right sidewalk.

**C. Whole-body Control (WBC):**
The low-level controller, $\pi_L$, is critical for stabilizing the robot's floating base and robustly executing motion commands. The Implicit-hierarchical Whole-body Control (IHWBC) formulation is employed.
- **Optimization:** A model-based optimal control method calculates motor torques. The optimization minimizes weighted norms of task acceleration errors (for body center, hands, feet), reaction force errors, and regularization terms for joint acceleration and reaction forces, subject to various constraints (e.g., robot dynamics, friction cone, joint limits, torque limits).
- **Prioritization Scheme:** Body pose stabilization is the highest priority, followed by tracking foot pose trajectories, and then hand pose trajectories. Hand pose tracking priority increases when the robot is balancing (not walking) to enhance manipulation precision, and decreases during walking to maintain overall stability.
- **Compliant Manipulation:** Target reaction forces at non-contact points on hands and feet are processed as virtual impedances relative to target trajectories. This allows the robot to exhibit adaptive, compliant manipulation during object interactions.
- **Torque Calculation:** The optimal joint accelerations and reaction forces from the optimization are used to determine the command torque, $\tau_{cmd}$, which is then augmented with PD control terms to compute the final joint torque inputs, $\tau_{joint}$.

**D. Training of Visuomotor Policies:**
The high-level policy, $\pi_H$, is trained using deep imitation learning from the collected demonstration dataset $D = \{(s_i, u_i)\}_{i=1}^N$.
- **Observation Space ($s_i$):** Includes stereo images (400x300 pixels RGB from two ResNet-18 encoders), actual positions of feet (14D) and hands (14D), controller state machine status (1D), and sine/cosine representations of joint positions (54D) and joint angular velocities (27D) for arms, legs, and neck. These features are concatenated after image encoding.
- **Action Space ($u_i$):** Consists of subsequent setpoints for hands (14D), grasping actions for both hands (2D), and locomotion instructions (1D).
- **Policy Architecture:** A behavioral cloning policy with recurrent neural networks (RNNs) is used to capture temporal dependencies. Specifically, a 2-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with 400 hidden units per layer processes the concatenated observation vector. The policy outputs are delivered through a 2-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with 1024 hidden units per layer.
- **Handling Multimodality and Locomotion:**
 - **Hand Pose Setpoints:** Sampled from a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with 5 modes to account for the noisy and multimodal nature of human demonstrations.
 - **Locomotion Actions:** A hierarchical decomposition is introduced for data efficiency. A Bernoulli distribution predicts a 'gait trigger' (whether to activate gait), and if activated, locomotion types are chosen from a categorical output.
 - **Loss Functions:** Cross-entropy loss is used for discrete grasping and locomotion type outputs. Negative log-likelihood loss is applied for the GMM (hand setpoints) and Bernoulli distribution (gait trigger).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of TRILL was evaluated through a series of experiments in both simulated environments and on a real-world humanoid robot.

**A. Quantitative Evaluation in Simulation:**
Experiments were conducted in two simulated environments, "Door" and "Workbench," with subtasks encompassing free-space locomotion, manipulation, and loco-manipulation. Policies were trained on datasets of 250 demonstration trajectories per task.

- **Deployment Results (Table I):**
 - TRILL achieved high success rates: 96% for free-space locomotion, 80% for manipulation, and 92% for loco-manipulation tasks.
 - The framework outperformed two baseline methods: a behavioral cloning (BC) policy and a state-of-the-art recurrent neural network behavioral cloning (BC-RNN) policy. Across all tasks, TRILL exhibited an average increase of 28% in success rate compared to the baselines.
 - Performance gaps were particularly noticeable in free-space locomotion and loco-manipulation tasks. The BC-RNN baseline, which models temporal dependencies, performed better than BC but struggled to learn from sparse locomotion commands, unlike TRILL's specialized formulation for locomotion.
 - Specific task success rates (full success): Walking to the door (100%), Unlocking the latch (92%), Pushing the door (96%), Walking to the cart (92%), Hammer pick-and-place (68%), Pushing the cart (88%).

- **Teleoperation Interface Evaluation (Figure 4):**
 - The VR-based teleoperation system was evaluated for intuitiveness and the impact of control delay and trajectory interpolation.
 - Increased control latency (250 ms and 500 ms) directly correlated with higher reported workload (NASA TLX scores) and increased task completion times, indicating a negative impact on demonstrator performance and demonstration quality.
 - The trajectory interpolation implemented in TRILL (for safety and smooth commands) showed only a minor impact on demonstration performance compared to direct raw input, suggesting it allows for high-quality data collection without significantly burdening the human operator or compromising robot safety.

- **Varying Dataset Sizes (Figure 5):**
 - An investigation into the effect of dataset size (from 50 to 250 demonstrations) indicated that an increase in the number of demonstrations generally led to improved performance of trained policies across various tasks, regardless of their inherent difficulty.

- **Ablation Studies (Table II):**
 - **`monoscopic` (single camera image):** Showed significant performance degradation, particularly in tasks requiring precise hand movements (e.g., Unlocking the latch, Hammer pick-and-place), highlighting the importance of depth information from stereoscopic vision.
 - **`no-joint-state` (excluding joint feedback):** Also resulted in reduced manipulation performance, indicating the role of proprioceptive feedback for successful task execution.
 - **`deterministic` (no GMM for hand poses):** Produced the most substantial decline in success rates across manipulation and loco-manipulation tasks. This suggests that a deterministic action output struggles to capture the multimodal nature of human demonstrations and the underlying complex robot dynamics.
 - **`categorical` (flat locomotion action space):** Led to a considerable reduction in performance for free-space locomotion and loco-manipulation tasks. This finding supports the efficiency of TRILL's hierarchical action space for locomotion, which separates gait triggering from locomotion types.

**B. Real-Robot Deployment:**
TRILL policies were deployed on the real-world DRACO 3 humanoid robot for bimanual manipulation tasks, trained on real-world demonstrations.

- **Teleoperation Interface Performance:** Tracking errors in the real-world teleoperation interface were noted, with hand position errors averaging 0.082 ± 0.032 m and hand orientation errors averaging 22.2 ± 10.9 degrees. The average control latency with the real system was 122 ± 190 msec. These factors emphasize the challenges of real-world deployment and the importance of consistency between demonstration and deployment environments.

- **Deployment of Trained Policies (Figure 6):**
 - The robot successfully performed the "Tool pick-and-place" task in 8 out of 10 trials.
 - The robot successfully performed the "Removing a spray cap" task in 9 out of 10 trials.
 - These tasks were performed consecutively without needing to reboot the robot, demonstrating the policies' robustness against real-world controller limitations, sensor inaccuracies, and floating-base dynamics. This work represents an early successful implementation of deep imitation learning for complex manipulation visuomotor policies on real-world humanoid systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TRILL framework addresses several fundamental challenges in enabling autonomous capabilities for humanoid robots, presenting significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotics.

Firstly, the work provides a practical and data-efficient deep imitation learning framework specifically tailored for humanoid loco-manipulation. By integrating whole-body control into both data collection and policy execution, TRILL effectively manages the inherent complexities of high-degree-of-freedom, floating-base systems. This hierarchical approach allows human operators to provide intuitive task-space demonstrations through a VR interface, which are then robustly translated into dynamically stable joint-torque commands. This abstraction simplifies the learning problem and makes complex humanoid behaviors more accessible for imitation learning.

Secondly, the quantitative results in simulation demonstrate the framework's effectiveness across various tasks—locomotion, manipulation, and combined loco-manipulation. TRILL's significant outperformance of state-of-the-art imitation learning baselines, particularly in tasks involving coordinated body and arm movements, highlights the benefit of its specialized observation and action space designs. The ablation studies further validate the critical role of stereoscopic vision, joint state feedback, multimodal action modeling (via GMM), and the hierarchical decomposition of locomotion actions, providing insights for future research in humanoid policy learning.

Thirdly, the successful deployment of trained policies on a real-world humanoid robot (DRACO 3) for complex bimanual manipulation tasks is a notable achievement. This real-world validation confirms the robustness of TRILL against practical challenges such as control latency, sensor inaccuracies, and the unpredictable dynamics of physical interaction. This demonstrates a step towards closing the sim-to-real gap for complex humanoid behaviors, which has traditionally been a major hurdle. The ability to perform tasks consecutively without reboots further underscores the reliability of the learned policies.

The potential impact of this research is multi-faceted:
- **Advancing Humanoid Autonomy:** TRILL contributes to overcoming the current limitations of manual programming and teleoperation, paving the way for more autonomous and versatile humanoid robots capable of performing diverse tasks in human-centric environments.
- **Enabling Complex Skills:** By demonstrating the learning of complex loco-manipulation skills, including contact-rich interactions, the framework broadens the scope of tasks that humanoids can perform autonomously.
- **Improved Imitation Learning:** The data-efficient nature of TRILL, achieving robust performance with relatively small datasets (250 trajectories), provides a valuable paradigm for imitation learning in complex robotic systems, reducing the burden of extensive data collection.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework lays a foundation for future work on long-horizon tasks, improving the generalization ability of learned skills, and exploring continuous learning from human interaction. The insights from the teleoperation interface and ablation studies can guide the development of more intuitive and effective human-robot interfaces and policy architectures.

In conclusion, TRILL represents a substantial step forward in enabling humanoid robots to learn and execute complex loco-manipulation skills from human demonstrations, bringing them closer to deployment in real-world scenarios.
