# Your Reward Function for RL is Your Best PRM for Search: Unifying RL and Search-Based TTS

> [!summary] Summary
> The AIRL-S framework unifies reinforcement learning (RL) and search-based test-time scaling (TTS) by learning a dynamic, policy-independent Process Reward Model (PRM) directly from correct reasoning traces, circumventing the need for expensive step-wise labels. This approach enhances LLM reasoning, achieving an average 9% performance gain over its base model and matching GPT-4o's overall accuracy with a 7B-parameter model across eight diverse reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with complex reasoning tasks, with existing Test-Time Scaling (TTS) approaches primarily relying on two separate, often limited, paradigms.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods for LLMs suffer from training instability and low sample efficiency due to sparse, outcome-based rewards.
- Search-based TTS techniques require Process Reward Models (PRMs) that are expensive to label for intermediate steps and prone to reward hacking or performance degradation under distribution shifts.

## Method

- ==AIRL-S== unifies RL and search-based TTS by learning a dense, dynamic, ==policy-independent Process Reward Model (PRM)== from correct reasoning traces using ==Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (AIRL)==, eliminating the need for step-wise labels.
- The LLM policy is optimized using a composite objective function that combines AIRL-derived dense process rewards with sparse outcome-based rewards from Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).
- The learned PRM is directly reused at inference time to guide various search-based TTS methods, including Best-of-N sampling, Beam Search, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), for verifying and steering the reasoning process.

## Results

- The AIRL-S trained policy (Qwen2.5-7B) achieved an average **9%** performance improvement over its base model across eight benchmarks, with a **13%** gain in mathematical and scientific reasoning, and matched the overall accuracy of GPT-4o.
- The Qwen2.5-AIRL-S-PRM consistently improved Best-of-N search performance for various generative LLMs, outperforming PRMs trained on labeled data and implicit PRMs by **1.4%** to **2.4%** on average.
- The learned PRM effectively guided MCTS, Beam Search, and Best-of-N methods, outperforming Self-Consistency and demonstrating enhanced inference computation scaling by generating longer, more elaborate responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The reward function learned during RL training inherently represents an ideal, dynamic Process Reward Model (PRM) for guiding downstream search, naturally unifying two distinct LLM reasoning paradigms.
> - Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (AIRL) can effectively learn a policy-independent, step-wise PRM from only correct final reasoning traces, eliminating the need for expensive intermediate process data labeling.
> - Combining dense, process-based rewards (from AIRL) with sparse, outcome-based rewards (from GRPO) during RL training leads to more stable policy learning, improved sample efficiency, and enhanced reasoning capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the challenge of enhancing the reasoning performance of large language models (LLMs) through Test-Time Scaling (TTS). Current TTS methods generally fall into two distinct categories: reinforcement learning (RL) based approaches and search-based techniques.

RL-based methods optimize sparse, outcome-based rewards. While effective in some large-scale implementations like OpenAI's o-series models and DeepSeek-R1, they often face issues with training instability and low sample efficiency due to the sparsity of rewards. These methods typically rely on Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) or Process Reward Models (PRMs) to guide training.

Search-based techniques, such as Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), beam search, and Best-of-N sampling, guide LLM reasoning at inference time by exploring multiple chains of thought (CoTs). These methods frequently leverage PRMs that provide step-wise supervision. However, static PRMs used in search-based methods typically require extensive and expensive human or LLM-generated labels for intermediate reasoning steps. Furthermore, a fixed PRM can suffer from distributional shifts when the underlying LLM policy evolves, potentially leading to "reward hacking" where the policy exploits the reward function rather than performing the desired behavior, thus undermining the stability and effectiveness of the search process.

This work aims to bridge these two paradigms by proposing a unified framework that leverages the strengths of both, specifically by learning a dense, dynamic PRM directly from successful reasoning traces, thereby eliminating the need for separate labeled process data and mitigating issues associated with static PRMs. This positions the research as an effort to improve the efficiency, robustness, and generalization capabilities of LLMs in complex reasoning tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:

1. **Unify RL-based and Search-based Test-Time Scaling (TTS):** The core goal is to integrate these two traditionally separate paradigms to enhance the complex reasoning capabilities of LLMs.
2. **Learn a generalizable, step-wise Process Reward Model (PRM) without labeled data:** The authors aim to develop a method for learning a PRM that can provide dense, step-wise rewards without requiring costly human or LLM-generated annotations for intermediate reasoning steps. This directly addresses the high cost and labor-intensive nature of creating such datasets for existing PRM-based approaches.
3. **Improve RL training stability and sample efficiency:** By learning a dense PRM, the framework seeks to provide more informative supervisory signals during RL training, thereby overcoming the instability and low sample efficiency often encountered with sparse, outcome-based rewards.
4. **Mitigate reward hacking and enhance cross-task generalization:** The goal is to develop a dynamic PRM that adapts with the policy, thereby reducing the risk of reward hacking caused by distributional shifts between a static PRM and an evolving policy. A generalizable PRM is also expected to perform robustly across different reasoning tasks and policy models.
5. **Facilitate robust reasoning chain extension at inference:** The learned PRM is intended to effectively guide various search procedures (e.g., MCTS, beam search) during inference, enabling more accurate selection and extension of reasoning chains.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the identified limitations of existing TTS methods: the instability and inefficiency of sparse outcome-based RL, and the expense, labeling dependency, and reward hacking susceptibility of static, labeled-data-trained PRMs in search-based TTS. By unifying these approaches and learning an effective PRM in a data-efficient manner, the authors aim to provide a more robust and cost-effective solution for scaling LLMs in complex reasoning domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **AIRL-S**, a framework that unifies RL-based and search-based TTS by integrating Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning (AIRL) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

**Problem Setup:**
The framework models LLM reasoning as a sequential decision-making process. For a given question `q`, a chain of thought `C(q) = {C1, C2..., CT}` is generated, where each `Ci` is a reasoning step. The objective is to learn a PRM `r_phi` that assigns a reward to each step `Ci` and a policy model `π_theta` that leverages these rewards for improved reasoning.

**Data Generation and Replay Buffer:**
Training begins by generating "reference rollouts" for questions in a dataset `Q`. These rollouts are obtained either by sampling multiple CoTs from the current policy `π_theta` or by utilizing existing CoTs. A binary outcome reward (correct/incorrect) is assigned to each CoT. CoTs that yield a correct final answer are stored in a replay buffer `B` and serve as reference data for training the AIRL reward model. The buffer is periodically updated to maintain reliability and diversity.

**Learning PRM via AIRL:**
To avoid the need for explicit step-wise labels, AIRL-S employs a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)-style approach to learn the PRM.
- **Discriminator (D_phi):** A discriminator `D_phi` is trained to distinguish between reference rollouts (from `B`) and rollouts generated by the current policy `π_theta`. The discriminator operates over state-action pairs. For LLM reasoning, the "state" is defined as the question `q` and preceding reasoning steps `{C1..., C_i-1}`, and the "action" is the current step `Ci`.
- **Step-wise Reward (r_phi):** The learned discriminator `D_phi` is then used to derive a step-wise reward function `r_phi(Ci | q, C_<i)` as `log(D_phi / (1 - D_phi))`. This `r_phi` serves as the dense, dynamic PRM.
- **AIRL Loss (L_AIRL):** The discriminator is trained by minimizing `L_AIRL`, which is a cross-entropy-like loss that encourages `D_phi` to output high values for reference rollouts and low values for policy rollouts.

**Policy Training with Combined RL Objectives:**
The policy model `π_theta` is optimized using a composite objective that combines rewards from AIRL and GRPO:
- **AIRL Objective (J_AIRL):** This objective encourages the policy to generate rollouts that "fool" the discriminator, i.e., make them indistinguishable from reference rollouts, thereby maximizing the discriminator-derived step-wise rewards. It uses a clipped Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)-style objective with advantages estimated using a REINFORCE-style method.
- **GRPO Objective (J_GRPO):** This objective, adapted from DeepSeek-R1, uses binary outcome rewards for groups of `G` CoTs. It also incorporates a KL divergence term (`D_KL`) to regularize the policy against a frozen reference model (`π_ref`), preventing large policy shifts.
- **Composite Objective (J(theta)):** The final policy update maximizes `J(theta) = λ * J_AIRL(theta) + (1 - λ) * J_GRPO(theta)`, where `λ` is a hyperparameter balancing the step-wise process rewards and the outcome rewards.

The training process alternates between updating the discriminator `D_phi` (and thus `r_phi`) and optimizing the policy `π_theta`.

**PRM-guided Test-Time Search:**
At inference time, the learned `r_phi` (PRM) is directly utilized to guide various search algorithms for TTS:
- **Best-of-N:** `N` full solutions are sampled from `π_theta`, and the PRM `r_phi` is used to score and select the best one.
- **Beam Search:** At each step, multiple candidate steps are generated, ranked by `r_phi`, and the top `N` are retained for the next iteration, forming a beam of `N` partial solutions.
- **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS):** `r_phi` is integrated into the MCTS process for selection (using UCT scores that combine PRM rewards with visit counts), simulation (estimating node values by rolling out using `r_phi`), and backpropagation (updating rewards).

**PRM Aggregation:**
After a search procedure yields `N` candidate solutions, an "aggregation score" `s(C_k)` is computed for each solution `C_k`, typically as the minimum `r_phi` reward among its steps (`PRM-Min-Sum`). For inter-answer aggregation, all solutions yielding the same final answer `a` have their `s(C_k)` summed to produce `S(a)`, and the answer with the highest `S(a)` is chosen.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across eight benchmarks, including mathematics (AIME2024, AMC, MATH500), scientific reasoning (GPQA-Diamond), and code generation (HumanEval, MBPP, LeetCode, LiveCodeBench-v4).

**Effectiveness of the Policy Model (Qwen2.5-7B-AIRL-S):**
- The `AIRL-S` policy model demonstrated a significant average performance improvement of 9% over its base model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) across all eight benchmarks. On mathematical and scientific reasoning tasks alone, the improvement was 13%.
- It outperformed existing RL-trained LLMs that use PRMs from labeled data (e.g., Math-Shepherd-Mistral-7B-RL, DeepSeek-Math-7B-RL) and models using implicit PRMs (e.g., Eurus-2-7B-PRIME), achieving higher reasoning accuracy across all tasks.
- `AIRL-S` surpassed models trained via direct imitation learning on reference rollouts (e.g., s1.1-7B) by an average of 15%.
- Despite having half the parameter count, `AIRL-S` outperformed Phi-4-14B on average and matched the performance of GPT-4o.

**Effectiveness of the PRM (Qwen2.5-AIRL-S-PRM):**
- When integrated with Best-of-N search (64 rollouts) across different generative LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Eurus-2-7B-PRIME, Phi-4-14B, Qwen2.5-7B-AIRL-S) and datasets (AIME2024, AMC, MATH500), the `Qwen2.5-AIRL-S-PRM` consistently improved performance.
- It outperformed PRMs trained on labeled step-wise data (Math-Shepherd-Mistral-7B-PRM, Llama3.1-8B-PRM-Deepseek-Data) and an implicit PRM baseline (EurusPRM-Stage2) by 2.4% and 1.4% respectively, on average.
- The combination of `Qwen2.5-7B-AIRL-S` policy and `Qwen2.5-AIRL-S-PRM` yielded the highest performance among all tested LLM-PRM combinations, demonstrating the synergistic effect of the unified approach.

**Effectiveness on Different Search-Based TTS Methods:**
- The `Qwen2.5-AIRL-S-PRM` effectively guided MCTS, Beam Search, and Best-of-N across varying rollout counts on AMC and MATH500. It consistently improved performance for all these methods as the number of rollouts increased.
- The PRM-guided search methods notably outperformed Self-Consistency (which uses majority voting without PRM feedback), indicating the value of reward-guided step generation and selection.
- MCTS, as a value-based method that estimates step values and uses UCT scores, showed particularly strong benefits from the PRM's guidance, leading to more accurate intermediate steps.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Impact of PRM on RL Training:** Comparing `AIRL-S` (with PRM) to GRPO (outcome rewards only) revealed that the inclusion of the PRM improved both training and validation accuracy. Furthermore, `AIRL-S` enabled the generation of longer responses at test time, indicating enhanced capacity for extended reasoning.
- **Impact of PRM Aggregation Techniques:** On MATH500, `PRM-Min-Vote` and `PRM-Last-Vote` achieved the highest accuracy, suggesting that step-level rewards facilitate effective answer aggregation. In contrast, `PRM-Min-Max` and `PRM-Last-Max` performed worse, highlighting the importance of aggregating scores via PRM-weighted voting across candidate solutions.
- **Comparison with Concurrent Implicit PRM Methods:** `AIRL-S` consistently outperformed PRIME (a concurrent method that also learns an implicit, policy-dependent dense reward function) both with and without test-time search (Best-of-N with 64 rollouts) on AIME2024, AMC, and MATH500. This further validates the effectiveness of the `AIRL-S` framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The `AIRL-S` framework represents a significant development by effectively unifying RL-based and search-based TTS for LLMs. Its key contributions and potential impacts are:

- **Cost-Effectiveness and Data Efficiency:** By learning a dense, step-wise PRM directly from correct reasoning traces using AIRL, the framework eliminates the need for expensive human or LLM-generated labels for intermediate process data. This substantially reduces the cost and effort associated with developing high-quality PRMs, making advanced reasoning capabilities more accessible.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Stability:** The dynamic nature of the PRM, learned jointly with the policy, helps to mitigate reward hacking and degradation under distributional shifts, issues commonly faced by static PRMs. This leads to more stable and reliable RL training and inference search procedures.
- **Improved Generalization:** The learned PRM is shown to be generalizable, performing effectively across different LLMs, datasets, and search algorithms. This portability allows the PRM to serve as a versatile verifier for diverse reasoning tasks, enhancing cross-task generalization.
- **Strong Performance Gains:** The `AIRL-S` policy model demonstrates notable performance improvements over base models and outperforms several state-of-the-art RL-trained and imitation-based LLMs, matching models like GPT-4o in performance on challenging benchmarks. This indicates that the unified approach effectively scales LLMs for complex reasoning.
- **Broader Applicability of Reward Functions:** The core insight that "your reward function for RL is your best PRM for search" unifies the utility of the reward function beyond just policy optimization, enabling it to act as an effective heuristic for test-time search.

**Potential Positive Impacts:**
- **Scientific Acceleration:** Open-sourced PRMs could act as verifiable tools in symbolic mathematics and formal methods, potentially expediting proof discovery.
- **Educational Equity:** A modest-sized model achieving near-GPT-4o performance could enable the development of accessible, low-cost tutoring tools in underserved regions.
- **Software Reliability:** The PRM could be used to rank candidate code patches, potentially reducing defects in critical software systems.

**Potential Negative Impacts and Misuse Scenarios:**
- **Disinformation:** The enhanced reasoning capabilities could be leveraged to automate the generation of convincing but false technical or persuasive content.
- **Fairness and Bias:** Reward models inherently reflect biases present in their training data. These biases could be amplified through MCTS selection processes, leading to unfair or biased outputs.
- **Dual-Use Technology:** Chain-level reward shaping could be exploited by malicious actors to explore large search spaces for purposes such as exploit generation or advanced surveillance analytics.
- **Labor Displacement:** The automation of tasks akin to competitive programming could potentially displace junior software engineering roles.

Overall, the `AIRL-S` framework offers an efficient, robust, and generalizable method for enhancing LLM reasoning, with significant implications for both fundamental research and practical applications, alongside a need for careful consideration of its societal impacts.
