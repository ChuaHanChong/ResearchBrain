# A Hierarchical, Model-Based System for High-Performance Humanoid Soccer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UCLA developed ARTEMIS, an integrated adult-sized humanoid robot system featuring novel hardware and a hierarchical, model-based software stack, which secured victory at the RoboCup 2024 Adult-Sized Humanoid Soccer Competition by enabling dynamic locomotion, powerful in-gait kicks, and robust autonomous gameplay.

## Problem

- Humanoid soccer demands continuous adaptation, robust visual perception despite noise/delays, and stable control under impacts for heavy adult-sized robots.
- Existing adult-sized humanoids often use servo-based actuation and ZMP-style locomotion, which restricts dynamic movements and powerful in-gait kicks, limiting mobility.
- Competitive software architectures typically feature simple behavior planners, lack mid-level navigation, have minimal collision avoidance, and treat perception/localization as loosely coupled modules.

## Method

- ARTEMIS utilizes a hierarchical, model-based control framework with a tightly integrated perception–planning–control stack, operating with a 1 kHz low-level control loop and ROS 2 nodes for higher-level functions.
- The hardware platform features lightweight structural components, high-torque quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuators, and a specialized quick-release foot design for powerful, mid-swing kicks.
- The software stack includes an integrated vision (YOLOv8m) and localization (CLAP) framework, a two-stage collision-aware navigation system (DAVG, cf-MPC), a hybrid behavior tree for decision-making, and a ==Perception-Locked Mid-Swing Kicking (PLMK)== module.

## Results

- ARTEMIS won the RoboCup 2024 Adult-Sized Humanoid Soccer Competition, scoring 45 goals in 6 official matches and demonstrating high-performance autonomous capabilities.
- The system achieved **92%** directional consistency and **84%** successful goals for autonomous shots, with the PLMK module enabling peak leg velocities of **3.2** m/s during mid-swing kicks.
- Localization demonstrated a mean absolute error (MAE) of **0.1651** m, and navigation achieved sub-decimeter tracking accuracy (MAE **0.0945** m) and consistent obstacle avoidance in trials.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A tightly coupled, hierarchical, model-based integration of hardware and software components is critical for achieving high-performance autonomous behavior in dynamic, adversarial environments.
> - Powerful and accurate in-gait kicks can be achieved without interrupting locomotion by combining proprioceptive QDD actuators, a specialized foot design, and a mid-swing kicking module.
> - Robust and stable pose estimation, perception, and collision-aware navigation in challenging, dynamic environments benefit from combining optimized deep learning with geometric clustering and model predictive control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen progress in recent years, largely due to advancements in hardware (e.g., quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuators, torque-controlled joints) and control methodologies (e.g., whole-body locomotion controllers, optimal control, learning-based techniques). These developments have enabled robots to exhibit dynamic behaviors and tackle a range of tasks, including loco-manipulation and assistance in structured environments.

However, humanoid robot soccer, particularly in the RoboCup competition's Adult-Sized Humanoid League, presents a distinct set of challenges that push the limits of integrated robotic systems. This environment demands continuous adaptation to unpredictable changes, robust visual perception despite noise, delays, and occlusions, and stable control under impacts and rapid possession changes. These challenges are amplified for adult-sized humanoids, which are heavier and capable of generating substantial forces, making balance recovery and momentum redirection more complex. RoboCup rules further restrict robots to onboard cameras and proprioception, necessitating autonomous perception, planning, and decision-making under limited sensing capabilities on a compact field with frequent physical interactions.

Existing approaches in RoboCup humanoid soccer often exhibit limitations. Many adult-sized humanoids rely on servo-based actuation and Zero Moment Point (ZMP)-style locomotion, which provide static stability but struggle with dynamic movements, limiting mobility and kicking efficiency. Most robots employ semi-static or weak in-gait kicks, constraining tactical flexibility. From a software perspective, competitive architectures frequently utilize simple behavior planners with limited role reasoning, lack mid-level navigation for global path planning, and have minimal collision avoidance. Additionally, vision, neck control, and localization are often treated as loosely coupled modules, hindering consistent performance in dynamic, adversarial settings.

Recent learning-based methods, such as deep reinforcement learning (RL) for reactive ball pursuit and shooting, have shown single-robot performance. However, these methods typically focus on isolated skills, without explicit modeling of teammates, opponents, or obstacles, requiring integration into hand-engineered stacks for coordination and strategy.

This work addresses these identified gaps by developing ARTEMIS, an integrated adult-sized humanoid soccer platform. It combines novel hardware features, such as lightweight structural components and high-torque QDD actuators for powerful in-gait kicks, with a comprehensive software stack. The software innovations include an integrated perception and localization framework, a mid-level navigation system for collision-aware trajectories, and a centralized behavior manager. The objective is to achieve a tightly integrated system that overcomes the limitations of previous modular or specialized learning-based approaches, thereby enabling robust, agile, and tactically effective performance in the demanding context of RoboCup.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop and demonstrate a high-performance humanoid robot system capable of winning the RoboCup 2024 Adult-Sized Humanoid Soccer Competition. This objective aligns with RoboCup's long-term goal of fostering the development of autonomous humanoid robots that can compete against human soccer players by 2050.

The motivation behind the specific hardware and software innovations can be detailed as follows:

**Hardware Motivation:**
- **Dynamic Performance and Kicking Capability:** Existing adult-sized humanoids often use servo-based actuation and ZMP-style locomotion, which restrict dynamic movements and powerful kicks. A key motivation was to develop a hardware platform that could execute fast, dynamic motions and powerful in-gait kicks without requiring the robot to stop and reconfigure its stance, thereby improving mobility and scoring efficiency. This led to the focus on lightweight structural components, high-torque quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuators, and specialized foot designs.
- **Locomotion Robustness:** While enabling powerful kicks, the hardware design also needed to maintain locomotion robustness. The specialized foot design aimed to balance effective ball interaction with stability during movement and impact mitigation in competitive scenarios.
- **Impact Mitigation and Force Control:** The robot operates in a dynamic environment with frequent physical interactions. Proprioceptive actuators with low reflected inertia and high transmission transparency were sought to enable robust and dynamic locomotion, impact mitigation, and precise force control.

**Software Motivation:**
- **Robust Perception and Localization:** Competitive soccer environments present challenges such as noisy, delayed, and frequently disrupted visual perception due to motion, occlusions, and lighting variations. The motivation was to develop an integrated perception and localization framework that provides reliable and accurate estimates of the ball, goals, teammates, and opponents under these conditions, using only onboard sensors. Proximity sensing was motivated as a complementary modality for redundancy and collision avoidance.
- **Collision-Aware and Dynamically Feasible Navigation:** Many existing RoboCup architectures lack a mid-level navigation layer for online global path planning and exhibit minimal collision avoidance. The objective was to generate collision-aware, dynamically feasible trajectories in real-time, accounting for moving obstacles and field geometry, to prevent interference and congestion.
- **Sophisticated High-Level Decision-Making:** Simple behavior planners with limited role reasoning are common in current systems. This work aimed to develop a centralized behavior manager capable of coordinating high-level decision-making, role selection, and kick execution based on the evolving game state, incorporating desired-state planning, memory handling, and motion prediction.
- **Tight Integration:** A overarching motivation was to achieve tight integration across perception, localization, planning, and control modules. Previous systems often suffered from weak integration, leading to inconsistent behavior. The goal was to ensure seamless information flow and rapid closed-loop responses, allowing for smooth transitions between behaviors and effective performance in dynamic and adversarial match conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ARTEMIS system utilizes a hierarchical, model-based control framework that integrates hardware and software components for high-performance humanoid soccer.

**System Architecture:**
The software framework is designed as a tightly integrated perception–planning–control stack, operating with a fast, shared-memory control loop and a set of ROS 2 nodes. The low-level control loop operates at 1 kHz, fusing proprioceptive data from actuators, IMU, and foot contact sensors into a state estimator, which feeds into the locomotion controller for whole-body control and in-gait kicking. Higher-level functions (perception, localization, mid-level navigation, high-level behavior planning) run as ROS 2 nodes, communicating through shared memory interfaces.

**Hardware Platform (ARTEMIS):**
The ARTEMIS humanoid platform features 5 degrees of freedom (DoF) per leg, 4 DoF per arm, and 2 DoF in the neck.
- **Leg Design:** The 5-DoF leg design omits the ankle roll joint to reduce distal leg mass, enhancing acceleration for rapid walking and disturbance recovery. Hip roll and yaw axes are tilted forward by 45° to equalize torque requirements and increase hip pitch range. Actuators are placed near the torso using parallel four-bar linkages, concentrating over 60% of leg mass near the hip joint for improved acceleration and stability. Topology-optimized femur and tibia components integrate actuator housings into the load-bearing structure for high strength-to-weight ratios.
- **Actuators:** The robot employs proprioceptive actuators with low reflected inertia and high transmission transparency for impact mitigation and precise force control.
- **Sensors and Computation:** A ZED 2 stereo camera provides environmental perception, and a MicroStrain 3DM-CV7 IMU is located in the lower torso for balance estimation. An HP Elite Mini 800 G9 computer, equipped with an Intel Core i7 processor and an NVIDIA RTX 3050 Ti GPU, performs computational tasks. Power is supplied by four LiPo batteries with integrated Battery Management System (BMS) modules.
- **Foot Design:** A modular and anisotropic foot attachment was developed to enhance kicking accuracy and success without compromising locomotion stability. Three designs (Bolt, Magnet, Buckle) were evaluated. The buckle-based design was selected for its balance of structural strength, connection reliability, replacement time, and cost. It features a Snap Cover, a Front Bill for forward kicks, and a Side Bill for side kicks and to prevent ball entrapment. The Front Bill has an asymmetric, flat-plane geometry to maximize contact area and ensure efficient force transfer. Structural modifications, including curved continuous supports and a rounded inner edge, were implemented for uniform load distribution and increased stiffness. A dual-hinge front connection and buckle–standoff scheme enable a quick-release mechanism, allowing foot attachment replacement in approximately 10 seconds.

**Software Components:**
- **Vision:** The vision system integrates a ZED stereo camera for synchronized RGB and depth data (sl.DEPTH_MODE.ULTRA, 60 FPS). A custom YOLOv8m deep learning model, optimized with NVIDIA TensorRT, is used for real-time object detection (soccer balls, red/blue robots, goalposts, L/T/X intersections). Detections are validated using green coverage, dimension, and height constraints for the ball. 2D detections are converted to 3D world coordinates using depth information; a geometric depth estimation fallback is used if depth data is unreliable. A Kalman filter tracks object positions and velocities in a six-dimensional state space, smoothing noisy detections and predicting future positions.
- **Proximity:** This module provides real-time, close-range obstacle detection from the stereo camera's depth data, serving as a fallback when vision-based detection fails. It processes the depth map by downsampling, handling near-field invalid depth values (NaN/Inf) through density checks and interpolation, and applying geometric field-of-view filtering to remove ground plane and distant background. Connected Component Analysis (CCA) segments remaining regions into discrete objects, from which bounding boxes and features are extracted. Overlapping boxes are merged, and the two closest obstacles are published to navigation and behavior modules.
- **Localization (CLAP):** The system employs CLAP (Clustering to Localize Across N Possibilities), a lightweight geometric strategy for 2D pose estimation (x, y, θ). It uses observed field features and a known field map to generate multiple pose hypotheses by calculating the robot's orientation and position from landmark pairs. This addresses ambiguities arising from field symmetry and measurement noise. A fast local clustering method exploits temporal consistency, while a slower global clustering aggregates estimates over multiple frames, supported by IMU predictions, for verification and recovery, using iterative K-means with outlier removal. The system runs at 100 Hz, with YOLOv8m limited to seven detected landmarks per frame to mitigate latency spikes.
- **Navigation:** A two-stage navigation stack combines a Dynamic Augmented Visibility Graphs (DAVG) planner with a Collision-Free Model Predictive Controller (cf-MPC) tracker.
 - **DAVG Planner:** Models obstacles as regular polygons and builds a visibility graph within an active region around the start-goal line. It augments the graph with a turn cost to penalize sharp heading changes, generating a C0-interpolated reference trajectory that biases solutions away from costly maneuvers for humanoids.
 - **cf-MPC Tracker:** Models the robot as a 3-DoF planar system. It solves a nonlinear MPC problem over a prediction horizon to minimize tracking error, control effort, and soft circular keep-out constraint violations for obstacles. Slack variables preserve feasibility during sudden obstacle intrusion or estimator noise. A linearized quadratic-program (QP) variant achieves higher control rates.
- **Behavioral Planner:** A hybrid behavior tree with Finite State Machine (FSM) logic manages high-level decisions, separated into Body and Neck Behaviors (Decisions), Calculations, and Communications (ROS2 interface).
 - **Calculations:** Determines optimal robot poses, generates kick commands (predicting robot position for timing, distance-based power model). A pass-finding algorithm selects open regions for passing by generating candidate circles from field boundaries and opponent positions. A cost function evaluates these candidates based on circle dimension, kick angle, distance to goals, self, teammates, opponents, and trajectory blockage. An Action Mode FSM translates decisions into discrete parameters and adjusts play strategy. A memory handler manages ball permanence, categorizing its state as Lost, In-View, Remembered, or Predicted, and handles occlusions.
 - **Body and Neck Behaviors:** These operate in parallel. The Body Tree interprets signals to select the robot's mode (Offense, Defense, Set Piece) and uses modular action blocks for tasks like ball searching, kicking, and navigation. The Neck Tree manages head orientation behaviors (tracking, prediction, search, re-localization) based on ball visibility and memory, providing frequent, short-horizon adjustments independently of body movements.
- **Locomotion (PLMK):** The locomotion system uses a gait generator, a contact-aided Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) for state estimation, and a footstep planner to select stabilizing footholds. A trajectory planner generates smooth swing motions.
 - **Perception-Locked Mid-Swing Kicking (PLMK):** This module enables autonomous kicks within a normal walking step without interrupting gait. The KickCommander node evaluates ball perception, gait phase timing, and re-arming conditions. The strike foot is chosen based on lateral ball position. At liftoff, the perceived ball position is projected into the kicking-foot frame and adjusted by geometric offset and kick power scaling to define a strike point. This strike point is passed as a mid-swing constraint to the swing-leg trajectory solver, forcing the sixth-order polynomial to intersect the ball at mid-swing, ensuring impact at maximum leg velocity while maintaining gait smoothness. This module integrates with the whole-body control pipeline and strategy planner for adaptive kick power.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The ARTEMIS system was evaluated through in-field subsystem verifications, material simulations, high-level strategy simulations, and full-stack autonomous soccer matches, culminating in its victory at the RoboCup 2024 Adult-Sized Humanoid Soccer Competition.

**In-Field Verification of Core Subsystems:**
- **Perception (Vision):** The custom YOLOv8m model demonstrated an overall precision of 0.690, recall of 0.673, mAP@50 of 0.648, and mAP@50:95 of 0.331 on a test set of 350 images. The model was tuned for high precision to avoid false positives. The soccer ball class showed strong individual performance with a precision of 0.858, recall of 0.900, mAP@50 of 0.946, and mAP@50:95 of 0.716.
- **Proximity:** Across three runs, the proximity system achieved an average precision of 0.94, perfect recall of 1.000, an F1 score of 0.97, and a low mean distance error of 0.019 ± 0.01 meters. This indicated reliable and accurate close-range obstacle detection.
- **Localization (CLAP):** During repeated five-pointed star trajectories, the CLAP localization method achieved a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.0357 m², a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.1651 m, and a standard deviation of 0.0916 m when compared against Vicon motion capture. This indicated stable, drift-resistant localization accuracy despite detection noise and field symmetry.
- **Navigation:**
 - **Trajectory Tracking:** Following a predefined star trajectory without obstacles, the robot achieved an MSE of 0.0141 m², MAE of 0.0945 m, and STD of 0.0722 m, demonstrating sub-decimeter average deviation and consistent cf-MPC tracking.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance:** In the presence of two virtual circular obstacles, the system consistently generated smooth, dynamically feasible paths that navigated around both obstacles across six independent trials, maintaining boundary clearance and exhibiting low trial-to-trial variation.
- **Kick (PLMK):** Over 50 autonomous shots towards the goal, 46 kicks (92%) remained within the preset ±15° kick cone, demonstrating directional consistency. 42 of 50 kicks (84%) resulted in successful goals. The PLMK module enabled the leg to reach a peak linear velocity of approximately 3.2 m/s during mid-swing kicks, which is higher than reported speeds for other humanoid kick methods (e.g., 0.8 m/s for static ZMP, 1.2 m/s for semi-swing).

**Foot Attachment Material Simulation:**
Finite Element Analysis (FEA) on nine different materials for the foot attachment demonstrated that PETG-CF offered a balance of mechanical performance and manufacturability. It withstood horizontal forces up to 1000 N without failure and fractured under vertical loads exceeding approximately 150 N, meeting design objectives for high strength in the horizontal direction and controlled flexibility vertically.

**High-level Strategy Simulation:**
A custom game simulator was developed to evaluate high-level strategies. Comparative analysis of passing algorithms indicated that strategies prioritizing "kicking away from opponents" and "kicking closest to goal" yielded better average performance and more favorable score distributions compared to always "shooting on goal." This provided quantitative insight for strategy optimization.

**Full-Stack Autonomous Soccer Performance:**
ARTEMIS successfully competed in and won the RoboCup 2024 Adult-Sized Humanoid Soccer Competition. The team scored a total of 45 goals over 6 official matches. Under competition rules requiring full autonomy and human-analog sensors, the system demonstrated:
- Stable locomotion across varying terrains.
- Robust perception and localization under different illumination conditions and field sizes.
- Effective obstacle avoidance in dynamic soccer environments.
- Reliable cooperative passing and goal-scoring strategies.
The system consistently exhibited high performance, resilience, and strategic effectiveness in real match environments, demonstrating the successful integration of its hardware and software components.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates the development and successful deployment of a highly integrated, model-based humanoid robot system, ARTEMIS, capable of high-performance athletic tasks in complex, adversarial environments. The system's victory at the RoboCup 2024 Adult-Sized Humanoid Soccer Competition serves as a validation of its design principles and robust functionality.

The significance and potential impact of this research can be outlined as follows:

- **Advancement in Humanoid Locomotion and Dexterity:** The hardware innovations, particularly the lightweight structural components, high-torque quasi-direct-drive (QDD) actuators, and the specialized, quick-release foot design, address the challenges of dynamic locomotion and powerful, in-gait kicking in adult-sized humanoids. The demonstrated ability to achieve high foot speeds during kicks without interrupting gait represents an advancement in the athletic capabilities of humanoid robots, moving beyond statically stable or semi-static maneuvers.
- **Integrated System Design for Complex Tasks:** The research highlights the effectiveness of a tightly coupled hardware and software architecture. Unlike systems with loosely integrated modules or those relying solely on learning-based approaches for isolated skills, ARTEMIS demonstrates that a holistic, model-based integration of perception, localization, planning, and control can achieve reliable, high-performance autonomy in real-world, dynamic scenarios. This approach contributes to a better understanding of how to build robust robotic systems for tasks requiring simultaneous execution of multiple complex behaviors.
- **Robust Perception and Navigation in Dynamic Environments:** The developed perception system, combining optimized deep learning for object detection with classical computer vision and Kalman filtering, provides accurate and validated detections. The CLAP localization method, with its clustering strategies, demonstrates stable and drift-resistant pose estimation in challenging environments characterized by symmetries, noise, and occlusions. The two-stage navigation stack (DAVG and cf-MPC) contributes to real-time, collision-aware, and dynamically feasible path planning and tracking, which are critical for safe and effective operation amidst moving obstacles.
- **Sophisticated Behavioral Intelligence:** The hybrid behavior tree with FSM, coupled with advanced calculation modules for optimal positioning, kick selection, and ball memory management, enables the robot to make tactical decisions and execute complex strategies autonomously. The simulation-based optimization of high-level strategies provides a methodology for developing more effective gameplay, contributing to the field of multi-robot coordination and strategic planning.
- **Benchmarking and Future Research Direction:** RoboCup serves as a demanding benchmark for integrated AI and robotics. ARTEMIS's success provides a high-performance baseline and an open-source platform for future research. The discussion acknowledges limitations, such as the deterministic nature of the current behavior layer and robustness under severe occlusions, pointing towards future work in more adaptive decision-making (e.g., combining model-based and learning-based approaches), enhanced multi-sensor fusion, predictive whole-body control, and advanced multi-robot coordination. This trajectory indicates a potential impact on moving humanoid robotics towards more general-purpose and resilient applications in unstructured human environments.

In summary, this work provides a comprehensive, validated system that pushes the boundaries of autonomous humanoid performance in a highly competitive domain. Its methodological rigor and demonstrated success contribute valuable insights and a tangible platform for advancing the capabilities and integration strategies in humanoid robotics.
