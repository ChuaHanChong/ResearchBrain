# Data Scaling Laws in Imitation Learning for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and associated AI labs uncovered approximate power-law data scaling relationships for robotic manipulation, showing that diversifying training environments and objects is more critical than increasing demonstration count for achieving zero-shot generalization of single-task policies. This work provides an efficient data collection strategy, enabling policies for new tasks to achieve around 90% success rates in novel, unseen conditions with moderate data.

## Problem

- Robotics lacked established scaling laws, unlike fields like NLP and CV, hindering the development of truly generalizable policies.
- Achieving zero-shot generalization for robotic policies in novel environments and with unseen objects remained a significant challenge.
- Existing large-scale robotic dataset collection efforts often lacked principled guidance on how to optimize for generalization, especially in diverse, real-world settings.

## Method

- Conducted a comprehensive empirical study on over 40,000 human demonstrations, collected across diverse real-world environments and objects using the ==Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI)==.
- Trained single-task robot policies using an improved Behavior Cloning approach (Diffusion Policy with fine-tuned DINOv2 visual encoder and temporal ensemble) and evaluated them rigorously on entirely unseen environments and objects.
- Systematically varied the number of training environments, objects, and demonstrations to analyze their individual and combined impact on policy generalization performance.

## Results

- Policy generalization, measured as the optimality gap, exhibited an approximate power-law relationship with the number of training objects, environments, or combined environment-object pairs.
- An efficient data collection strategy was derived: prioritize collecting data in as many diverse environments as possible, with one unique object per environment, and approximately 50 demonstrations per environment-object pair.
- This strategy was validated on two new tasks, achieving approximately **90%** success rates in novel environments with unseen objects, demonstrating its practical utility and efficiency in real-world scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generalization performance in robotic manipulation follows approximate power-law relationships with the diversity of training environments and objects.
> - Increasing the diversity of training environments and objects is significantly more effective for achieving zero-shot generalization than merely increasing the number of demonstrations per instance.
> - Zero-shot generalization for single-task policies to novel environments and unseen objects is achievable with a moderate data investment by prioritizing diversity in data collection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep learning, particularly natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV), has witnessed significant advancements through the identification and application of scaling laws. These laws demonstrate that model performance often improves predictably with increases in dataset size, model size, and computational resources, leading to the development of highly generalizable foundation models. However, comprehensive scaling laws have not been extensively established in robotics, preventing the field from fully leveraging similar benefits. Most contemporary robotic policies typically exhibit limited zero-shot generalization capabilities, often requiring fine-tuning for deployment in novel environments or with unseen objects.

Existing large-scale robotic datasets, such as Open X-Embodiment (OXE), primarily aim to facilitate positive transfer learning across different robot embodiments. While valuable, these efforts often still necessitate additional data collection for fine-tuning when deploying models in new environments. Other research focuses on in-domain compositional generalization or specific aspects of object/environment adaptation. This paper addresses a gap by specifically investigating data scaling laws in imitation learning for *single-task* robotic manipulation, with a direct focus on achieving *zero-shot generalization* to novel environments and unseen objects without any further fine-tuning. It builds upon previous efforts to develop generalizable robotic systems, such as UMI and RUMs, by providing a systematic empirical analysis of how different dimensions of data scaling (number of environments, objects, and demonstrations) quantitatively impact generalization performance. By focusing on real-world generalization across diverse settings, this work aims to guide the efficient creation of large-scale robotic datasets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether data scaling laws, analogous to those observed in natural language processing and computer vision, exist within the domain of robotic manipulation. This investigation is motivated by the potential for such laws to guide the development of robot policies with enhanced generalization capabilities, thereby moving beyond the limitations of policies that perform well only in controlled laboratory settings or require extensive fine-tuning for new scenarios.

Specifically, the authors aim to address the fundamental question: Can appropriate data scaling strategies produce robot policies capable of operating effectively on nearly any object within a specified category, in any novel environment, without prior exposure or additional training?

To pursue this overarching goal, the research identifies several detailed objectives:
- **Empirical Study of Data Scaling:** Conduct a comprehensive empirical study to systematically observe how varying dimensions of training data impact policy performance.
- **Characterize Generalization Performance:** Analyze how a single-task policy's generalization performance changes with:
 - The number of distinct training environments.
 - The number of distinct manipulation objects within the same category used during training.
 - The quantity of demonstrations provided per environment or object.
- **Define Generalization Dimensions:** Categorize generalization into "environment generalization" (handling variations in lighting, background, distractor objects) and "object generalization" (handling variations in color, size, geometry of objects within the same category). The study emphasizes real-world diversity over isolated factor variations.
- **Focus on Single-Task Policies:** While acknowledging the eventual need for task-level generalization, the current scope is limited to single-task policies, viewing robust single-task generalization as a foundational step for broader robotic capabilities.
- **Propose Efficient Data Collection Strategies:** Based on the observed data scaling laws and empirical insights, formulate practical guidelines for efficiently collecting data to achieve desired levels of policy generalization.
- **Validate Proposed Strategy:** Apply the derived data collection strategy to new, unseen tasks to verify its general applicability and effectiveness in achieving high generalization performance with moderate resources.
- **Preliminary Model Scaling Exploration:** Extend the investigation beyond data scaling to include preliminary explorations into the effects of model size and visual encoder training strategies on policy performance.

The motivation for this work stems from the current limitations in robotic manipulation, where policies often lack the zero-shot generalization capabilities seen in other AI domains. By understanding the principles of data scaling, the authors aim to provide actionable insights that can enable the creation of robust, deployable robotic skills that require minimal setup for new real-world scenarios, fostering progress towards more general-purpose robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research employs a comprehensive empirical study to investigate data scaling laws in robotic manipulation using imitation learning. The methodology involves structured data collection, a specific policy learning architecture with enhancements, and a rigorous evaluation protocol.

#### 3.1. Generalization Dimensions and Data Scaling Formulation

The study defines two primary dimensions of generalization:
- **Environment Generalization:** The policy's ability to perform in unseen environments, encompassing variations in lighting conditions, presence of distractor objects, and background changes.
- **Object Generalization:** The policy's ability to interact with novel objects belonging to the same category as those encountered during training, differing in attributes such as color, size, and geometry.
The approach focuses on real-world diversity by collecting data across various "in-the-wild" environments and a wide array of everyday objects, rather than isolating specific factors.

The data scaling laws are formulated by considering a dataset with `M` training environments, `N` manipulation objects of the same category, and `K` demonstrations collected for each object in each environment. The policy's performance is measured by test scores (`S`) on unseen environments and objects, with the aim of characterizing the relationship between `S` and `M`, `N`, and `K`.

#### 3.2. Data Source

Existing robotic manipulation datasets were deemed insufficient for the study's requirements regarding environmental and object diversity for single tasks. Therefore, the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI), a hand-held gripper, was utilized for data collection. UMI's portability, intuitive design, and cost-effectiveness enabled efficient collection of a substantial number of demonstrations (over 40,000). Data collection considerations included:
- **Randomized Initial Poses:** Varying the gripper's initial height and orientation, and the objects' initial positions, to ensure coverage of diverse starting conditions.
- **Visually Rich Environments:** Selecting environments with sufficient visual features to support UMI's SLAM-based camera pose tracking, often by adding distractor objects or textures.
- **Appropriate Object Sizes:** Avoiding objects that extensively obstruct camera view, which could lead to SLAM tracking failures.
- **Consistent Demonstrator Behavior:** Standardizing behavioral patterns and task completion times among data collectors to minimize multimodal behavior.
Approximately 90% of the collected demonstrations were deemed valid after SLAM filtering.

#### 3.3. Policy Learning

Diffusion Policy, a visuomotor policy learning method, was chosen due to its performance in real-world manipulation tasks. The policy utilizes a CNN-based U-Net as its noise prediction network and employs DDIM for real-time inference. Two key enhancements were implemented:
- **DINOv2 Visual Encoder:** A fine-tuned DINOv2 ViT-Large/14 model was used as the visual encoder. This choice was based on its superior performance compared to ImageNet pre-trained ResNet and CLIP ViT, attributed to DINOv2's ability to capture scene layout and object boundaries, enhancing spatial reasoning for robot control. A sufficiently large model capacity (ViT-L/14) was selected to prevent it from becoming a bottleneck during data scaling.
- **Temporal Ensemble:** The temporal ensemble strategy from ACT was adopted to mitigate jerky motions caused by discontinuities between executed action sequences. This involves predicting overlapping action sequences and averaging multiple predicted actions with an exponential weighting scheme to smooth transitions.
Training parameters included adjusting epochs based on dataset size for convergence, using BFloat16 precision, and increasing the observation horizon for challenging tasks like "Pour Water" and "Unplug Charger" to include more distant history steps.

#### 3.4. Tasks

The study focused on four manipulation tasks:
- **Primary Case Studies:**
 - "Pour Water": A three-step task requiring grasping a bottle, pouring water into a mug, and placing the bottle on a coaster. It demands precision and rotational movements.
 - "Mouse Arrangement": A two-step task involving picking up a mouse and placing it on a mouse pad, potentially requiring non-prehensile actions to orient the mouse. Challenges include low object thickness and varying geometry.
- **Validation Tasks:**
 - "Fold Towels": A two-step task involving grasping and folding a towel, challenging due to deformable object manipulation.
 - "Unplug Charger": A two-step task requiring grasping a charger plugged into a power strip and pulling it out. It tests force application and adaptability to varying charger shapes.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Protocol

A rigorous evaluation protocol was implemented to ensure reliability and minimize bias:
- **Unseen Test Conditions:** Policies were exclusively tested in environments or with objects not present in the training data.
- **Tester-Assigned Normalized Scores:** The primary metric was a normalized score (0-1), derived from human-assigned points for each step of a task (max 3 points per step). This provided more granularity than sparse success rates and was found to correlate better with real-world performance than validation set Mean Squared Error (MSE) in many cases.
- **Blind Testing:** Multiple policies trained on different data sizes were evaluated simultaneously. Each rollout was randomly selected, and initial conditions (object and robot arm positions) were kept identical for comparison within a batch. This minimized subjective bias from testers.
- **Extensive Rollouts:** Over 15,000 real-world robot rollouts were conducted across all experiments.

#### 3.6. Experimental Design for Data Scaling Laws

Experiments were designed to systematically vary `M`, `N`, and `K`:
- **Object Generalization Experiments:** Used a single environment with 32 distinct objects. Varied the number of training objects (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32) and the fraction of demonstrations per object (3.125% to 100%). Evaluated on 8 unseen objects.
- **Environment Generalization Experiments:** Used a single object across 32 distinct environments. Varied the number of training environments (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32) and the fraction of demonstrations per environment. Evaluated on 8 unseen environments.
- **Combined Generalization Experiments:** Used 32 unique environment-object pairs (each environment with one unique object). Varied the number of training pairs (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32) and the fraction of demonstrations per pair. Evaluated on 8 unseen environments, each with 2 unseen objects.
- **Power-Law Fitting:** The optimality gap (1 - Normalized Score) was log-transformed and fitted against the log-transformed number of objects, environments, or environment-object pairs to determine power-law relationships.

#### 3.7. Efficient Data Collection Strategy Exploration

Further experiments explored:
- **Multiple Objects per Environment:** Investigated if collecting multiple objects per environment improved performance more efficiently than unique object-environment pairs.
- **Demonstration Saturation:** Identified the threshold for the number of demonstrations beyond which additional data yields minimal benefit.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding data scaling laws, data collection strategies, and model-related factors in robotic manipulation.

#### 4.1. Data Scaling Laws and Generalization Performance

- **Object Generalization:** As the number of training objects increased, the policy's performance on unseen objects consistently improved. A notable observation was that with more training objects, fewer demonstrations were required per object to achieve comparable performance; the performance gap between policies trained with minimal vs. maximal demonstrations per object diminished significantly when trained on a larger diversity of objects. This generalization appeared relatively easier to achieve, with policies trained on 8 objects often exceeding 0.8 normalized score and 32 objects surpassing 0.9.
- **Environment Generalization:** Increasing the number of training environments enhanced the policy's generalization to unseen environments. While initial increases in demonstrations per environment boosted performance, this improvement quickly saturated, with marginal gains beyond a certain threshold (e.g., 50% vs. 100% demonstration usage often showed similar performance). Environment generalization was observed to be more challenging than object generalization for the tasks studied, characterized by a lower initial slope in the performance curve.
- **Generalization Across Both Environments and Objects:** Increasing the number of training environment-object pairs substantially improved the policy's generalization performance. In this combined setting, the benefit of additional demonstrations saturated even faster than in single-dimension generalization experiments. This indicates that simultaneous variation in both environments and objects contributes to data diversity, leading to more efficient policy learning and reduced dependence on the absolute number of demonstrations.
- **Power-Law Relationships:** The generalization ability of the policy, measured by the optimality gap (1 - Normalized Score), was found to follow an approximate power-law relationship with the number of training objects, training environments, or training environment-object pairs. This was supported by high Pearson's correlation coefficients (ranging from 0.942 to 0.991 in the normalized score analysis). This quantitative relationship provides a basis for predicting performance with larger datasets.
- **Demonstration Quantity:** When the number of environments and objects was fixed, a clear power-law relationship between the number of demonstrations and policy generalization performance was not consistently observed. Performance typically increased rapidly with initial demonstrations but then plateaued, indicating a saturation point beyond which additional demonstrations provided minimal benefits.

#### 4.2. Efficient Data Collection Strategy

Based on the observed scaling laws and qualitative analyses, an efficient data collection strategy was proposed:
- **Diversity is Key:** Increasing the diversity of environments and objects is more effective than merely increasing the absolute number of demonstrations for each individual environment or object.
- **Optimal Number of Environments and Objects:** Collecting data in as many diverse environments as possible, with only one unique manipulation object per environment, is recommended. When the total number of environment-object pairs reaches approximately 32, it was generally sufficient to train a policy capable of operating in novel environments and with previously unseen objects. The benefit of collecting multiple objects per environment diminished as the number of environments increased.
- **Demonstrations per Pair:** For tasks of similar difficulty, collecting around 50 demonstrations per environment-object pair was found to be a suitable threshold, as policy performance tended to saturate beyond this point.

#### 4.3. Verification of Data Collection Strategy

The proposed strategy (32 environment-object pairs, 50 demonstrations per pair) was applied to two new tasks: "Fold Towels" and "Unplug Charger." The policies trained with this strategy achieved approximately 90% success rates in novel environments with unseen objects for both new tasks, as well as for the original "Pour Water" and "Mouse Arrangement" tasks. This performance was achieved with a moderate data collection effort (one afternoon for two tasks with four data collectors), demonstrating the efficiency and general applicability of the strategy.

#### 4.4. Model Size and Training Strategy Exploration

- **Visual Encoder Training Strategy:** Both pre-training (with DINOv2) and full fine-tuning of the visual encoder were found to be indispensable. Learning-from-Scratch (LfS) or using frozen pre-trained features resulted in near-zero performance. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like LoRA did not match the performance of full fine-tuning.
- **Visual Encoder Scaling:** Scaling up the visual encoder size (from ViT-Small to ViT-Large) consistently improved the policy's generalization performance.
- **Action Diffusion Model Scaling:** Contrary to expectations, scaling the action diffusion U-Net (from small to large) did not lead to performance improvements; in some instances, performance slightly decreased. This suggests that the capacity of the smaller U-Net might already be adequate for modeling the action distribution or that alternative scalable architectures are required for this component.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by providing empirical evidence and quantitative insights into data scaling laws, a domain previously less explored compared to NLP and CV. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Guidance for Data-Driven Robotics:** The identification of approximate power-law relationships between generalization performance and the diversity of training environments and objects offers a principled framework for designing and understanding large-scale robotic datasets. This moves beyond anecdotal observations, providing quantitative guidance for data collection efforts.
- **Optimized Data Collection Strategies:** The proposed efficient data collection strategy, which prioritizes diversity in environments and objects over a high number of demonstrations per instance, has direct practical implications. It suggests that robotics researchers can achieve strong zero-shot generalization with a more targeted and less resource-intensive approach than simply amassing vast quantities of redundant data. This can significantly reduce the time and cost associated with building generalizable robotic skills.
- **Feasibility of Zero-Shot Generalization:** The study demonstrates that single-task robot policies capable of zero-shot deployment in novel environments and with unseen objects are achievable with a "relatively modest investment of time and resources." This finding addresses a long-standing aspiration in robotics, suggesting that the goal of robots operating in varied real-world scenarios without extensive fine-tuning is closer than previously assumed.
- **Foundation for Future Robotic Systems:** The ability to learn robust, generalizable primitive skills for individual tasks serves as a critical foundation for developing more advanced robotic capabilities. These primitive skills can be integrated into planning algorithms or form the building blocks for future research into multi-task generalist policies and broader robot intelligence.
- **Impact on Model Development:** Insights into the importance of visual encoder pre-training and scaling, contrasted with the saturation observed in action diffusion model scaling, offer guidance for architectural choices and training methodologies in visuomotor policy learning. This can inform the development of more effective and efficient robotic learning models.
- **Open Science and Community Enablement:** By releasing code, data, and models, the authors facilitate reproducibility and encourage further research. This fosters a collaborative environment for verifying these scaling laws across different tasks, hardware, and algorithms, accelerating progress towards truly general-purpose robots.
- **Direction for Future Research:** The identified limitations (focus on single-task, imitation learning only, UMI-specific data quality, limited task scope) explicitly outline avenues for future investigation, including multi-task generalization with language conditioning, data scaling in reinforcement learning, and exploring the impact of data quality and diverse policy learning algorithms.
