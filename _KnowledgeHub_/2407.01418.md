# RoboPack: Learning Tactile-Informed Dynamics Models for Dense Packing

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboPack learns a tactile-informed dynamics model for manipulation under heavy occlusion. A recurrent graph neural network state estimator fuses tracked 3D keypoint particles with Soft-Bubble tactile encodings, inferring per-object latent physics vectors; a separate GNN predictor rolls these forward for MPPI planning. Trained on roughly 30 minutes of real interaction per task, it beats vision-only, observation-as-state, and physics-simulator baselines on box pushing and dense packing.

## Problem

- Physics-based simulators assume full-state information and show large sim-to-real gaps, especially in unstructured multi-object scenes containing deformable objects with unknown physical properties.
- Prior graph-network dynamics learners rely on visual observations alone, so they cannot recover latent variables such as softness or mass distribution that occlusion hides.
- Naively placing tactile readings inside a model's state space forces prediction of intricate contact signals, which degrades prediction accuracy instead of helping.

## Method

- Perception extends optimization-based ==3D point tracking== with a deformation term and ==mask-consistency loss==, plus an ==autoencoder== embedding point-wise shear and global force into gripper particle attributes.
- A state estimator combining a ==GNN== with an ==LSTM== autoregressively infers particle positions and an unsupervised object-level ==latent physics vector== from tactile history and one initial visual frame.
- A tactile-free dynamics predictor, conditioned on frozen estimated physics vectors, is trained jointly under an ==MSE particle loss== enabled by tracked correspondences, then planned over with ==MPPI==.

## Results

- Long-horizon prediction MSE on box pushing is **1.48e-3** versus **1.75e-3** without tactile, **2.11e-3** for observation-as-state, and **2.65e-3** for the physics simulator.
- Real-world box pushing succeeds **16/20** versus **8/20** without tactile, **10/20** for the simulator, **6/20** for observation-as-state, using **5.3** pushes versus **7.3-8.4**.
- Dense packing succeeds **12/15** on seen and **10/15** on unseen objects versus **6/15** and **5/15**; a linear probe on latent physics rises from **0.26** to **0.93**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating state estimation from dynamics prediction beats treating touch as observed state: forcing a model to predict high-dimensional tactile futures degrades its visual predictions.
> - Latent physical properties emerge without supervision from interaction history alone, and most information arrives within the first roughly 20 steps, with longer histories giving marginal returns.
> - Correspondence-preserving keypoint tracking permits a clean MSE objective over noisy EMD or chamfer losses, and small prediction gains can drive large real-world planning gaps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is continuously striving to enable robots to perform complex tasks in unstructured, human-centric environments. "RoboPack" positions itself at the intersection of several critical research areas: learning dynamics models, multimodal sensing, and model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL), specifically addressing challenges related to partial observability and object property inference.

Historically, robotic manipulation has relied heavily on either analytical physics models or extensive trial-and-error (model-free reinforcement learning). **Physics-based simulators** offer interpretability and generalizability but suffer from significant "sim-to-real" gaps due to imperfect modeling of real-world phenomena, especially with deformable objects, complex contact dynamics, and unknown material properties. **Model-free reinforcement learning (RL)**, while powerful, often demands vast amounts of real-world interaction data, which is impractical and unsafe for physical robots.

**Learning dynamics models directly from data** has emerged as a promising alternative, leveraging deep neural networks (particularly Graph Neural Networks, GNNs) to capture complex interactions without explicit physical equations. Prior works have demonstrated success in modeling cloth, ropes, and fluids using various representations like keypoints or latent vectors. However, many of these approaches predominantly rely on **visual observations**, which become severely limited in scenarios with heavy **occlusion**—a common occurrence in tasks like dense packing. Visual input alone cannot infer crucial latent physical properties, such as an object's softness or mass distribution, making it difficult for robots to adapt to unknown objects.

This is where **multimodal sensing**, specifically incorporating **tactile feedback**, becomes vital. Human dexterity relies profoundly on touch for close-contact manipulation, assessing object properties, and navigating tight spaces. Integrating tactile sensing into robotic systems allows for richer perception, especially for inferring unobserved properties or making precise adjustments during contact. However, effectively incorporating high-dimensional and noisy tactile data into dynamics models, and using it to infer latent physical parameters, remains a significant challenge, often leading to models that struggle to predict intricate contact dynamics accurately (as shown by the RoboCook + tactile baseline).

"RoboPack" makes a distinct contribution by proposing a framework that specifically learns a **tactile-informed dynamics model** to overcome these limitations. Unlike previous MBRL approaches that might treat raw multi-modal observations as the state, RoboPack explicitly recognizes the **partially observable nature** of these tasks. It introduces a novel state estimation module that processes historical visuo-tactile information to infer *latent physics vectors* for objects. This separation of state estimation (inferring hidden properties from touch history) and dynamics prediction (predicting future states given inferred properties) is a key architectural innovation. By learning these latent properties, RoboPack avoids the pitfalls of directly predicting complex tactile signals, making the learning process more robust and data-efficient.

Furthermore, RoboPack distinguishes itself by focusing on **multi-object manipulation** with unknown physical properties and **indirect interactions** (e.g., pushing one object to move another, or inferring properties of objects *not* directly held). This expands beyond prior work that might estimate properties of only the handheld object. The tasks chosen—non-prehensile box pushing and dense packing—are archetypal examples where partial observability and unknown object properties are paramount.

In essence, RoboPack pushes the boundaries of robotic manipulation by demonstrating a data-efficient, learning-based approach that intelligently fuses visual and tactile information to understand and predict complex physical interactions in dynamic, partially observable multi-object scenes. This work paves the way for robots that are more adaptive, robust, and capable of performing tasks demanding human-like dexterity and physical intuition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the RoboPack research is to enable robots to perform dexterous manipulation tasks, such as dense packing, that are currently very challenging for autonomous systems but natural for humans. The authors are driven by the need to develop robots that can adapt to environments characterized by heavy occlusions and objects with unknown or variable physical properties, mirroring how humans rely on both sight and touch.

The core motivations and specific objectives can be broken down as follows:

- **Addressing Limitations of Vision-Only Systems:** In complex manipulation tasks like packing a suitcase, visual information alone is insufficient. Objects can be heavily occluded, and their internal physical properties (e.g., how soft an item is, where its mass is distributed) cannot be visually ascertained. Robots need richer sensory feedback to understand and interact with such scenes effectively.
- **Overcoming "Sim-to-Real" Gaps in Physics Simulators:** Traditional physics-based dynamics models, while providing explicit physical understanding, often struggle with accurately simulating real-world complexities, especially for deformable objects or intricate contact dynamics. They frequently assume full-state information and suffer from significant discrepancies when transferred to real robots. The motivation is to move towards models learned directly from real-world data to circumvent these gaps.
- **Effective Integration of Tactile Sensing:** Tactile feedback is recognized as critical for close-contact manipulation, allowing robots to infer object properties, detect fine contacts, and navigate tight spaces. However, the optimal method for incorporating this high-dimensional and intricate data into dynamic models is unclear. Naively adding tactile signals to a model's state space can be counterproductive due to the challenge of accurately modeling complex contacts. A key objective is therefore to develop an *effective* and *robust* way to leverage tactile information.
- **Inferring Latent Physics Information:** Robots need to understand not just the current state of objects but also their underlying physical characteristics (e.g., stiffness, mass) to predict how they will react to forces. Since these properties are often unobservable or unknown, a primary objective is to develop a method that can *infer* these latent physics properties from robot interactions, particularly through tactile feedback.
- **Data-Efficient Learning:** Training complex deep learning models for robotics typically requires vast amounts of data. A practical objective is to achieve robust dynamics learning and effective control with relatively small datasets of real-world interaction data, making the approach feasible for real-world deployment.
- **Enabling Model-Predictive Control in Partially Observable Environments:** The ultimate goal is to use the learned dynamics model for planning and control. This requires a system that can continuously update its understanding of the world (including latent properties) and make accurate predictions to generate effective action sequences, even when visual feedback is intermittent or incomplete.

In summary, RoboPack's key objectives are to:
1. **Learn dynamics directly from real-world physical interaction data** using powerful deep function approximators.
2. **Integrate vision and compliant tactile sensing** (Soft-Bubble sensor) for richer perception.
3. **Develop a learning-based method for effective estimation of latent physics information** from tactile feedback and interaction histories, rather than directly predicting raw tactile signals.
4. **Utilize this tactile-informed dynamics model for model-predictive control** to solve challenging real-world robotic tasks like non-prehensile manipulation and dense packing, demonstrating adaptivity and generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoboPack introduces a comprehensive framework designed to integrate visual and tactile sensing for learning dynamics models, enabling effective model-predictive control in complex manipulation tasks. The methodology is structured into four main components: Perception, State Estimation, Dynamics Prediction, and Model-Predictive Control.

**A. Perception Module:**
The system begins by transforming raw sensor data into a structured representation suitable for dynamics learning.
1. **Visual Perception:** This module extends `D3Fields` for multi-view RGB-D observations, focusing on tracking 3D keypoints for each object of interest. Crucially, these keypoints maintain correspondence over time, providing consistent object representations. It uses pre-trained `DINOv2` for visual features and `Grounded SAM` for segmentation masks. Keypoint tracking is formulated as an optimization problem minimizing losses related to distance to surface, semantic alignment, motion regularization, and mask consistency (especially important for multi-object scenes with occlusion and deformation). For deformable objects, it also tracks axis-aligned shearing scales.
2. **Tactile Perception:** The system uses `Soft-Bubble` tactile sensors, known for their compliance and high-resolution contact force perception via an embedded RGB-D camera. Each sensor provides global force-torque and local shear force vectors. An auto-encoder is trained to map these point-wise tactile signals into compact latent embeddings, effectively reducing dimensionality and extracting salient contact information.
3. **Integrating Visual and Tactile Perception:** Object particles from visual observation are combined with particles extracted from the `Soft-Bubble` gripper surface. The tactile embeddings (from the auto-encoder) and latent physics vectors (learned during state estimation) are appended as extra attributes to these unified particle representations.

**B. State Estimation and Latent Physics Vector Inference:**
This is a core innovation of RoboPack, designed to infer hidden object properties from interaction history in partially observable environments.
1. **State Definition:** The robot's state `s` is defined as a tuple containing object particles (`o_vis`) and object-level latent physics vectors (`ξ`). `o_vis` captures geometry, while `ξ` encapsulates physical properties.
2. **Recurrent Graph Neural Network (GNN) and LSTM:** The state estimator `g` uses a GNN to process the particle graph and an LSTM to integrate historical information.
3. **Process:** At each time step `t` (from 0 up to a history length `T`), the estimator takes the estimated previous state `s_{t-1}` and current tactile feedback `o_tact_{t-1:t}`. A graph is constructed where nodes are object particles and edges connect neighboring particles. Node attributes include tactile readings and the latent physics vector of the object the particle belongs to.
4. **Prediction and Update:** The GNN propagates features through the graph to predict updated particle positions (`o_vis_t`). Concurrently, an LSTM processes aggregated node features for each object, using its internal cell state (`m_t`) to update the object's latent physics vector (`ξ_η,t`), where `η` is the object index. This means the model learns to refine its estimate of `ξ` based on ongoing tactile interactions. Crucially, the learning of `ξ` is *not explicitly supervised*; the model infers parameters that best improve its ability to predict future states.

**C. Dynamics Prediction:**
After the state estimator generates an informed state `s_T`, the dynamics predictor `f` forecasts future states to facilitate planning.
1. **Architecture:** It shares a similar GNN structure with the state estimator.
2. **Key Differences:** (i) It *does not* take tactile observations as input for future predictions, as future tactile readings are unknown during planning. (ii) It is conditioned on the *frozen* latent physics parameters (`ξ_T`) estimated by the state estimator, meaning these inferred properties guide future dynamics without further modification.
3. **Forward Prediction:** The dynamics predictor recursively infers future particle positions (`o_vis_{t+1}`) given the current state (`s_t`) and action (`a_t`). The estimated physics parameters (`ξ_t`) remain constant during this forward pass.
4. **Training:** Both the state estimator and dynamics predictor are trained jointly, end-to-end, on real-world interaction data. The loss function is Mean Squared Error (MSE) on predicted visual observations (particle positions), leveraging the point-to-point correspondences established by the tracking module.

**D. Model-Predictive Control (MPC):**
RoboPack employs Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) for planning.
1. **Planning Process:** Given a goal state, MPPI samples action sequences, uses the learned dynamics model to predict future states, and evaluates these predictions against a cost function. Actions are re-weighted based on their estimated costs, and distribution parameters are updated.
2. **Execution and Replanning:** The robot executes the best initial actions from the plan. It then continuously receives tactile feedback, uses the state estimator to update its understanding of object properties, and replans for subsequent actions, enabling adaptive control.

**Tasks and Data Collection:**
The approach is demonstrated on two challenging real-world tasks:
- **Non-Prehensile Box Pushing:** Manipulating a rigid box with varying, unknown mass distributions using an in-hand rod. Visual feedback is sparse (between pushes), but tactile feedback is continuous.
- **Dense Packing:** Placing an object into an already densely packed box, requiring inference of deformable regions. Visual feedback is minimal (initial frame only), but tactile sensing guides interactions.
Data collection for both tasks involves human teleoperation, generating diverse "random" interactions (not explicitly labeled demonstrations) for approximately 30 minutes per task, highlighting the data efficiency of the approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate RoboPack's superior performance in dynamics prediction and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, validating its core hypotheses regarding the importance of tactile-informed latent physics inference.

**A. Long-Horizon Dynamics Prediction (Table I, Figure 6):**
- **Superior Accuracy:** RoboPack significantly outperforms all baseline methods—RoboPack (no tactile), RoboCook + tactile, and a Physics-based simulator—across all metrics (MSE, Earth Mover's Distance (EMD), Chamfer Distance (CD)) for both Non-Prehensile Box Pushing and Dense Packing tasks. This indicates its ability to generate more accurate future state predictions.
- **Impact of Tactile Sensing:** Comparing RoboPack to RoboPack (no tactile) highlights the crucial role of tactile information. For the Box Pushing task, tactile feedback helps the model better estimate the box's mass distribution, leading to more accurate predictions of translation and rotation. For Dense Packing, while global metrics (EMD, CD) show subtle differences, the MSE loss indicates RoboPack's superior ability to capture fine details of object deformation, critical for such tasks.
- **Challenge of Direct Tactile Prediction:** The RoboCook + tactile baseline, which directly incorporates tactile observations into the state representation (as opposed to inferring latent physics), performs poorly, even worse than the vision-only ablation. This supports the authors' hypothesis that the intricate and high-dimensional nature of tactile contact makes direct prediction challenging, distracting the model from learning visual dynamics effectively.
- **Limitations of Physics Simulators:** The physics-based simulator baseline performs considerably worse than RoboPack, particularly for Box Pushing. This is attributed to limitations in system identification with sparse visual feedback and the simulator's inability to fully capture the complex, compliant interactions (e.g., between the Soft-Bubble gripper, in-hand tool, and box).

**B. Analysis of Learned Physics Parameters (Figure 7):**
- **Meaningful Latent Representation:** The study provides compelling evidence that the state estimator successfully learns meaningful latent physics parameters (`ξ`).
- **Improved Classification with History:** A linear classifier trained on the learned latent physics vectors for the Box Pushing task showed increasing accuracy in classifying box types (which have different mass distributions) as more interaction history (and thus tactile feedback) was processed. Accuracy rose significantly after the first push (~20 steps), indicating that the model effectively integrates tactile history to discern object properties.
- **Clustering in Latent Space:** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) visualization of the learned 16-dimensional latent vectors, reduced to 2D, clearly showed that these latents gradually separated into distinct clusters corresponding to different box types as interaction time steps increased. This visually confirms that the model is indeed encoding object-specific physical properties into `ξ`.

**C. Benchmarking Real-World Planning Performance (Table II, III, Figure 8, 9):**
- **Non-Prehensile Box Pushing:**
 - RoboPack consistently achieved lower final errors (point-wise MSE), required fewer pushes to reach the goal, and demonstrated higher success rates (16/20 trials) across all box configurations, including an unseen box with distinct dynamics.
 - It demonstrated more precise control and faster progress towards goals compared to all baselines (Figure 9), highlighting the practical advantage of its accurate dynamics model.
 - The performance gap between RoboPack and RoboPack (no tactile) underscored the benefits of tactile sensing for precise control with unknown object properties.
- **Dense Packing:**
 - RoboPack achieved significantly higher success rates (12/15 for seen objects, 10/15 for unseen objects) compared to RoboPack (no tactile) (6/15 and 5/15 respectively).
 - This demonstrates its effectiveness in identifying feasible insertion locations and deformable regions, allowing it to apply force appropriately without causing damage, even when generalizing to objects with diverse visual appearances, geometries, and deformability (cups, cloth, hats).
 - The model's ability to operate successfully in partially observable dense packing scenarios, where a physics-based simulator was impractical to set up, showcases its real-world applicability.
- **Data Efficiency:** All models were trained on an average of only 30 minutes of real-world interaction data per task, showcasing RoboPack's remarkable data efficiency for complex robotic tasks.

In conclusion, the findings robustly support RoboPack's efficacy. The tactile-informed dynamics model, by inferring latent physics parameters from interaction history, leads to superior prediction accuracy and, critically, enables more effective and adaptive real-world robotic planning in challenging, partially observable manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoboPack framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, offering a pathway for robots to perform complex tasks with greater dexterity, adaptability, and intelligence. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Robustness in Partially Observable and Unstructured Environments:** RoboPack directly addresses the critical challenge of operating in environments with heavy occlusion and unknown object properties. By intelligently integrating visual and tactile feedback to infer latent physical parameters, it enables robots to "understand" their surroundings beyond what visible cues alone provide. This makes robots more robust to variations in object properties and environmental uncertainty, a crucial step towards generalized robotic agents.

- **Effective Tactile Integration:** The paper provides a novel and empirically validated method for incorporating high-dimensional tactile data into dynamics models. Instead of attempting the notoriously difficult task of predicting raw tactile signals, RoboPack's approach of inferring *latent physics vectors* based on tactile history is a powerful abstraction. This method effectively distills useful physical information from complex contact interactions, making tactile sensing a much more practical and impactful modality for real-world robotics.

- **Overcoming Sim-to-Real Gaps:** By learning dynamics directly from real-world interaction data, RoboPack bypasses the inherent limitations and "sim-to-real" gaps associated with purely physics-based simulators. This data-driven approach allows the robot to adapt to the true complexities of real-world physics, including friction, compliance, and material properties that are often difficult to model precisely.

- **Data Efficiency for Real-World Deployment:** The ability to achieve high performance with a relatively small amount of real-world training data (an average of 30 minutes per task) is a major practical advantage. Collecting extensive datasets for robotics is time-consuming, expensive, and often dangerous. RoboPack's data-efficient learning makes it more feasible to train and deploy intelligent manipulation capabilities in diverse applications.

- **Enhanced Robotic Dexterity and Adaptability:** The demonstrated success in tasks like non-prehensile box pushing (manipulating objects with unknown mass distributions using an in-hand tool) and dense packing (fitting objects into tight spaces with deformable contents) showcases a significant leap in robotic dexterity. Robots can now adapt their actions based on inferred properties, leading to more human-like, nuanced manipulation strategies that avoid damage and achieve precise placement.

- **Generalizable Framework:** The core components of RoboPack—perception, state estimation, and dynamics prediction—are designed to be general and task-agnostic. While the cost function and planning module need to be adapted, the fundamental learning architecture can be transferred to a wide range of visual and tactile manipulation tasks. This modularity promises broad applicability and could accelerate research in various subfields of robotics.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** RoboPack opens several exciting avenues for future work. Integrating higher-fidelity particle models could enable even finer-grained deformation control. Exploring alternative trajectory optimization methods with the differentiable neural dynamics models could enhance planning efficiency and optimality. Furthermore, combining learned models with stronger physics priors could lead to even better generalization and sample efficiency.

- **Potential for Industrial and Domestic Applications:** The capabilities demonstrated by RoboPack have direct implications for industries requiring automated handling of diverse and often unpredictable items, such as warehousing, logistics, manufacturing, and food processing. In the domestic sphere, such advancements could lead to more capable service robots that can assist with household chores like organizing and packing.

In conclusion, RoboPack pushes the frontier of robot learning by providing a robust, data-efficient, and generalizable framework for tactile-informed dynamic modeling. Its ability to infer and leverage latent physical properties from multimodal sensory history will pave the way for a new generation of robots capable of seamless and intelligent interaction with the complex physical world.
