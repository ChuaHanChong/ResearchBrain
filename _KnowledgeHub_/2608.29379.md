# Bridging Semantics and Physics with Constrained LLMs for Safe and Trustworthy Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A three-layer framework for language-guided manipulation that makes the reasoning-execution boundary a typed contract. An LLM emits only schema-validated MCP tool calls over semantic object references, each bound to a MoveIt Task Constructor template and replayed against a perception-grounded collision scene in a verify-then-act gate. On a real UFactory 850, it reaches 72.5% mean pouring success and 90% on grasp-and-place.

## Problem

- A language-action gap persists: LLM plans can be linguistically valid yet kinematically infeasible or collision-unsafe once grounded in a cluttered physical workspace.
- Learned world models used for pre-execution outcome prediction hallucinate under partial observability and yield physically inconsistent forecasts without strong physics priors or explicit 3D structure.
- Prior pouring work targets perception or low-level control after the transfer goal is fixed, leaving verified semantic grounding, action sequencing, and recovery unaddressed.

## Method

- **Semantic Layer** resolves instructions into a symbolic task-state tuple (source id, target id, pour flag) with a dual-track policy: a constrained ==ReAct== query-only clarification loop or a deterministic ==FSM== task graph.
- **Protocol Layer** exposes robot skills as ==MCP== tools with JSON schemas, applying schema, range, and referential validation, then binding each atomic call to a composite ==MTC== stage pipeline.
- **Execution Layer** grounds ==YOLO== RGB-D detections into ==MoveIt== collision geometry via ==ChArUco== extrinsics, runs staged tilt-and-hold pouring under bounded constraints, and gates hardware behind ==verify-then-act== plus safe-pose reset and retry.

## Results

- Mean end-to-end success over four real-robot pouring tasks is **72.5%**, versus **50.0%** for a scripted FSM/BT policy and **47.5%** for the same LLM without canonical task-graph sequencing.
- The scripted baseline wins the easiest task (**90%** vs **80%**) but collapses to **10%** on the cluttered T4 scene, where the proposed method holds **60%**; multi-material T3 reaches **80%**.
- Ablations: removing retry costs **47.5** percentage points of mean success, removing safe-pose reset costs **32.5** points, and removing the task graph costs **25.0** points; grasp-and-place transfer hits **90%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Constraining an LLM to semantic references and bounded skill parameters, never poses or trajectories, lets one verification stack extend to new skills via a schema plus motion template.
> - Explicit geometric verification against a reconstructed scene is a viable alternative to learned dynamics prediction when auditability matters more than modeling material behavior.
> - Recovery mechanisms outweighed reasoning structure here: retry and safe-pose reset mattered more than task-graph sequencing, though ten trials per condition limits per-task confidence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic systems capable of interpreting and executing natural language instructions in complex, real-world environments is a significant area of research in robotics and artificial intelligence. Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated an ability to decompose high-level instructions into symbolic plans or structured action sequences, addressing the semantic understanding component of this challenge. However, a persistent "language-action gap" exists: linguistically plausible plans generated by LLMs may be physically infeasible or unsafe when subjected to the kinematic and collision constraints of the real world. This gap poses a barrier to the safe and trustworthy deployment of language-guided robots.

Previous approaches to address this gap include using learned world models to predict action outcomes. While capable, these models can exhibit limitations such as hallucination under partial observability or physically inconsistent predictions, particularly in safety-critical contexts, if they lack strong physics priors or explicit 3D structure.

This work contributes to the landscape by proposing a complementary approach. Instead of relying on learned dynamics models for outcome prediction, it emphasizes explicit verification against a reconstructed, collision-aware representation of the current scene. This positions the research within the domain of Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), which integrates symbolic task reasoning with geometric feasibility. Specifically, it builds upon frameworks like MoveIt Task Constructor (MTC), which provides tools for constructing and validating multi-stage manipulation motions.

Furthermore, the paper addresses the necessity of robust perception for grounding linguistic references in observed environments. It also contextualizes its contributions within the challenging domain of robotic pouring and material transfer, a task complicated by the difficult-to-model and partially observable dynamics of liquids and granular media. While existing work often focuses on perception or low-level control for pouring, this research addresses the higher-level problem of executing language-specified transfer tasks through a structured and verified reasoning-execution interface. By formalizing the planning-execution boundary as a typed contract, this research aims to enhance the reliability and safety of language-guided manipulation systems, distinguishing itself from end-to-end vision-language-action models by maintaining an explicit separation between probabilistic language reasoning and deterministic physical execution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to interpret ambiguous natural-language instructions and execute them safely and reliably within cluttered, contact-rich, and uncertain real-world environments, such as domestic kitchens.

The motivation for this work arises from several key challenges in current language-guided robotic manipulation:

1. **Bridging the Language-Action Gap:** While LLMs excel at decomposing natural language instructions into sequential actions, the resulting plans may be linguistically sound but physically infeasible due to unaddressed kinematic, dynamic, or collision constraints. This creates a disparity between the abstract linguistic representation and the concrete physical reality. The paper aims to bridge this gap by establishing a robust connection between semantic reasoning and physical execution.
2. **Ensuring Safety and Trustworthiness:** In safety-critical tasks like pouring or cooking, merely producing a linguistically correct plan is insufficient. The robot must also execute that plan without causing damage to itself, its environment, or its contents. The current limitations of learned world models, which can hallucinate or produce inconsistent physical predictions, underscore the need for an explicit and verifiable approach to safety. The research is motivated by the necessity of building trustworthy robot systems suitable for real-world deployment.
3. **Formalizing the Reasoning-Execution Boundary:** The authors identify the need for a clear, auditable interface between the probabilistic nature of language understanding and the deterministic requirements of robot control. They propose formalizing this boundary as a typed contract to prevent malformed or physically impossible commands from reaching the robot's motion planner.
4. **Improving Execution Reliability:** Beyond safety, the research aims to improve the overall reliability of task execution, particularly in scenarios involving perception uncertainty and unexpected environmental conditions. This includes developing mechanisms for pre-execution verification and run-time recovery from transient failures.
5. **Generalization across Tasks and Materials:** The goal is to develop an architecture that can generalize to different manipulation skills and material types without extensive re-tuning or task-specific planning logic, by focusing on a structured interface and verification process rather than highly specialized controllers.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines three core contributions:
- The development of a typed planning contract that provides a validated interface between language reasoning and robot execution, implemented through the Model Context Protocol (MCP) as a portable schema layer.
- The implementation of a "verify-then-act" grounding mechanism with explicit pre-execution checks, ensuring that all candidate motions are evaluated against a perception-grounded scene model for kinematic feasibility and collision avoidance before execution.
- The provision of empirical evidence for the effectiveness of structured interfaces and recovery mechanisms in improving task execution reliability through real-robot experiments across various pouring and manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates semantic reasoning with physical execution through a layered architecture designed to ensure safety and reliability. This system comprises three distinct layers: the Semantic Layer, the Protocol Layer, and the Execution Layer, which together implement a typed reasoning-execution interface.

#### 3.1. System Overview

The architecture enforces an explicit and typed boundary between the language module, which produces structured, schema-validated action specifications, and the control stack, which executes only those actions satisfying predefined feasibility and safety constraints.

#### 3.2. The Semantic Layer

This layer is responsible for interpreting unstructured natural-language instructions and translating them into a structured symbolic representation.
- **Language-Model Instantiation:** Claude Sonnet 4 serves as the LLM backbone for high-level reasoning. It receives natural-language instructions and a compact, perception-grounded representation of the scene, rather than raw visual data. Visual interpretation, pose estimation, collision checking, and trajectory generation are delegated to other modules.
- **Semantic Resolution and State Abstraction:** The semantic resolver maps user instructions (U) to a compact task-state tuple (S). For pouring, this tuple includes symbolic object identifiers for the source (`id_src`) and target (`id_tgt`) containers, grounded in the perception-injected planning scene, and a Boolean flag (`B_pour`) indicating material transfer. This abstraction focuses on task-relevant semantics, leaving geometric details to lower layers.
- **Dual-Track Policy:** The planner operates via two paths based on instruction specificity:
 - **Clarification Track:** For ambiguous or underspecified requests, a constrained ReAct-style agent engages in reasoning and information gathering, querying perception or the user for clarification. This track resolves referential ambiguity (e.g., multiple visible cups) without invoking actuation tools.
 - **Deterministic Execution Track:** For well-specified commands, the planner selects a pre-validated finite-state-machine template (e.g., pick, move, pour, place) that defines the canonical action sequence. The LLM determines the task intent but does not generate the execution sequence directly.

#### 3.3. The Protocol Layer

This layer acts as a typed interface, validating every action against a predefined schema and mapping accepted calls to motion-planning procedures. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is used to expose robot capabilities as schema-defined tools.
- **Schema-based Action Definition:** Each robot capability is defined as a tool using a rigorous JSON schema. Tool calls specify semantic object references and bounded skill-level parameters (e.g., `id_tgt`, `theta_tilt`, `tau_duration`). Robot poses, joint configurations, and trajectory generation are encapsulated within motion-planning templates.
- **Validation Stages:** The MCP server performs three stages of validation at runtime:
 1. **Schema Validation:** Checks required fields and data types.
 2. **Range Validation:** Ensures physical parameters are within predefined admissible bounds.
 3. **Referential Validation:** Verifies semantic references correspond to objects present in the scene.
 Calls failing any check are rejected, preventing invalid commands from propagating to the robot.
- **Atomic-to-Composite Binding:** Each atomic tool call from the Semantic Layer is associated with a predefined MoveIt Task Constructor (MTC) template. For example, a `pick` call expands into `approach`, `grasp`, `lift`, and `retreat` stages. A validated call instantiates the MTC template, which searches for a kinematically feasible, collision-free solution.
- **Auditability and Platform Agnosticism:** The Protocol Layer standardizes runtime behavior by logging invocations and normalizing failure responses (e.g., `PlanningFailed`). This allows the agent to handle errors programmatically (retry, query user) without parsing low-level exceptions. This interface also enables binding to different backends, supporting verification environments and physical robot drivers.

#### 3.4. The Execution Layer

This layer translates validated tool calls into safe, executable motions through scene grounding, constraint-based motion generation, and pre-execution verification.
- **Dynamic Scene Grounding:**
 - **Hardware:** UFactory 850 robot with a two-finger gripper and an Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D camera in an eye-to-hand configuration.
 - **Perception Pipeline:** RGB streams are processed by a YOLO-based detector. Detections are lifted into 3D using aligned depth, median-filtered within bounding boxes. These points are transformed into the robot base frame via calibrated extrinsics.
 - **Scene Representation:** Rather than using online-reconstructed meshes, detected objects instantiate collision geometries (cylindrical proxies) at unit scale within the MoveIt planning scene via the `setup_scene` tool. This provides an explicit geometric representation for collision checking and kinematic analysis.
- **Constraint-based Motion Generation:**
 - MTC factorizes skills into stages (generators, propagators, connectors) with explicit feasibility checks, allowing for localized problem-solving and failure attribution.
 - For material transfer, a conservative staged pouring profile extends the pipeline. This involves a sequence of tilt-and-hold stages, subject to predefined bounds on tilt angle, angular velocity, and dwell time. Each stage is checked for kinematic feasibility and collisions. The system does not model detailed material dynamics but verifies the executability of the commanded motion.
- **Verify-then-act Protocol:**
 - Before hardware actuation, every candidate plan (from approach to retreat) is replayed against the reconstructed planning scene.
 - The plan is verified for continuity, reachability, and collision-free execution with respect to the modeled geometry.
 - Only trajectories that pass these checks are sent to the UFactory 850 controller. If verification detects a collision, singularity, or discontinuity, execution is aborted, and a structured failure is returned.
 - Collision-checked "safe poses" are inserted between high-risk stages (e.g., after grasping, before pouring) to mitigate error accumulation and facilitate recovery. This ensures only validated motions reach the hardware, preventing physically infeasible commands.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed framework through a series of experiments on a physical UFactory 850 robot, focusing on task performance, generalization, multi-material robustness, and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Perception and Scene Grounding

Object pose grounding accuracy was assessed by comparing estimated object poses with manually verified references. The results showed that most estimates were sufficiently accurate for collision-aware planning and stable pouring. However, non-negligible tails in the distributions of both world-frame position error (Euclidean distance between centroids) and orientation error (angular deviation) were observed. Larger errors were particularly evident under conditions of clutter and partial occlusion. These findings motivated the protocol-level verification prior to execution, as perception inaccuracies could lead to model-world mismatches and potential failures if not explicitly checked.

#### 4.2. Task Performance

The system was evaluated on four end-to-end pouring tasks (T1-T4) of increasing complexity, each involving 10 independent physical trials. Task success was defined as the material reaching the target container and the source container being returned. The performance was compared against two baselines: a hand-crafted Finite State Machine/Behavior Tree (FSM/BT) scripted policy and an unstructured LLM baseline (LLM w/o task graph) that used the same tools and verification stack but lacked canonical action sequencing.

- **Overall Performance:** The proposed method achieved a mean end-to-end success rate of 72.5% across all tasks.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - The FSM/BT baseline achieved 50.0% mean success. It slightly outperformed the proposed method on the simplest task (T1: Single Pour, 90% vs. 80%), but its success rate significantly declined on harder tasks (e.g., T4: Complex Scene, 10% vs. 60%). This indicates its limitations in adapting to complex scenarios, distractors, or tighter geometric constraints.
 - The LLM w/o task graph baseline achieved 47.5% mean success, performing below the proposed method on every task. This suggests that flexible language reasoning alone is insufficient without structured action sequencing, leading to grounding and ordering errors.
- **Conclusion:** The results indicate that the structured approach, combining language reasoning with predefined action sequencing and verification, improves overall task execution reliability, especially in more constrained and complex scenes.

#### 4.3. Generalization Beyond Pouring

To assess the modularity and transferability of the framework, a fruit grasp-and-place task (moving an apple or orange into a bowl) was conducted. This task required only a new task-specific tool schema and a corresponding MTC template, while the core planner, protocol layer, and verify-then-act mechanisms remained unchanged. The system achieved 90% end-to-end success over 10 trials. The single failure was attributed to a pose-estimation error near the camera field-of-view boundary, leading to a collision during grasp approach. This experiment provided evidence that the framework can generalize to new manipulation behaviors by adapting only skill-specific components.

#### 4.4. Multi-Material Robustness

Task T3 specifically tested robustness to material variation by requiring the transfer of water, coffee beans (granular), and discrete solids (eggs). Despite the distinct flow characteristics and failure modes of these materials, the method achieved 80% success on T3, matching its performance on the simpler T1. This suggests that the performance is relatively insensitive to the material type, as pouring behavior is managed by parameterized tilt limits and execution-time verification rather than material-specific tuning. Failures observed were often associated with grasping stability rather than the pouring motion itself, indicating the abstraction's effectiveness across different material dynamics.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to determine the contribution of specific protocol-level recovery mechanisms:
- **Safe-Pose Reset:** Removing the safe-pose reset mechanism, which returns the robot arm to a predefined, collision-checked configuration between stages, reduced the average success rate by 32.5 percentage points (from 72.5% to 40.0%).
- **Retry Mechanism:** Disabling the retry mechanism, which allows replanning for transient failures (e.g., inverse-kinematics or planning failures), reduced the average success rate by 47.5 percentage points (from 72.5% to 25.0%).
- **Contribution of Task Graph:** Comparing the proposed method to the LLM w/o task graph baseline indicates that canonical sequencing contributes approximately 25.0 percentage points to the average success rate.

The results suggest that both structured sequencing and protocol-level recovery mechanisms contribute to end-to-end robustness. The retry mechanism showed the largest impact, indicating that transient failures are frequent on the platform and successful recovery significantly boosts overall reliability. Safe-pose resets also contributed by preventing error accumulation. Schema validation served as a front-end safeguard against malformed calls.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of language-guided robotics by presenting a framework that enhances the safety and trustworthiness of robotic manipulation systems operating in real-world environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Trustworthiness:** By formalizing the reasoning-execution boundary as a typed contract and implementing a "verify-then-act" protocol, the system establishes an explicit and auditable safety gate. This design prevents physically infeasible or malformed commands, originating from probabilistic language models, from being executed by the robot. This contributes to the development of more reliable and safer robotic systems, which is critical for deployment in human-centric environments like homes and workplaces.

2. **Robustness to the Language-Action Gap:** The work provides a structured methodology to address the persistent language-action gap. Instead of relying solely on learned dynamics models, which can be prone to hallucination, it leverages explicit 3D scene modeling and geometric verification. This approach grounds semantic instructions in physical reality, ensuring that high-level linguistic plans translate into kinematically feasible and collision-free robot motions.

3. **Modularity and Generalization:** The layered architecture, particularly the Protocol Layer implemented with the Model Context Protocol (MCP), promotes modularity. By abstracting robot capabilities into schema-validated tool calls, the system allows the LLM to reason at a semantic level without needing to generate low-level poses or trajectories. This modularity facilitates generalization, as demonstrated by the successful transfer to a grasp-and-place task with minimal changes (only tool schema and MTC template updates), indicating that the core framework is adaptable across different manipulation skills and material types.

4. **Improved Task Reliability through Recovery Mechanisms:** The ablation studies highlight the importance of protocol-level recovery mechanisms, such as safe-pose resets and retry strategies. These mechanisms significantly improve the system's resilience to transient failures and uncertainties inherent in real-world robotic operations, leading to higher end-to-end task success rates. This underscores that robust execution requires not only intelligent planning but also effective error handling and recovery.

5. **Foundation for Future Advanced Manipulation:** The framework establishes a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages the integration of richer sensory feedback (visual, tactile, audio) for closed-loop control and material transfer, which could further enhance robustness in dynamic and highly cluttered environments. By decoupling high-level semantic reasoning from low-level physical execution and ensuring explicit verification, this work paves the way for more sophisticated and capable language-guided robotic manipulation systems.

In conclusion, this research advances the state of the art in language-guided robotics by proposing a practical and verifiable framework that directly addresses critical issues of safety, reliability, and generalizability in real-world robotic manipulation.
