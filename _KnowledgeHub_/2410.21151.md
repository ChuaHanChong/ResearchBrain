# BraVE: Offline Reinforcement Learning for Discrete Combinatorial Action Spaces

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Virginia and MBZUAI developed BraVE (Branch Value Estimation), an offline reinforcement learning algorithm for discrete combinatorial action spaces. It addresses the scalability-expressivity tradeoff by structuring the action space as a tree and using a Q-guided neural network for linear-time action selection. BraVE consistently outperformed state-of-the-art baselines in high-dimensional environments with strong sub-action dependencies.

## Problem

- Offline reinforcement learning (RL) faces computational intractability when applied to high-dimensional, discrete combinatorial action spaces due to the exponential growth of possible joint actions.
- Accurately modeling complex interactions and dependencies among sub-actions from static, pre-collected datasets is challenging for existing offline RL methods.
- Prior approaches exhibit a scalability-expressivity tradeoff: factorized methods are scalable but sacrifice modeling of sub-action dependencies, while standard value-based methods are intractable in high dimensions.

## Method

- **BraVE** structures the ==combinatorial action space== as a tree, where each node represents a partial action, allowing a neural network to guide traversal and evaluate a linear number of actions.
- A Q-guided tree traversal mechanism uses predicted Q-values and branch values (estimated maximum Q-values within subtrees) to efficiently identify the greedy action.
- The training objective combines a behavior-regularized temporal difference (TD) loss with a novel branch value supervision loss that recursively propagates TD targets up the action tree to stabilize learning.

## Results

- BraVE consistently outperformed Factored Action Spaces (FAS) and Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) across increasing action dimensionalities (2-D to 11-D, over 4 million actions) in the Combinatorial Navigation Environment (CoNE).
- It maintained stable performance in environments with strong, non-factorizable sub-action dependencies (e.g., 50-**100%** pit density in 8-D CoNE), while baselines like FAS showed sharp declines.
- The method demonstrated robustness to the ordering of sub-action dimensions and achieved comparable or slightly superior performance to IQL during online fine-tuning after offline pretraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structuring the combinatorial action space as a tree enables efficient, linear-time action selection in exponentially large spaces while preserving the ability to model complex sub-action dependencies.
> - A branch value supervision loss, which recursively propagates TD targets, effectively stabilizes learning and provides reliable gradient updates to ancestor nodes in the action tree, even for rarely sampled regions.
> - Sparsifying the action space tree by pruning unobserved sub-action combinations from the offline dataset reduces computational overhead and prevents value overestimation due to extrapolation to unrealistic actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) aims to enable agents to learn optimal decision-making policies. Offline reinforcement learning, a subfield, focuses on learning these policies from pre-collected, static datasets, thereby circumventing the risks and expenses associated with online exploration in real-world environments. While existing offline RL methods have demonstrated efficacy in low-dimensional discrete action spaces and continuous control tasks, they encounter significant challenges when applied to high-dimensional, discrete combinatorial action spaces.

Combinatorial action spaces are characterized by actions that are formed as a Cartesian product of multiple sub-action dimensions. The number of possible joint actions in such spaces grows exponentially with the number of sub-action dimensions. This structure presents two primary difficulties for offline RL:
1. **Computational Intractability:** Evaluating and optimizing over an exponentially large discrete action space becomes computationally infeasible for standard value-based methods, as they typically require processing all possible actions.
2. **Modeling Sub-Action Dependencies:** Accurate decision-making in many real-world scenarios necessitates modeling how individual sub-actions interact and depend on each other. Fixed datasets further complicate the reliable inference of these dependencies.

Prior approaches to offline RL in these challenging settings exhibit a scalability-expressivity tradeoff:
- **Standard Value-Based Methods:** These methods can, in principle, capture sub-action dependencies by evaluating the complete action vector. However, their computational cost scales exponentially with the action space size, making them intractable in high dimensions.
- **Factorized Q-Function Approaches (e.g., FAS):** These methods reduce computational cost by factorizing the Q-function under an assumption of conditional independence among sub-actions. While this improves tractability, it sacrifices expressivity, as it precludes the modeling of crucial sub-action interactions. This limitation can lead to inaccurate estimations when dependencies are significant, such as in medical treatment recommendations where drug combinations can have synergistic or antagonistic effects.
- **General-Purpose Offline RL Algorithms (e.g., IQL):** While robust in various settings, these algorithms are not specifically designed to handle the combinatorial structure and high dimensionality of such action spaces, often struggling with their inherent complexity.

The proposed work, Branch Value Estimation (BraVE), positions itself within this landscape by seeking to overcome the aforementioned scalability-expressivity tradeoff. It aims to develop a method that can scale to high-dimensional combinatorial action spaces while preserving the ability to model complex sub-action dependencies, without resorting to exhaustive action space evaluation or restrictive independence assumptions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the prevalence of high-dimensional, discrete combinatorial action spaces in critical real-world decision-making domains, such as healthcare and logistics. In these fields, an agent or practitioner must often select a combination of multiple discrete sub-actions (e.g., thousands of combinations of procedures, medications, and tests in healthcare) at each decision point. Learning effective policies in these contexts from existing datasets via offline RL is highly desirable due to the safety and cost constraints of online experimentation.

The key objectives of this work are to:
1. **Address Computational Intractability:** Develop a mechanism that allows for efficient evaluation and optimization over exponentially large combinatorial action spaces without exhaustively enumerating all possible joint actions.
2. **Preserve Sub-Action Dependency Modeling:** Ensure that the learned value function can accurately capture complex interactions and dependencies among sub-actions, which are critical for making effective decisions in many real-world domains where the effect of one sub-action can be critically dependent on others.
3. **Avoid the Scalability–Expressivity Tradeoff:** Create an offline RL method that simultaneously achieves scalability to high dimensions and maintains the expressive power to model joint sub-action effects, a challenge that existing methods have not adequately resolved.
4. **Demonstrate Empirical Superiority:** Provide empirical evidence that the proposed method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art offline RL baselines in environments characterized by large combinatorial action spaces and significant sub-action dependencies.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to advance the applicability of offline RL to complex, high-stakes environments where current methods are either too computationally demanding or too limited in their ability to model intricate action dynamics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

BraVE is a value-based offline RL method that addresses the challenges of combinatorial action spaces by structuring the action space as a tree and employing a neural network to guide a targeted traversal, thereby evaluating a linear number of joint actions while preserving dependency structure.

#### 3.1. Tree Construction

BraVE imposes a tree structure on the combinatorial action space A, where A is a Cartesian product of N discrete sub-action spaces (A = A_1 × A_2 × · · · × A_N). Each node in this tree corresponds to a complete N-dimensional action vector. This vector is formed by explicitly assigning values to sub-actions along the path from the root and filling unassigned dimensions with predefined default values (e.g., 0 for a multi-binary space). A node at depth *k* represents an action where the first *k* sub-actions are explicitly set, and the remaining *N-k* sub-actions are at their default values. This dimension-by-dimension construction ensures that a leaf node, where all N sub-actions are specified, is reached in at most N levels, resulting in a number of node evaluations linear in N. The tree structure, including sub-action ordering and default values, is fixed during training to maintain consistent semantics.

#### 3.2. Tree Sparsification

To enhance learning stability and mitigate overestimation errors, BraVE incorporates an inductive bias by sparsifying the action space tree. This process prunes branches corresponding to sub-action combinations not observed in the offline dataset B. By restricting the tree to plausible actions, BraVE avoids evaluating unrealistic behaviors, reduces computational overhead, and prevents value inflation caused by extrapolating to unobserved or invalid action combinations. This is particularly relevant in real-world contexts where certain sub-actions are inherently incompatible.

#### 3.3. Q-Guided Tree Traversal

During policy evaluation, BraVE utilizes a neural network, `f(s, a_node; θ^-)`, to guide traversal of the action space tree and identify the greedy action `a_hat'` = arg max_a' Q(s', a'; θ^-). For any given state `s` and a node `a_node` in the tree, the network outputs a scalar Q-value `q = Q(s, a_node; θ^-)` for the complete action represented by that node, and a vector of branch values `v`. Each `v_j` in the vector estimates the maximum Q-value achievable within the subtree rooted at the *j*-th child of `a_node`. The network is a standard MLP; the tree structure is external and guides the argmax computation. Traversal begins at the root. At each node, if `q` (the Q-value of the current node's action) is greater than or equal to `max_j v_j` (the highest estimated value in any child's subtree), traversal terminates, and the current `a_node` is returned as `a_hat'`. Otherwise, the algorithm proceeds to the child node corresponding to `arg max_j v_j`. This process continues until a termination condition is met or a leaf node is reached. This mechanism ensures O(N) complexity for action selection, evaluating only a linear number of nodes instead of an exponential number.

#### 3.4. Loss Computation

BraVE's training objective is a combination of a behavior-regularized temporal difference (TD) loss (`L_TD`) and a branch value supervision loss (`L_BraVE`), weighted by a coefficient `α`: `L = α * L_TD + L_BraVE`.
- **Behavior-Regularized TD Loss (`L_TD`):** This loss penalizes Q-value estimates for actions that are unlikely under the dataset's behavior policy, similar to TD3+BC. For a sampled transition `(s, a, r, s', a')`, the TD target `Y` is computed as `λ(r + γQ(s', a_hat'; θ^-)) - ||a_hat' - a'||`, where `a_hat'` is the action selected via Q-guided tree traversal, and the `L2` norm term `||a_hat' - a'||` regularizes against deviations from the observed behavior action `a'`. The loss then minimizes `(Y - Q(s, a; θ))^2`.
- **Branch Value Supervision Loss (`L_BraVE`):** This loss propagates the TD target `Y` recursively up the tree from the sampled action `a`. At each step, `Y` is used to update the corresponding branch value in the parent node's Q-network output. The propagated target is updated at each level to be the maximum of the current node's Q-value and its children's branch values (`Y = max(q, v)`), ensuring consistency with the max-based traversal logic. This recursive propagation stabilizes learning by providing gradient updates to ancestor nodes, thereby improving the reliability of branch value estimates throughout the tree, even in rarely sampled regions.

#### 3.5. Enhancements for Stability and Policy Quality

Two mechanisms are employed to further enhance BraVE's performance:
- **Depth Penalty (δ):** A depth-based weighting factor `δ` is applied during `L_BraVE` computation. This factor amplifies the influence of errors closer to the root of the tree, recognizing that mistakes at higher levels can propagate widely.
- **Beam Search:** During inference, beam search is used instead of a purely greedy traversal. This allows the algorithm to retain the top-W candidate actions (ranked by predicted values) at each tree level, exploring a broader set of high-value sub-action combinations and improving the robustness of the final action selection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

BraVE's performance was evaluated in the Combinatorial Navigation Environment (CoNE), a high-dimensional discrete control domain designed to assess policy learning under large action spaces and significant sub-action dependencies. Actions in CoNE are combinations of atomic motion primitives, leading to action spaces of size |A| = 2^(2D) and state spaces |S| = M^D. The agent navigates a grid towards a goal, receiving rewards based on distance, goal achievement (+10), or falling into pits (-10). Datasets were generated using a stochastic A* policy. BraVE was compared against Factored Action Spaces (FAS), implemented as a factored BCQ, and Implicit Q-Learning (IQL).

#### 4.1. Performance Across Reward Structures and Sub-Action Dependencies

The evaluation involved two main settings:
- **Non-Factorizable Reward Structures Only:** In environments without pits, where transition dynamics were factorizable but rewards were not.
 - **Results:** BraVE consistently outperformed FAS and IQL as action dimensionality increased from 2-D (|A|=16) to 11-D (|A|>4 million). BraVE maintained high returns and stable policies, while FAS performance degraded due to the modeling bias introduced by linear Q-function decomposition, and IQL performed the worst across all settings.
- **Non-Factorizable Rewards and Sub-Action Dependencies:** In an 8-D environment (|A|=65,536), the density of pits (inducing hazardous transitions and sub-action dependencies) was varied from 5% to 100% of interior states.
 - **Results:** BraVE exhibited stable performance across all pit densities. FAS showed a sharp decline in performance once pits covered at least 50% of the interior states, indicating its struggle with strong sub-action dependencies. IQL's performance also declined steadily with increasing dependency strength. Normalized episode-length scores confirmed BraVE's near-optimal behavior (scores above 70), while FAS collapsed to random policy levels (scores approaching 0) in highly dependent environments.

#### 4.2. Sparse but Critical Sub-Action Dependencies

This setting focused on environments with only five pits (sparse dependencies) while scaling the state space.
- **Results:** BraVE performed reliably even in small state spaces where each action carried substantial risk. FAS required significantly larger state spaces (over 15,000 joint actions) before its performance approached BraVE's, demonstrating that even a few critical dependencies can invalidate naive factorizations. IQL performed worse than BraVE in all configurations.

#### 4.3. Robustness to Action Order

To assess sensitivity to the fixed sub-action ordering in BraVE's tree structure, experiments were conducted with five random permutations of sub-action dimensions in the challenging 8-D, 50% pit CoNE setting.
- **Results:** BraVE's performance remained consistent and superior to both FAS and IQL, demonstrating its robustness to the specific ordering of sub-action dimensions.

#### 4.4. Online Fine-Tuning

BraVE's capacity for online adaptation after offline pretraining was evaluated by comparing it to IQL in the 8-D, 50% pit CoNE environment. Both methods were trained offline for 1,500 gradient steps, then fine-tuned online for an additional 5,000 environment steps.
- **Results:** BraVE achieved comparable final performance to IQL during online fine-tuning, slightly outperforming it. This indicates that BraVE's data-driven tree sparsification does not hinder its ability to adapt online.

#### 4.5. Ablations and Hyperparameters

Studies quantified the contribution of BraVE's design choices:
- **Depth Penalty (δ):** A small depth penalty (δ=1) yielded the best results, emphasizing the importance of accuracy in higher levels of the tree.
- **Loss Weight (α):** Performance was generally stable across a range of α values, but entirely omitting the TD term (α=0) led to significant performance degradation, especially in higher-dimensional tasks.
- **Tree Structure:** A baseline standard DQN, constrained to select actions observed in the dataset and trained with BraVE's behavior-regularized TD loss but without the tree-based action selection or branch value propagation, failed to learn a viable policy. This confirmed the critical role of BraVE's structured decomposition.
- **Beam Width:** A beam width of 10 was found to be sufficient for strong performance, with larger widths offering negligible gains at minimal runtime cost. Inference times remained low (under one second on a CPU) even for large beam widths.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

BraVE represents a methodological advancement in offline reinforcement learning for environments characterized by high-dimensional, discrete combinatorial action spaces. Its core contribution is the introduction of a tree-structured action space traversal guided by a neural network, which effectively addresses the long-standing scalability-expressivity tradeoff in this domain. By evaluating only a linear number of joint actions while explicitly preserving sub-action dependencies, BraVE provides a mechanism for learning effective policies that was previously computationally prohibitive or reliant on restrictive independence assumptions.

The empirical results demonstrate BraVE's consistent superiority over state-of-the-art baselines, including factorized Q-function approaches (FAS) and general-purpose offline RL algorithms (IQL), particularly as action space size and the strength of sub-action dependencies increase. BraVE's stable performance in environments with non-factorizable rewards and critical sub-action interactions highlights its practical utility in complex real-world scenarios. Its robustness to the ordering of sub-action dimensions and its ability for effective online fine-tuning further underscore its potential for real-world deployment.

The potential impact of BraVE extends to various domains where complex, combinatorial decisions must be made from historical data, and where online experimentation is costly or unsafe. Examples include:
- **Healthcare:** Optimizing treatment plans, medication combinations, or diagnostic procedures where interactions between interventions are crucial.
- **Logistics and Resource Allocation:** Managing complex systems with many interdependent components, such as supply chain optimization or energy grid management.
- **Robotics:** Learning control policies for robots with many discrete actuation options that must be coordinated.

While BraVE demonstrates strong performance, the authors acknowledge several limitations that suggest avenues for future research. These include exploring methods for learning or adaptively selecting the sub-action dimension sequence for tree construction, addressing potential scalability challenges when dealing with extremely high sub-action cardinality (which affects network output layer size), optimizing the computational cost of the recursive `L_BraVE` update during training, and validating its generalizability across a broader range of combinatorial tasks and real-world applications beyond the synthetic CoNE benchmark. Furthermore, adapting the BraVE framework for online learning to manage exploration-exploitation within its structured action space is an important future direction. Despite these limitations, BraVE provides a flexible and extensible foundation, with potential for broader applicability, such as in constrained RL where its tree structure could naturally facilitate the exclusion of inadmissible action combinations.
