# Mind the Gap: Promises and Pitfalls of Hierarchical Planning in LeWorldModel

> [!summary] Summary
> This study explores the integration of minimal temporal abstraction into a frozen latent world model, LeWorldModel (LeWM), for long-horizon goal-conditioned control tasks. It reveals that naive hierarchical planning can degrade performance due to unconstrained high-level search, but with planning-side mitigations, the approach achieves up to a 14.7% increase in success rate on challenging long-horizon tasks.

## Problem

- Flat latent world models like LeWM exhibit significant performance degradation in long-horizon planning due to compounding model errors and the computational difficulty of optimizing extended primitive action sequences.
- Temporal abstraction is a theoretical solution for long-horizon control, but its effective integration as a minimal extension to existing, frozen world models without extensive retraining remains challenging.
- Understanding the specific conditions under which hierarchy can benefit such models and identifying the failure modes when these conditions are not met is crucial.

## Method

- Introduced **Hi-LeWM**, a hierarchical extension built upon a frozen **LeWM** by adding trainable high-level modules: a ==Latent Macro-Action Encoder== and a ==High-Level Predictor==.
- Developed **Empirical-Macro CEM** to constrain the high-level search space by biasing ==macro-action== selection towards distributions supported by training data, mitigating ==planner-induced support mismatch==.
- Implemented **Staged Execution**, where the high-level planner generates an initial sequence of ==latent subgoals== for the episode, and the low-level planner executes towards the active target in stages.

## Results

- Naive Hi-LeWM underperformed the flat LeWM baseline, achieving only **38.7%** success at **d=50** and **15.3%** at **d=75**, compared to LeWM's **52.7%** and **18.0%** respectively on PushT.
- The Empirical-Macro CEM mitigation improved online replanning success at **d=75** to **32.7%**, surpassing the flat LeWM baseline by **+14.7%** on PushT.
- Staged execution combined with Empirical-Macro CEM achieved the highest success at **d=50** on PushT (**64.0%**), an **+11.3%** improvement over the flat LeWM baseline, demonstrating its effectiveness at intermediate horizons.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Naive hierarchical planning with unconstrained high-level CEM can lead to 'planner-induced support mismatch,' where macro-actions outside the training data distribution are selected, resulting in poor, unexecutable intermediate subgoals.
> - The low-level LeWM controller is capable of executing valid intermediate targets if provided with high-quality subgoals, indicating that the problem lies in the high-level subgoal generation.
> - Constraining the high-level macro-action search to empirically-supported sequences, as demonstrated by Empirical-Macro CEM, is crucial for making temporal abstraction beneficial for long-horizon planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of model-based reinforcement learning, specifically focusing on the challenges of long-horizon planning for embodied agents using latent world models. Latent world models, such as PlaNet, Dreamer, and LeWorldModel (LeWM), enable agents to plan in compact representation spaces, abstracting away from raw pixel data. While effective for short-horizon tasks, these "flat" planners typically struggle with long-horizon control due to compounding model errors and the increasing difficulty of optimizing extended sequences of primitive actions.

Temporal abstraction is a recognized approach to address the limitations of long-horizon planning. Prior research has explored hierarchical world models (e.g., THICK, DreamerV3) and hierarchical latent-planning methods (e.g., Zhang et al., 2026; Hafner et al., 2022) that operate across multiple temporal scales. These methods often involve modifying the base model architecture, training pipelines, or planning interfaces.

The present study contributes to this landscape by specifically investigating whether a *minimal*, lightweight temporal abstraction can be retrofitted onto an *existing and frozen* flat latent world model (LeWM). This approach aims to isolate the impact of hierarchy itself, without introducing complex architectural redesigns or extensive retraining of the base model. The work also aligns with observations from other studies (e.g., IMWM) suggesting that long-horizon planning failures can stem from search limitations, even with robust world models. This paper thus focuses on the specific conditions under which such a hierarchical addition can be beneficial and analyzes the failure modes that arise when these conditions are not met, particularly concerning the high-level search space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether temporal abstraction, specifically in the form of a waypoint-based latent-planning interface, can enhance the performance of LeWorldModel (LeWM) on long-horizon, goal-conditioned control tasks. The authors frame this as an investigation into adding hierarchy as a *minimal extension* to LeWM, without deviating from its core JEPA-style latent prediction and Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)-based planning framework.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several factors:
1. **Limitations of Flat Planning:** LeWM, like other flat latent world models, experiences significant performance degradation as the planning horizon increases. This is attributed to the compounding of model errors over imagined rollouts and the computational challenge of optimizing increasingly long sequences of primitive actions.
2. **Promise of Temporal Abstraction:** Temporal hierarchies are a natural and theoretically appealing solution for long-horizon problems. They enable planning at a coarser temporal scale using macro-actions, which can reduce the effective planning horizon and mitigate error accumulation.
3. **Specific Focus on LeWM:** LeWM's simplicity and compact latent dynamics are appealing, but its effectiveness on long-horizon tasks is limited. The research seeks to determine if this specific model can benefit from temporal abstraction under a highly constrained setup.
4. **Isolating Hierarchy's Impact:** A deliberate design choice is to keep the pretrained low-level LeWM components (encoder, low-level predictor, action encoder) fixed. Only new high-level modules are introduced and trained. This restrictive approach is intended to isolate the benefits and pitfalls attributable solely to the addition of a hierarchical layer, rather than confounding them with changes to the underlying world model.
5. **Understanding Failure Modes:** Initial explorations indicated that a naive hierarchical extension did not automatically improve performance and sometimes even degraded it. This observation motivated a detailed analysis to understand *why* the hierarchy failed and under what specific conditions it might become beneficial, particularly identifying issues related to subgoal quality and the high-level planning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **Hi-LeWM**, a hierarchical extension built upon the LeWorldModel (LeWM) framework.

#### 3.1. LeWorldModel (LeWM) Foundation

LeWM serves as the base, performing goal-conditioned control by planning in a learned latent space. An encoder ($f_\theta$) maps observations and goal images to latent representations ($z_t$, $z_g$). A frozen low-level action-conditioned predictor ($p_{lo}$) propagates the current latent state forward given an action sequence. CEM planning then selects the action sequence that minimizes the distance between the predicted terminal latent and the goal latent. LeWM operates as a flat planner, directly over primitive actions in a receding-horizon manner.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical LeWorldModel (Hi-LeWM) Design

Hi-LeWM augments the frozen LeWM components by adding two trainable high-level modules:
- **Latent Macro-Action Encoder ($g$):** Implemented as a transformer encoder, it maps a sequence of primitive actions (an action chunk) occurring between two consecutive waypoints into a fixed-dimensional latent macro-action ($\ell_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$). The main configuration uses $d_\ell = 32$.
- **High-Level Predictor ($p_{hi}$):** Modeled as a transformer with adaptive layer normalization, it predicts abstract latent transitions. Given a current latent state ($z_t$) and a macro-action ($\ell_t$), it predicts the next waypoint latent ($\hat{z}_{t+k}$), where $k$ is the temporal gap. The predictor operates in the same latent space as LeWM ($d_z = 192$).

#### 3.3. Training and Optimization

- **Frozen Low-Level:** The LeWM encoder ($f_\theta$), low-level predictor ($p_{lo}$), and low-level action encoder are kept frozen during Hi-LeWM training.
- **Waypoint Sampling:** High-level supervision is generated by sampling $N=5$ ordered waypoint indices from demonstration trajectories. The first waypoint is anchored, and subsequent ones are sampled randomly within a bounded span (min stride 1, max total span 15 steps). This results in variable temporal gaps ($k_i$) and corresponding macro-actions summarizing action segments of non-constant duration.
- **High-Level Objective:** The high-level predictor is trained using a mean-squared latent prediction loss: $L_{high} = \mathbb{E}[\|p_{hi}(z_{\tau_i}, \ell_i) - z_{\tau_{i+1}}\|^2_2]$.
- **Optimizer:** AdamW with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay of $10^{-3}$, batch size of 128, and gradient clipping. Models were trained for up to 15 epochs (50 for VQ variants).

#### 3.4. Evaluation Benchmarks and Metrics

- **Environments:** Hi-LeWM is evaluated on PushT-v1 and OGBCube-v0. PushT is the primary environment due to its clear degradation of flat planner performance with increasing goal offsets.
- **Goal Offsets:** Task difficulty is varied by manipulating the temporal distance ($d$) between the start observation and the goal observation, using $d \in \{25, 50, 75\}$ environment steps.
- **Metric:** Success rate, defined as the percentage of episodes where the agent reaches the goal, averaged over three random seeds and 50 episodes per run.
- **Planning Budget:** Sweeps were conducted over high-level and low-level planning horizons, receding-horizon execution lengths, and CEM sampling budgets.

#### 3.5. Diagnostic Analyses

To understand the reasons behind Hi-LeWM's initial underperformance, two diagnostics were employed on PushT at $d=50$:
- **Acting Decomposition:**
 - *Oracle-subgoal acting:* Replaced learned high-level subgoals with true future latents extracted from the dataset, to assess if the low-level planner could execute valid intermediate targets.
 - *Generated-subgoal acting:* Used subgoals generated by the learned high-level model.
- **High-Level Forecast Diagnostic:** Compared the high-level model's predictions in isolation under three modes:
 - *Teacher-forced:* Conditioned on true previous latent states.
 - *Open-loop true:* Autoregressively rolled forward using true macro-actions.
 - *Open-loop CEM:* Autoregressively rolled forward using macro-actions selected by high-level CEM.
- **Latent-to-Pixel Decoder Probe (Appendix D.1):** A lightweight transformer decoder was trained to reconstruct pixel images from latent states. This probe was used for visualization to confirm that latent states retained visually meaningful information.

#### 3.6. Planning-Side Mitigations

Based on diagnostic findings, the authors introduced two planning-side mitigations:
- **Empirical-Macro CEM (Hi-LeWM-C):** Instead of sampling candidate macro-action sequences from an unconstrained Gaussian, this method biases the search distribution. It constructs a bank of macro-action sequences encoded from training trajectories. During planning, candidates are generated as local residual perturbations around these empirical anchors. CEM optimizes only the residual distribution, effectively constraining the high-level search to regions supported by training data.
- **Staged Execution:** In contrast to standard online replanning where the high-level CEM periodically generates a new first subgoal, staged execution involves running high-level CEM once at the beginning of an episode to generate an ordered list of latent targets. The active target advances after a fixed stage duration, while the low-level planner replans toward the current target in a closed loop.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation and diagnostics revealed specific conditions under which temporal abstraction is beneficial for LeWM, as well as the reasons for initial performance degradation.

#### 4.1. Baseline LeWM and Naive Hi-LeWM Performance

- **Flat LeWM Baseline (PushT):** Demonstrated strong performance at short horizons (94.0% at $d=25$), but significant degradation at longer horizons (52.7% at $d=50$, 18.0% at $d=75$).
- **Naive Hi-LeWM (Unconstrained High-Level CEM):** This straightforward hierarchical extension largely *underperformed* the flat LeWM baseline, particularly in regimes where hierarchy was expected to help. Success rates were 89.3% ($d=25$), 38.7% ($d=50$), and 15.3% ($d=75$). At $d=50$, it was 14.0% below the flat baseline, and at $d=75$, it was still 2.7% below. For $d=25$, the best hierarchical configuration effectively acted as a shallow one-step subgoal planner, indicating no meaningful temporal abstraction benefit.

#### 4.2. Failure Analysis through Diagnostics

- **Acting Decomposition:**
 - When the low-level planner was given *oracle future latents* as subgoals (PushT, $d=50$), it achieved 73.3% success. This indicated that the frozen low-level controller *could* execute valid intermediate targets if provided with high-quality subgoals.
 - However, subgoals generated by the naive high-level planner were observed to be "diffuse, poorly localized, and inconsistent with reachable intermediate configurations" (Figure 1), suggesting the issue resided in the high-level subgoal generation quality.
- **High-Level Forecast Diagnostic (Table 1):**
 - Teacher-forced and open-loop true rollouts showed expected, relatively small errors in high-level predictions.
 - In contrast, open-loop CEM-selected macro-actions produced a poor first-stage prediction (high MSE) but achieved a low second-stage error. This suggested that high-level CEM exploited the terminal latent objective by selecting macro-actions that appeared optimal for the *final* prediction but resulted in *unsuitable intermediate subgoals* for control. This pointed to a "planner-induced support mismatch," where CEM explored macro-actions outside the distribution encountered during training.
- **Decoder Probe:** The latent-to-pixel decoder probe confirmed that both true and predicted latents were highly decodable into plausible pixel observations (high SSIM, low MSE), indicating that the latent space itself was informative and the failures were more likely due to planning or temporal alignment than a non-informative representation.

#### 4.3. Impact of Planning-Side Mitigations

- **Empirical-Macro CEM (Hi-LeWM-C):** This mitigation, designed to constrain high-level search, yielded improvements.
 - Online Replanning (PushT): At $d=50$, success improved from 38.7% (naive) to 48.7% (constrained). At $d=75$, it improved from 15.3% to 32.7%, *surpassing the flat LeWM baseline* by +14.7%. Qualitatively, this method produced sharper, more aligned CEM subgoals (Figure 2).
- **Staged Execution (with Empirical-Macro CEM):**
 - PushT $d=50$: Staged execution with empirical-macro CEM achieved the "clearest win," reaching 64.0% success, an improvement of +11.3% over the flat LeWM baseline (52.7%). This indicates its effectiveness at intermediate horizons.
 - PushT $d=75$: Staged execution's performance dropped to 22.0%, falling below online constrained replanning (32.7%). This suggested a limit to committing to long, potentially inaccurate, staged sequences.

#### 4.4. Alternative Macro-Action Representations

A sweep over alternative macro-action representations (Table 2), including fixed-stride waypoints, different continuous latent dimensions, and Vector-Quantized (VQ) macro-actions, showed varying performance. While the reference continuous $d_\ell=32$ model performed best at $d=50$, the VQ 128-code variant achieved the highest success at $d=75$ (34% vs. 20% for the reference). This suggested that discrete macro-action bottlenecks could improve robustness at longer horizons by restricting the high-level action space, though it introduced additional architectural and optimization complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study provides a detailed analysis of integrating temporal abstraction into a compact latent world model, LeWorldModel (LeWM), with frozen low-level components. The findings challenge the assumption that simply adding a hierarchical layer will automatically improve long-horizon planning performance.

#### 5.1. Key Contributions and Insights

- **Conditional Benefit of Hierarchy:** The research demonstrates that temporal abstraction *can* benefit LeWM for long-horizon planning, but this benefit is highly conditional. At short horizons ($d=25$), hierarchy provided no meaningful advantage, while at intermediate ($d=50$) and long ($d=75$) horizons, improvements of +11.3% and +14.7% over flat LeWM were observed, respectively, under specific configurations.
- **Identification of Failure Mode:** A significant contribution is the identification of "planner-induced support mismatch" as a primary failure mode. Unconstrained high-level CEM, optimized for a terminal objective, can select macro-actions that appear favorable in the learned model but correspond to poor, unexecutable intermediate subgoals outside the training data distribution.
- **Effective Mitigations:** The introduction of empirical-macro CEM and selective use of staged execution are shown to be effective planning-side interventions. Empirical-macro CEM successfully constrains high-level search to data-supported macro-action sequences, while staged execution proves beneficial at intermediate horizons by providing a stable temporal scaffold.
- **Guidance for Hierarchical Design:** The study underscores the importance of considering the compatibility between the high-level search space and the low-level controller. It emphasizes that the interaction between representation, search, and execution mode is crucial for successfully integrating hierarchy into existing model-based control frameworks.

#### 5.2. Limitations and Future Directions

- **Generalizability of Environments:** The primary evaluation relied on PushT, which was chosen for its diagnostic value in highlighting long-horizon planning issues. However, broader and more diverse environments are needed to fully assess the generalizability of these conclusions.
- **Focus on Inference-Time Interventions:** The study prioritized inference-time planning modifications over retraining-based architectural changes. While promising results were observed with VQ macro-actions for the longest horizon, the authors opted against them for the main analysis due to their increased architectural and optimization complexity.
- **Model Scale:** The findings are specific to the compact, frozen LeWM setup. Higher-capacity hierarchical world models (e.g., DINO-WM setups as reported by Zhang et al., 2026) may exhibit different behaviors, indicating that the identified limitations are not necessarily universal to all hierarchical designs.

Future work could explore several avenues, including:
- Systematically investigating retraining-based architectural choices, particularly VQ-based hierarchies, to develop intrinsically data-supported high-level action spaces.
- Applying the identified principles and mitigation strategies to a wider range of robotic manipulation and control tasks to confirm generalization.
- Further refining the interplay between support constraints, execution modes (online vs. staged), and temporal scales to develop more robust and adaptive hierarchical planning agents.
