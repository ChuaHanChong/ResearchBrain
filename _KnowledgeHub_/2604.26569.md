# LLM-Flax: Generalizable Robotic Task Planning via Neuro-Symbolic Approaches with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> LLM-Flax presents a neuro-symbolic framework that uses a large language model to autonomously configure task planners, generating planning rules and performing zero-shot object scoring. This approach eliminates manual engineering and training data requirements, achieving an average success rate of 0.945 on the MazeNamo benchmark, which is a 0.117 improvement over manual baselines.

## Problem

- Deploying existing neuro-symbolic task planners requires extensive manual effort from domain experts to author relaxation and complementary rules for each new domain.
- Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) used in these planners for object importance prediction necessitate significant training data collection, involving hundreds of solved problems offline.
- The cumulative manual labor makes the application of such systems to new domains costly and impractical, limiting their generalizability.

## Method

- An open-source Large Language Model is used to automatically generate structured ==relaxation and complementary planning rules== from a ==PDDL domain file==, replacing manual authoring.
- ==LLM-guided failure recovery== is integrated to analyze plan failures and suggest additional relevant objects, enhancing robustness during ==GNN pruning timeouts==.
- The system **LLM-Flax** employs the LLM for ==zero-shot object scoring==, eliminating the need for GNN training data and enabling immediate deployment on new domains.

## Results

- LLM-Flax achieved an average success rate of **0.945** across eight MazeNamo benchmarks, outperforming the manual baseline (**0.828**) by **0.117**.
- The system particularly excelled on complex tasks, achieving an SR of **0.733** on 12x12 Expert problems where the manual planner had an SR of **0.000**.
- LLM-guided failure recovery maintained performance without significant regressions, and zero-shot object scoring demonstrated feasibility on 12x12 Hard tasks (SR **0.720**), although performance degraded on 15x15 Hard tasks (SR **0.200**) due to context window constraints.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Open-source LLMs can comprehend PDDL semantics sufficiently to generate structurally and semantically valid planning rules, often matching or exceeding the quality of manually crafted rules.
> - Robust validation and self-correction mechanisms are essential for LLM-generated outputs, as initial responses can contain structural errors or hallucinated predicates.
> - While zero-shot object scoring is feasible for small to medium-scale problems, scaling to very large, object-rich environments is currently bottlenecked by the LLM's context window limitations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of artificial intelligence planning, specifically addressing the challenges of scalability and generalizability in complex, object-rich environments. Classical symbolic planners, while providing completeness guarantees, often face computational expense due to large state spaces. Neuro-symbolic methods emerged to mitigate this by integrating neural networks to manage state complexity (e.g., identifying relevant objects) before invoking a symbolic solver. The paper directly extends Flax, a representative neuro-symbolic framework that combines Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for object importance prediction with domain-specific symbolic rules (relaxation and complementary rules) to achieve efficient planning.

The broader landscape also includes the increasing application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to various AI tasks, including formal reasoning, code generation, and task planning. Previous work has explored LLMs for grounding natural language commands in robot affordances (SayCan), translating natural language to PDDL (LLM+P, PDDLego), and enhancing structured reasoning (Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thoughts). However, a gap exists in the automated generation of specific rule configurations (like relaxation and complementary rules) for existing neuro-symbolic planners, as well as addressing budget-aware failure recovery in such systems.

This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on the automation of the manual engineering effort traditionally required to deploy neuro-symbolic planners like Flax. Rather than learning from interaction traces, it leverages LLMs' pre-trained knowledge to infer predicate semantics and generate structured planning rules, aiming to eliminate human intervention in domain configuration and GNN training, thereby enhancing cross-domain generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the significant manual effort currently required to deploy neuro-symbolic task planners on new domains. Specifically, the authors identify three distinct forms of manual labor associated with frameworks like Flax:
1. **Manual Authoring of Relaxation Rules**: Domain experts are required to write rules that specify which non-essential objects can be removed to simplify a planning problem, along with the conditions for their removal and the effects on the state.
2. **Manual Authoring of Complementary Rules**: Experts must also create rules defining which object pairs must be included together in a simplified problem due to spatial or structural constraints.
3. **Extensive Training Data Collection for Neural Networks**: Hundreds of training problems must be solved offline to supervise the Graph Neural Network (GNN) object scorer, which predicts the importance of objects.

These requirements collectively make it impractical and costly to apply existing neuro-symbolic planning systems to new domains without substantial engineering investment.

The key objective of LLM-Flax is to eliminate all three of these sources of manual effort using a locally hosted Large Language Model, given only a PDDL domain file. The authors hypothesize that an LLM can automatically:
- Infer predicate semantics from a PDDL domain file.
- Generate valid planning rules in the required structured format.
- Semantically guide recovery from plan failures.
- Score object importance in a zero-shot manner, eliminating the need for training data.

This overarching objective aims to enable immediate deployment of neuro-symbolic planners on new domains without any domain expert involvement or training data collection, thereby increasing the generalizability and practical applicability of such systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LLM-Flax is a three-stage framework designed to automate the deployment of neuro-symbolic task planners. It preserves the core three-step planning loop of the Flax system (GNN Pruning, Relaxation, Complementary Expansion) while replacing its manual components with LLM-driven automation.

**A. Rule Format Background**
The Flax planner utilizes relaxation and complementary rules stored as structured JSON configurations.
- **Relaxation rules** specify `pre_compute` (related object lookup), `precond` (unary predicates identifying removable objects), `delete_objects` (argument index of object to remove), `delete_effects` (literals to retract), and `add_effects` (literals to assert). All values are 0-based integer argument indices.
- **Complementary rules** map a binary predicate name to `cond`/`cmpl` index lists, indicating that if an object at `cond[i]` is included, the object at `cmpl[i]` must also be included.

**B. Stage 1: LLM Rule Generation**
This stage automates the creation of relaxation and complementary rules. A locally hosted open-source LLM (Gemma3-12B) is used via Ollama with a native REST API.
- **Prompt Design**: Each prompt is carefully constructed to guide the LLM. It includes:
 1. A natural-language explanation of the rule type's purpose.
 2. The exact JSON schema with field semantics.
 3. Explicit emphasis on using integer indices, not strings.
 4. A correct worked example.
 5. A "wrong example" illustrating common formatting errors.
 The full PDDL domain text is included verbatim to allow the LLM to inspect predicate signatures.
- **Validation and Self-Correction**: LLM outputs are validated by a `VALIDATERULES` function. This function checks for: (i) presence of all required keys, (ii) integer lists in index fields, (iii) existence of all predicate names in the PDDL domain, and (iv) absence of duplicate rules. If validation fails, error messages are fed back to the LLM for self-correction, with up to `K=3` attempts.
- **Post-processing**: After successful validation, two steps are applied: (i) Deduplication, merging identical rules, and (ii) Predicate filtering, silently dropping rules referencing non-existent predicates. The final rules are saved as JSON.

**C. Stage 2: LLM-Guided Failure Recovery**
This stage enhances the planner's ability to recover from GNN pruning (Step 1) timeouts.
- **LLMRECOVERYGUIDANCE**: When Step 1 times out, the LLM analyzes the PDDL state, goal, currently included objects, and excluded candidates. It identifies excluded objects likely blocking the plan and returns a JSON list of object names to be added. The planner then retries with the expanded object set. If successful, the plan is returned; otherwise, the pipeline falls back to Step 2 (relaxation).
- **Budget Policy**: To manage the latency cost of LLM API calls, a three-part policy is implemented:
 1. **Feasibility Check**: Recovery is skipped if the remaining time before the Step 2 deadline is less than `t_LLM + t_replan,min + t_step2,min` (conservatively set to 11 seconds). This prevents starving the downstream relaxation step.
 2. **Recovery Cap**: The recovery replan is limited to 15% of the total problem timeout.
 3. **Step 2 Guarantee**: Step 2 (relaxation) is guaranteed at least 5 seconds of budget regardless of the recovery outcome.

**D. Stage 3: LLM Zero-Shot Object Scoring**
This stage eliminates the need for GNN training data by replacing the domain-trained GNN with an LLM-based zero-shot object scorer (`LLMOBJECTGUIDANCE`).
- **Interface**: The LLM implements the `score_object(obj, state)` interface.
- **Scoring Process**: On the first call for a given state, the LLM receives the goal, a list of all objects with types, and up to 80 state facts. It then returns a JSON dictionary mapping object names to scores in. These scores are cached to avoid repeated LLM calls for the same planning problem.
- **Failure Mode**: If the LLM fails to provide scores, all objects receive a default score of 0.5, allowing the planner's γ-decay fallback mechanism to proceed. The `train` method is a no-op as no training data or learning is required.

**E. Full LLM-Flax System**
Combining all three stages, Full LLM-Flax requires only a PDDL domain file as input. Stage 1 generates rules offline (approximately 10 seconds). At test time, Stage 3 scores objects zero-shot, while Stage 2 handles failure recovery. This integrated system eliminates all manual domain engineering and training data requirements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LLM-Flax was conducted on the MazeNamo benchmark across various grid sizes (10x10, 12x12, 15x15), totaling eight distinct benchmarks.

**A. Stage 1: LLM Rule Generation Performance**
- **Overall Performance**: LLM-Flax, using LLM-generated rules, achieved an average Success Rate (SR) of 0.945 across all eight benchmarks, compared to the manual baseline's SR of 0.828, representing a +0.117 improvement. LLM-Flax matched or outperformed manual rules on every one of the eight benchmarks.
- **Specific Improvements**:
 - On 12x12 Expert tasks, the manual planner failed entirely (SR 0.000), while LLM-Flax achieved an SR of 0.733 with an average planning time of 5.24 seconds.
 - On 15x15 Hard tasks, LLM-Flax attained SR 1.000, outperforming Manual's SR 0.900.
 - For stacking-heavy tasks (e.g., 10x10 Easy/Hard, 15x15 Medium), LLM-Flax tied Manual SR.
 - For navigation-heavy and moderate stacking tasks (e.g., 10x10 Medium, 12x12 Medium/Hard), LLM-Flax showed gains of +0.020 to +0.040 SR.
- **Rule Quality Analysis (Gemma3-12B)**:
 - **Relaxation Rules**: The Gemma3-12B model generated a relaxation rule that was structurally and semantically identical to the manually crafted baseline, explaining the consistent performance. Other models (Qwen2.5-14B, Llama3.1-8B, Mistral-7B) generated rules with various deviations, such as being over-conservative or structurally invalid.
 - **Complementary Rules**: The LLM generated seven complementary rules (upon, oat, rat, upto, downto, leftto, rightto) compared to the single manual rule (oat). The `oat` rule was identical to the manual version. The additional `rat` (robot-position), `upon` (stacked-box), and directional rules were semantically valid and generally improved robustness by preventing incomplete object sets, particularly in navigation-heavy problems. However, the inclusion of all four direction rules was noted as potentially over-inclusive, contributing to a lower SR on 12x12 Expert compared to a Qwen model with fewer complementary rules.
- **Effect of Validation and Post-Processing**: Validation and self-correction were found to be essential. Raw LLM outputs initially contained structural errors (e.g., strings as indices, duplicate rules, hallucinated predicates). One correction turn resolved structural errors, and post-processing steps (deduplication, predicate filtering) further refined the rule set.

**B. Stage 2: LLM-Guided Failure Recovery Performance**
- **Impact**: On the two benchmarks where Step 1 frequently timed out (12x12 Hard, 15x15 Medium), LLM-Flax + Recovery showed neutral results. The SR remained identical to LLM-Flax without recovery, and planning time increased only marginally.
- **Budget Policy Effectiveness**: The feasibility-gated budget policy successfully prevented LLM API latency from starving the Step 2 relaxation budget. In cases with tighter budgets (e.g., 30s timeout on 12x12 Hard), the LLM call was correctly skipped. For longer timeouts (e.g., 40s on 15x15 Medium), the LLM call proceeded but provided no additional benefit as the relaxation fallback was already effective. This avoided significant SR regressions observed in earlier, less robust budget policies.

**C. Stage 3: LLM Zero-Shot Object Scoring Performance**
- **Feasibility**: On 12x12 Hard, Full LLM-Flax (with no GNN training data) achieved an SR of 0.720, demonstrating the feasibility of zero-shot object scoring. However, this came at a higher planning time cost (22.2s vs. 4.5s for LLM-Flax due to LLM API call per problem).
- **Scale Limitation**: On 15x15 Hard, Full LLM-Flax's performance significantly degraded to SR 0.200 (compared to LLM-Flax's SR 1.000). This collapse was attributed to a context-window bottleneck: problems with 240+ objects exceeded the LLM's 80-fact context cap, providing insufficient information for accurate zero-shot scoring at scale. Investigated prompt engineering approaches (goal-biased fact selection, Chain-of-Thought, Top-K scoring) consistently underperformed the direct baseline, primarily due to increased LLM generation time further depleting the planning budget or distorting the score distribution.

**D. LLM Model Comparison (Stage 1 Rules Only)**
- Gemma3-12B achieved the best average SR (0.945).
- Qwen2.5-14B (0.912) uniquely excelled on 12x12 Expert (1.000 SR), likely due to a smaller, less over-inclusive set of complementary rules.
- Llama3.1-8B (0.755) and Mistral-7B (0.660) showed lower performance, generating invalid or overly aggressive rules, highlighting that rule generation quality, rather than just model size, is crucial.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LLM-Flax framework represents a notable step towards reducing the engineering overhead associated with deploying neuro-symbolic task planners. Its primary significance lies in automating the three key sources of manual effort: rule authoring (relaxation and complementary) and GNN training.

**Reduced Deployment Cost and Increased Generalizability**: By requiring only a PDDL domain file, LLM-Flax reduces the cost of deploying a neuro-symbolic planner to a new domain from potentially hours of expert engineering to an automated process. This significantly enhances the practical applicability and cross-domain generalizability of such planning systems. The results demonstrate that LLM-generated rules can match or even outperform manually crafted ones, especially on more complex tasks, without any domain-specific examples beyond the format template.

**Validation of LLMs in Formal Reasoning**: The work provides empirical evidence that open-source LLMs possess sufficient PDDL comprehension to infer predicate semantics and generate structured planning rules. The necessity of validation and self-correction mechanisms highlights both the capabilities and the current limitations of LLMs in producing formally correct outputs.

**Insights into Neuro-Symbolic System Design**: The comprehensive four-way comparison (Pure FD, PLOI, Manual Flax, LLM-Flax) underscores the critical role of relaxation and complementary rules in neuro-symbolic planning. It shows that GNN scoring alone (PLOI) is often insufficient, and that rules are key to achieving high success rates on challenging problems. LLM-Flax successfully automates this crucial rule generation component.

**Future Directions and Challenges**: The identified context-window bottleneck for zero-shot object scoring (Stage 3) on large problems presents a clear direction for future research. Overcoming this limitation will be crucial for scaling the fully automated system to even more complex, object-rich environments. Potential solutions include leveraging larger context window LLMs or developing lightweight few-shot adaptation techniques. The work also suggests refining prompt instructions to guide LLMs towards generating more context-appropriate rule sets (e.g., avoiding over-inclusive complementary rules for large grids) or developing online rule quality evaluation methods.

In conclusion, LLM-Flax establishes a practical path toward truly domain-agnostic neuro-symbolic planning for small-to-medium problems by effectively replacing expert human engineering with LLM-driven automation. This contributes to making advanced planning systems more accessible and deployable across diverse robotic and AI domains.
