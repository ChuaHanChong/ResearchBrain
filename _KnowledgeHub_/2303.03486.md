# Sampling-based Exploration for Reinforcement Learning of Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework integrating sampling-based planning with reinforcement learning allows robots to acquire dexterous in-hand manipulation skills for complex and concave objects without external support surfaces. The learned policies, relying solely on intrinsic tactile and proprioceptive feedback, successfully transfer to a real robot, enabling robust multi-revolution object reorientation.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces significant exploration challenges in the high-dimensional, constrained state spaces of dexterous manipulation, often leading to inefficient learning or task failure.
- Existing RL methods for in-hand manipulation frequently rely on simplifying assumptions like convex objects, passive support surfaces, or extensive extrinsic sensing, limiting their real-world applicability.
- Learning complex finger-gaiting skills to achieve large object reorientation using only intrinsic sensing and maintaining a stable grasp without external support is difficult due to non-holonomic transitions and narrow state space passages.

## Method

- The research integrates ==Sampling-Based Planning (SBP)== algorithms, specifically a ==General-purpose RRT (G-RRT)== leveraging full dynamics simulation and parallel execution in IsaacGym, to generate effective and diverse ==reset distributions== for RL training.
- A custom policy architecture uses only ==intrinsic observations== (hand joint positions and contact feedback) for its actor, while the critic receives additional ==privileged information== (object pose and contact forces).
- Reinforcement Learning is performed using an ==Asymmetric Actor-Critic PPO== algorithm with a reward function designed to encourage object angular velocity around a hand-centric axis, penalizing translation and promoting stable grasp through contact maintenance.

## Results

- **G-RRT** consistently explored the manipulation state space farther and enabled more efficient RL training, particularly for 'hard' concave objects (e.g., L-shape) where baseline exploration methods failed to learn.
- Policies trained with SBP-informed resets successfully transferred to a physical robot hand, demonstrating robust finger-gaiting and multi-revolution reorientation for various objects, including challenging L-shapes (median **1.5 revolutions**).
- An ablation study confirmed that a sufficiently large SBP tree (e.g., **~10⁵ nodes**) is necessary for reliable RL training, and tactile feedback significantly contributes to learning performance on complex objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - SBP-generated state distributions are crucial for overcoming exploration difficulties in dexterous manipulation, enabling RL to learn skills for complex, concave objects where random or simpler exploration methods fail.
> - Leveraging full physics simulation for SBP (G-RRT) is more effective and efficient than approximate kinematic methods (M-RRT) for generating diverse and stable manipulation states, especially when parallel simulation is available.
> - Tactile feedback is essential for learning robust manipulation policies for challenging objects without object pose information, proving more beneficial and leading to faster learning compared to relying solely on proprioception or even global object pose.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has advanced the capabilities of robot sensorimotor control, particularly in manipulation, enabling dexterity levels previously difficult to achieve, such as in-hand object re-orientation. However, a significant challenge in training RL policies for manipulation tasks lies in the complexity of the problem's state space. This complexity often manifests as "narrow passages" or regions where random actions can easily lead to undesirable, irrecoverable states (e.g., dropping an object). Current research often mitigates these difficulties by using simple, convex objects, relying on passive support surfaces, or employing extensive extrinsic sensing for object pose tracking.

The issue of navigating complex state spaces is not new in robotics. Sampling-Based Planning (SBP) algorithms, such as Rapidly-Exploring Random Trees (RRT) and Probabilistic Road Maps (PRM), were developed to address this by exploring high-dimensional spaces efficiently and offering probabilistic completeness. However, SBP methods primarily generate trajectories between known start and goal states rather than robust, closed-loop control policies capable of handling deviations or unseen conditions. They are also not designed for real-time online use in dynamic environments.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these two paradigms. Previous attempts to combine SBP and RL have largely focused on navigation tasks, where local connections between states are relatively straightforward. For dexterous in-hand manipulation, the state space is significantly more constrained, characterized by non-holonomic transitions and small useful regions, making direct application of prior SBP-RL integration challenging. Some works have used SBP for expert demonstrations or populating replay buffers, but these approaches have their own limitations, such as requiring action information from SBP or compatibility issues with parallelized on-policy RL. Existing dexterous manipulation research with RL has also often relied on extrinsic sensing or support surfaces. While advancements in using intrinsic sensing (tactile and proprioceptive) for manipulation have been made, they have typically been demonstrated on simpler convex objects.

This work addresses the exploration challenge in RL for dexterous manipulation by leveraging the exploration efficiency of SBP. It aims to develop a method that enables RL policies to learn complex finger-gaiting skills for difficult, including concave, objects, without relying on support surfaces and using only intrinsic (tactile and proprioceptive) sensing. The core contribution is the use of SBP-generated state distributions as reset points for RL training, bridging the gap between SBP's global exploration capabilities and RL's policy learning strengths.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel method for achieving dexterous in-hand manipulation of complex objects, specifically including concave shapes, while simultaneously ensuring the object remains securely grasped without the aid of passive support surfaces. A related objective is to achieve large object re-orientation solely through intrinsic sensing (tactile and proprioceptive feedback), without relying on extrinsic information like global object pose. The authors consider large in-hand object rotation, which necessitates extensive finger gaiting, as a representative challenge for this class of problems.

The motivation for this work stems from several key difficulties inherent in training such manipulation policies using Reinforcement Learning:

1. **Challenging State Space Exploration:** Dexterous manipulation tasks, particularly those involving finger gaiting and maintaining a stable grasp, possess a high-dimensional state space. The accessible and useful regions of this space are highly constrained, forming complex structures often described as "narrow passages." Random exploration in the action space, a common RL strategy, is inefficient and unlikely to discover these critical regions, often leading to premature episode termination (e.g., dropping the object).
2. **Non-Holonomic Transitions:** The system dynamics for in-hand manipulation are non-holonomic. The coordinated movement of fingers and the object, with constant contact maintenance and stability, means that the hand-object system must evolve along specific, complex manifolds within the state space. This further complicates exploration.
3. **Limitations of Existing RL Approaches:** Current RL successes in dexterous manipulation often rely on simplifying assumptions (e.g., convex objects, support surfaces) or extensive extrinsic sensing (e.g., object pose tracking). The goal is to overcome these limitations and achieve more general and robust manipulation.
4. **Desire for Intrinsic Sensing:** Biological organisms achieve high dexterity using primarily intrinsic sensing. Emulating this in robots can lead to policies that are robust to environmental factors like occlusion and lighting, and enable operation in constrained spaces where external sensors may be impractical.
5. **Leveraging SBP Strengths:** Sampling-Based Planning (SBP) algorithms are effective at exploring high-dimensional, complex state spaces and finding feasible trajectories. The motivation is to integrate SBP's exploration power with RL's ability to learn robust, closed-loop policies. SBP can efficiently identify valid manipulation states, which can then inform RL training to accelerate policy learning.

In summary, the core motivation is to enhance the exploration capabilities of RL for complex dexterous manipulation problems by integrating insights from SBP, thereby enabling the learning of advanced skills on challenging objects with minimal and robust intrinsic sensing, ultimately facilitating effective sim-to-real transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on using Sampling-Based Planning (SBP) algorithms to generate effective reset distributions for Reinforcement Learning (RL) training. The problem is defined as learning a policy for issuing finger motor commands to achieve large object rotation around a hand-centric axis, while maintaining a stable precision grasp.

**A. Problem Description and Sensing:**
The system state $x_t = (q_t, p_t)$ includes hand joint positions ($q \in R^d$) and object pose relative to the hand ($p \in R^6$). Actions are joint position setpoints. The physics simulator provides the forward dynamics $x_{t+1} = F(x_t, a_t)$. Crucially, the policy's observation $o_t = [q_t, q_s_t, c_t]$ consists only of intrinsic hand-centric data: current joint positions ($q_t$), joint setpoints ($q_s_t$), and binary contact/no-contact feedback ($c_t$) from $m$ fingers. A stable precision grasp must be maintained, meaning at least three fingers in contact and a stable contact distribution. This necessitates navigating a high-dimensional state space with non-holonomic transitions.

**B. Manipulation RRT (M-RRT):**
This SBP variant leverages domain-specific kinematic constraints and analytical approximations to explore the manipulation state space.
1. **Contact and Stability Conditions:** For any state to be considered useful, the hand must maintain at least three fingers in contact with the object, and the contact distribution must allow for a stable grasp. These are necessary, though not sufficient, conditions for dynamic stability.
2. **Contact Maintenance Projection:** To transition from $x_{start}$ to $x_{end}$ while maintaining contact with a set of fingers $S$, the desired state change $\Delta x_{des}$ must satisfy $J_S(q_{start})\Delta q_{des} = G_S(p_{start})\Delta p_{des}$, where $J_S$ is the contact Jacobian and $G_S$ is the grasp map matrix. This can be expressed as $N_S(x_{start})\Delta x_{des} = 0$. If $\Delta x_{des}$ violates this, it is projected onto the null space of $N_S$ using $\Delta x_{proj} = (I - N^T N)\Delta x_{des}$. The new state is $x_{new} = x_{start} + \alpha \Delta x_{proj}$. A practical modification brings fingers within a 5mm threshold back into contact.
3. **Grasp Stability Check:** To ensure a stable contact distribution, a Quadratic Program (QP) is formulated to minimize the net wrench on the object ($w$) by applying non-negative normal forces ($c_i$) at the $k$ contacts. If the minimized $||w||$ is below a threshold $\epsilon_{stab}$, the grasp is deemed stable. This check is conservative as it does not rely on friction forces and ensures the ability to generate internal forces, but not the specific motor torques.
4. **Algorithm Flow:** M-RRT expands a tree by repeatedly sampling a random state $x_{sample}$, finding the closest tree node $x_{node}$, and computing a desired direction $\Delta x_{des}$. It then iterates through all possible sets of three contacting fingers from $x_{node}$, projects $\Delta x_{des}$ onto each corresponding constraint manifold, and selects the projection that results in the closest new state $x_i$ to $x_{sample}$. This $x_i$ is added to the tree if it passes the analytical stability check. This method is fast but approximate, not using the environment's full dynamics.

**C. General-purpose non-holonomic RRT (G-RRT):**
This variant is designed for problems where the system dynamics $F$ are available and efficient to evaluate.
1. **Random Action Sampling:** G-RRT grows the tree by sampling a fixed number ($K_{max}$) of random actions at each iteration. For each action, the transition function $F$ is used to simulate the next state.
2. **Dynamic Stability Check:** A new node $x_a$ is added to the tree only if it passes a stability check under full system dynamics. This involves simulating for an additional 1 second with no action; if the object has not been dropped (height above threshold), the state is considered stable. This replaces the analytical approximations of M-RRT.
3. **Algorithm Flow:** Similar to M-RRT, it samples $x_{sample}$ and finds $x_{node}$. It then samples $K_{max}$ random actions, simulates the resulting states $x_a$ using $F$, and selects the stable $x_a$ that is closest to $x_{sample}$ to add to the tree. This algorithm is simpler and more general but can be computationally intensive if $K_{max}$ is large or $F$ is slow. The authors leverage IsaacGym for parallel simulation to make this practical.

**D. Reinforcement Learning (RL) Integration:**
The RRT-generated trees are used to inform the RL policy training by generating a specialized reset distribution.
1. **Task-Specific Path Selection:** Since the RRT trees are task-agnostic, relevant paths are selected. For the object reorientation task, the top ten paths from the RRT tree that achieve the largest angular change around the z-axis are chosen. These paths typically comprise 100-400 nodes.
2. **Reset Distribution:** A uniform distribution over the states (nodes) from these selected paths is used as the reset distribution for RL episodes.
3. **RL Algorithm:** Asymmetric Actor-Critic PPO is used, with training conducted in the IsaacGym simulator.
4. **Reward Function:** The reward function encourages object angular velocity about the z-axis, provided at least three fingertip contacts are maintained. Penalties are applied for the object's translational velocity and deviation from its initial position. Episodes terminate early if fewer than two contacts are present.
5. **Critic Input:** The critic receives additional feedback beyond the policy's observation, including object pose ($p$), object velocity ($\dot{p}$), and net contact force on each fingertip ($t_m$).

**E. Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
For real-world evaluation, policies are transferred to the physical robot hand (Columbia Hand). Several steps are taken to bridge the sim-to-real gap:
1. Imposing velocity and torque limits in simulation matching real hardware.
2. Including a 0.05s hardware latency in simulation.
3. Modifying the angular velocity reward to maintain a desired velocity rather than maximizing it.
4. Employing curriculum learning for domain randomization: randomizing joint origins (0.1 rad), friction coefficient (1-40), and introducing perturbation forces (1N).
5. Sensing adjustments: Using current position and setpoint from motor controllers. For tactile data, a contact force threshold of 1N is applied in both simulation and real hardware, as readings below this threshold are unreliable. The authors highlight the benefit of intrinsic sensing for a smaller sim-to-real gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings regarding the effectiveness of sampling-based exploration for RL in dexterous manipulation across different object complexities.

**A. Exploration Tree Performance (Figure 3):**
Both G-RRT and M-RRT algorithms demonstrated the ability to explore the state space effectively.
- **G-RRT Performance:** G-RRT consistently explored farther (achieved greater maximum object z-axis rotation) with fewer iterations compared to M-RRT. Its performance further improved with an increased number of random actions ($K_{max}$) tested at each iteration. Notably, G-RRT performed acceptably even with $K_{max}=1$, suggesting that the selection of the closest node to the random sample at each iteration encourages effective growth at the tree's periphery and along real constraint manifolds.
- **M-RRT Performance:** M-RRT's exploration was somewhat limited by the small steps it took, attributed to the linearization of contact constraints used in its projection method.
- **Parallelization:** The use of IsaacGym for parallel simulation made both algorithms practical for generating large trees (up to $10^5$ nodes), facilitating their integration with RL training.

**B. Reinforcement Learning Training Performance (Figure 4):**
The efficacy of the proposed methods was evaluated across three categories of objects: "easy" (sphere, cube, cylinder), "moderate" (elongated cuboids), and "hard" (concave L- or U-shapes), comparing G-RRT and M-RRT against two baselines: Stable Grasp Sampler (SGS) and Explored Restarts (ER), plus Fixed Initialization (FI).
- **Easy Objects:** For easy objects, all three methods (G-RRT, M-RRT, SGS) learned rotation gaits effectively, with similar performance levels. This confirms prior findings that random grasp sampling (SGS) can be sufficient for simpler shapes.
- **Moderate Objects:** On moderate objects, G-RRT, M-RRT, and SGS again enabled learning. However, policies trained with G-RRT-based exploration exhibited higher effectiveness, suggesting an advantage in discovering more optimal or diverse initial states.
- **Hard Objects:** For complex "hard" objects, the random grasp-based reset distribution (SGS) was insufficient, failing to enable policy learning. Only G-RRT and M-RRT were able to learn manipulation skills, with G-RRT demonstrating greater efficiency in the learning process.
- **Baselines (ER, FI):** The domain-agnostic exploration methods, Explored Restarts (ER) and Fixed Initialization (FI) (even with gravity curriculum), failed to learn viable in-hand manipulation policies for any object set within the allotted training time. This highlights the specific challenge of exploration in dexterous manipulation that these methods do not address.

**C. Impact of Tree Size (Figure 5):**
An ablation study on the L-shaped object (a "hard" object) showed that the size of the RRT tree used for generating reset states impacts learning performance.
- A sufficiently large tree (at least $10^4$ nodes) was necessary to enable learning.
- Training was most reliable and efficient when using reset states derived from a tree of $10^5$ nodes. This suggests a correlation between the quality/diversity of states in the reset distribution and the sample efficiency of subsequent RL training.

**D. Ablation Study of Policy Feedback (Figure 6):**
The importance of different feedback components for policy learning was investigated, particularly for the L-shaped object.
- **Touch Feedback Importance:** Tactile feedback was identified as essential for learning manipulation skills for moderate and hard objects in the absence of object pose feedback.
- **Comparison with Object Pose:** Replacing tactile feedback with object pose feedback resulted in slower learning. This indicates that tactile information provides valuable local contact cues that can be more beneficial than global object pose for these skills. Proprioceptive-only feedback was insufficient for hard objects.

**E. Sim-to-Real Transfer (Table I and Figure 7):**
The learned policies were successfully transferred to a real robot hand for a subset of representative objects (cylinder, cube, cuboid, and L-shape).
- **Transfer Process:** The transfer involved incorporating real-world constraints into the simulation (velocity/torque limits, hardware latency), applying domain randomization via curriculum learning (joint origins, friction, perturbation forces), and filtering tactile data based on reliability thresholds.
- **Performance on Real Hardware:** The policies demonstrated robust finger-gaiting, achieving multiple complete object revolutions before dropping for all tested objects. For example, the cylinder achieved a median of 5 revolutions, and the L-shape achieved 1.5 revolutions. Mean rotation speeds ranged from 0.24 rad/s (L-shape) to 0.44 rad/s (cube, cuboid). This indicates effective transfer and real-world applicability of the learned skills.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates a methodological framework for addressing a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: the efficient exploration of complex state spaces in Reinforcement Learning. By integrating Sampling-Based Planning (SBP) algorithms with model-free RL, the authors have opened avenues for learning dexterous manipulation skills that were previously difficult to achieve.

The primary significance lies in enabling RL to learn in-hand manipulation for **objects with challenging geometries**, specifically concave or elongated shapes, which have not been widely demonstrated in prior literature under these constraints. This expands the scope of objects that can be manipulated by learning-based robotic systems. Furthermore, achieving these complex skills **without relying on external support surfaces** and using **only intrinsic tactile and proprioceptive sensing** represents a substantial advancement. This minimizes dependence on external environmental conditions (e.g., specific lighting, clear lines of sight) and sensor types (e.g., cameras), making the learned policies more robust to occlusion and varying lighting, and potentially more suitable for operation in confined spaces.

The successful **sim-to-real transfer** of these policies to physical hardware, with a minimal sim-to-real gap, highlights the practical applicability of the approach. By relying on intrinsic sensing and careful domain randomization within a curriculum, the policies demonstrate real-world utility, moving beyond purely simulated demonstrations. The finding that tactile feedback is crucial for challenging objects and leads to faster learning compared to object pose feedback also emphasizes the importance of contact information for fine-grained manipulation.

The work's impact extends to providing a **generalizable strategy for overcoming exploration barriers** in other constrained, high-dimensional robotics tasks where traditional RL exploration struggles. The complementary strengths of SBP (global exploration of complex manifolds) and RL (learning robust, closed-loop control policies) are effectively combined. This paradigm could accelerate the development of advanced robotic skills across various domains, reducing the need for extensive manual feature engineering or simplified task environments.

Future work suggested by the authors, such as exploring alternative mechanisms for SBP-RL integration beyond reset distributions (e.g., action suggestions from SBP, direct integration into policy training), indicates potential for even more synergistic approaches. The exploration of richer tactile feedback is also a promising direction. Overall, this research contributes a valuable methodology to the field of robotic manipulation, paving the way for more autonomous and versatile dexterous robot capabilities.
