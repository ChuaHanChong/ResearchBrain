# Structured Prompting Enables More Robust Evaluation of Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework from Stanford University researchers, DSPy+HELM, integrates structured prompting into language model evaluation, revealing that fixed-prompt benchmarks often yield unrepresentative performance estimates and lead to altered leaderboard rankings. The study found LMs achieved an average +4% absolute accuracy increase across diverse tasks when using structured prompting, with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning identified as the main contributor to performance improvements.

## Problem

- Fixed, hand-crafted prompts in existing benchmarks frequently underestimate the true capabilities of large language models (LLMs).
- LLMs are highly sensitive to prompt design, leading to unreliable performance comparisons and potentially inaccurate leaderboard rankings.
- Manual prompt engineering for optimal performance is labor-intensive, requires domain expertise, and does not scale across many models or tasks.

## Method

- Developed a reproducible ==DSPy+HELM framework== that integrates DSPy's declarative structured prompting methods into HELM's comprehensive evaluation suite.
- Systematically evaluated various structured prompting methods, including ==Zero-Shot CoT==, ==Bootstrap Few-Shot with Random Search (BFRS)==, and MIPROv2, against HELM's baseline fixed prompts.
- Conducted experiments on four frontier LLMs across seven diverse general and medical benchmarks to quantify the impact of optimized prompting.

## Results

- Structured prompting methods consistently improved LM performance, yielding an average **+4%** absolute accuracy gain across the seven benchmarks compared to the HELM baseline.
- Leaderboard rankings were altered on **3** out of **7** benchmarks, and the average performance gap between the top two models (o3 Mini and Claude 3.7 Sonnet) shrank from **6%** to **3%** under structured prompting.
- Zero-Shot CoT achieved most of the performance improvements with only **164** additional prompt tokens, whereas more complex few-shot methods required up to **1,700** tokens for marginal additional gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning is the dominant driver of performance gains, significantly reducing an LM's sensitivity to further prompt optimization, as explained by a theoretical model.
> - Fixed-prompt benchmarks provide unrepresentative performance estimates and can lead to materially altered leaderboard rankings and performance gap assessments between models.
> - While few-shot optimizers achieve the performance ceiling, Zero-Shot CoT provides most of the gains with minimal computational overhead, making it a cost-effective method for robust evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and widespread adoption of large language models (LMs) across various domains have necessitated robust and reliable benchmarking frameworks to assess their capabilities and guide deployment decisions. Current benchmarking practices, exemplified by frameworks such as the Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM), typically assess LMs using a single, fixed prompt configuration. This approach implicitly assumes that the chosen prompt adequately captures the model's full capabilities. However, prior research indicates that LM performance is highly sensitive to prompt design, raising concerns that reported benchmark scores may reflect the prompt's efficacy as much as the model's inherent ability. This sensitivity can lead to an inaccurate characterization of model performance and misleading relative rankings on public leaderboards.

The broader research landscape has seen the emergence of prompt engineering and automatic prompt optimization (APO) techniques as methods to enhance LM performance. These techniques range from manual prompt crafting, incorporating strategies like few-shot learning and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, to more sophisticated automated approaches that treat prompt design as an optimization problem. Declarative prompting frameworks, such as DSPy, offer a structured and scalable approach to prompt design, allowing for the composition of modular LM pipelines and the programmatic optimization of prompts.

This work addresses a critical gap within this landscape: the lack of a systematic study integrating structured prompting methods into established, standardized evaluation frameworks to quantify the isolated impact of prompt choice on benchmark outcomes. Existing benchmarks often use generic prompts to foster "generic language interfaces," potentially underestimating actual LM capabilities. This study positions itself as the first to systematically bridge structured prompting frameworks with comprehensive evaluation platforms, thereby providing empirical evidence on how variations in prompt configuration can alter benchmark conclusions. It builds upon and extends the understanding of LM evaluation by moving beyond static prompt configurations to explore a family of prompting strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while language models are increasingly deployed, their evaluation on standard benchmarks often relies on static, single-prompt configurations. This practice is considered inadequate because LM behavior is known to be highly dependent on the choice of prompt. Consequently, published benchmark scores and leaderboards may not accurately reflect the true capabilities of LMs but rather their performance under a specific, potentially suboptimal, prompt. This can lead to distorted absolute scores, inaccurate relative rankings, and suboptimal deployment decisions.

The core objectives of this study are designed to systematically investigate and quantify this issue:

1. **Develop a Reproducible Framework:** To create a reproducible DSPy+HELM framework. This framework aims to enable researchers to systematically study how prompt choice influences LM evaluation across a diverse set of HELM benchmarks. The integration of DSPy, a declarative prompting framework, with HELM, an established evaluation framework, is intended to facilitate the exploration of various structured prompting strategies.
2. **Evaluate Multiple Prompting Methods and Models:** To evaluate a range of five distinct prompting methods (HELM Baseline, Zero-Shot Predict, Zero-Shot CoT, BFRS, MIPROv2) across a selection of four frontier LMs and two open-source LMs. This evaluation is conducted across seven HELM benchmarks that span general and medical domains, covering tasks such as reasoning, knowledge QA, problem-solving, and error classification. This broad evaluation aims to provide a comprehensive view of how different prompting strategies affect diverse models on varied tasks.
3. **Quantify the Impact of Prompt Choice:** To provide empirical evidence demonstrating that prompt choice can materially impact leaderboard outcomes. Specifically, the study aims to quantify improvements in performance, observe shifts in leaderboard rankings, and identify the primary sources of these gains (e.g., Chain-of-Thought vs. more advanced optimizers). This objective seeks to move beyond anecdotal observations to systematic, measurable insights into the sensitivity of LM evaluation to prompting strategies.

By pursuing these objectives, the researchers aim to underscore the limitations of current static prompting paradigms in LM evaluation and advocate for more robust evaluation protocols that consider the variability introduced by prompt design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study integrates the DSPy declarative prompting framework with the HELM evaluation framework to systematically analyze the impact of prompt choice on language model performance.

#### 3.1. DSPy Framework Integration

DSPy is utilized as a tool for composing modular LM pipelines. In this context, an LM program (Φ) comprises multiple modules, each associated with a prompt template (p_i). These templates contain variables for instructions and K demonstration examples. DSPy's optimization objective is to maximize an evaluation metric (μ) by learning optimal assignments of these variables to concrete strings, effectively optimizing the prompts. The integration allows DSPy to generate structured prompt variants based on HELM's baseline prompts, which are then used by HELM for evaluating LMs.

#### 3.2. Prompting Methods

The study evaluated LMs under five distinct prompting methods, categorized into baseline and structured prompting approaches:

- **HELM Baseline:** This method uses the fixed, zero-shot, hand-crafted prompt configuration commonly reported in HELM, without Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning. It serves as the primary reference point.
- **Zero-Shot Predict:** Implemented using DSPy's `dspy.Predict` module, this is an unoptimized, non-adaptive baseline. It uses the same HELM baseline instruction but within DSPy's standardized interface and without demonstrations (K=0).
- **Zero-Shot CoT:** Utilizing DSPy's `dspy.ChainOfThought` module, this method employs the same structure as Zero-Shot Predict but explicitly instructs the LM to generate a step-by-step reasoning trace before producing the final output.
- **BFRS (Bootstrap Few-Shot with Random Search):** This method bootstraps demonstrations by running the LM program on a subset of training inputs. Input-output pairs achieving a sufficiently high score are selected as candidate demonstrations. Subsequently, a random search samples sets of K demonstrations, inserts them into modules, and evaluates the program on a validation split, returning the highest-scoring configuration.
- **MIPROv2 (Instruction + Few-Shot Optimized):** This advanced optimizer extends BFRS by optimizing both instructions and few-shot demonstrations. It involves bootstrapping demonstrations, generating grounded instruction proposals from a proposer LM conditioned on dataset summaries and program structure, and employing Bayesian search over instruction-demo pairs to identify the best prompt configuration.

#### 3.3. Benchmarks

Seven publicly available HELM benchmarks were selected for their diversity in task type and domain coverage (general and medical):

- **MMLU-Pro:** Enhanced version of MMLU, focusing on challenging reasoning questions.
- **GPQA:** Graduate-level multiple-choice questions in biology, physics, and chemistry.
- **GSM8K:** Grade school math word problems for numeric problem-solving.
- **MedCalc-Bench:** Medical calculation tasks from patient notes to categorical values.
- **Medec:** Error detection and correction in medical narratives.
- **HeadQA:** Biomedical multiple-choice questions resembling board exams.
- **MedBullets:** USMLE-style multiple-choice questions.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Language Models:** Six LMs were evaluated: four frontier models (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0 Flash, GPT 4o, o3 Mini) and two open-source models (Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen3 4B).
- **Data Partitioning:** DSPy programs utilized a fixed train/validation split (default 90/10 with a consistent seed). Demonstrations (bootstrapped or labeled) were capped at K ≤ 3 per module.
- **Optimization Trials:** BFRS used 16 optimization trials, while MIPROv2 used 13. Neither optimizer had access to the HELM leaderboard test set.
- **Evaluation:** All final scoring was performed via HELM, ensuring consistent output judgment. Runs were single and deterministic (temperature = 0), matching HELM's standard setup.
- **Statistical Analysis:** To determine systematic improvements, an exact two-sided McNemar test was applied to paired correct/incorrect outcomes, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons. A pooled analysis indicated statistically significant improvements for all structured prompting methods over the baseline.
- **Generalizability Check:** An additional experiment with GEPA, a reflective prompt optimizer not based on DSPy, was conducted on MMLU-Pro to verify whether observed leaderboard sensitivity was specific to DSPy.
- **Controlled CoT Analysis:** To isolate CoT's effect, matched non-CoT variants were compared against CoT-based variants, holding instructions and demonstrations constant on MedCalc-Bench for GPT 4o and Qwen3 4B.
- **Computational Cost Analysis:** Inference-time token usage was quantified for each method by measuring additional prompt tokens relative to the HELM baseline. Prompt optimization was treated as a one-time amortized setup cost.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings concerning the impact of structured prompting on language model evaluation within the HELM framework:

- **Improved Performance:** Structured prompting consistently led to improved performance across the evaluated models. On average, accuracy increased by 6% over the HELM baseline when using the best-performing prompt configuration for each model. Models not inherently optimized for reasoning tasks, particularly smaller open-source models, exhibited the most substantial gains, while highly reasoning-capable models like o3 Mini showed comparatively smaller improvements.
- **Shifted Leaderboard Rankings:** The choice of prompt materially altered relative model rankings. Leaderboard positions shifted on 5 out of 7 benchmarks when comparing results from the HELM baseline to those from the best-performing structured prompt. For example, on MMLU-Pro, o3 Mini, initially outperforming Claude 3.7 Sonnet (77.1% vs. 76.3%), was subsequently surpassed by Claude 3.7 Sonnet (80.6% vs. 78.4%) under optimal prompting. Similar shifts were observed on GSM8K and MedCalc-Bench.
- **Altered Performance Gaps:** Structured prompting impacted the performance gaps between models. Averaged across benchmarks, the gap between the top two models (o3 Mini and Claude 3.7 Sonnet) narrowed from 6% to 3%. However, this trend was not uniform; on GPQA, the gap widened significantly from 0.6% to 4.3%.
- **Benchmark and Model-Dependent Sensitivity:** Sensitivity to prompting methods varied by benchmark and model. Reasoning tasks (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, GSM8K, MedCalc-Bench, MedBullets) showed larger performance gains, while HeadQA and Medec exhibited smaller improvements, possibly due to baseline score saturation or knowledge base limitations, respectively. Smaller open-source models consistently demonstrated larger performance gains. Notably, Qwen3 4B, an 18x smaller model than Llama 3.3 70B, achieved slightly higher scores on MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, and MedCalc-Bench when evaluated with its best-performing structured prompt.
- **Dominance of Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** The most significant performance improvements were attributed to the introduction of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning. Moving from Zero-Shot Predict to Zero-Shot CoT resulted in statistically significant gains (61.4% to 66.2% mean accuracy across models and benchmarks). Once CoT was integrated, further application of more sophisticated prompt optimizers like BFRS and MIPROv2 provided only marginal additional benefits (66.2% to 66.3%), suggesting that CoT largely captures the value of structured prompting. A controlled analysis isolating CoT's effect on MedCalc-Bench for Qwen3 4B and GPT 4o demonstrated that CoT-based variants consistently yielded higher and more stable performance compared to non-CoT variants, even with similar prompt changes.
- **Generalizability of Findings:** An evaluation using GEPA, a non-DSPy reflective prompt optimizer, on MMLU-Pro also showed performance improvements over the baseline for most models. This indicates that the observed shifts in leaderboard outcomes are a general phenomenon related to prompt choice, not merely an artifact of the DSPy framework.
- **Computational Cost-Effectiveness:** An analysis of inference-time token usage revealed that Zero-Shot CoT achieved most of the performance gains with minimal additional prompt tokens (164 tokens overhead relative to baseline). In contrast, few-shot optimizers like BFRS (1,741 additional tokens) and MIPROv2 (1,816 additional tokens) incurred substantially higher token costs due to the inclusion of task-specific demonstrations. This positions Zero-Shot CoT as the most cost-effective structured prompting method for performance enhancement in this study.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study provides empirical evidence demonstrating that prompt choice is a critical factor in the evaluation of language models, materially altering reported performance and relative rankings on established benchmarks. This finding has several significant implications:

- **Reassessment of Benchmarking Practices:** The work challenges the widely adopted practice of evaluating LMs with single, static prompt configurations. It suggests that such limited evaluation protocols may provide an incomplete and potentially misleading picture of an LM's true capabilities. This calls for a re-evaluation of current benchmarking methodologies to incorporate a broader range of prompting strategies.
- **Enhanced LM Evaluation Robustness:** By showing that evaluating models across a "family of prompt configurations" can yield different and often higher performance, the study advocates for more robust and comprehensive evaluation frameworks. Future public leaderboards could benefit from reporting performance under multiple standardized prompting strategies, allowing practitioners to understand the dependence of conclusions on prompt design.
- **Improved Deployment Decisions:** The observed shifts in rankings and performance gaps, especially for smaller or non-reasoning models, indicate that optimal prompting can significantly enhance an LM's utility. This has a direct impact on deployment decisions, as it suggests that models that appear less capable under static prompts might be highly competitive and cost-effective when deployed with appropriate structured prompting. For instance, the notable improvement of Qwen3 4B suggests that smaller models might be under-recognized without proper prompt engineering.
- **Guidance for Prompt Engineering and Optimization:** The finding that Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning accounts for the majority of performance gains, with diminishing returns from more complex optimizers once CoT is introduced, offers practical guidance for prompt engineering efforts. It suggests that integrating CoT should be a priority, as it offers a substantial and cost-effective pathway to improved performance without necessarily requiring extensive and token-expensive few-shot optimization.
- **Open-Source Contributions and Reproducibility:** The open-sourcing of the DSPy+HELM Evaluation framework and the Prompt Optimization Pipeline contributes to research transparency and reproducibility. This allows other researchers to build upon this work, explore a wider range of models and benchmarks, and further investigate prompt sensitivity.

The broader impact considerations acknowledge potential challenges, such as the incentive for "leaderboard gaming" if prompt families and search budgets are not standardized. Furthermore, the use of high-stakes framing in some medical prompts, while consistent for benchmarking, is explicitly noted as not being intended for clinical use, highlighting the importance of careful application of prompt design in sensitive domains. Overall, this research underscores the necessity of moving towards a more dynamic and comprehensive approach to LM evaluation to accurately assess capabilities and facilitate informed deployment.
