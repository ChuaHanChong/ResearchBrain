# HumanoidArena: Benchmarking Egocentric Hierarchical Whole-body Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> HUMANOIDARENA is a new simulation benchmark that evaluates egocentric hierarchical whole-body learning for humanoid robots, emphasizing complex "leg-critical" interaction tasks. It reveals that learned policies struggle with cross-General Motion Tracker (GMT) transfer and are sensitive to visual perturbations, experiencing significant performance drops in deployment across different low-level execution backends.

## Problem

- Developing scalable policies for humanoid robots that seamlessly integrate high-level task decision-making with low-level whole-body dynamic execution, especially when the lower body is structurally involved.
- Existing benchmarks often evaluate end-to-end control stacks or fixed action representations, hindering the isolation of high-level policy learning from low-level execution and the assessment of the policy-tracker interface.
- A lack of controlled settings to study and benchmark 'leg-critical' interactions that demand precise foot placement, balance maintenance, and whole-body reorientation, beyond simple locomotion or manipulation.

## Method

![[2606.17833-method.png]]
*The HUMANOIDARENA benchmark framework, showcasing the hierarchical control structure where a high-level policy predicts whole-body actions that are executed by a bank of low-level motion trackers.*

- Introduced HUMANOIDARENA, a simulation-first benchmark built on Isaac Lab, which explicitly decouples high-level, perception-conditioned policies from low-level ==General Motion Trackers (GMTs)==.
- Developed seven 'leg-critical' egocentric Human-Object Interaction (HOI) and Human-Scene Interaction (HSI) tasks, requiring coordinated whole-body movements and collected via VR-based teleoperation into a canonical 40D intermediate action space.
- Defined controlled evaluation protocols for robustness to visual, semantic, and execution perturbations, and for cross-GMT transferability, using metrics like Success Rate, Average Fall Rate, and transfer retention.

## Results

- In-GMT evaluation showed Flow Matching (FM) achieved the best average success rates on HOI (**36.11%**) and HSI (**58.75%**) with TWIST2, while Diffusion Policy (DP) performed best with SONIC (**52.22%** on HOI, **65.83%** on HSI).
- Perturbation-conditioned evaluations revealed performance drops across all baselines, with visual perturbations causing consistent decreases, for example, ACT's HOI success rate with TWIST2 dropping from **28.33%** to **14.44%**.
- Cross-GMT tests demonstrated average success rate drops of **39.9%** when policies trained with TWIST2 were deployed with SONIC, and **36.0%** for the reverse, with relative transfer retention often reaching **0.0%** for many tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The performance of hierarchical whole-body policies is significantly a property of the policy-tracker pair, with certain GMTs enabling higher success rates than others (e.g., SONIC generally outperformed TWIST2).
> - Policies exhibit varied robustness; visual perturbations consistently caused the most substantial performance degradation, highlighting sensitivity to egocentric appearance shifts and the need for robust perception.
> - There are significant and asymmetric failures in cross-GMT transfer, indicating that high-level policies tend to overfit to the motion priors and dynamics of the GMT used during training, rather than learning truly tracker-invariant intermediate actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of embodied intelligent agents, particularly humanoid robots, capable of perceiving, reasoning, and acting in complex physical environments, is a significant objective in artificial intelligence. Humanoid robots are considered a natural fit for human-centered environments due to their morphology aligning with human-designed spaces and objects. This alignment is particularly relevant in interaction tasks that necessitate coordinated whole-body movement beyond mere locomotion or isolated manipulation. Prior research has explored proprioceptive motion tracking and perception-driven interaction, utilizing reinforcement learning and expanding into underactuated object interaction. While some approaches rely on external sensors, egocentric perception has gained prominence for human-object interaction (HOI) and human-scene interaction (HSI), enabling robots to navigate diverse terrains and interact with objects.

A challenge in this field is the scalable learning of policies that seamlessly integrate high-level task decision-making with low-level whole-body dynamic execution, especially when the lower body is structurally involved in task completion, not just for transport. Hierarchical control systems have emerged as a solution, where a high-level policy predicts intermediate whole-body actions and low-level general motion trackers (GMTs) execute them as stable humanoid motion. Existing hierarchical systems, such as TWIST2 and SONIC, demonstrate advancements in decoupling visual-semantic decision-making from high-frequency whole-body stabilization.

However, the existing research landscape, including benchmarks like LIBERO, BEHAVIOR-1K, HumanoidBench, LeVERB, HumanoidVerse, and Humanoid Everyday, often falls short in providing a controlled setting for studying hierarchical humanoid whole-body learning. Current benchmarks frequently evaluate end-to-end control stacks or fixed action representations, making it difficult to isolate high-level policy learning from low-level execution. They also tend to emphasize locomotion, manipulation, or specific task diversity without specifically centering "leg-critical" interactions where lower-body coordination is structurally required. Furthermore, existing evaluation protocols rarely disentangle visual perception, semantic grounding, spatial execution, and tracker-induced deployment shifts. HUMANOIDARENA addresses these limitations by introducing a benchmark specifically designed for egocentric hierarchical whole-body learning, focusing on the policy-tracker interface and leg-critical tasks with robust evaluation protocols.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of HUMANOIDARENA is to establish a simulation-first benchmark for egocentric hierarchical whole-body learning in humanoid robots, specifically addressing the gap in evaluating the policy–tracker interface and leg-critical interactions. The motivation stems from several interconnected research challenges:

1. **Scalable Policy Learning for Whole-Body Interaction:** Humanoid robots are expected to perform complex, goal-directed interactions that require coordinated whole-body movements. This involves a tension between high-level task intent and low-level dynamic realization. Hierarchical control offers a solution by abstracting intent, but there is a need to understand how well high-level policies generalize and transfer across different execution layers.

2. **Evaluating the Policy–Tracker Interface:** Existing benchmarks often treat the low-level execution layer as a fixed component, making it difficult to assess the executability, robustness, and transferability of intermediate whole-body actions predicted by high-level policies. The authors aim to explicitly benchmark this interface to understand how policies and trackers interact.

3. **Emphasis on Leg-Critical Interactions:** Many current benchmarks either simplify mobility to navigation actions or focus on fixed-arm manipulation. Humanoid tasks in human-centered environments often demand the lower body to be intrinsically involved in task completion, requiring precise foot placement, balance maintenance, posture adjustment, and whole-body reorientation. The benchmark is motivated to evaluate policies in such "leg-critical" scenarios.

4. **Controlled Evaluation of Robustness and Transferability:** A major motivation is to disentangle various sources of failure and success. Existing evaluations can obscure whether a policy has learned truly robust hierarchical whole-body behavior. HUMANOIDARENA seeks to provide controlled protocols to assess robustness to visual, semantic, and execution perturbations, as well as the transferability of learned policies across different GMT backends.

Specifically, the research aims to investigate four key topics:
- **(T1) Goal-directed hierarchical humanoid interaction:** By providing a hierarchical setting for leg-critical HOI/HSI tasks that demand coordinated whole-body actions from egocentric observations and instructions.
- **(T2) Robust policy learning with egocentric perception:** Through controlled generalization dimensions (visual, semantic, execution) to test policy robustness against appearance shifts, distractors, and spatial variations.
- **(T3) Cross-GMT generalization in hierarchical control:** By enabling policies trained with one tracker to be deployed with another, evaluating the transferability and tracker-invariance of intermediate actions.
- **(T4) Multimodal coordination under whole-body motion:** By studying how policies fuse egocentric vision and proprioception under drastic viewpoint changes caused by dynamic whole-body movements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HUMANOIDARENA adopts a simulation-first approach, built on the Isaac Lab platform, to create a benchmark for egocentric hierarchical whole-body learning.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Whole-Body Protocol

The benchmark explicitly decouples high-level, perception-conditioned decision-making from low-level dynamic execution.
- **High-level Policy ($\pi_\theta$):** At each time step, the policy receives an egocentric visual observation ($I_{ego_t}$), a 64D proprioceptive state ($p_t$), and a task instruction ($\ell$). It then predicts a 40D intermediate whole-body action ($u_t$). The proprioceptive state includes root orientation (6D), 29D G1 joint positions, and their velocities. The 40D action specifies root movement in the horizontal plane (2D), root height (1D), root orientation (6D), 29D body joint targets, and binary open/close commands for both hands (2D). This compact action representation focuses on task-level intent rather than low-level torques, delegating dynamic feasibility to the GMTs.
- **Low-level General Motion Tracker (GMT) Bank:** A set of GMTs executes the intermediate action. Each GMT ($G^{(m)}$) has a specific adapter ($\psi_m$) that maps the canonical 40D action ($u_t$) into its required reference format, producing dynamically feasible G1 joint-position targets ($q^{(m)}_{t+1}$). The benchmark includes two representative GMTs: TWIST2 and SONIC.

#### 3.2. Data Collection Pipeline

Demonstrations are collected in Isaac Lab using a VR-based egocentric teleoperation system.
- **Teleoperation:** An operator, wearing a PICO headset, observes the humanoid's egocentric visual stream and controls the robot in a closed loop via General Motion Retargeting (GMR).
- **Action Canonicalization:** The human motion captured by GMR is retargeted into a 35D robot-space reference signal. This signal is then canonicalized into the 40D policy-facing intermediate whole-body action and executed by a selected GMT.
- **Multi-GMT Data:** By switching the GMT backend (TWIST2 or SONIC) under the same teleoperation and action interface, multi-GMT datasets are generated. These datasets share the same policy-facing action semantics but exhibit different low-level tracking dynamics.
- **Dataset Size:** Each task provides 100 successful demonstration episodes per GMT backend, totaling 200 episodes per task and 1,400 episodes across all seven tasks. Episodes contain 50Hz synchronized egocentric observations, 64D canonical state, and 40D canonical action.

#### 3.3. Leg-Critical Egocentric Task Suites

The benchmark includes seven tasks categorized as Human-Object Interaction (HOI) and Human-Scene Interaction (HSI), all requiring significant lower-body coordination.
- **HOI Tasks:**
 - **Football:** Kicking a ball into a goal, requiring visual localization, stance adjustment, balance on one leg, and coordinated leg swing.
 - **DoubleDesk:** Transferring an object between desks with a height discontinuity, necessitating walking while carrying an object and coordinating locomotion with grasp stability.
 - **P&PBox:** Picking up a box and placing it on a high shelf, demanding walking, posture adjustment (leg bending), and arm reach coordination.
- **HSI Tasks:**
 - **OpenDoor:** Opening an articulated door and passing through, requiring coordinated base placement, torso orientation, arm contact, and balance recovery under contact forces.
 - **SitSofa:** Navigating to and sitting on a sofa, involving obstacle avoidance, body alignment, and a controlled sitting transition with leg flexion.
 - **Boxing:** Striking a target on a punching bag at randomized heights, requiring crouching or reaching with whole-body posture adjustment for balance.
 - **VisNavi:** Visually navigating through obstacles to a target zone, emphasizing egocentric visual navigation, lower-body steering, and precise foot placement.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocols

The benchmark defines controlled evaluation protocols to diagnose policy generalization and compatibility.
- **Perturbation-Conditioned Evaluation:**
 - **Visual Generalization:** Changes lighting direction, pose, intensity, and color (DistantLight vs. DomeLight) to test robustness to egocentric appearance shifts.
 - **Semantic Generalization:** Introduces semantically similar distractors, substitutes asset appearances/geometries, or adds scene clutter, evaluating target grounding and robustness to visual variation.
 - **Execution Generalization:** Expands the initialization range of task-relevant objects, requiring policies to adapt to new spatial conditions for foot placement and body pose.
- **GMT-Conditioned Evaluation:**
 - **In-GMT Protocol:** Training and inference use the same GMT, measuring performance under matched tracking dynamics.
 - **Cross-GMT Protocol:** Policies are trained with one GMT (e.g., TWIST2) and deployed with another (e.g., SONIC), assessing whether intermediate actions encode tracker-transferable intent or overfit to a specific GMT's dynamics.
- **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) is the primary metric. Average Fall Rate (AFR) assesses stability. For cross-GMT, absolute drop (∆cross) and relative transfer retention (Ret) are also reported. Each configuration is evaluated with 3 random seeds and 20 rollout trials per seed (60 rollouts total).

#### 3.5. Policy Baselines

Four representative visuomotor policy families are benchmarked: ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), Flow Matching (FM), and π0.5. All baselines share the same observation space, 40D action interface, and GMT backends to ensure that performance differences primarily reflect policy design.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted within HUMANOIDARENA provide insights into the capabilities and limitations of existing hierarchical whole-body learning policies.

#### 4.1. In-GMT Evaluation (Table 1)

- **Feasibility and Variability:** Learned policies can complete diverse leg-critical HOI/HSI tasks using GMT-based execution. However, performance varies considerably across different policy architectures and GMT backends.
- **Tracker-Conditioned Performance:** The choice of GMT significantly influences policy performance. For instance, Flow Matching (FM) achieved the best average success rates on HOI (36.11%) and HSI (58.75%) when paired with TWIST2, while Diffusion Policy (DP) performed best with SONIC, achieving 52.22% on HOI and 65.83% on HSI. This indicates that the performance is a property of the policy-tracker pair, not solely the high-level policy. SONIC generally enabled higher success rates than TWIST2.
- **Stability:** The $\pi_{0.5}$ policy demonstrated the lowest average fall rate (AFR) under both TWIST2 (3.33%) and SONIC (5.24%), suggesting it is the most stable baseline, which is an important consideration for humanoid interaction.

#### 4.2. Perturbation-Conditioned Evaluation (Figure 3, Table S7)

- **Performance Degradation:** Policies generally experience a decrease in success rate under visual, semantic, and execution perturbations compared to in-distribution performance.
- **Visual Sensitivity:** Visual perturbations resulted in the most consistent performance drops, particularly for HOI tasks. For example, ACT's HOI average success rate with TWIST2 dropped from 28.33% to 14.44% under visual perturbation.
- **Varied Failure Modes:** The three perturbation axes (visual, semantic, execution) exposed distinct failure modes, indicating that robustness is not a monolithic concept. Policies need to be robust to egocentric appearance changes, correct target grounding, and adaptation to spatial variations. Diffusion Policy (DP) exhibited comparatively more consistent robustness across perturbation splits when used with SONIC.

#### 4.3. Cross-GMT Evaluation (Table 2, Figures S8, S9)

- **Substantial and Asymmetric Transfer Failures:** Replacing the deployment GMT caused significant performance degradation in both training-to-deployment directions (TWIST2→SONIC and SONIC→TWIST2). The average success rate drop was slightly larger for TWIST2→SONIC (39.9%) compared to SONIC→TWIST2 (36.0%).
- **Low Retention:** Relative transfer retention values were consistently low, with many tasks showing 0.0% retention in the TWIST2→SONIC direction. This indicates that policies do not inherently learn tracker-invariant intermediate actions and often overfit to the motion priors and behaviors of the GMT used during training.
- **Stability Differences:** SONIC→TWIST2 deployments showed a higher average fall rate (7.56%) compared to TWIST2→SONIC (1.36%), suggesting that policies trained with SONIC might rely on its specific stabilization characteristics, leading to instability when executed by TWIST2.
- **Qualitative Evidence of Specialization:** t-SNE visualizations of policy-predicted actions (Figures S8, S9) revealed that policies conditioned on observations from different GMTs often generate distinct action distributions. This supports the conclusion that high-level policies tend to specialize to the dynamics and correction behaviors of the demonstration tracker rather than learning fully tracker-invariant representations.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Results (Figure 2, S10)

- **Coherent Whole-Body Actions:** Qualitative rollouts demonstrated that learned policies can generate temporally coherent intermediate whole-body actions, which GMTs successfully execute into stable humanoid motions. Examples included a robot kicking a ball, navigating and sitting on a sofa, and transferring an object across desks.
- **Recoverable Failures:** The analysis of failure cases (e.g., in Football, Figure S10) showed that policies can exhibit closed-loop recovery. After an initial unsuccessful kick, the robot re-localized the ball, re-aligned, and made a second successful attempt, indicating task intent preservation and conditional behavior rather than fixed trajectory replay.

#### 4.5. Merge-All Evaluation (Table S8)

- **Dataset Transfer Potential:** Training policies with merged datasets (e.g., all 7 tasks for a single GMT) showed that the canonical action interface can facilitate some degree of dataset transfer and improve performance in certain settings (e.g., DP on TWIST2 HSI tasks improved from 55.83% to 73.33%).
- **GMT-Conditioned Mismatch:** However, gains were not uniform, and some policies experienced performance drops with merged training across GMTs. This suggests that while the 40D action space is shared, the distinct tracking capabilities, motion priors, and contact responses of different GMTs lead to GMT-specific action distributions, impacting the effectiveness of multi-GMT policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HUMANOIDARENA establishes a dedicated and structured benchmark that addresses several critical needs in the field of embodied intelligence and humanoid robotics. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

1. **Advancing Hierarchical Whole-Body Learning:** The benchmark provides a controlled environment to study and advance hierarchical control for humanoid robots. By explicitly separating high-level policy learning from low-level motion execution and defining a shared intermediate action interface, it allows researchers to isolate and investigate challenges related to perception, decision-making, and dynamic realization. This contributes to the development of more scalable and effective humanoid control systems.

2. **Focus on Leg-Critical Interactions:** By emphasizing tasks where lower-body coordination is essential for success, HUMANOIDARENA pushes the boundaries beyond conventional locomotion or fixed-arm manipulation benchmarks. This highlights the importance of integrated whole-body motion, foot placement, balance, and posture adjustment in complex human-centered environments, which is crucial for truly capable humanoid robots.

3. **Diagnosis of Policy Robustness and Generalization:** The introduction of perturbation-conditioned evaluation (visual, semantic, execution) offers a systematic framework for diagnosing policy robustness to various forms of shifts. This enables a more granular understanding of where policies succeed or fail (e.g., perception, grounding, or spatial adaptation), guiding the development of more robust vision-language-action (VLA) models and imitation learning policies.

4. **Quantifying Cross-GMT Transferability:** A key contribution is the ability to explicitly measure cross-GMT transfer, revealing whether learned intermediate actions encode tracker-invariant intent or overfit to specific low-level dynamics. The findings, indicating substantial and asymmetric transfer failures, highlight a fundamental open challenge: designing action representations and learning methods that generalize across different execution backends. This directly motivates future research into more robust and portable whole-body action representations.

5. **Standardized Evaluation and Reproducibility:** By providing a simulation-first benchmark with well-defined tasks, evaluation protocols, observation/action spaces, and baseline implementations, HUMANOIDARENA offers a common testbed for comparing different approaches. The availability of code and data ensures reproducibility and facilitates collaborative research, accelerating progress in the field.

6. **Informing Future Humanoid Design:** The insights gained from HUMANOIDARENA can inform the design of both future humanoid robots and their control architectures. Understanding the limitations of current hierarchical systems, particularly concerning tracker compatibility and generalization, can lead to the development of more synergistic policy-tracker interfaces and more adaptable low-level controllers.

In summary, HUMANOIDARENA is positioned to serve as a critical tool for driving advancements in egocentric hierarchical whole-body learning, fostering the development of humanoid robots capable of robustly performing complex, integrated interactions in diverse environments.
