# DISK: Learning local features with policy gradient

> [!summary] Summary
> DISK introduces an end-to-end learning framework for local features, leveraging policy gradient reinforcement learning to address the inherent discreteness of keypoint selection and matching. This approach consistently achieved top performance on standard image matching and 3D reconstruction benchmarks, demonstrating robust feature generation for geometric computer vision tasks.

## Problem

- Learning local features end-to-end for computer vision tasks is challenging due to the discrete nature of keypoint selection and matching, which hinders gradient-based optimization.
- Traditional hand-crafted features often remained competitive or superior to many learned methods for sparse 3D reconstruction tasks like Structure-from-Motion.
- Prior reinforcement learning approaches for local features suffered from weak and high-variance training signals, making them difficult to train effectively from scratch.

## Method

- A ==probabilistic framework== models both feature extraction (keypoint sampling and acceptance) and ==relaxed cycle-consistent matching==, enabling discrete operations within a differentiable learning setup.
- **DISK** uses a ==policy gradient== method to optimize for a ==reward function== based on the geometric correctness of feature matches, incorporating both true positive rewards and penalties for false positives and excessive keypoints.
- ==Exact gradients== are computed for the matching step, significantly reducing variance in the ==reinforcement learning== signal and allowing for stable training from scratch.

## Results

- DISK secured the **#1** position in the Image Matching Challenge 2020 for the 2k-keypoints category, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance in both stereo and multiview tasks, with **9.4%** and **6.7%** relative improvements in mAA respectively.
- The method achieved state-of-the-art results on the HPatches benchmark, outperforming its closest competitor by **12%** relative in Mean Matching Accuracy (MMA), particularly excelling in viewpoint change robustness.
- It generated significantly more RANSAC inliers and 3D landmarks with longer track lengths compared to other methods, confirming its ability to produce highly effective features for large-scale 3D reconstruction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement learning, specifically policy gradient with analytically derived gradients for the matching step, effectively bridges the gap between discrete feature operations and end-to-end deep learning.
> - It is possible to learn dense, discriminative local features from scratch that are highly effective for geometric computer vision tasks, even in image regions traditionally considered textureless.
> - The framework's flexibility allows adaptation to different types of supervision (e.g., pixel-to-pixel depth or epipolar constraints), influencing the characteristics of learned features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Local features have been a cornerstone of computer vision for decades, with Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) being a seminal example. These features enable critical applications such as Structure-from-Motion (SfM), Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), and re-localization. Historically, methods like SIFT and SURF relied on hand-crafted heuristics for keypoint detection, orientation estimation, and descriptor computation.

The advent of deep learning has revolutionized many computer vision tasks, but 3D reconstruction, particularly sparse methods, has remained a domain where hand-crafted features often stay competitive or even superior to their dense, learned counterparts. This is primarily due to the inherent difficulty of learning local features in a fully end-to-end, differentiable manner. The challenges stem from several factors:
1. **Discreteness:** Keypoint selection and matching are inherently discrete processes. Keypoints are specific pixel locations, and matches are binary (either a match or not). These discrete operations are not easily amenable to standard gradient-based optimization in deep learning.
2. **Computational Complexity:** Matching features between two images involves a combinatorial explosion (O(|F_A| * |F_B|)) if every pixel could be a feature. This makes direct end-to-end optimization intractable.
3. **Context-Dependency:** The "quality" of a feature is not isolated; it depends on its distinctiveness relative to other features in the image, making it hard to define a simple, local differentiable objective.

Earlier learning-based approaches typically addressed only parts of the local feature pipeline. Some learned descriptors on pre-defined keypoints (e.g., L2-Net, HardNet), while others focused on learning keypoints or orientations. More recent efforts attempted end-to-end pipelines, but often resorted to inexact approximations, broke differentiability, or made strong simplifying assumptions. For instance, SuperPoint uses self-supervision and a grid-based approach but still involves discrete steps that are approximated. D2-Net focuses on joint detection and description but relies on assumptions about descriptor extrema.

DISK directly tackles the challenge of end-to-end learning by introducing a novel approach that bridges the gap between discrete operations at inference time and differentiable optimization during training. It leverages **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, specifically **policy gradient methods**, to optimize for a high number of *correct* feature matches. This places DISK in a category of methods attempting to use RL for geometric computer vision tasks, similar to GLAMpoints or Reinforced Feature Points (RFP). However, DISK distinguishes itself by:
- **Learning both detectors and descriptors end-to-end:** Unlike GLAMpoints or which use hand-crafted descriptors, DISK learns the entire feature representation.
- **Tackling the "weak training signal" problem of RL:** Reinforced Feature Points suffer from weak signals when supervising all detection/matching decisions with a single reward. DISK addresses this through a careful probabilistic model that allows for analytical gradient computation for the matching step, significantly reducing variance and enabling training from scratch. This is a critical differentiation, allowing for more robust and effective learning.

By offering a robust end-to-end learning framework that directly optimizes a match-quality objective using RL, DISK pushes the boundaries of learned local features, aiming to outperform hybrid or partially learned methods, especially on downstream tasks where precise feature matching is paramount.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of DISK is to develop a novel method for learning local features that can be trained **end-to-end**, directly optimizing for the quality of feature matches in a downstream task. The authors explicitly state their motivation to overcome the difficulties associated with learning local feature frameworks, particularly the inherent **discreteness** in keypoint selection and matching, which makes traditional gradient-based deep learning challenging.

Specifically, the key motivations and objectives are:
- **Overcoming discreteness with Reinforcement Learning:** The core motivation is to employ principles from Reinforcement Learning (RL) and policy gradient to handle the non-differentiable, discrete nature of sparse keypoint selection and matching. This allows the model to learn a "policy" for choosing and matching keypoints that maximizes a given reward.
- **Optimizing for correct feature matches:** The ultimate goal is to generate features that lead to a high number of *correct* feature matches between images. This directly correlates with better performance in downstream tasks like SfM and camera pose estimation.
- **Bridging the gap between training and inference:** Many learned feature methods either use inexact approximations during training that deviate from inference, or break differentiability. DISK aims to keep the training and inference regimes "close" while maintaining good convergence properties.
- **Enabling training from scratch:** A significant challenge for RL-based methods is unstable training. DISK aims to develop a formulation that is robust enough to reliably train local features from scratch, without relying on pre-trained models or extensive fine-tuning.
- **Achieving state-of-the-art performance:** The overarching objective is to deliver state-of-the-art results on standard benchmarks, demonstrating that the proposed RL-based approach can yield features superior to existing hand-crafted and learned methods.
- **Challenging assumptions about "good keypoints":** The authors observe that their features can be extracted very densely and discriminatively, even in seemingly textureless regions where traditional methods like SIFT fail. This suggests a motivation to explore and validate a broader definition of useful keypoints.

In essence, DISK seeks to develop a principled, end-to-end learning framework that harnesses the power of CNNs and RL to produce highly effective local features, directly optimized for the practical goal of robust feature matching in geometric vision tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DISK (DIScrete Keypoints) addresses the challenges of end-to-end local feature learning by reformulating feature extraction and matching within a probabilistic framework, optimizing using a policy gradient method.

#### 3.1. Probabilistic Formulation

The core idea is to treat both feature extraction and matching as probabilistic processes.
- **Feature Distribution P(F_I | I, θ_F):** A network learns to output a set of features F_I from an image I, governed by parameters θ_F.
- **Match Distribution P(M_A↔B | F_A, F_B, θ_M):** Given feature sets F_A and F_B, matches M_A↔B are generated probabilistically, controlled by matching parameters θ_M.
The goal is to maximize the expected reward of matches, E[R(M_A↔B)], using gradient ascent.

#### 3.2. Feature Extraction Network

- **Backbone:** A U-Net architecture is used, modified with 4 down- and up-blocks, single convolutional layers (5x5 kernels), instance normalization, and PReLU non-linearities. This compact network comprises 1.1M parameters.
- **Outputs:** The network outputs two feature maps:
 - **K (Detection Map):** One channel for keypoint detection.
 - **D (Descriptor Map):** N=128 channels for descriptors (for comparison with SIFT).
- **Probabilistic Keypoint Sampling:** To select discrete keypoints from the continuous detection map K, a grid-based approach (h x h cells, similar to SuperPoint) is employed. For each grid cell *u*:
 - A pixel *p* within the cell is sampled with probability P_s(p|K_u) = softmax(K_u)_p. This models relative preference.
 - The sampled pixel *p* is accepted as a keypoint with probability P_a(accept_p|K_u) = σ(K_u)_p. This models absolute quality.
 - The total probability of sampling a feature at pixel *p* in cell *u* is P(p|K_u) = softmax(K_u)_p * σ(K_u)_p.
- **Descriptor Association:** Once keypoint locations are sampled, their l2-normalized descriptors are extracted from map D at those locations.
- **Inference:** At inference, softmax is replaced with arg max for feature selection within a cell, and σ with the sign function. The paper also discusses replacing the 1-per-cell sampling with Non-Maxima Suppression (NMS) for denser feature extraction at inference time.

#### 3.3. Match Distribution

- **Relaxed Cycle-Consistent Matching:** Instead of traditional hard cycle-consistency or ratio tests (which are non-differentiable), DISK relaxes this.
 - L2 distances between descriptors of F_A and F_B form a distance matrix *d*.
 - Forward matches (A → B) are drawn from categorical distributions defined by rows of *d*: P_A→B(j|d, i) = softmax(−θ_M d(i, ·))_j, where θ_M is an inverse softmax temperature.
 - Reverse matches (B → A) are defined analogously using d^T.
 - A match (i ↔ j) is declared if both the forward (i → j) and reverse (j → i) samples are consistent.
- **Exact Gradients for Matching:** Crucially, given F_A and F_B, the probability of any particular match P(i ↔ j) can be computed exactly. This means the matching step itself contributes no variance to gradient estimation, a key advantage over methods like RFP.

#### 3.4. Reward Function R(M_A↔B)

- The total reward is a sum of individual match rewards: R(M_A↔B) = Σ r(i ↔ j).
- **Ground Truth:** Utilizes geometric ground truth (COLMAP-derived poses and depth maps) to define match correctness.
 - **Correct Match (λ_tp):** If depth is available at both keypoints, and their reprojected distance is below a threshold (ε pixels). Rewards λ_tp (set to 1).
 - **Incorrect Match (λ_fp):** In all other cases where depth is available and reprojected distance is above ε. Penalizes λ_fp (set to -0.25).
 - **Plausible Match:** If depth is unavailable but epipolar distance is below ε. Neither rewarded nor penalized.
- **Keypoint Regularization (λ_kp):** A small penalty (λ_kp = -0.001) is applied to each sampled keypoint to discourage an excessive number of unmatchable features, improving efficiency.
- **Annealing:** λ_fp and λ_kp are annealed from 0 to their full values over the first 5 epochs to stabilize early training.

#### 3.5. Gradient Estimator

- The method uses a basic policy gradient formulation. Given that R factorizes over matches and P(i ↔ j) can be computed exactly, the gradient ∇_θ E[R(M_A↔B)] can be derived.
- The gradient estimate involves an expectation over sampled F_A, F_B, and a sum over potential matches (Equation 1 in the paper). The crucial insight is that the matching part's gradients are exact, minimizing overall variance.
- **Supervision:** The feature extraction network is supervised only through the quality of matches, meaning keypoints that never match (e.g., non-co-visible) are considered neutral unless penalized by λ_kp.

#### 3.6. Training Details

- **Dataset:** Subset of MegaDepth (135 scenes, 63k images) with COLMAP-generated poses and depth. Image pairs are sampled based on a co-visibility heuristic (0.15 ≤ r ≤ 0.8 common 3D points).
- **Batching:** Three co-visible images (A, B, C) are processed per batch, yielding three image pairs (A↔B, A↔C, B↔C) to maximize match opportunities.
- **Optimization:** ADAM optimizer with a learning rate of 10^-4. θ_M (softmax temperature) is increased gradually with a fixed schedule.
- **Evaluation:** Best checkpoint selected based on mean Average Accuracy (mAA) for pose estimation in stereo on a validation set.

#### 3.7. Inference

- The probabilistic matching framework is discarded in favor of standard cycle-consistency checks and the ratio test.
- NMS is applied on the detection heatmap K to select features, rather than the grid-based sampling used in training. This offers denser, more accurate feature extraction, even if it introduces a slight misalignment with training.
- Features are subsampled by their "score" (value of K) to meet fixed feature budgets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DISK demonstrates state-of-the-art performance across multiple standard benchmarks, validating its novel policy gradient approach for end-to-end local feature learning.

#### 4.1. Image Matching Challenge (IMC) 2020

- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** DISK achieved #1 position in the 2k-keypoints category for both stereo and multiview tasks (using off-the-shelf matchers), outperforming all baselines and other user submissions by a significant margin.
 - **2k Features:** Outperformed all methods by 9.4% relative in stereo (mAA) and 6.7% relative in multiview (mAA). It produced substantially more RANSAC inliers (49.7% more) and 3D landmarks (30.1% more with 18.3% longer tracks) than the next best.
 - **8k Features:** Averaging stereo and multiview, DISK still outperformed all published baselines but placed slightly below the top three user submissions (which potentially used more complex matching pipelines).
- **Feature Density and Quality:** DISK consistently found significantly more matches (2-3x more RANSAC inliers or 3D landmarks) than other methods, demonstrating its ability to extract dense, discriminative features, even in regions where SIFT fails (e.g., Fig. 1).
- **Placement with Learned Matchers:** Even when compared against submissions that used advanced neural-network based matchers, DISK (with a built-in matcher) placed #2 overall in the 2k category, only below SuperGlue.
- **Rotation Invariance:** While generally robust, DISK, like other methods bypassing orientation detection, showed decreased performance under large in-plane rotations outside the range observed in the training data (Fig. 6). This suggests potential for improvement with data augmentation.

#### 4.2. HPatches

- **Overall SOTA:** DISK achieved state-of-the-art performance on this dataset, outperforming its closest competitor, Reinforced Feature Points (RFP), by 12% relative in Mean Matching Accuracy (MMA) summarized by AUC up to 5 pixels.
- **Viewpoint vs. Illumination:** It was #1 on viewpoint changes and #2 on illumination changes (trailing DELF), indicating its robustness to geometric variations.
- **Training Data Generalization:** Notably, these results were achieved with models trained on non-planar MegaDepth data, without specific tuning for HPatches, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities.

#### 4.3. ETH-COLMAP benchmark

- DISK produced more landmarks than SIFT and SuperPoint, with longer track lengths (more observations per landmark) and comparable reprojection errors, further validating its effectiveness in large-scale SfM reconstruction.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Match Supervision (Depth vs. Epipolar):**
 - Models trained with pixel-to-pixel depth supervision performed better for stereo pose estimation.
 - Models trained with weaker epipolar constraints performed slightly better for multiview tasks.
 - This demonstrates DISK's flexibility to learn effectively with different levels of supervision and its ability to generate features on different types of image regions (e.g., object boundaries with epipolar supervision, Fig. 7).
- **Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) vs. Grid-based Sampling:**
 - Applying NMS at inference time (instead of the 1-per-cell grid sampling used in training) significantly improved performance (e.g., +3.0% mAA for stereo, +2.6% for multiview on the validation set, Table 4). This highlights the benefit of a more flexible feature selection mechanism at inference, even if it slightly deviates from the training regime.
 - Smaller grid cell sizes during training generally led to better performance but increased the number of extracted features and computational cost (Table 5). An 8x8 grid with 5x5 NMS at inference achieved optimal results.
- **Feature Duplication:** The paper notes that grid-based sampling can lead to close-by or "duplicated" features (within 2px), potentially increasing memory and slightly impacting spatial precision. However, DISK's strong performance on benchmarks with 1-pixel thresholds suggests this is not a significant limitation in practice.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DISK represents a significant advancement in the field of local feature learning, primarily due to its novel and effective use of policy gradient reinforcement learning to train detectors and descriptors end-to-end. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Pioneering End-to-End Learning for Sparse Local Features:** DISK successfully overcomes the long-standing challenge of training sparse local feature frameworks in an end-to-end, differentiable manner. By reframing keypoint selection and matching as probabilistic processes and optimizing with policy gradient, it addresses the fundamental discreteness problem that has hindered deep learning's full potential in this domain. This opens new avenues for applying RL to other traditionally discrete problems in geometric computer vision.
- **Robust and Stable Training from Scratch:** A critical innovation is the careful probabilistic modeling that allows for **analytical gradients** for the matching step. This significantly reduces the variance typically associated with Monte Carlo approximations in RL, enabling DISK to train effectively from scratch without relying on pre-trained models or extensive fine-tuning. This robustness is a major advantage for practical research and deployment.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance on Downstream Tasks:** The core contribution translates into tangible performance gains. DISK achieves state-of-the-art results on challenging benchmarks like the Image Matching Challenge and HPatches, particularly in metrics directly relevant to downstream applications such as stereo pose estimation and 3D reconstruction (mAA, number of landmarks, track length). Its ability to generate a significantly higher number of *correct* matches highlights its practical utility.
- **Challenging Assumptions about Keypoint Salience:** By extracting dense and discriminative features even in seemingly textureless regions, DISK challenges traditional notions of what constitutes a "good keypoint." This could lead to more complete and robust 3D reconstructions, particularly in environments with limited texture or challenging lighting conditions.
- **Bridging Academic Rigor and Industrial Application:** The affiliations with EPFL and Google Research underscore the blend of theoretical innovation and practical applicability. The partial funding from Google's Visual Positioning System suggests direct interest in deploying such technology, hinting at its potential impact on areas like augmented reality, robotics, and large-scale mapping.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper explicitly mentions future work involving the replacement of its match relaxation with learned matchers like SuperGlue. This indicates that DISK's core framework for detection and description can be integrated with even more advanced matching techniques, potentially leading to even greater performance. The flexibility with different supervision signals (depth vs. epipolar) also provides a basis for adapting the method to diverse datasets and scenarios.

**Broader Impact and Ethical Considerations:**
The authors thoughtfully include a "Broader Impact" statement, acknowledging that while DISK can make existing keypoint-reliant applications more effective, it also carries potential ethical implications inherent in many powerful computer vision technologies:
- **Dual-Use Technology:** The ability to accurately localize and reconstruct environments with high precision could be misused in applications such as visually guided missiles.
- **Privacy Concerns:** Enhanced capabilities for localizing photographs without user consent could further compromise privacy on the web.
- **Economic Disparity:** The automation of data processing often disproportionately benefits established players with vast data and resources, potentially exacerbating global competitiveness imbalances, despite the open nature of the research.

In conclusion, DISK offers a principled and highly effective solution to a long-standing problem in computer vision. Its novel integration of policy gradient with a carefully designed probabilistic model enables robust, end-to-end learning of local features, setting a new benchmark for accuracy and opening up exciting avenues for future research and applications, while also prompting consideration of its societal implications.
