# Understanding Self-Supervised Learning via Latent Distribution Matching

> [!summary] Summary
> The Latent Distribution Matching (LDM) framework unifies diverse self-supervised learning algorithms, demonstrating how existing methods derive from it and enabling the design of new models, such as a Kalman-based predictive approach for uncertainty quantification. This framework further establishes formal identifiability guarantees for learned latent representations, proving their capacity to recover true underlying variables up to affine transformations.

## Problem

- A cohesive theoretical framework for self-supervised learning (SSL) was missing, hindering the understanding and systematic development of diverse SSL methods.
- Existing interpretations, such as geometric alignment and mutual information maximization, provided incomplete or insufficient explanations for the success of various SSL algorithms.
- There was a need to formally explain the empirical success of predictive SSL models and provide guarantees for the identifiability of their learned representations.

## Method

- Formulated self-supervised learning as a ==Latent Distribution Matching== (**LDM**) objective, minimizing the ==KL divergence== between an empirical latent distribution and an assumed latent model.
- Decomposed the **LDM** objective into alignment and entropy maximization terms, connecting it to maximum likelihood and encouraging encoder invertibility on the data manifold.
- Demonstrated that various existing SSL algorithms arise from specific choices of latent models and entropy estimation techniques within the LDM framework, and used it to derive a novel ==Kalman-based predictive SSL== model.

## Results

- Unified a wide range of existing SSL algorithms (e.g., SimCLR, VICReg, BYOL) by demonstrating their derivation as specific instances of the LDM framework based on choices of latent models and entropy estimators.
- Empirically confirmed that explicit Mutual Information maximization offers no systematic performance advantage over LDM, suggesting its success stems from implicit distribution matching and entropy maximization.
- Derived a novel Kalman-based predictive SSL model that achieved R^2=0.99 for latent variable recovery in synthetic data, quantified uncertainty in real-world time-series, and was theoretically proven to yield identifiable latent representations (up to affine transformations).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Latent Distribution Matching (LDM) serves as a unifying theoretical framework, explaining how diverse SSL methods are specific instantiations of matching empirical and assumed latent distributions.
> - Explicit mutual information (MI) maximization contributes little to representation quality beyond the entropy maximization inherent in LDM, with MI-based successes often stemming from implicit distribution matching.
> - Latent entropy maximization within LDM encourages the encoder to be approximately invertible on the data manifold, a property crucial for learning effective and identifiable representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a prominent paradigm for representation learning across diverse data modalities, including vision, language, and audio. Modern SSL methods often yield representations superior to traditional likelihood-based approaches like autoencoders, which tend to focus on low-level reconstruction and can overfit to superficial data details. Despite this empirical success, the field of SSL currently lacks a unifying theoretical framework that can systematically explain the mechanics of existing diverse methods and provide principled guidance for the design of new ones.

Prior attempts to understand SSL have included geometric alignment perspectives, which offer intuition but do not constitute a formal statistical foundation, particularly for non-contrastive methods. Another influential approach frames SSL through the lens of mutual information (MI) maximization. However, MI maximization faces a fundamental limitation: it is invariant under arbitrary invertible transformations, meaning it does not inherently promote semantically meaningful or identifiable representations. Empirical evidence has also indicated that MI maximization is neither necessary nor sufficient for successful SSL.

Formal guarantees for representation quality in SSL have been linked to the latent distribution matching (LDM) principle, which suggests that if a learned latent distribution matches that of the true underlying latent variables, these variables can be recovered up to trivial equivalences. However, the role of LDM as a unifying objective for the broad spectrum of SSL methods has not been extensively explored. Most existing identifiability results in SSL are typically derived for specific algorithms, underscoring the need for a more general theoretical framework. This work positions itself to address these gaps by proposing LDM as a foundational, unifying principle for SSL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research arises from the disconnect between the widespread empirical success of self-supervised learning (SSL) and the absence of a comprehensive theoretical framework to explain its mechanisms and guide its development. While SSL models consistently learn effective general-purpose latent representations from unlabeled data, their design often relies on heuristic choices and regularization strategies. This leads to an incomplete understanding of why certain methods work and limits the systematic innovation of new, more robust, or interpretable algorithms.

Specifically, the paper identifies several critical objectives:

- **To establish a unified distribution-matching framework for SSL:** The authors aim to cast the diverse array of existing SSL algorithms, including contrastive, non-contrastive, and predictive methods (e.g., those employing stop-gradients), as specific instances of a latent distribution matching (LDM) problem. This involves learning representations by matching the empirical latent data distribution to an assumed latent model. The motivation is to provide a common theoretical lens to understand the underlying principles and commonalities across various SSL approaches, demonstrating how their differences naturally emerge from distinct choices in latent models or entropy estimators.
- **To clarify the role of mutual information (MI) maximization in SSL:** Many SSL methods are motivated by or interpreted through the lens of maximizing mutual information between different views of data. This work aims to investigate whether MI maximization genuinely contributes to representation quality beyond simply maximizing latent entropy. The hypothesis is that MI maximization, due to its invariance to invertible transformations, offers limited benefits for representation quality in LDM contexts and that its empirical success might stem from implicitly performing distribution matching rather than promoting semantically meaningful features. Clarifying this role is crucial for avoiding unnecessary complexity in loss function design.
- **To derive new SSL objectives from the LDM framework:** The research seeks to demonstrate the generative power of the LDM framework by using it to derive novel SSL variants with beneficial properties. As a concrete example, the authors aim to derive a predictive SSL model that incorporates a Kalman-based latent dynamics predictor with a deep encoder. This specific objective is motivated by the potential to enable principled uncertainty quantification over latent states in high-dimensional time series, a capability often lacking in current predictive SSL methods that rely on less structured predictors.
- **To provide identifiability guarantees for predictive SSL models:** A significant motivation is to formally prove that predictive SSL models, when trained using LDM, can recover the true underlying latent variables up to affine transformations. This objective is crucial for providing a robust theoretical explanation for the observed strong empirical performance of predictive SSL and addresses the need for more general identifiability results that are not confined to specific algorithms. Such guarantees are vital for fostering trust in the learned representations, especially in scientific and engineering applications where interpretability is paramount.

In summary, the research is driven by a fundamental need to shift SSL development from a largely empirical endeavor to a more theoretically grounded discipline, offering clarity, principled design guidance, and formal guarantees for the learned representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research centers on formulating self-supervised learning (SSL) as a problem of **Latent Distribution Matching (LDM)**. This framework proposes that SSL objectives can be understood as optimizing representations to maximize their log-probability under an assumed latent model (alignment) while simultaneously maximizing their entropy to prevent collapse (uniformity).

**Core LDM Formulation:**
The mathematical foundation of LDM is rooted in minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between an empirical latent distribution, $R(z, z')$, and an assumed latent model distribution, $P_\theta(z, z')$. The primary objective function, $F_{LDM}$, is defined as:
$F_{LDM} = -D_{KL}[R(z, z') \parallel P_\theta(z, z')] = \langle \log P_\theta(z, z') \rangle_{R(z,z')} + H_R[z, z']$
Here, $R(z, z')$ represents the joint distribution of latent representations $z$ and $z'$ derived from paired data views $(x, x')$ through a deterministic encoder $f(x)$ (i.e., $R(z|x) = \delta(z - f(x))$). $P_\theta(z, z')$ denotes the parameterized probabilistic model assumed for the latent variables. The first term in the objective encourages "alignment" of the learned representations with the specified latent model, while the second term, $H_R[z, z']$, acts as a "uniformity" regularization, preventing representational collapse and encouraging the encoder to be invertible on the data manifold.

**Connection to Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Independent Component Analysis (ICA):**
The LDM framework is motivated by drawing parallels with classical ML estimation and linear ICA. The paper shows that for an encoder that is invertible on the data manifold, maximizing the data log-likelihood is equivalent to performing LDM in the latent space. In this context, the latent entropy term in LDM directly relates to the log-determinant of the Jacobian, which is a key component in change-of-variables formulas for density estimation and ICA. This connection underscores that entropy maximization serves to regularize the encoder towards invertibility, a property essential for disentanglement.

**Analysis of Mutual Information (MI) Maximization:**
The research explicitly addresses the role of MI maximization by comparing $F_{LDM}$ with an objective function, $F_{MI}$, that explicitly includes an MI term. While $F_{MI}$ often appears in MI-based SSL interpretations, the authors argue that if the encoder maintains invertibility on the data manifold (a state encouraged by LDM's entropy maximization), MI between data views is already maximized. Consequently, any additional explicit MI maximization beyond entropy regularization is theorized to offer limited practical benefit, as empirical MI maximization implicitly performs distribution matching.

**Unifying Existing SSL Algorithms:**
The LDM framework unifies various SSL methods by demonstrating that they can be recovered as specific instantiations, differing primarily in their choices of $P_\theta(z, z')$ (i.e., latent prior and conditional distributions) and the method used for entropy estimation:
- **VICReg:** Derived by choosing a flat prior and a conditional Gaussian distribution for $P_\theta(z'|z)$, combined with a parametric entropy estimator (log-determinant of the covariance matrix).
- **SimCLR:** Derived by assuming latent variables on a unit sphere, a uniform prior, and a conditional von Mises-Fisher distribution for $P_\theta(z'|z)$, with entropy estimated via Kernel Density Estimation (KDE).
- **Predictive SSL with Stop-Gradient (e.g., BYOL, SimSiam, JEPA):** These methods are shown to implicitly implement approximate conditional entropy maximization through the use of stop-gradients, where the predictor's parameters are effectively fixed when computing gradients for the encoder, thus contributing to an implicit estimation and maximization of conditional entropy.

**Entropy Estimation Techniques:**
The methodology details several techniques for approximating the entropy term $H_R[z]$ or $H_R[z, z']$:
- **Kernel Density Estimation (KDE):** Approximates the continuous probability density function (PDF) using probability kernels around data samples, often leading to contrastive terms.
- **K-Nearest Neighbor (kNN) Estimation:** Calculates entropy based on distances to k-nearest neighbors, providing a robust estimator for high-dimensional distributions.
- **Parametric Estimation:** Assumes a specific parametric form (e.g., Gaussian) for the latent distribution, allowing for closed-form entropy calculations like the log-determinant of the covariance matrix.
- **Conditional Entropy Estimation with Predictor:** In temporal or predictive models, a learned predictor $\hat{R}_\theta(z'|z)$ minimizing cross-entropy effectively provides an estimate of the conditional entropy.

**Derivation of New SSL Objectives:**
Leveraging the LDM framework, the authors derive a new predictive SSL model specifically for high-dimensional time series. This model incorporates a Kalman filter as the backbone for its latent predictor $P_\theta(z_t|z_{:t})$. This approach enables the analytical quantification of uncertainty in latent representations within a nonlinear dynamical system with linear latent dynamics, thereby allowing for principled uncertainty quantification.

**Identifiability Guarantees:**
A significant methodological contribution is the formal proof of identifiability. Theorems 1 and 2 establish that, under certain assumptions (e.g., Gaussian or von Mises-Fisher predictive model, encoder invertibility on the data manifold, and predictor covering the latent space), LDM training recovers the true underlying latent variables up to an affine transformation. This provides a formal statistical explanation for the efficacy of predictive SSL without requiring explicit observation-level likelihoods or contrastive objectives.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents a combination of empirical validations and theoretical proofs to support the proposed Latent Distribution Matching (LDM) framework for self-supervised learning (SSL).

**Empirical Comparison of LDM and MI Maximization:**
The study empirically compared SSL algorithms derived from two formulations: $F_{LDM}$ (which directly minimizes KL divergence and maximizes entropy) and $F_{MI}$ (which explicitly includes a mutual information (MI) maximization term in addition to the LDM objective). Experiments on natural image datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, Imagenet-100) using linear probing demonstrated **no systematic performance difference** in validation accuracy between models trained with and without explicit MI maximization. This finding supports the theoretical argument that if the encoder is invertible on the data manifold, MI maximization provides little additional benefit beyond entropy maximization. Instead, representation quality was observed to be primarily influenced by the chosen latent space (e.g., plane vs. sphere) and the specific entropy estimator employed (KDE, kNN, or parametric LogDet). The eigenspectrum and t-SNE embeddings of learned representations also indicated that MI maximization had minimal impact, while latent space and entropy estimators had a more pronounced effect.

**Predictive Learning with Kalman-Based SSL:**
The LDM framework was extended to temporal models, leading to the derivation of a nonlinear Bayesian filtering model with a Kalman-based predictor.
- **Latent Variable Recovery:** On synthetic video datasets featuring linear hidden dynamics and nonlinear observations, both a contrastive predictive coding (CPC) approach (implicitly maximizing MI) and a predictive stop-gradient SSL method (not explicitly maximizing MI) successfully recovered the underlying latent variables, achieving an R² of 0.99.
- **Gradient Alignment:** Analysis of network gradients revealed that the gradients from the kNN single-variable entropy estimator and the stop-gradient conditional entropy estimator consistently aligned during training, suggesting that stop-gradient methods implicitly align with conditional entropy maximization.
- **Uncertainty Quantification:** The Kalman-based predictor enabled principled uncertainty quantification over latent states. This was demonstrated using *in vivo* rat hippocampal spike train data, where the model arranged encoded spike trains on a circle, consistent with the rat's periodic movement on a linear track. The Kalman filter's uncertainty estimates directly translated into confidence intervals for predictions of rat position, a capability not typically offered by other predictive SSL methods.

**Identifiability Guarantees for Predictive SSL:**
A central theoretical finding is the proof of identifiability for predictive SSL models trained via LDM.
- **Affine Identifiability:** Theorem 1 proves that under assumptions of a Gaussian predictive model (or von Mises-Fisher for Theorem 2), an encoder invertible on the data manifold, and a predictor covering the latent space, the learned latent representations recover the true underlying latent variables up to an affine transformation. This result holds even with nonlinear predictors and does not require explicit observation-level likelihoods or contrastive objectives. Identifiability arises from the local noise structure embedded in the predictive errors.
- **Empirical Validation:** Empirical validation on a nonlinear prediction task demonstrated that the model linearly encoded the true position (R² = 0.94), thereby recovering the true latent space up to affine transformations. Furthermore, entropy maximization in LDM encouraged the encoder to become injective, even when initially non-injective, a prerequisite for identifiability.
- **Robustness:** The identifiability property was found to be robust to limited deviations from Gaussian noise in the data generation process. However, performance could deteriorate in higher latent dimensions due to increased sampling requirements and challenges in effective entropy maximization.

**Probabilistic Representations in Categorical SSL (Appendix A.8):**
- In a categorical SSL model applied to MNIST digits, LDM alone (without explicit MI maximization) successfully learned probabilistic representations that captured epistemic uncertainty. Digits with low-entropy encodings were stereotypical, while high-entropy encodings corresponded to ambiguous outliers.
- In this categorical context, explicit MI maximization was observed to cause category collapse, necessitating a misspecified latent model (with lower matching probability) to prevent it. This contrasts with the findings in continuous latent spaces and suggests a context-dependent role for MI.

In summary, the research provides empirical evidence that explicit MI maximization often offers negligible benefits over LDM's entropy regularization. It demonstrates the LDM framework's capacity to derive new, principled predictive SSL methods with uncertainty quantification, and, critically, offers theoretical proofs for the affine identifiability of learned latent representations in predictive SSL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the theoretical understanding and practical development of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL), with implications across several key areas:

**Theoretical Unification and Conceptual Clarity:**
The most immediate impact is the provision of a unifying theoretical framework, Latent Distribution Matching (LDM), that encapsulates a wide spectrum of existing SSL algorithms. By demonstrating how diverse methods—including contrastive, non-contrastive, and predictive approaches with stop-gradients—can be understood as instances of LDM arising from specific choices of latent models and entropy estimators, the paper brings conceptual clarity to a field often characterized by disparate techniques. This unification fosters a common language and set of principles, which can streamline future research and reduce the reliance on ad-hoc designs.

**Principled Algorithm Design and Innovation:**
The LDM framework offers a principled approach to designing new SSL algorithms, moving beyond heuristic-driven development. The derivation of a nonlinear, sampling-free Bayesian filtering model with a Kalman-based predictor for high-dimensional time series exemplifies this. This new model not only performs effectively but also enables principled uncertainty quantification over latent states, a feature that enhances the interpretability and reliability of SSL models, particularly in sensitive applications such as neuroscience, medical diagnostics, or autonomous systems where confidence estimates are critical. This approach could inspire further development of structured latent models within SSL.

**Re-evaluation of Mutual Information's Role:**
The research provides a critical re-evaluation of the role of mutual information (MI) maximization in SSL. By demonstrating that for encoders invertible on the data manifold, entropy maximization within LDM often implicitly achieves MI maximization, the paper suggests that explicit MI terms may contribute little to representation quality beyond preventing collapse. This insight can lead to simpler and more effective loss function designs, allowing researchers to focus on the core mechanisms of alignment and uniformity rather than potentially redundant MI objectives.

**Guarantees of Latent Representation Identifiability:**
The formal proof of identifiability for predictive SSL models under LDM is a significant theoretical advance. Guaranteeing that learned latent variables correspond to the true underlying factors up to affine transformations provides a strong theoretical basis for the meaningfulness and interpretability of these representations. This moves beyond specific algorithm-dependent proofs to a more general framework, bolstering confidence in SSL for scientific discovery and applications where extracting true underlying causes or features is paramount.

**Revised Understanding of SSL Mechanics:**
The work implies a revised understanding of how SSL operates. Rather than primarily functioning through lossy compression, the LDM framework suggests that SSL models excel at precisely reparameterizing the data manifold, maintaining substantial detail while disentangling abstract features by embedding appropriate inductive biases into the latent model. This perspective can guide future analyses and evaluations of SSL-learned latent spaces, focusing on properties like manifold structure and disentanglement rather than solely on compression ratios.

**Direction for Future Research:**
The paper explicitly identifies the development of more accurate, sample-efficient, and easily optimizable entropy estimators as a critical challenge and a key avenue for advancing SSL. This highlights a clear research direction, suggesting that improvements in entropy estimation techniques will directly translate into more robust and higher-performing SSL models. Furthermore, the exploration of probabilistic encoders in categorical LDM models opens pathways for designing SSL systems that inherently quantify and manage epistemic uncertainty in their representations.

In conclusion, this research provides a robust theoretical foundation for SSL, unifying existing methods, guiding principled algorithm design, clarifying the role of MI, and offering formal guarantees for representation identifiability. Its impact is expected to foster a more theoretically grounded, interpretable, and effective future for SSL.
