# Enabling Extensible Embodied Capabilities with Tools

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Huazhong University of Science and Technology and collaborators introduce a framework for embodied AI that externalizes heterogeneous capabilities into independently optimized tools. The work establishes the Embodied Tool Protocol (ETP) and a benchmark, EmbodiedToolBench, showing consistent performance gains across various tasks, with average improvements of up to 36% in navigation, while revealing persistent challenges in tool-need recognition and selection.

## Problem

- Existing monolithic embodied AI policies struggle with complex, long-horizon tasks due to opaque coupling, capability isolation, and entangled optimization of diverse capabilities.
- The inherent hierarchical and heterogeneous nature of embodied decision-making is poorly addressed by single, unified models.
- There was a lack of a unified framework, standardized protocol, comprehensive tool suite, and systematic evaluation for applying tool-use paradigms to embodied AI.

## Method

- A capability externalization approach is proposed, decoupling heterogeneous embodied capabilities into independently optimized and dynamically invokable tools.
- The **Embodied Tool Protocol** (ETP) is established as a standardized method for tool registration, discovery, invocation, and isolated execution, defining tools as callable units with declared interfaces and a registry-based system.
- EmbodiedToolBench, a comprehensive benchmark, was created, featuring over 100 validated tools and tasks specifically designed to evaluate tool-need recognition, selection, execution, and tool-chain composition.

## Results

- Tool augmentation consistently improved performance across benchmarks, with average gains of +**31%** on EB-ALFRED and +**36%** on EB-Navigation, and enabled agents to achieve success in real-world tasks where monolithic models failed.
- Tool augmentation narrowed the performance gap, allowing open-source models to surpass stronger closed-source models without tools in certain scenarios, such as Qwen3-8B with tools outperforming GPT-4o without tools on EB-ALFRED.
- EmbodiedToolBench evaluations revealed specific bottlenecks: low recall for tool-need recognition (as low as 0.31), tool selection accuracy (CSR 0.45-0.74), and difficulties in interpreting tool outputs and predicting subsequent actions.
- The most substantial performance improvements were observed for perception and cognition capabilities, while gains for execution capabilities were less pronounced.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Externalizing capabilities through tools consistently enhances embodied task performance and improves the competitiveness of open-source models, enabling them to match or exceed the performance of stronger monolithic models.
> - Models exhibit challenges in 'tool competence,' particularly regarding reliable tool-need recognition, precise tool selection from similar options, and effective interpretation of tool outputs.
> - Perception and cognition capabilities benefit most significantly from tool augmentation, while improvements in low-level execution and fine-grained manipulation are comparatively smaller.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied intelligence involves agents interacting with physical environments, requiring integrated perception, reasoning, planning, and control capabilities. Historically, much of the research in this field has focused on developing unified, parameterized policies, such as vision-language-action models and hierarchical vision-language systems. While these monolithic architectures have shown improvements on standard benchmarks, the paper identifies limitations when addressing complex challenges such as long-horizon, compositional, or safety-critical tasks. These limitations are attributed to the inherently hierarchical and heterogeneous nature of embodied decision-making.

The paper posits that encoding diverse capabilities within a single shared parameterization leads to opaque coupling, preventing selective invocation or diagnosis; capability isolation, hindering transfer and reuse across systems; and entangled optimization, where improvements in one capability may degrade others due to conflicting signals.

In parallel, the field of Large Language Model (LLM) agents has demonstrated significant advancements through the outsourcing of specialized capabilities to external tools. This "tool-use" paradigm offers greater scalability, flexibility, and performance. The presented work aims to bridge this concept into embodied AI, acknowledging existing fragmented efforts but noting the absence of a unifying framework, a standardized protocol, a validated full-stack tool suite, and systematic evaluation of tool selection and coordination within embodied contexts. This research seeks to provide these missing elements, thereby advancing the field towards more modular, robust, and extensible embodied intelligent systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the intrinsic limitations of monolithic embodied policies by proposing a capability externalization approach. This approach decouples heterogeneous embodied capabilities into independently optimized tools that can be dynamically invoked during inference. The core motivation stems from the recognition that embodied decision-making is fundamentally hierarchical and heterogeneous, making it difficult to reliably learn and modularize within a single, unified model.

To achieve this, the authors delineate three fundamental questions that the work aims to answer:

1. **Protocol:** To establish a principled and extensible method for representing, registering, and invoking embodied capabilities. This aims to create a shared abstraction that separates "what a capability does" from "how an agent invokes it," allowing for the composition of diverse capabilities with varying interfaces and requirements.
2. **Efficacy:** To empirically determine if current models can reliably utilize external tools and to quantify the practical performance improvements achieved through tool augmentation in embodied tasks. This addresses the question of whether the theoretical benefits of tool-based approaches translate into measurable gains.
3. **Externalization Boundary:** To identify which types of embodied capabilities benefit most from being decoupled into external tools and where the advantages of such decomposition diminish. This objective seeks to characterize the limits and optimal application areas for capability externalization.

By addressing these questions, the research endeavors to lay a unified foundation for tool-based embodied intelligence, enabling the development of more modular, reusable, and diagnosable embodied AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a unified framework centered on the Embodied Tool Protocol (ETP) and constructs a benchmark called EmbodiedToolBench to evaluate tool-use competence.

#### 3.1. Embodied Tool Protocol (ETP)

ETP is proposed as a standardized protocol for embodied tool registration, discovery, invocation, and execution. It aims to provide a shared abstraction to decouple what a capability does from how an agent invokes it, facilitating the composition of heterogeneous embodied capabilities.

- **Tool as a Capability Unit:** Each embodied capability is treated as a callable unit with a declared interface. A tool `z_m` is defined by its input-output spaces (`X_m`, `Y_m`), a realized capability subset (`C(z_m)`), and an executable mapping (`f_m`). This separation allows the implementation of `f_m` to be a learned model, classical algorithm, or program without altering the agent's interaction method.
- **Registry-Based Discovery and Invocation:** ETP maintains a capability registry `R = {(z_m, ρ_m, κ_m)}`, where `ρ_m` describes the tool's capability and applicability conditions, and `κ_m` specifies schema constraints for its input-output spaces. Agents query `R` via `ρ_m` to find relevant tools and then construct queries satisfying `κ_m`. This design ensures tool selection is based on declared functionality, allowing new tools to be registered without modifying the agent policy.
- **Isolated Execution with Runtime Feedback:** Each tool invocation operates in an isolated session, capturing output and runtime feedback. This isolation supports concurrent calls and remote execution, enabling scalability without tightly coupling tool implementations to the agent.

#### 3.2. EmbodiedToolBench

EmbodiedToolBench is developed to systematically evaluate models' embodied tool-use capabilities using the ETP and a curated tool suite.

- **Tool Suite Construction:**
 - Four core capability dimensions are identified: Perception and Grounding, Cognition and State Modeling, Reasoning and Planning, and Execution and Control. These are further decomposed into fine-grained problems (e.g., object recognition, spatial grounding).
 - State-of-the-art methods for each problem are identified, encapsulated as unified APIs, and described with LLM-generated structured tool cards detailing functionality, interface, applicability, and constraints.
 - This process yields over 100 validated embodied tools, distributed across various subcategories to ensure comprehensive coverage. Each tool undergoes validation beyond its original benchmark to ensure robustness across different environments.

- **Evaluation Tasks:** EmbodiedToolBench evaluates tool-use from four perspectives across navigation, planning, and manipulation scenarios. Each instance is defined by a decision state (`l`, `τ_t`, `Z_cand`).
 - **Task 1: Tool-Need Recognition:** Evaluates the model's ability to determine when tool invocation is necessary. Models output a binary decision (`true`/`false`). Performance is measured by accuracy and F1 score against ground truth. The dataset includes positive (requiring tools) and negative (solvable without tools or tool-redundant) instances, with balanced classes and distractor difficulty.
 - **Task 2: Tool Selection:** Assesses the model's capacity to choose the most appropriate tool from a fixed candidate set (`Z_cand`) of four options (one ground truth, three distractors, including semantically similar and irrelevant tools). Performance is measured by the Correct Selection Rate (CSR). Distractor selection is hierarchical, considering semantic similarity and precondition checks to ensure uniqueness of the ground-truth answer.
 - **Task 3: Tool Execution:** Evaluates correct tool invocation and action based on its output, broken into two stages:
 - **Stage 1 (Query Construction):** Model constructs a query `x` for a designated tool `z*`. Success requires a well-formed invocation (ISR = Valid(`x`, `z*`)).
 - **Stage 2 (Output Comprehension):** Model receives task context and tool output `y`, then predicts the next action `a`. Success requires consistency with the reference action (AMR = Match(`a`, `a*`)). The overall score is Tool Usage Success Rate (TUSR). Datasets ensure diversity in tool output formats and parameter values dependent on current observation.
 - **Task 4: Tool-Chain Composition:** Measures the model's ability to select the minimal necessary tools and arrange them in the correct dependency order. Models output an ordered tool sequence. Metrics include Accuracy (ACC) and F1 for tool set quality, and Order Consistency Rate (OCR) for dependency adherence. Tool chains are derived from expert trajectories, with dependency constraints annotated (data and state dependencies), and distractors include functionally overlapping or redundancy-inducing tools.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted across simulation (EB-ALFRED, EB-Habitat, EB-Navigation, EB-Manipulation) and real-world robotic platforms, involved eight representative open- and closed-source models.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness of Tool Augmentation (RQ1)

- **Consistent Performance Improvement:** Tool augmentation consistently improved embodied task performance across all benchmarks. Notable gains were observed in EB-ALFRED (average +31%) and EB-Navigation (average +36%). These tasks, requiring instruction following, visual grounding, planning, and long-horizon decision-making, benefited significantly from external tools. For example, Qwen3-8B's performance on EB-ALFRED increased from 0.20 to 0.72.
- **Narrowed Gap Between Models:** Tool augmentation substantially improved the competitiveness of open-source models, enabling them to outperform stronger closed-source models without tools in certain scenarios (e.g., Qwen3-8B with tools outperformed GPT-4o without tools on EB-ALFRED). Stronger closed-source models also benefited, indicating that tools complement both weaker and stronger base models.
- **Limited Gains in Fine-Grained Manipulation:** The benefits were less pronounced in tasks requiring precise spatial grounding, contact-rich interaction, and low-level physical control, such as EB-Habitat (average +14%) and EB-Manipulation (average +7%). Some long-horizon subtasks also showed minimal or no improvement.
- **Real-World Robot Experiments:** On three real-world robotic tasks (desktop cleaning, balance scale manipulation, building blocks), all models failed without tools (0/10 success). With tool augmentation, agents achieved substantial success rates, demonstrating the practical utility of external tools for real-world decision-making without specific training.

#### 4.2. Proficiency of Tool Use Behavior (RQ2)

Evaluations on EmbodiedToolBench revealed specific bottlenecks in how models utilize tools:

- **Tool-Need Recognition:** This remained challenging for all models, with open-source models showing particularly low recall (as low as 0.31), indicating difficulty in reliably judging when tool assistance is necessary.
- **Tool Selection:** Models faced non-trivial challenges in selecting the most appropriate tool from candidates, with CSR ranging from 0.45 (open-source) to 0.74 (closed-source). This points to difficulties in grounding the correct tool among semantically similar options.
- **Tool Execution:** While most models achieved high scores in input construction (ISR 0.94–0.96), interpreting and acting upon tool outputs effectively (AMR, TUSR) proved to be a primary bottleneck.
- **Tool-Chain Composition:** Composition accuracy correlated with overall model capability (0.36 to 0.71). A common failure mode was "over-composition," where models included superfluous tools (high recall, varied precision), indicating challenges in precise multi-tool coordination.

#### 4.3. Task-Specific Amenability to Tool Offloading (RQ3)

- **Largest Gains in Perception and Cognition:** Tool augmentation yielded the most significant performance improvements in these capability dimensions (e.g., +0.26 to +0.28 for closed-source and +0.26 to +0.34 for open-source models). This suggests that external tools are highly effective for visual parsing and knowledge-driven tasks.
- **Consistent Reasoning Improvement:** Reasoning capabilities also improved consistently, indicating tools can complement internal inference processes.
- **Variable Execution Gains:** Gains in Execution were smaller and more variable, reflecting its reliance on precise input construction and tool generalization for low-level physical control.

#### 4.4. Inference Time Overhead Analysis

The latency introduced by tool usage was generally acceptable for EB-ALFRED and EB-Habitat (10-30 seconds additional cost). However, EB-Manipulation showed higher latency increases (exceeding 30 seconds for some models), primarily due to higher-cost tools and network latency, highlighting the need for reasoning about temporal costs in real-time embodied tasks.

#### 4.5. Error Analysis

A fine-grained error analysis categorized failures into five types:
- **Missed Tool Invocation:** Most severe in action-intensive tasks (36-45% of errors in EB-Navigation and EB-Manipulation).
- **Ignored Output:** Dominated errors in perception-heavy settings (47-43% in EB-ALFRED and EB-Habitat).
- **Tool-induced Bias:** Consistently contributed to errors, peaking at 56.5% for one open-source model in EB-Manipulation.
- **Wrong Selection and Invalid Tool Call:** Relatively minor overall, but open-source models exhibited higher rates in certain environments (e.g., EB-Habitat), suggesting weaker tool-interface understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a structured approach to addressing core challenges in embodied intelligence by decoupling heterogeneous capabilities.

The primary contributions of this work are:
- **Embodied Tool Protocol (ETP):** The establishment of a standardized protocol for embodied tool registration, discovery, invocation, and execution provides a unifying framework. This protocol is intended to facilitate the principled integration of diverse capabilities, moving beyond fragmented approaches in tool-based embodied AI.
- **Comprehensive Embodied Tool Base:** The curation of over 100 validated tools spanning perception, cognition, reasoning, and execution represents a substantial resource. This tool base offers a full-stack collection of externalized capabilities, providing a foundation for future research and development in modular embodied systems.
- **EmbodiedToolBench Benchmark:** The creation of a systematic benchmark for evaluating embodied tool-use competence is a significant advancement. By assessing tool-need recognition, tool selection, tool execution, and tool-chain composition, EmbodiedToolBench offers a rigorous testbed for understanding the capabilities and limitations of current models in tool-augmented embodied scenarios.

The findings demonstrate that externalizing capabilities through tools consistently improves embodied task performance across various benchmarks and significantly narrows the performance gap between open-source and closed-source models. This suggests that the tool-use paradigm can enhance the robustness and accessibility of embodied AI.

However, the research also highlights that "tool competence," rather than mere "tool availability," is a central bottleneck. Models face persistent challenges in recognizing when a tool is needed, selecting the most appropriate one, correctly utilizing its output, and composing multiple tools effectively. These limitations are particularly evident in tasks requiring fine-grained manipulation and for execution-type capabilities.

The potential impact of this work includes:
- **Enhanced Modularity and Reusability:** By externalizing capabilities as independently optimizable tools, the framework promotes modular design, allowing components to be developed, improved, and reused across different embodied systems and tasks.
- **Improved Diagnosability and Transparency:** Decoupling capabilities can lead to clearer attribution of failures, making it easier to diagnose issues in complex embodied systems and fostering more transparent evaluation.
- **Advancements in Robustness and Safety:** A modular approach may contribute to more reliable and safer embodied systems by enabling the verification and adaptation of individual tools.
- **Future Research Directions:** The identified bottlenecks, such as failures in tool selection, invocation timing, multi-tool coordination, and the limited gains in execution-type capabilities, indicate critical areas for future research. This includes developing adaptive strategies that balance task success, invocation cost, and error propagation in dynamic environments.
