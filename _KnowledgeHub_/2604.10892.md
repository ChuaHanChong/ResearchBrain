# HECTOR: Human-centric Hierarchical Coordination and Supervision of Robotic Fleets under Continual Temporal Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Peking University researchers developed HECTOR, a human-centric hierarchical framework enabling online coordination and supervision of large-scale heterogeneous robotic fleets performing continual, uncertain temporal tasks. The system reduced average mission response time to 34.6s and maintained planning times under 2.0s for fleets up to 170 robots, demonstrating efficient human-in-the-loop adaptability.

## Problem

- Human operators face significant challenges in coordinating and supervising large, heterogeneous robotic fleets for complex, dynamic missions in uncertain environments.
- Existing multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) and temporal logic planning methods struggle with scalability, dynamic task changes, and real-time environmental uncertainties, often relying on static assumptions.
- Prior approaches often lack robust mechanisms for online human interaction and real-time adaptation, leading to computational intractability or unstable plans for large fleets.

## Method

- A human-centric hierarchical framework named **HECTOR** is proposed, structured into three layers: human-fleet interaction, task assignment/team formation, and local team coordination.
- Implements a ==bidirectional, multimodal online interaction protocol== with a GUI, enabling operators to supervise and intervene at various granularities with lean communication.
- Utilizes a ==receding-horizon, automaton-guided search tree== for simultaneous task decomposition and team assignment, followed by a ==Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP)== for capacity-based robot instantiation within teams.

## Results

- HECTOR successfully managed **32** tasks and **482** subtasks over **12** replanning events in **180.4s**, with average replanning times of **0.36s** and a maximum of **0.87s** in human-in-the-loop simulations.
- The framework achieved an average mission response time of **34.6s** and a **100%** success rate, outperforming **eight** baseline methods in response time and planning efficiency metrics.
- Planning times remained below **2.0s** even when scaled to **170** robots, **100** missions, and **1509** subtasks, demonstrating strong scalability and robustness to robot failures and communication delays.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hierarchical decomposition of planning, from fleet-level assignment via automaton-guided search and MILP-based robot instantiation to local team coordination, combined with a receding-horizon strategy, enables scalable, efficient, and optimal management of large robotic fleets under temporal logic constraints.
> - Seamless, bidirectional human-fleet interaction with a multimodal interface is critical for effective human oversight and dynamic mission adaptation in complex, evolving environments.
> - Task-specific local coordination strategies, such as distributed dynamic coalition formation, are vital for efficient execution and real-time adaptation to diverse and uncertain subtasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of robotic fleets for concurrent and collaborative missions such as delivery, surveillance, and search and rescue is a growing area of interest. However, directly controlling numerous individual robots poses significant challenges for human operators, necessitating high levels of autonomy combined with effective online human-fleet interaction, especially in dynamic and partially unknown environments.

Existing research in multi-robot systems and task allocation often operates under assumptions that limit their practical applicability in complex scenarios. Many approaches for multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) and temporal logic planning assume relatively small fleet sizes, static task sets, and offline computation of assignments. This makes them unsuitable for dynamic environments where tasks can be added, cancelled, or prioritized in real time, and where environmental uncertainties may lead to the online discovery of subtasks. Conventional replanning methods for dynamic environments can become computationally intractable or lead to unstable, oscillatory allocations.

While temporal logic (such as Linear Temporal Logic, LTL, and its variants like Counting LTL or Hierarchical LTL) provides a formal language for specifying complex missions, most existing works in this area tend to overlook online interaction with human operators and the varying types of local tasks requiring specific coordination and adaptation. Similarly, research into human-swarm interaction often focuses on interface design or small teams, providing limited support for scalable online validation and intervention for large, heterogeneous fleets operating under dynamic and uncertain conditions. Such limitations often necessitate a fallback to teleoperation, which does not scale efficiently with increasing fleet sizes and operator workload.

This work addresses these gaps by proposing a unified, human-centric, and hierarchical framework designed for large-scale, heterogeneous robotic fleets. It integrates explicit mechanisms for online human interaction and supervision with scalable planning algorithms that can adapt to continually evolving, uncertain temporal tasks. The framework is intended to overcome the computational and interactive limitations of prior work by decoupling planning layers and enabling real-time responsiveness to operator commands and environmental changes, without requiring full system model recomputation for every update.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical need for human operators to coordinate and supervise a large number of heterogeneous autonomous robots, such as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs), to accomplish complex collaborative missions like patrol, delivery, or search and rescue in dynamic and partially unknown environments. The authors note that current approaches frequently make simplifying assumptions regarding fleet size and task knowledge, often neglecting the crucial aspect of human interaction during online execution.

The key objectives of this work are to:

1. **Develop a Human-Centric Coordination and Supervision Scheme**: Propose "HECTOR," a novel framework designed for large-scale robotic fleets (potentially dozens of robots) operating under continual and uncertain temporal tasks. This framework aims to integrate human oversight and intervention seamlessly.
2. **Design a Bidirectional and Multimodal Online Human-Fleet Interaction Protocol**: Establish a communication protocol and graphical interface that allows operators to interact with and supervise the entire fleet at various granularities. This includes capabilities to specify complex temporal missions, approve team formations, monitor task progress, dynamically add or cancel tasks, and adjust task priorities or deadlines with lean communication.
3. **Implement a Hierarchical Planning Scheme**: Decompose the overall coordination problem into three distinct layers to improve computational efficiency and reduce human cognitive load. These layers are:
 - **Layer I**: The human-fleet interaction protocol for online supervision.
 - **Layer II**: A rolling assignment mechanism for currently known tasks to teams within a specified planning horizon.
 - **Layer III**: Dynamic coordination strategies within each team for detected subtasks during online execution.
4. **Support General Temporal Logic Missions**: Enable the specification of complex missions using Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) formulas over collaborative actions, allowing for formal and expressive mission descriptions that encompass safety, temporal order, and collaboration requirements.
5. **Address Practical Aspects of Robotic Fleets**: Explicitly account for heterogeneous robot capabilities, parameterized collaboration utilities (where subtask duration depends on assigned robots), continual temporal task constraints, and variability in the number and distribution of subtasks (including initially unknown or dynamically moving subtasks).
6. **Optimize Mission Response Time**: Synthesize collective plans that minimize the average mission response time, explicitly measuring the fleet's responsiveness, which is critical in dynamic and safety-critical scenarios.
7. **Ensure Scalability and Adaptability**: Design the framework to be fully online and continuously adaptable to operator inputs, external events, and runtime observations, while maintaining computational tractability for large fleets.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to bridge the gap between theoretical multi-robot coordination and the practical requirements of real-world deployments involving human operators in complex, evolving environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HECTOR, the proposed human-centric framework, employs a hierarchical hybrid architecture consisting of three interconnected layers designed to manage large-scale robotic fleets under dynamic and uncertain temporal tasks.

**A. Protocol and Interface of Human-Fleet Interaction**

This layer establishes the communication and coordination mechanisms for online human-fleet interaction.
1. **Protocol for Online Requests**: The system defines four types of operator requests (κ1-κ4) with explicit parameters.
 - κ1 (new mission): Released as a syntactically co-safe LTL (sc-LTL) specification over collaborative tasks, processed by a "task command module" and "task decomposition module."
 - κ2 (cancellation), κ3 (deadline/priority modification), κ4 (robot reassignment): Handled by a "human intervention module" that updates active missions and resources.
 - The protocol ensures bidirectional, closed-loop communication, where operator directives propagate downwards, and feedback on planning results, execution progress, and environmental changes flows upwards.
2. **Design of Graphical Interface**: A graphical user interface (GUI) integrates the interaction protocol with real-time visualization. It allows operators to issue requests (voice, text, templates), view scenario visualizations (task distribution, robot positions/trajectories), monitor mission progress (decomposed task graphs, LTL formulas), and track task allocations and execution timelines via Gantt charts.
3. **Hierarchical Coordination and Communication**: Communication mirrors the fleet's organizational hierarchy. The operator interacts with designated team leaders, who then translate high-level directives into concrete plans for their respective teams. Team members communicate with their leaders for assignments and status reports, minimizing operator overload and enhancing scalability and robustness.

**B. Task Assignment and Team Formation**

This layer addresses two interdependent problems: decomposing high-level missions and assigning them to subteams.
1. **Simultaneous Task Decomposition and Team Assignment**: This component, formalized in Algorithm 1, tackles Problem 1 (optimal number of teams K, team composition, and local plans to minimize mission response time). It avoids the computationally expensive construction of a synchronous product automaton by employing an automaton-guided search tree (Fig. 5).
 - **Search Tree Structure**: Each node in the tree represents partial local plans for teams and the current reachable states in the B¨uchi Automata (Bφm) for all active missions.
 - **Four Stages**:
 - **Selection**: A set of candidate nodes is selected for parallel expansion based on a value function (χ) that considers makespan, estimated cost, and mission progress, weighted by human-specified priorities. Fleet capacity constraints are checked.
 - **Expansion**: Selected nodes are expanded by assigning a new task (one that enables a valid transition in a Bφm) to an existing or newly formed team. This dynamically determines team formation and task allocation. Capacity constraints (e.g., minimum robots for an action) and estimated task ending times are updated.
 - **Bounding**: A performance profile (ζ) is used to evaluate and prune strictly inferior (dominated) nodes, focusing the search on promising branches and maintaining a non-dominated frontier.
 - **Termination**: The process repeats until a computational budget is met or no new non-dominated nodes emerge. The optimal node minimizing the value function among complete assignments is selected.
 - **Receding-Horizon Strategy**: Only a finite set of H tasks (user-defined horizon) is assigned per cycle. This balances batch assignment efficiency with online responsiveness.
 - **Correctness**: Theorem 1 proves completeness and optimality for static, known tasks with a full horizon. Lemma 2 guarantees consistency and feasibility for online, receding-horizon planning.
2. **Capacity-based and Redundancy-aware Team Formation**: Following task assignment, Problem 2 focuses on instantiating the subteams with specific robots (Fig. 6). This is formulated as a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problem.
 - **Capacity Constraints**: Binary variables (b_ik) indicate if robot i is assigned to team k. Constraints ensure that each team meets minimum robot requirements (β_jk) for specific actions while allowing for a redundancy margin (α_j ≥ 1) to account for uncertainties (e.g., unknown subtasks, robot failures).
 - **Min-Max Objective**: The MILP minimizes the worst-case response time across all teams, thereby ensuring overall mission efficiency.
 - The solution provides the precise robot assignments (N_k) and generates concrete local plans for each robot.

**C. Local Task and Trajectory Coordination within Teams**

This layer focuses on detailed subtask assignment and trajectory optimization for robots within an assigned team (Problem 3), aiming to minimize local task makespan. Strategies vary based on the nature of subtasks:
1. **Static and Known Subtasks**: For fixed, known subtasks (e.g., delivery points), the problem is a Multi-Vehicle Routing Problem (MVRP).
 - **Holonomic Robots**: Straight-line trajectories and combinatorial optimization for task sequencing.
 - **Non-Holonomic Robots**: Hybrid optimization of task sequencing and feasible trajectories using motion primitives (e.g., Dubins curves), often solved with a hybrid-A*-like search method to ensure dynamic feasibility (Eq. 20).
2. **Static and Unknown Subtasks**: For subtasks revealed during execution (e.g., search and rescue), a Simultaneous Exploration and Coordination (SEC) method is used.
 - **Collaborative Exploration**: Teams survey the area using coverage methods (obstacle-free) or frontier-based exploration (cluttered workspaces).
 - **Dynamic Assignment**: Newly discovered subtasks are inserted into robot plans using a minimum-disruption planning approach (Eq. 21) to minimize makespan increase.
3. **Dynamic and Known Subtasks**: For moving subtasks (e.g., collaborative pursuit), a Distributed Dynamic Coalition Formation (DCF) method (Algorithm 2) is employed.
 - **Coalition Formation**: Each robot updates its coalition membership using local information and peer communication, forming disjoint and exhaustive coalitions (R_j(t)).
 - **Cost Function**: Robots evaluate coalition costs (χ(R_j)) based on factors like robot-target distances and velocities.
 - **Local Switch Rule**: Robots switch coalitions if it reduces the overall team cost (χ(R_t), Eq. 22), resolving conflicts via priority rules. This drives the system toward a locally optimal configuration, yielding continuously adaptive plans and trajectories.
 - **Correctness**: Theorem 3 guarantees feasible plans and trajectories that minimize local task makespan for these strategies.

**D. Online Execution, Interaction and Adaptation**

1. **Online Execution and Receding-horizon Adaptation**: After initial assignment of H tasks, teams execute their local plans, cycling through navigation, execution, and synchronization states. Replanning is triggered by execution progress (e.g., half tasks completed), new mission specifications, or detected infeasibility. During replanning, active tasks are not preempted, and robot states are rolled forward to ensure consistent future formations.
2. **Fulfillment of Online Human Requests**: The framework seamlessly integrates the four operator request types (κ1-κ4) into the receding-horizon planning. New missions update the task set and Bφm. Cancellations remove tasks and prune associated nodes. Priority changes re-weight cost functions. Robot reassignments re-solve the team formation MILP with updated constraints. Conflicting requests are detected, and the operator is prompted for resolution.
3. **Complexity and Scalability Analysis**: The authors analyze the computational complexity of each module, highlighting that the hierarchical decomposition and receding-horizon strategy confine NP-hard problems to smaller, more manageable sub-problems, thus maintaining scalability for large fleets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validates HECTOR through extensive human-in-the-loop simulations, including comparisons with baselines and a high-fidelity ROS simulation.

**A. Simulation Environment and Setup**
The simulations involve N=80 heterogeneous robots (Type A, B, C with specific capabilities) in a 260m x 42m open environment. Five missions are released online following sc-LTL templates, generating 32 tasks and 482 subtasks. Tasks include delivery (static/known), surveillance (static/unknown), and dynamic capture (dynamic/known). Robot dynamics include curvature constraints (3 m^-1). A receding horizon H=6 is used, and replanning is triggered by execution progress, new missions, or infeasibility.

**B. Key Results**

1. **Mission Decomposition and Subteam Formation (Fig. 12)**:
 - The initial mission (9 tasks) leads to the computation of its B¨uchi automaton in 0.20s. Algorithm 1 then optimizes for 5 subteams, predicting a makespan of 37.5s, within 1.36s.
 - Online human requests (e.g., κ1 for new task ω3, κ3 for priority change of ω8, κ2 for task cancellation of ω6) are seamlessly integrated, demonstrating the framework's adaptability. For instance, priority changes trigger revisiting tasks and forming new teams, while cancellations free up resources.
 - The system successfully manages a total of 32 tasks and 482 subtasks in 180.4s over 12 replanning events. Mean and maximum replanning times are 0.36s and 0.87s, respectively.
 - The framework detects conflicting operator requests (e.g., boosting a task's priority while simultaneously restricting capable robots), allowing the operator to resolve them.
2. **Local Task Execution (Fig. 13)**:
 - **Delivery (Static & Known)**: Task ω3 (18 subtasks, 8 robots) is efficiently executed, with robots completing 2-3 subtasks each. Operator request κ4 (robot reassignment) effectively reallocates robot 21 to assist with specific subtasks of ω3.
 - **Surveillance (Static & Unknown)**: Task ω7 involves exploration. Robots follow region-covering trajectories, and newly discovered subtasks are allocated with minimum disruption (e.g., robot 43 for subtask 10). Another κ4 request demonstrates dynamic reallocation of robot 42 to assist with subtask 9.
 - **Dynamic Capture (Dynamic & Known)**: Task ω5 (18 moving targets, 22 robots) demonstrates the Distributed Dynamic Coalition Formation (DCF) algorithm (Alg. 2). Coalitions dynamically reform and dissolve as targets move and subtasks are completed, indicating adaptive resource allocation.
 - Mean response times for delivery, surveillance, and dynamic capture tasks are 29.39s, 46.37s, and 35.65s, respectively, with local planning times of 0.31s, 0.22s, and 0.50s.
3. **Overall Resource Utilization and Adaptation (Fig. 14, 15)**:
 - Execution states dominate robot activity, with low proportions for navigation and waiting.
 - Unassigned tasks are rapidly addressed, with counts spiking after new mission releases and falling to zero within 15 seconds.
 - The scheme dynamically reconfigures subteams (49 subteams formed in total), demonstrating efficient robot reuse and reallocation across missions.
4. **Comparisons with Baselines (Table II, Fig. 16)**:
 - HECTOR consistently outperforms eight baselines (MILP, SAMP-Task/Subtask, ScRATCHeS, Hulk, Flow, Inf-H, Greedy) across most metrics.
 - **Response Time**: HECTOR achieves a total response time of 184.4s and an average of 34.6s, significantly lower than MILP (327.8s), SAMP-Task (286.0s), ScRATCHeS (257.4s), and Greedy (325.7s).
 - **Planning Time**: HECTOR demonstrates efficient planning (0.46s average, 0.76s max), orders of magnitude faster than MILP (90.3s average) and Inf-H (53.4s average), and notably faster than Hulk (4.66s average).
 - **Success Rate**: HECTOR achieves a 100% success rate, comparable to some baselines (MILP, ScRATCHeS) but with substantially lower computational overhead. Baselines like SAMP-Subtask (86%) and Flow (96%) show lower success rates, potentially due to inadequate handling of uncertainties.
5. **Generalization (Table III, Fig. 17, 18, 19)**:
 - **Scalability**: The framework maintains consistently low planning times (below 2.0s) even when scaling up to N=170 robots, M=100 tasks, and J=1509 subtasks. Response time scales with increased problem size.
 - **Robustness to Failures**: A 100% success rate is maintained even with robot failure probabilities of 0.05 and 0.1, indicating sufficient fleet capacity and effective recovery mechanisms.
 - **Runtime Decomposition**: Task assignment typically requires more computation than team formation. Local coordination strategies show varying runtimes, with static and known tasks generally taking the longest.
 - **Parameter Sensitivity**: Analysis of parameters like horizon H, replanning trigger frequency, redundancy margin α, and robot curvature κ demonstrates the trade-offs involved (e.g., larger H increases planning time but potentially improves local optimality; higher α increases robustness by over-allocating robots). The system remains robust across parameter variations.
 - **Communication Constraints**: The distributed DCF algorithm performs successfully even under stochastic communication delays (10-100ms), although task execution time may increase slightly (e.g., from 31.0s to 41.2s).
6. **High-fidelity ROS-Simulation (Fig. 20)**:
 - A ROS-based simulation with 12 UAVs in a disaster relief scenario (10 tasks, 36 subtasks) validates the framework's effectiveness in a more realistic setting.
 - All tasks are successfully completed. Four replanning events occur, each taking less than 1s.
 - Average task response time is 34.6s, and mean replanning time is 0.46s.
 - The simulation demonstrates the real-time integration of various operator requests (new tasks, reassignments, cancellations, deadline modifications) via the GUI, confirming flexible and responsive adaptation to dynamic mission conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HECTOR represents a notable advancement in the coordination and supervision of large-scale robotic fleets operating under complex and uncertain temporal tasks. Its primary significance lies in the development of a unified, human-centric, and hierarchical framework that explicitly addresses the challenges of scalability, online adaptability, and human interaction, which are often overlooked in existing multi-robot planning literature.

The key advantages of HECTOR include:
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Teaming**: It provides a robust protocol and intuitive interface for multimodal human interaction, enabling operators to maintain high-level supervision and intervene effectively without being bogged down by low-level details. This significantly reduces the operator's cognitive load compared to direct control of individual robots.
- **Scalable Coordination**: The hierarchical decomposition of planning (global task assignment to teams, local subtask coordination within teams) coupled with a receding-horizon strategy allows the framework to manage large, heterogeneous fleets efficiently, even with hundreds of robots and dynamic task streams. This addresses the combinatorial complexity that plagues many centralized or flat planning approaches.
- **Adaptability to Uncertainty**: HECTOR's design inherently supports dynamic and uncertain environments, including the online arrival and modification of tasks, discovery of unknown subtasks, handling of moving targets, and resilience to robot failures, which is crucial for real-world applications.
- **Guaranteed Correctness and Feasibility**: The integration of temporal logic and capacity-based constraints, combined with rigorous proofs for correctness and feasibility (under specified conditions), ensures that the generated plans are valid and executable.
- **Superior Performance**: Numerical experiments demonstrate HECTOR's ability to achieve lower mission response times and higher planning efficiency compared to established baselines, while maintaining a high success rate.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for various domains. It could enable the reliable deployment of large-scale autonomous robotic fleets in demanding, safety-critical applications such as disaster relief operations (search and rescue, damage assessment), large-area surveillance, automated logistics and delivery, and defense scenarios. By providing a framework that is both highly autonomous and amenable to human oversight and intervention, HECTOR contributes to bridging the gap between theoretical multi-robot systems research and the practical requirements for real-world robotic deployments, fostering more effective and efficient human-robot collaboration.
