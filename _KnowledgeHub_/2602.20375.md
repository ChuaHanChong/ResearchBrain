# Generalizing from References using a Multi-Task Reference and Goal-Driven RL Framework

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from RAI Institute and Carnegie Mellon University developed a multi-task reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid robots to acquire agile, human-like motor skills while generalizing to novel conditions. The approach achieves high success rates and adaptive strategies across complex parkour tasks in simulation and on hardware, bridging the gap between motion fidelity and adaptability.

## Problem

- Humanoid robot control often faces a trade-off between achieving natural, human-like motion quality and robust adaptability to novel task conditions.
- Existing reference-driven imitation methods are often brittle and lack generalization beyond the specific demonstration data.
- Purely task-driven reinforcement learning frequently yields unnatural motions and requires extensive reward shaping to learn complex, coordinated skills.

## Method

- A unified ==multi-task Reinforcement Learning== framework trains a single ==goal-conditioned policy== by optimizing both ==reference-guided imitation== and ==goal-driven generalization== tasks.
- The policy conditions solely on the current robot state and a low-dimensional goal variable, ensuring independence from reference motions or explicit phase variables during deployment.
- A ==curriculum learning== strategy, combining an ==assistive wrench== and a ==task mixing schedule==, gradually shifts the policy's focus from dense imitation rewards to sparser, goal-driven supervision.

## Results

- The method achieved high success rates (up to **1.00** nominally and **0.62-0.98** for out-of-distribution conditions) for walk-climb, walk-jump, and climb-down tasks in simulation, and demonstrated adaptive strategies on the Unitree G1 humanoid hardware.
- Quantitative comparisons showed superior generalization over tracking-based RL (ZEST mocap) and pure task-driven RL, maintaining motion quality while adapting to diverse initial conditions.
- Learned policies could be modularly composed by a simple rule-based state machine to execute long-horizon parkour sequences, successfully combining walking, climbing, and jumping skills in a MuJoCo environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating reference motion as a behavioral prior for shaping during training, rather than a deployment-time constraint, allows the policy to acquire both natural motion quality and strong generalization capabilities.
> - A gradual curriculum that transitions from dense reference-based supervision to sparse, goal-driven supervision is essential for the stable acquisition of complex, contact-rich dynamic behaviors.
> - The synergistic combination of an imitation task (to instill structured motor skills) and a generalization task (to foster robustness and adaptability) within a single framework is crucial for high performance across varied conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a core, persistent trade-off in the field of humanoid robot control: the challenge of simultaneously achieving both natural, human-like motion quality and robust adaptability to novel, unforeseen task conditions. Existing approaches typically lean towards one extreme or the other, struggling to combine both effectively.

The current landscape can be broadly categorized:

- **Motion Tracking (Reference-Driven RL):** Methods like DeepMimic, GMT, and ZEST have excelled at learning agile and coordinated whole-body behaviors by faithfully imitating human motion capture data. However, their fundamental limitation lies in their strong coupling to the demonstration data. Policies trained this way are often brittle outside the exact demonstration distribution, making it difficult to generalize to new goals, environments, or task conditions. They essentially "replay" learned trajectories.
- **Distillation Approaches:** These methods attempt to relax strict tracking by using tracking-based controllers as "teachers" whose behaviors are distilled into more adaptable "student" policies. While improving steerability, distilled policies still inherit constraints from the original fixed reference motions, limiting their capacity to handle significantly out-of-distribution conditions or requiring extensive additional fine-tuning. This often leads to multi-stage, complex pipelines.
- **Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL):** AIL approaches aim to match the distribution of generated motion with reference data, offering greater flexibility than step-by-step tracking. However, they face challenges in dynamic, contact-rich environments where small deviations can lead to physically invalid or unstable behaviors. Furthermore, stabilizing the adversarial objective and resolving conflicts between task and imitation rewards can be difficult.
- **Purely Task-Driven Reinforcement Learning (Tabula Rasa RL):** While offering high adaptability and robustness to diverse task objectives, these methods often require extensive reward shaping and iteration to achieve complex, coordinated skills. Without a strong behavioral prior, the resulting motions can be unnatural, uncoordinated, or "greedy," prioritizing task completion over human-like quality.

This paper positions itself as a solution to bridge the critical gap left by these existing methods. It aims to develop controllers that acquire structured, human-like motor skills from dense reference supervision *while simultaneously* learning to adapt these skills to novel goals and initial conditions. The key distinction is treating reference motion as a *prior for behavioral shaping* rather than a deployment-time constraint. By avoiding adversarial objectives, explicit trajectory tracking, phase variables, or reference-dependent inference at runtime, the proposed framework seeks to offer a more unified, stable, and generalizable approach to learning agile humanoid control. It moves beyond locomotion-centric skills to encompass more athletic parkour behaviors, pushing the boundaries of what humanoid robots can robustly achieve.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a unified multi-task Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework that enables humanoid robots to acquire agile, natural, and deployable motor skills. The core motivation stems from the persistent trade-off observed in previous work: while reference-tracking policies can yield natural, coordinated behaviors, they often lack the adaptability needed to generalize beyond the specific demonstrations. Conversely, purely task-driven RL approaches can achieve adaptability but often at the expense of motion quality and the need for arduous reward shaping.

The key objectives and motivations can be summarized as follows:

- **Bridging the Gap:** To develop a methodology that combines the strengths of reference-guided imitation (naturalness, coordination) with goal-driven generalization (adaptability, robustness) into a single, cohesive framework.
- **Decoupling Motion Quality from Reference Data:** To ensure that the learned policies can exhibit human-like motion quality without being explicitly tied to or requiring reference motions as input during deployment. This means the reference motions serve as an *inductive bias* or *behavioral prior* during training, rather than a runtime constraint.
- **Enabling Generalization to Novel Conditions:** To allow humanoid robots to adapt to a wide range of novel goals, initial conditions, and environments that were not explicitly included in the reference demonstration dataset. This is crucial for real-world deployment where conditions are highly variable.
- **Simplifying Policy Structure:** To create a policy that is goal-conditioned and operates purely from the current robot state, eliminating the need for complex elements like adversarial discriminators, explicit trajectory tracking mechanisms, phase variables, or constant access to reference trajectories at inference time.
- **Acquiring Complex, Agile Behaviors:** To enable the learning of diverse, contact-rich athletic skills, such as jumping and climbing, beyond typical locomotion, particularly in challenging environments like parkour playgrounds.
- **Facilitating Long-Horizon Tasks:** To demonstrate that the learned, generalized skills can be modularly composed to execute complex, long-horizon tasks, signifying a step towards more autonomous and intelligent robot navigation.
- **Improving Training Stability and Efficiency:** To provide dense, human-demonstration-based rewards during an imitation phase that significantly enhance training stability and efficiency, guiding the policy towards high-quality motor skills more effectively than sparse task rewards alone.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is centered around a **unified multi-task Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework** designed to train a single goal-conditioned policy. This policy learns from both reference-guided imitation and goal-driven generalization tasks, sharing the same observation and action spaces but differing in their initialization, command spaces, and reward structures.

**A. Core Framework and Policy Design:**
- **Single Goal-Conditioned Policy:** The framework trains one policy that conditions solely on the current robot state ($s_t$) and a low-dimensional goal variable ($g_t$). Critically, the policy *never* receives reference motions, future trajectories, or explicit phase variables as input at any point, ensuring deployment-time independence from demonstrations.
- **Observation Space:** Includes humanoid's joint configuration and velocity, projected gravity, and torso angular velocities (in a character-centric frame). The goal $g_t$ is a 2D target root (torso) location relative to the character.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs residual actions ($a_t$) that modify default joint positions ($\bar{q}$) for joint-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controllers. This residual approach, unlike feedforward reference joint positions, allows the policy to deviate from reference behaviors when necessary for task objectives.

**B. Two Jointly Optimized Tasks:**

1. **Reference-Guided Imitation Task:**
 - **Purpose:** To instill structured, human-like motor skills by providing dense behavioral shaping. It acts as a form of representation learning, enabling the policy to map goal conditions to a family of motor skills rather than specific demonstrations. It also provides strong, informative learning signals for stability and efficiency.
 - **Goal Definition:** Goals ($g_t$) are derived directly from the reference trajectories. For box-based tasks, this translates to the center of the box's top surface for climbing/jumping, or a specific point on the ground for climbing down.
 - **Reward Structure:** $r_{imit} = r_{track,t} + r_{reg,t} + r_{surv,t}$
 - **Tracking Reward ($r_{track,t}$):** Densely encourages matching reference motion in terms of base pose, velocity, and joint configurations using exponentially weighted penalties.
 - **Regularization Reward ($r_{reg,t}$):** Penalizes physically implausible or undesirable behaviors (e.g., action smoothness, joint torque usage, joint limit violations, large horizontal foot forces, foot slippage, foot jerk, flat ankle configurations).
 - **Survival Reward ($r_{surv,t}$):** A constant positive term to encourage longer, stable rollouts.
 - **State Initialization:** Humanoid state and goal are initialized by sampling from reference motions with only small perturbations, anchoring learning in reference-like configurations.

2. **Goal-Conditioned Generalization Task:**
 - **Purpose:** To adapt the motor skills acquired during imitation to novel goals and conditions, ensuring robustness and transferability.
 - **Goal Definition:** Goals are randomly sampled, independently of any reference motion.
 - **Reward Structure:** $r_{gen} = r_{goal,t} + r_{reg,t} + r_{surv,t}$
 - **Goal-Driven Task Reward ($r_{goal,t}$):** A sparse reward combining penalties for base position and orientation errors relative to the goal, and a constant success reward upon reaching the target. This sparsity forces the policy to leverage the learned motor skills rather than discovering new ones from scratch.
 - **Regularization & Survival Rewards:** Identical to those in the imitation task.
 - **State Initialization:** Both humanoid state and goal are sampled from wide, diverse distributions, exposing the policy to conditions that deviate from the reference data.

**C. Curriculum Learning:**
An automatic difficulty schedule, controlled by a scalar $\lambda \in$, is crucial for stable learning of dynamic behaviors:
- **Assistive Wrench Curriculum:** A virtual spatial assistive wrench is applied at the robot's base. Its magnitude, scaled by $\beta(\lambda) = (1 - \lambda) \beta_{max}$, decreases as $\lambda$ increases, vanishing as training progresses. This support stabilizes early-stage learning and reduces catastrophic failures.
- **Task Mixing Schedule:** The probability of sampling the imitation task ($p_{imi}(\lambda)$) linearly interpolates from a high initial value (e.g., $p_0=1.0$) to a lower final value (e.g., $p_{target}=0.5$) as $\lambda$ increases. This gradually shifts the emphasis from dense reference-based shaping to sparser, goal-driven supervision.

**D. Domain Randomization and Training Setup:**
- **Domain Randomization:** Applied to simulation parameters (contact friction, link masses), external torso pushes, and observation noise to enhance robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer.
- **Training Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Architecture:** Asymmetric actor-critic. The actor (policy) is a 3-layer MLP. The critic (value function) is also a 3-layer MLP but receives additional privileged information (full root state, contact forces, assistive wrench, task indicator, reference look-ahead joint delta) available only in simulation, improving training stability and sample efficiency.
- **Simulation Environment:** Isaac Lab, with additional evaluation in MuJoCo for skill composition.
- **Reference Motion Acquisition:** Single human demonstrations (walk-jump, walk-climb, climb-down) captured via iPhone, reconstructed using CRISP, and retargeted to the Unitree G1 humanoid using GMR. Mirrored motions augment the dataset for walk-climb and walk-jump.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides compelling evidence through extensive simulation and hardware experiments that the proposed multi-task RL framework effectively enables humanoid robots to acquire agile, natural, and generalizable motor skills.

**A. Robustness and Generalization in Simulation and on Hardware:**
- **High Success Rates:** In simulation, the method consistently achieved high success rates across a wide range of perturbed initial conditions (varying root position, heading orientation, and box height) for walk-climb and walk-jump tasks. This demonstrated that the learned behaviors were not narrowly tied to specific reference executions but generalized well beyond the nominal starting state.
- **Adaptive Strategies on Hardware:** Qualitative hardware experiments on the Unitree G1 humanoid (Fig. 3) showcased the policy's ability to adapt its strategies based on initial conditions. For instance, in walk-climb, the robot adapted its lead leg and approach distance; for walk-jump, it chose between walking then jumping or directly jumping. In climb-down, it exhibited diverse controlled descent strategies, including pushing off with one foot. These observations underscore the policy's capacity for genuine generalization rather than mere trajectory replay.

**B. Comparison to Alternative Training Paradigms:**
- **Superior Performance:** Quantitative comparisons (Table I) against tracking-based RL (ZEST mocap) and tabula rasa RL (pure task-driven RL) demonstrated the proposed method's superiority.
 - **Ours:** Achieved the highest success rates under both nominal (1.00 for most tasks) and significantly out-of-distribution (0.62-0.98) initializations, while maintaining low root orientation error and reasonable joint position error, indicating both stability and naturalness.
 - **ZEST mocap:** Showed excellent motion fidelity (low joint position error) under nominal conditions but suffered a substantial drop in success rate (as low as 0.17 for walk-jump) under beyond-nominal conditions. This brittleness was attributed to its strong bias toward strict reference tracking, which hindered adaptation when deviations were necessary.
 - **Tabula rasa RL:** Exhibited less structured and often "aggressive" or "greedy" behavior, prioritizing task completion over coordinated motion (e.g., jumping directly off a 50cm box instead of controlled descent). While achieving high success rates nominally, its performance degraded significantly (e.g., 0.54 for walk-jump) under beyond-nominal conditions, highlighting the limitations of sparse rewards without a behavioral prior.

**C. Long-Horizon Skill Composition:**
- **Modular Skill Reusability:** The learned policies could be effectively composed into long-horizon parkour sequences (Fig. 4) by a simple rule-based state machine that issued task-level goals. This was demonstrated in a sim-to-sim transfer setting (MuJoCo).
- **Reliable Multi-Skill Execution:** The robot successfully combined walking, climbing up/down, and jumping behaviors across varied initial configurations and target goals without requiring careful tuning or task-specific resets, confirming the modularity and generalizability of the learned skills for complex scenarios.

**D. Key Components and Ablation Studies:**
- **Task Mixing Curriculum is Essential:** Removing the task mixing curriculum ("Ours w/o task curriculum") led to complete failure (0.00% success) on dynamic skills like walk-jump and walk-climb. This critical finding emphasizes that gradually transitioning from dense imitation shaping to sparse generalization is vital for acquiring complex, contact-rich behaviors.
- **Imitation Task is Crucial for Skill Acquisition:** Ablating the imitation task ("Ours w/o imitation") resulted in significant degradation of both task success (0.00% for walk-jump/climb) and motion quality (very high root orientation and joint position errors). This confirmed that sparse goal rewards alone are insufficient to guide the learning of coordinated, whole-body behaviors, often leading to greedy and uncoordinated motions.
- **Generalization Task is Key for Robust Adaptation:** Training without the generalization task ("Ours w/o generalization") preserved nominal performance and motion quality but caused a sharp drop in success rates (e.g., 0.27% for walk-jump) under beyond-nominal conditions. This highlights that while imitation can teach natural movements, the generalization task is indispensable for robust adaptation to novel situations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the field of humanoid robot control, offering a powerful and practical solution to a long-standing dilemma. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Breaking the Trade-off:** The most profound impact is the successful demonstration of a framework that effectively resolves the persistent trade-off between motion naturalness (typically from imitation) and adaptability (typically from task-driven RL). By treating reference motion as a behavioral prior for shaping rather than a deployment constraint, the authors have achieved both qualities within a single policy.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The ability of the learned policies to generalize beyond the training distribution and operate robustly under diverse and novel initial conditions is critical for deploying humanoid robots in unstructured, real-world environments. This moves away from brittle, pre-programmed or highly specialized controllers.
- **Human-like Agility in Complex Tasks:** The framework enables the acquisition of a diverse set of agile, human-like parkour skills, including jumping and climbing. This expands the repertoire of complex behaviors that humanoid robots can autonomously perform, extending beyond basic locomotion to more athletic and dynamic interactions with their environment.
- **Simplified Deployment:** The resulting controllers operate purely from current state and high-level goal commands, eliminating the need for complex runtime components like adversarial discriminators, explicit trajectory tracking, or phase variables. This simplifies the control architecture and makes the policies more practical for real-world integration.
- **Foundational for Autonomous Systems:** The demonstrated capability for modular skill composition, even with a simple state machine, lays crucial groundwork for developing higher-level autonomous systems. It suggests a future where robots can autonomously sequence and parametrize learned motor primitives to achieve complex, long-horizon objectives, paving the way for truly intelligent humanoid agents.
- **Practical Pathway for Robotics Researchers:** The detailed methodology, including curriculum learning, reward design, and actor-critic architecture, provides a clear and reproducible pathway for other researchers to develop sophisticated humanoid controllers. The use of widely adopted tools like Isaac Lab and PPO further aids adoption.
- **Future Research Catalyst:** The work identifies clear and exciting avenues for future research, such as integrating perception and scene understanding, enriching the goal interface, scaling skill libraries, and, most importantly, developing learned high-level policies for long-horizon decision-making. These directions are essential for achieving fully autonomous and versatile humanoid robots.
- **Cutting-Edge Contribution:** Given its arXiv date in 2026, this paper is positioned at the very forefront of research, likely influencing the direction of humanoid robotics and embodied AI for years to come.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust, elegant, and highly impactful framework that not only pushes the boundaries of humanoid robot control by enabling natural and adaptable behaviors but also establishes a practical foundation for the development of truly autonomous and agile robotic agents in complex environments.
