# Calibrated Predictive Safety for Heterogeneous Robots: An Action-Conditioned JEPA Framework with Model-Based Safety Shields

> [!summary] Summary
> Action-conditioned JEPA world model that predicts task progress and collision/stuck/failure risk for candidate action chunks from a VLA policy, MPPI/CEM, or a skill library, then feeds a calibrated ranking into a deterministic per-embodiment safety shield that alone enforces admissibility. On LIBERO-Long, the full framework raises closed-loop success 7 points over shield-only and halves collision false-negative rate at matched recall.

## Problem

- VLA policies emit action chunks with no statement of whether they will collide, wedge, or fail to advance the task, since imitation training gives no anticipation mechanism.
- Classical model-based shields (MPPI, CEM, predictive safety filters) reason about kinematics and geometry but have no notion of semantic task progress or visual failure modes like occlusion or slip.
- Prior test-time action-screening and critic work reports success-rate gains but no explicit calibration objective, so confidence scores are not guaranteed error rates usable for a safety threshold.

## Method

- ==Action-conditioned JEPA== predictor rolls a frozen encoder's latent state forward under each candidate action chunk and an embodiment embedding via ==AdaLN/FiLM conditioning==, trained against an EMA target encoder with stop-gradient.
- ==Risk and progress heads== output calibrated progress, collision, stuck, and failure probabilities plus predictive uncertainty, trained with ==focal BCE==, a pairwise ranking loss, and an explicit ==calibration loss== combining temperature scaling and a conformal wrapper.
- **Deterministic embodiment-specific safety shield**, a conjunction of eight hard predicates (collision, joint limits, stability, stopping-distance feasibility, geofence, etc.) with no learned parameters, filters the learned ranking and falls back through a stop-recover-replan-escalate ladder when no candidate is admissible.

## Results

- On LIBERO closed-loop, the full framework (shield plus reranking) improves success over shield-only by **7 points** (**62%** vs **55%**, 600 episodes, p ≈ **0.014**) and reduces collision rate from **4%** to **3%** versus shield-only.
- Collision false-negative rate drops from **0.21** (V-JEPA-style feature baseline) to **0.14** (full model) at fixed **0.90** recall, while AUROC moves comparatively little (**0.83** to **0.88**).
- Removing the calibration loss barely changes AUROC (**0.87** vs **0.88**) or Brier score (**0.09** vs **0.08**) but degrades expected calibration error by roughly **3.5x** (**0.14** vs **0.04**), showing ranking quality and calibration are dissociable.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Discriminative accuracy and calibration are separable properties; a model can rank risk correctly while its probabilities remain unusable as a decision threshold.
> - Safety guarantees should come from a deterministic, parameter-free shield rather than a learned score, since no finite penalty term prevents a miscalibrated model from ranking an unsafe action first.
> - Reporting rejection and fallback rates alongside collision rate is necessary, since a controller that stops constantly looks safe but is a degenerate, non-useful solution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a recognized challenge in robotic control: integrating the broad generalization capabilities of vision-language-action (VLA) policies with the rigorous constraint satisfaction offered by classical model-based planners.

**VLA policies** (e.g., RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, Octo) have demonstrated progress in proposing plausible actions directly from multimodal inputs like language and pixels. However, these policies are typically trained through imitation and lack an inherent mechanism to anticipate the consequences of their actions, such as collisions, gripper malfunctions, or task failures. They do not provide execution-time guarantees regarding physical safety or task progress. This paper positions VLA policies as effective "proposal distributions" for candidate actions.

Conversely, **classical model-based control methods** like Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) planning, and predictive safety filters are designed to reason rigorously about kinematics, geometry, and constraint satisfaction. Their predictive capabilities, however, are confined to hand-specified dynamics models, which often do not encompass semantic task progress or visual failure modes like occlusion or slip. These methods offer strong guarantees but exhibit limited generalization across diverse tasks and environments.

**Latent world models, particularly Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)** (e.g., V-JEPA 2, V-JEPA 2.1, DINO-WM), are identified as a bridge between these two paradigms. JEPAs predict future representations rather than raw pixels, thereby focusing computational capacity on task-relevant structural information while discarding photometric details. Action-conditioned JEPAs have shown promise in enabling zero-shot manipulation tasks by planning in latent space.

A parallel line of research focuses on **runtime safety filtering and action screening**, aiming to secure learned controllers at execution time without constraining their training. This includes techniques like formal shielding, model predictive shielding, predictive safety filters, and control barrier functions. More recent work has explored using predictive models or critics to screen candidate actions from VLA policies, rejecting failure-prone proposals (DreamAvoid), verifying actions with 3D reasoning (VeriSpace), or fine-tuning critics to detect subtle failure cues (Sudhakar et al.).

The current paper positions itself at the intersection of action-conditioned latent world models, VLA policy-based action proposal, and runtime safety filtering. It aims to develop a deployable architecture that leverages an action-conditioned JEPA world model to predict task progress and execution risk for candidate actions, and couples these predictions with an embodiment-specific, model-based safety shield. A key observation in the related work analysis is that while action screening methods improve performance, they typically lack a hard admissibility stage and an explicit objective for calibrating risk estimates, which is critical for safety-critical applications. The paper also acknowledges cross-embodiment learning efforts (e.g., Open X-Embodiment) for shared representations but argues against sharing safety-critical parameters across heterogeneous robot platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether an action-conditioned Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) world model can predict task progress and physical execution risk for candidate actions *before* they are executed, and whether integrating these predictions with an embodiment-specific model-based safety shield can lead to a deployable architecture for heterogeneous robots that reduces failures, collisions, and the need for human interventions.

The core motivation behind this objective stems from the limitations of existing robotic control paradigms:
1. **Lack of execution-time guarantees in VLA policies:** While VLA policies offer broad generalization and can propose plausible actions, they are trained via imitation and do not inherently reason about the physical consequences or safety implications of their proposed actions. Robots using such policies cannot anticipate collisions, mechanical jams, or task failures.
2. **Limited generalization of classical model-based planners:** Traditional model-based control offers rigorous guarantees regarding kinematic, geometric, and dynamic constraints. However, its predictive horizon is typically limited to hand-specified models, which struggle to account for semantic task progress or visually complex failure modes (e.g., occlusion, slip), limiting their adaptability to diverse, real-world scenarios.
3. **Need for deployable, defensible safety:** In safety-critical applications, it is essential for robotic systems to offer robust guarantees. Learned components alone, even with high accuracy, may not be sufficient due to potential miscalibration or out-of-distribution performance. The authors motivate a clear separation between learned ranking and hard admissibility to ensure that fundamental safety constraints are never overridden by the learned model, making the system "defensible in deployment."

To address these motivations, the paper outlines specific contributions:
1. **A formal problem formulation:** This includes a receding-horizon decision-making process that distinctly separates a learned scoring rule (J_k) for ranking candidates from a deterministic, hard admissibility predicate (C_emb) provided by the safety shield. It also specifies an explicit fallback ladder for scenarios where no safe action is found. The formulation incorporates a multi-task training objective for the JEPA world model, encompassing multi-step latent prediction, progress estimation, and predictions for collision, stuck states, and general task failure, alongside a calibration loss.
2. **A comparative synthesis of prior systems:** The authors conduct a structured review of existing literature, including capabilities and reported headline results, to identify gaps and contextualize their approach.
3. **A reproducible evaluation protocol:** This protocol defines fixed metrics, baselines, and ablations across multiple experiment levels (predictive performance, calibration, closed-loop execution, and deployment efficiency). A central safety statistic for evaluation is the false-negative rate (FNR) of collision prediction, emphasizing the criticality of avoiding missed hazards.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, termed "Calibrated Predictive Safety for Heterogeneous Robots," operates on a receding-horizon principle, integrating learned action ranking with a deterministic safety shield.

**System Architecture:**
1. **Observation Encoding:** At each control step, a frozen video encoder (E_θ) processes multimodal observations (RGB frames, depth images/point clouds, proprioception, task goal) into a latent state (z_t). Proprioception and goal information are concatenated as additional tokens.
2. **Candidate Action Generation:** A "proposer" (Π) component generates K candidate action chunks for a future horizon H. This proposer is a mixture of three sources:
 - **VLA policy sampling:** Draws K_vla action chunks from a pre-trained VLA policy (π_VLA) at a specified temperature, providing semantic and language-grounded diversity.
 - **Sampling-based optimization:** Uses MPPI or CEM, warm-started from the VLA policy's mean, to generate dynamically feasible and smooth candidates within a semantically relevant region.
 - **Skill library:** Instantiates parameterized primitives (e.g., open, grasp, retreat) with sampled parameters for repetitive sub-tasks.
 All candidates are expressed in a shared protocol. A cheap, conservative pre-filter discards immediately inadmissible candidates to optimize world model computation.
3. **Action-Conditioned JEPA Prediction:** The core of the learned component is an action-conditioned predictor (F_ϕ). This predictor takes the current latent state (z_t), a candidate action sequence (a_t:t+h), and an embodiment embedding (e_rob) as input. It then rolls the latent state forward in time (ˆz_t+h) for multiple horizons. F_ϕ is implemented as a block-causal transformer, with actions and e_rob injected via adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN/FiLM). Long-horizon rollouts are performed autoregressively in the latent space, avoiding the need for a pixel decoder.
4. **Risk and Progress Prediction:** A head network (R_ψ) processes the latent rollouts (ˆZ_k) and the embodiment embedding (e_rob) to produce a vector of estimates (ˆr_k). This vector includes:
 - Task progress probability (p_prog)
 - Collision probability (p_coll)
 - Stuck probability (p_stuck)
 - Terminal execution failure probability (p_fail)
 - A scalar predictive uncertainty (u)
5. **Uncertainty Calibration:** To ensure the probabilistic estimates are meaningful for safety decisions, calibration is treated as an explicit objective. Methods include temperature scaling of risk logits, ensemble/MC-dropout estimates for predictive variance (contributing to 'u'), and a conformal wrapper to convert collision probabilities into set-valued decisions with distribution-free miscoverage guarantees. An uncertainty-gated intervention rule is used: the world model only arbitrates when uncertainty is high or top candidates are close in score, otherwise the proposer's action is passed directly to the shield to bound latency.
6. **Candidate Scoring:** A scalar objective (J_k) ranks the candidates. This score is a weighted sum combining negative task progress, positive probabilities of collision, stuck states, and failure, predictive uncertainty, and a control cost (e.g., penalizing jerk). A positive weight for uncertainty (λ_u > 0) makes the system risk-averse under ignorance.
7. **Embodiment-Specific Safety Shield:** This is a deterministic component (C_emb) that forms the core of the safety guarantee. It contains no learned parameters and is specific to each robot embodiment (e_rob). C_emb evaluates a conjunction of eight checks on a candidate action's forward-simulated state sequence:
 - Swept-volume collision against an occupancy map (inflated by noise margin).
 - Joint position, velocity, and acceleration limits.
 - Footprint/stability checks (e.g., support polygon for legged robots).
 - Terrain slope and step height limits.
 - Stopping-distance feasibility (ensuring a certified braking maneuver is always possible).
 - Self-collision.
 - Geofence and forbidden-region membership.
 The shield's behavior is invariant to the world model's performance; it enforces hard constraints.
8. **Action Selection:** The controller selects the candidate (k*) that minimizes J_k, *subject to* the hard constraint that C_emb(a_k, e_rob) = 1. This "shield dominance" principle means the learned model can only reorder admissible actions, never admit an unsafe one.
9. **Fallback Ladder:** If the admissible set A_adm is empty (no candidate satisfies C_emb), an ordered fallback ladder is invoked: safe stop (F1), recovery maneuver (F2), replanning with enlarged budget (F3), or human escalation (F4).

**Training and Data:**
The JEPA world model and risk/progress heads are trained with a multi-task objective (Eq. 9). This includes the latent prediction loss, regression for progress, and focal binary cross-entropy for risk predictions (due to rare positive classes, emphasizing false negatives). An optional pairwise ranking loss is also included. Labels for progress, collision, stuck, and failure are derived from simulator states or proprioceptive contact detection. Crucially, failure trajectories are explicitly included in training data to provide positive examples for the risk heads.

**Cross-Embodiment Design:**
The encoder and predictor are shared across embodiments and conditioned on an embodiment embedding (e_rob). However, the safety shield (C_emb) is explicitly *not* shared, as physical properties like stopping distance, joint limits, and footprint are embodiment-specific and require exact specification and verification, rather than interpolation from learned data.

**Edge–Cloud Execution:**
To manage computational latency, the system employs an asynchronous edge-cloud architecture. The proposer, shield, and fallback ladder run on-robot in the hard real-time loop (low latency, high priority). World model rollouts (encoder, predictor, heads) can run asynchronously either on-robot or on a co-located edge server. The controller always uses the most recently available ranking. If the ranking is stale or the link fails, the system degrades to a proposer-plus-shield mode, maintaining safety guarantees despite reduced task performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research executed a pre-registered evaluation protocol across multiple levels: literature synthesis (Level 1), predictive and calibration performance (Level 2), and closed-loop deployment in simulation (Level 4). Level 3 (action-conditioned prediction and reranking offline) was not executed.

#### 4.1. Literature-Reported Comparison (Level 1)

The survey of fifteen prior systems confirmed a gap in the existing literature: no published system was identified that simultaneously screens actions with a learned score, couples it with a hard admissibility predicate, *and* reports calibration of its risk estimates. This finding directly motivated the core contributions of the paper.

#### 4.2. Predictive Performance (Level 2)

- **Collision False-Negative Rate (FNR):** The full proposed model demonstrated a significant reduction in FNR for collision prediction. Compared to a V-JEPA-style feature baseline, the FNR decreased from 0.21 to 0.14 (at a fixed recall of 0.90), indicating improved safety.
- **Discriminative Accuracy vs. Calibration:** The study revealed that discriminative accuracy (measured by AUROC and Brier score) and calibration (measured by Expected Calibration Error, ECE) are dissociable. Removing the explicit calibration loss from training had minimal impact on AUROC (0.87 vs. 0.88) and Brier score (0.09 vs. 0.08) but degraded ECE by approximately 3.5x (0.14 vs. 0.04). This demonstrates that a model can rank correctly without providing numerically reliable probability estimates.
- **Encoder Family Impact:** Using DINOv2 features as the encoder family (B6) yielded slightly lower performance than the V-JEPA-style features (B7) in AUROC (0.79 vs. 0.83) and FNR (0.27 vs. 0.21).

#### 4.3. Risk Calibration Diagnostics (Level 2)

Reliability diagrams illustrated that the uncalibrated risk head was over-confident. Applying temperature scaling significantly improved calibration, reducing ECE. ROC and Precision-Recall curves were provided to comprehensively characterize the collision head's performance, especially given the class imbalance (collisions are rare).

#### 4.4. Closed-Loop Performance (Level 4, in simulation)

Evaluated on the LIBERO-Long simulator over 600 episodes per configuration:
- **Full Framework (B5) vs. Model-Based Shield Only (B3):** The full framework (B5) significantly improved task success rate by 7 points (62% vs. 55%) over the model-based shield-only baseline (B3), with a statistical significance of p ≈ 0.014. B5 also achieved a lower collision rate (3% vs. 4%), lower stuck rate (9% vs. 17%), and higher recovery success (70% vs. 63%). This indicates the learned ranking component effectively guides the robot towards safer and more successful actions within the admissible set defined by the shield.
- **Full Framework (B5) vs. JEPA Reranking Only (B4):** B5 demonstrated a 3-point increase in success rate (62% vs. 59%) over the JEPA reranking-only baseline (B4). However, this difference was not statistically significant at the given sample size (p ≈ 0.29). B5 did show lower collision (3% vs. 9%) and stuck rates (9% vs. 10%) compared to B4.
- **Impact of Shielding Alone:** A purely rule-based shield (B2) reduced collision rate compared to a base policy without any screening (B1), but also slightly decreased success rate (53% vs. 54%), indicating that a shield without an intelligent ranking mechanism can be overly cautious and hinder task progress.
- **Fallback and Rejection Metrics:** The study explicitly reported rejection rates and fallback frequencies. The full framework (B5) had a 14% rejection rate and an 8% fallback rate, demonstrating that safety was achieved not by constant stopping, but by effective selection and principled fallback mechanisms.
- **Impact of Failure Trajectories:** An ablation showed that removing failure trajectories from training data negatively impacted both success (4 points reduction) and collision rates (3 points increase), underscoring the necessity of negative-class data for effective risk prediction.

#### 4.5. Deployment Efficiency

Measurements on target accelerators (Jetson AGX Orin for on-robot, RTX 4090 for edge-assisted) provided practical insights:
- **On-robot Latency:** The median end-to-end decision latency on-robot was 590 ms (approx. 1.7 Hz).
- **Edge-assisted Latency:** The edge-assisted setup significantly reduced median latency to 165 ms.
- **Safety-Critical Path:** Crucially, the shield evaluation latency was 14 ms, operating solely on-robot. This ensures that the safety-critical components function within real-time constraints, irrespective of the world model's computational demands or network availability.
- **Network Effects:** While faster, the edge-assisted setup introduced network round-trip delays (median 9 ms, p99 41 ms) and a 0.4% timeout frequency (for ∆_max=300ms), which could trigger the on-robot fallback path.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a framework for predictive safety in robotics that has implications for the deployment of advanced AI-driven systems.

1. **Enhanced Safety and Trustworthiness:** By rigorously separating learned action ranking from a deterministic, model-based safety shield, the framework establishes a foundation for deployable robots where safety guarantees are rooted in verifiable, hard constraints. This approach ensures that the system's core safety properties do not degrade with updates to the learned model or in the event of model failure, which is critical for building trust and enabling deployment in human-centric environments.
2. **Bridging Generalization and Guarantees:** The work addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics by effectively combining the broad generalization capabilities of VLA policies (for action proposals) with the precise constraint satisfaction of model-based control (for safety enforcement). The JEPA world model acts as an intelligent intermediary, improving performance within the safe operating envelope.
3. **Actionable Uncertainty Quantification:** The explicit focus on calibration for risk estimates transforms uncertainty outputs from arbitrary scores into numerically reliable probabilities. This allows for the definition of meaningful, data-driven thresholds for intervention, thereby making the system's decision-making more interpretable and controllable for safety engineers.
4. **Support for Heterogeneous Robot Fleets:** The design, which conditions the learned perception and prediction components on an embodiment embedding while maintaining separate, explicit safety shields for each platform, facilitates deployment across diverse robot types. This reduces the retraining burden when introducing new robot models, requiring primarily a specification and verification task for the new embodiment.
5. **Robustness to Degraded Operation:** The asynchronous edge-cloud execution architecture provides resilience. If the world model's predictions are stale or unavailable due to network issues, the system can gracefully degrade to a proposer-plus-shield mode, maintaining essential safety guarantees even while task performance might be temporarily reduced.
6. **Standardized Evaluation:** The comprehensive, pre-registered evaluation protocol, with its emphasis on false-negative rates for collision prediction, calibration metrics, and detailed reporting of fallback behaviors, contributes to more rigorous and transparent research in robotic safety. This can help to establish benchmarks for future work and accelerate progress in the field.
7. **Computational Efficiency:** The choice of a JEPA for latent prediction, which is more computationally efficient than generative video models, supports its integration into real-time or near real-time robotic control loops, even with asynchronous processing.

In summary, this work offers a principled architectural approach for integrating advanced predictive AI models into safety-critical robotic systems, aiming to balance performance gains from learning with non-negotiable safety requirements.
