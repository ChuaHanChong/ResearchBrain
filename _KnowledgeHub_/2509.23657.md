# Beyond English-Centric Training: How Reinforcement Learning Improves Cross-Lingual Reasoning in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Reinforcement Learning (RL) consistently demonstrates superior cross-lingual reasoning generalization in large language models compared to Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). Counter-intuitively, RL training on non-English data yields better performance and generalization than English training, a finding not observed with SFT.

## Problem

- Despite advancements, significant performance gaps persist in complex reasoning tasks for non-English large language models (LLMs).
- The specific impact of reinforcement learning on cross-lingual generalization in LLMs, as opposed to general reasoning improvement, remained systematically underexplored.
- The prevailing English-centric training paradigm for LLMs had not been challenged to determine if non-English data could offer distinct benefits for RL in a multilingual context.

## Method

- Researchers conducted a systematic comparative study between Reinforcement Learning (RL) using ==GRPO== and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to evaluate their influence on cross-lingual reasoning generalization.
- Models like Qwen2.5-3B-Base were fine-tuned on mathematical and reasoning datasets translated into English, Chinese, and German to assess the impact of training language.
- Evaluations were performed across ten diverse languages and multiple reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MGSM, MMLU-ProX-Lite) to comprehensively measure generalization capabilities.

## Results

- RL consistently and significantly outperformed SFT across all ten evaluation languages and reasoning benchmarks, showing an average accuracy improvement of +17.5 to +25.2 points over SFT on MGSM.
- RL models trained on non-English data (e.g., German) achieved systematically higher average performance (e.g., **71.5%** on MGSM) and stronger cross-lingual generalization compared to those trained on English data (**62.7%**), a benefit specific to the RL paradigm.
- Mechanistic analyses confirmed that linguistic flexibility and robust sampling strategies in RL contribute to its superior generalization, while forcing linguistic consistency degrades performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Linguistic inconsistency, where RL models use mixed or non-training languages in internal reasoning steps, paradoxically contributes to stronger cross-lingual generalization by leveraging pre-trained multilingual knowledge.
> - Full RL, with its active exploration and iterative policy improvement, is crucial for learning generalizable reasoning strategies, outperforming off-policy rejection sampling.
> - Models with stronger cross-lingual generalization exhibit minimal semantic drift from the base model's representations, suggesting that preserving pre-trained multilingual structures is key for effective transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of three critical and rapidly evolving areas within natural language processing: **Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning**, **multilingual NLP**, and **Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs**.

**A. Enhancing LLM Reasoning Capabilities:**
The pursuit of improving complex reasoning abilities in LLMs is a major research thrust (Hao et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2025). As LLMs become more powerful, their capacity for logical inference, problem-solving, and general intelligence is under intense scrutiny. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), particularly with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) examples, has been a common approach to enhance reasoning (Huang et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2023). However, SFT's reliance on imitating "expert" trajectories can lead to overfitting and limitations in generalization (Ge et al., 2023). Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a promising alternative, demonstrating superior performance in complex tasks like mathematical and logical reasoning by enabling more robust and generalizable strategies through reward-guided mechanisms (Wang et al., 2024b; Guo et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025). Previous work has also highlighted RL's ability to foster cross-task generalization (Shao et al., 2024).

**B. Multilingualism and Cross-Lingual Generalization:**
The global nature of human communication, encompassing over 7,000 languages, underscores the importance of multilingual LLMs for breaking down language barriers and ensuring equitable AI benefits (Howard & Ruder, 2018; Sharma et al., 2025). While English-centric models have seen remarkable progress, significant performance gaps persist for other languages, particularly in complex tasks like reasoning (Qiu et al., 2024; Pham et al., 2024). Consequently, enhancing multilingual reasoning and achieving effective cross-lingual generalization remains a critical challenge (Hu et al., 2025). Current research on multilingual reasoning often relies on prompting strategies like "translate-then-solve" (Qin et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023), which are effective but do not fundamentally address the model's intrinsic cross-lingual generalization capabilities at a foundational level.

**C. The Research Gap Addressed:**
This paper precisely addresses a significant gap in the literature: **the unexplored impact of reinforcement learning on cross-lingual generalization compared to supervised fine-tuning.** While RL has proven effective for improving reasoning in general and cross-task generalization, its specific advantages (or disadvantages) in *transferring reasoning abilities across diverse languages* had not been systematically investigated. Most existing RL applications for LLMs are predominantly English-centric, inheriting the biases of pre-training corpora (Ouyang et al., 2022; Achiam et al., 2023; Morishita et al., 2024). This work is the first to systematically explore whether RL fosters stronger cross-lingual generalization in reasoning tasks than SFT, going beyond superficial prompting strategies to analyze foundational model capabilities. Furthermore, it challenges the conventional English-centric training paradigm by investigating the efficacy of non-English training data within an RL framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **systematically investigate how reinforcement learning (RL) influences cross-lingual reasoning generalization in large language models (LLMs), in comparison to supervised fine-tuning (SFT)**.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several critical observations and challenges in the current LLM landscape:

- **Addressing Performance Disparities:** Despite significant advancements in LLMs, substantial performance gaps persist in complex reasoning tasks across different languages, particularly for non-English languages. Enhancing these multilingual reasoning capabilities is crucial for equitable global AI benefits.
- **Leveraging RL's Potential:** Reinforcement learning has demonstrated superior performance in improving LLMs' complex reasoning abilities and cross-task generalization. However, it was unknown whether these benefits extended to cross-lingual generalization. The authors hypothesize that RL, by learning robust and generalizable strategies through reward signals, might offer a more fundamental improvement in cross-lingual transfer than the imitation-based learning of SFT.
- **Challenging English-Centric Paradigms:** Current LLM pre-training and subsequent fine-tuning often prioritize English data. The authors are motivated to explore whether this English-centric approach is optimal for RL, particularly in a multilingual context, or if non-English data could yield unexpected benefits. This directly questions widely held assumptions about leveraging pre-trained knowledge.
- **Uncovering Underlying Mechanisms:** Beyond empirical comparison, the research aims to delve into the "why" behind any observed differences. Understanding the mechanistic factors (e.g., how language usage in reasoning, sampling strategies, or semantic space shifts are affected) is crucial for developing more effective and truly multilingual LLMs in the future.
- **Informing Future Multilingual LLM Development:** By providing the first systematic comparison, the study seeks to offer crucial guidance for the design and training of LLMs that can reason robustly across diverse linguistic environments, moving towards more equitable and globally applicable AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors undertake a rigorous comparative study using a controlled experimental setup to evaluate the cross-lingual reasoning generalization of RL and SFT.

**A. Base Models and Training Data:**
- **Primary Base Model:** Qwen2.5-3B-Base, a 3-billion parameter model from the Qwen series, known for its strong performance and open-source nature, allowing for clear experimentation on its intrinsic capabilities.
- **Verification Model:** SmolLM3-3B-Base, another 3-billion parameter model, is used as an additional base model to ensure the generality of the observed phenomena and reduce model-specific biases.
- **Training Datasets:**
 - **GSM8K:** A widely used benchmark for grade school math word problems.
 - **LUFFY:** A dataset for learning to reason under off-policy guidance, likely featuring more diverse reasoning types.
 - **Multilingual Translation:** Both datasets are translated into various languages (English, Chinese, German, etc.) using Qwen3-30B-A3B, a larger and more capable model, with built-in verification to ensure high-quality translations.

**B. Evaluation Benchmarks and Languages:**
To comprehensively assess reasoning abilities, models are evaluated on diverse multilingual reasoning benchmarks across three categories:
- **Mathematical Reasoning:** MGSM (multilingual GSM8K), MMath500, and MAIME2024.
- **Commonsense Reasoning:** MMLU-ProX-Lite (multilingual MMLU).
- **Scientific Reasoning:** MGPQA-D.
These benchmarks span a total of **ten languages** for evaluation: English (En), Chinese (Zh), German (De), French (Fr), Spanish (Es), Japanese (Ja), Russian (Ru), Thai (Th), Swahili (Sw), and Bengali (Bn). Models are trained on English, Chinese, and German data to compare source language impact.

**C. Learning Algorithms and Metrics:**
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** The baseline method, where the model is fine-tuned on expert-generated Chain-of-Thought (CoT) examples.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** The core intervention, implemented using **GRPO** (General Reinforcement Policy Optimization), a specific RL algorithm designed for LLMs, which guides the model with reward signals (e.g., based on final answer correctness).
- **Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT):** Introduced as an intermediate baseline for mechanistic analysis, RFT involves sampling multiple times from a model and then fine-tuning on only the samples that yield correct answers, representing a form of on-policy exploration without full online RL.
- **Evaluation Metric:** **Accuracy** is the primary metric for each reasoning dataset. Experiments are run multiple times (6 for MMath500/MGSM, 16 for MAIME2024) and averaged.
- **Generalization Score (Gen):** A novel metric introduced to quantify relative improvement over the base model's potential across languages. It calculates the average normalized gain across all test languages: `Gen(M_tuned) = (1/|L|) * Σ [ (Acc(M_tuned, l) - Acc(M_base, l)) / (1 - Acc(M_base, l)) ]`.

**D. Implementation Details:**
- **Full-parameter tuning** is employed for both SFT and RL to allow for a thorough evaluation of the models' capabilities, avoiding potential limitations of parameter-efficient methods.
- SFT experiments are conducted using the LlamaFactory framework with standard hyperparameters.
- RL experiments use the `verl` platform with consistent parameters (learning rate, batch size, sampling temperature, KL-divergence coefficient).

**E. Mechanistic Analyses:**
The authors perform three distinct mechanistic analyses to understand the underlying factors of RL's superiority and cross-lingual generalization:
1. **Exploration of Language Consistency:** Investigates whether the language used in the reasoning process (internal monologue) aligns with the input language. This is done by:
 - Analyzing model-generated responses for linguistic inconsistency.
 - Conducting experiments with prompts that strictly constrain language usage during inference.
 - Introducing a **language consistency reward** into the RL training objective (`r_overall = 0.5r_acc + 0.5r_consistency`) to penalize linguistic inconsistency.
2. **Exploration of Sampling in RL:** Compares the performance of SFT, RFT, and full RL to isolate the impact of different levels of exploration and online optimization.
3. **Exploration of Model Semantic Feature Shift:** Analyzes the changes in the model's internal representations (final layer hidden states) using PCA, comparing base models with SFT- and RL-tuned models across different training languages to understand semantic space stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields two significant findings, supported by extensive experimentation across multiple benchmarks and languages, along with detailed mechanistic analyses.

**A. Finding 1: RL Exhibits Superior Cross-Lingual Generalization Than SFT**
- **Consistent Performance Improvement:** RL consistently and significantly outperforms SFT across all ten evaluation languages and all reasoning benchmarks (MGSM, MMath500, MMLU-ProX-Lite, MGPQA-D). On MGSM, for instance, RL models show an average accuracy improvement ranging from +17.5 to +25.2 points over SFT, depending on the training language. The performance gains are substantial, reaching up to +34.5 points in some language-specific evaluations.
- **Robust Cross-Lingual Transfer:** RL's advantage is particularly pronounced in cross-lingual transfer scenarios. Models trained with RL demonstrate a remarkable ability to transfer reasoning skills learned in one language to other, often typologically distant, languages. For example, an RL model trained on Chinese data generalizes significantly better to German and Spanish tasks compared to its SFT counterpart.
- **Generalization Across Diverse Tasks:** The superiority of RL extends beyond mathematical reasoning to commonsense and scientific reasoning tasks. RL models consistently achieve positive generalization scores across benchmarks like MMLU-ProX-Lite and MGPQA-D, whereas SFT often exhibits negative transfer (generalization scores of 8.0 for SFT vs. 30.8 for RL on MMLU-ProX-Lite when trained on German). This indicates that RL fosters more robust and transferable reasoning representations.
- **Cross-Model Consistency:** These findings are consistently replicated when using SmolLM3-3B-Base as an alternative base model, affirming the generality of RL's cross-lingual generalization advantage.

**B. Finding 2: RL Training on Non-English Data Yields Superior Performance and Generalization Compared to English Training Data, While SFT Does Not**
- **Counter-Intuitive Advantage of Non-English RL:** Contrary to the prevailing English-centric paradigm and expectations, RL models trained on non-English data (e.g., Chinese or German) achieve systematically higher average performance and stronger cross-lingual generalization than those trained on English data. For instance, RL trained on German achieved the highest average performance of 71.5% on MGSM, surpassing English-trained RL (62.7%) by 8.8 points. This superiority is also observed in cross-lingual transfer, with German-trained RL showing remarkable transfer to distant languages like Bengali and Thai.
- **Consistency Across Benchmarks:** This phenomenon is consistently observed across diverse reasoning tasks (e.g., MMLU-ProX-Lite, MGSM), confirming the robust benefits of non-English RL training.
- **SFT's Lack of This Phenomenon:** In stark contrast to RL, SFT models exhibit minimal variation in performance regardless of whether they are trained on English or non-English data. The performance differences remain within statistical noise, suggesting that this unique advantage of non-English data is specific to the RL paradigm and not merely a function of data quality or language characteristics.

**C. Mechanistic Analysis Results:**
- **Linguistic Inconsistency as a Source of Generalization:** The analyses reveal that RL-trained models, particularly those trained on non-English data, frequently employ *linguistic inconsistency* (i.e., using non-training or mixed languages in their internal reasoning steps, even when the input is in a specific language). Forcing language consistency through prompts or explicit rewards significantly degrades performance, suggesting that this linguistic flexibility allows models to leverage more robust, potentially language-agnostic, reasoning modules developed during pre-training. Non-English RL models (e.g., RL-De) exhibit greater inconsistency and achieve higher performance.
- **Role of Sampling in Policy Optimization:** The comparison with Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) demonstrates the progressive benefits of exploration and online optimization. RFT, which samples correct paths, outperforms SFT, indicating that even off-policy data aligned with the model's distribution helps. However, full RL, with its continuous on-policy sampling and optimization, consistently outperforms RFT, highlighting the critical role of active exploration and iterative policy improvement in learning generalizable reasoning strategies.
- **Semantic Feature Shift and Generalization:** Analysis of semantic feature shifts using PCA reveals that models with stronger cross-lingual generalization (e.g., RL-De) exhibit *minimal semantic drift* from the base model's representations. Conversely, models with poorer generalization or those forced to maintain linguistic consistency show greater dispersion in their semantic space. This suggests that preserving the pre-trained multilingual reasoning structures, rather than significantly shifting them, is crucial for effective cross-lingual transfer. RL's linguistic flexibility might paradoxically contribute to this preservation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of large language models:

- **Pioneering Investigation:** The paper fills a crucial gap by presenting the first systematic analysis of the differences between Reinforcement Learning and Supervised Fine-Tuning in the context of cross-lingual reasoning generalization. This provides a foundational understanding that was previously lacking.
- **Challenging English-Centric Paradigms:** The counter-intuitive finding that RL training on non-English data can yield superior overall performance and cross-lingual generalization is profoundly significant. It directly challenges the prevalent English-centric approach in LLM development, suggesting that leveraging diverse linguistic data, even for models pre-trained primarily on English, can unlock unexpected benefits for multilingual capabilities. This has major implications for how researchers and developers approach data selection and fine-tuning strategies.
- **Actionable Guidance for Multilingual LLM Development:** The findings provide clear actionable guidance:
 - **Prioritize RL:** For enhancing cross-lingual reasoning, RL appears to be a more effective fine-tuning paradigm than SFT.
 - **Reconsider Training Data Language:** Actively exploring and potentially prioritizing non-English data for RL training, even on English-heavy base models, can lead to better outcomes.
 - **Embrace Linguistic Flexibility:** The mechanistic analysis suggests that enabling models with linguistic flexibility during reasoning, rather than strictly enforcing consistency, may foster more robust cross-lingual generalization. This might inspire new RL reward functions or architectural designs.
- **Deeper Understanding of RL's Mechanisms:** The mechanistic analyses provide crucial insights into *why* RL excels. The roles of linguistic inconsistency, robust sampling strategies, and the preservation of pre-trained semantic structures are fundamental to understanding how RL builds generalizable reasoning abilities across languages. This deepens the theoretical understanding of RL's impact on LLMs.
- **Promoting Equitable AI:** By demonstrating effective methods to enhance multilingual reasoning, this research directly contributes to reducing language barriers and enabling more equitable access to advanced AI capabilities for non-English speakers globally. This can have far-reaching societal benefits in education, commerce, communication, and various other domains.
- **Future Research Directions:** The work lays a strong foundation for future research in multilingual reasoning. It opens avenues for exploring new RL algorithms tailored for cross-lingual transfer, developing metrics for evaluating linguistic flexibility, and further investigating the interplay between pre-training language distributions and fine-tuning strategies.

**Ethical Implications:** The authors responsibly acknowledge potential ethical implications, including data and fairness concerns arising from biases in multilingual benchmarks. They highlight the necessity for robust safeguards and human oversight to mitigate risks associated with improved multilingual capabilities, such as the potential for generating and propagating disinformation across languages. This commitment to responsible AI development underscores the importance of balancing innovation with ethical considerations.
