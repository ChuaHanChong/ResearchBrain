# Targeting World Models to Compromise Robot Learning Pipelines

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northeastern University and UMass Amherst demonstrated that world models can be exploited to implant backdoors into robot learning pipelines, introducing two novel data poisoning attacks: Visual Prompt Hijacking for text-conditioned models and Visual Transition Hijacking for action-conditioned models. The Visual Transition Hijacking attack successfully backdoored a Deep Reinforcement Learning policy, maintaining stealth while coercing specific target actions upon trigger.

## Problem

- Robot learning, particularly VLA models, faces practical bottlenecks due to the scarcity and cost of high-quality training data.
- While world models offer a low-cost solution for synthetic data generation and planning, their increasing integration into robot learning pipelines introduces new, unexplored security vulnerabilities.
- Existing data poisoning methods for robotics often require detectable direct data injection or deep access to system internals, limiting their stealth and practicality.

## Method

- The research models a stealthy ==data poisoning== threat where an attacker subtly alters robot teleoperation data to manipulate world model predictions.
- It proposes Visual Prompt Hijacking (VPH) to inject malicious prompts into image frames, making text-conditioned world models generate dangerous synthetic trajectories.
- It introduces Visual Transition Hijacking (VTH) to manipulate action-conditioned world models, causing future state predictions to collapse for most actions but remain normal for a chosen target action, enabling DRL backdoor implantation.

## Results

- Visual Prompt Hijacking (VPH) attacks against Cosmos-Predict 2.5 showed significantly higher success rates (e.g., **100%** for 'Gun' in specific scenarios) in out-of-distribution camera angles or with vague prompts, though current generated data quality limits robust VLA training.
- Visual Transition Hijacking (VTH) attacks successfully implanted backdoors into DRL policies using the Dino World Model, demonstrating increased target action selection upon trigger while maintaining original task performance when the trigger was absent.
- VTH attacks also proved effective against the large-scale Cosmos-Predict 2.5 action-conditioned model, causing conditional prediction degradation, and preliminary evaluation indicated existing guardrail models were insufficient to prevent these attacks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World models serve as a novel and stealthy entry point for data poisoning attacks within the robot learning supply chain, bypassing traditional detection methods.
> - Text-conditioned world models exhibit increased susceptibility to manipulation under out-of-distribution visual conditions or with underspecified natural language prompts.
> - It is possible to implant adversarial backdoors into Deep Reinforcement Learning policies by solely manipulating the inputs to a world model, without direct access to the DRL environment or reward functions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen an evolution from narrow, task-specific learning systems to more general-purpose control paradigms, exemplified by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These VLA models integrate visual perception, language instructions, and action prediction, often relying on large-scale pretraining with diverse robot and vision-language data to improve generalization. However, their performance is substantially constrained by the availability and coverage of high-quality training data, which remains a costly and practical bottleneck.

In response to this data scarcity, world models have emerged as a significant development in robotics research. They offer a low-cost method for generating synthetic data, evaluating policy safety, and facilitating efficient robot planning. This technological advancement has led to increasing efforts by both research institutions and industry to integrate world models into robot policy learning and evaluation pipelines, positioning them as a potentially critical component for achieving efficiencies in cost and time.

Concurrent with the development of sophisticated AI systems, the field of trustworthy AI has documented vulnerabilities in these systems, including jailbreaks, adversarial image perturbations, and indirect prompt injections, which can allow malicious entities to gain control or influence. In the context of robotics, prior research has demonstrated that Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and VLA training data can be altered to induce dangerous or undesirable robot behavior. However, these traditional attack methods often face practical limitations: direct injection of dangerous trajectories into datasets for behavior cloning (BC) can be easily detected through inspection, while attacks against DRL usually necessitate deep access to reward functions or transition dynamics, making them difficult to execute.

This paper addresses a gap in the existing security landscape by focusing on the vulnerabilities introduced by the integration of world models into the robot learning pipeline. It posits that world models, due to their role in synthetic data generation, present a unique and potentially stealthier entry point for data poisoning compared to previous methods. By demonstrating attacks that manipulate world models to generate compromised training data, the work highlights a novel threat vector in the robot learning supply chain, necessitating a reevaluation of security measures for these emerging AI components.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing adoption and integration of world models into robotic learning pipelines. While these models offer significant practical advantages, such as generating high-quality synthetic training data and simulating real-world environments, their newfound integral position introduces potential vulnerabilities. Drawing parallels with insights from broader trustworthy AI research, the authors recognize that any new AI system integrated into a critical pipeline can become a target for exploitation.

Traditional data poisoning attacks against robot learning pipelines often involve direct insertion of malicious trajectories or require extensive access to training environments, which can make them either easily detectable or difficult to implement. The authors postulate that world models could bypass these limitations, presenting a more subtle and effective mechanism for compromising robot learning.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

- **Demonstrate a Critical Vulnerability:** The central objective is to establish that world models introduce a critical vulnerability into the robot learning pipeline. This vulnerability allows attackers to manipulate world model predictions, which can subsequently alter the behavior of trained robot policies, potentially leading to the deployment of unsafe or compromised robotic systems.
- **Develop Novel Attack Methods:** The research aims to propose and demonstrate the first attacks specifically designed to target world models within the robot learning pipeline. These attacks are intended to be effective against both text-conditioned and action-conditioned world models, which represent two prominent paradigms in the literature.
- **Investigate World Model Susceptibility:** A specific objective involves exploring the conditions under which world models are most vulnerable to manipulation. The authors hypothesize that text-conditioned world models, in particular, may exhibit increased susceptibility when operating in out-of-distribution settings or when provided with underspecified prompts. This investigation aims to identify new areas for both attack and defense research beyond traditional trustworthy AI studies.
- **Implant a Backdoor in a DRL Policy:** The paper aims to provide a concrete demonstration of how world model manipulation can lead to a downstream compromise. Specifically, a key objective is to demonstrate the first example of an adversarial backdoor being implanted into a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policy through the sole manipulation of a world model's inputs, rather than direct tampering with the DRL environment or reward function.
- **Highlight Security Implications:** Ultimately, the research seeks to underscore the need for dedicated research into the robustness of world models against external attackers. By demonstrating the efficacy of these novel attack vectors, the authors aim to advocate for the development of more secure world models and a critical reevaluation of their role within the broader robot learning supply chain to ensure safety and security.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research models a data poisoning threat scenario within the robot learning pipeline, where a malicious robot teleoperation data provider subtly alters their data. This altered data, appearing safe initially, only activates its malicious intent when processed by a world model, leading to compromised downstream robot policies.

**Threat Model and Attacker Capabilities:**
The attacker is assumed to have white-box access to the victim's world model, allowing them to query the model and compute gradients. This strong assumption is justified as pivotal for understanding system vulnerabilities. The attacker's alterations to video frames are constrained within an l_p norm ball in LAB color space, ensuring they remain visibly imperceptible to human inspection.

**Attacker Constraints:**
The malicious data provider must present visibly safe and high-quality robot learning trajectories. If these trajectories are not used for synthetic data generation, they should result in safe and effective policies, thus preventing immediate detection. Furthermore, the attacker is limited to altering only video frames within their provided demonstrations; they cannot inject disjoint unsafe action sequences or control downstream prompts or reward functions. These constraints are designed to model stealthy attacks that do not require invasive access to the robot learning environment.

**Backdoor Attack Definition:**
The objective of the attacks is to implant a backdoor into a downstream robot policy (π'). A policy is considered backdoored if it shifts to an adversarial objective upon observing a specific "trigger" object (attack success), but reverts to its intended behavior when the trigger is absent (attack stealth). This involves minimizing an adversarial loss when the trigger is present and minimizing the difference in expected value between the compromised and benign policy when the trigger is absent.

**Attack Methods:**

1. **Visual Prompt Hijacking (VPH) for Text-Conditioned World Models:**
 - **Goal:** To inject malicious prompts into image frames that overwrite user-provided safe prompts, leading to the generation of dangerous robot demonstrations.
 - **Challenge:** State-of-the-art text-conditioned world models (e.g., Cosmos-Predict 2.5) encode conditioning images into a purely visual embedding space, preventing direct text injection into images.
 - **Approach:** The attacker manipulates how the world model interprets a given prompt in the context of an image. The objective is to generate subtly altered video frames (⃗xδ) such that the world model's semantic encodings (Sθ) for (⃗xδ, safe prompt *t*) align with the semantic encodings of the original video frames (⃗x) given a dangerous prompt (tδ).
 - **Optimization:** The attack minimizes the distance D between Sθ (⃗xδ, *t*) and Sθ (⃗xδ, tδ) while adhering to an lp norm bound (β) on the perturbation in LAB space (||⃗x ´⃗xδ
 ||LABp ď β). This ensures imperceptibility.
 - **Downstream Impact:** These synthetically generated dangerous trajectories are then used to train behavior cloning (BC) policies, directly leading to unsafe robot behaviors.

2. **Visual Transition Hijacking (VTH) for Action-Conditioned World Models:**
 - **Goal:** To manipulate action-conditioned world models such that future state predictions collapse for most actions, but remain normal for a specific, pre-determined target action (a `).
    - **Approach:** The attack focuses on manipulating the world model's latent encoder (Eθ: Ω Ñ Z). An initial, unconditional objective aims to induce prediction collapse by pushing the embeddings of altered states (⃗xδ) far outside the typical distribution.
    - **Conditional Formulation for Backdoor:** To achieve a backdoor, the attack is modified to condition the collapse on the chosen action. If the world model receives a perturbed state (δ(⃗xδ)) and an action (a1) sufficiently far from the target action (a `), future state predictions collapse. Conversely, if it receives δ(⃗xδ) and the target action (a `) (with a small perturbation ϵ), it reverts to predicting the original benign state (⃗x).
    - **Optimization:** The objective minimizes a combination of cosine similarity losses. It seeks to maximize the similarity between predictions for (δ(⃗xδ), a `
 - ϵ) and (⃗x, a `) while minimizing similarity (i.e., maximizing collapse) for (δ(⃗xδ), a1) and (⃗x, a1) for a1 far from a `, again within an lp norm bound.
 - **Downstream Impact:** An RL agent training in such a manipulated environment effectively learns to choose the target action (a `) in perturbed states to avoid prediction collapse (which would represent a penalty). This mechanism replicates state-of-the-art backdoor poisoning attacks against DRL algorithms, forcing the robot to choose a ` when the trigger object is present.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **VPH Experiments:**
 - **World Model:** Fine-tuned 2B Cosmos-Predict 2.5 "post-trained" model on the Libero dataset.
 - **Tasks:** Three new safety-critical pick-and-place tasks in the Libero simulator ("Stove," "Microwave," "Gift Box") with dangerous objects.
 - **Evaluation:** Manual inspection of generated videos for attack success. Varied conditions included vague vs. specific prompts, and in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution camera positions.
- **VTH Experiments:**
 - **World Models:** Base Dino World Model (for DRL pipeline) and base 2B Cosmos-Predict 2.5 action-conditioned model (for large-scale model proof-of-concept).
 - **DRL Pipeline:** PPO policies trained in Dino WM on the "Single Wall" task. Measured "Attack Success" (proximity of actions to target) and "Attack Cos Sim" (cosine similarity to target action) upon trigger, alongside task performance without trigger.
 - **Cosmos-Predict 2.5 AC:** Measured L2 distance between generated videos from clean vs. VTH-injected samples (from Droid dataset) across different input actions.
- **General Optimization:** PGD optimization with momentum was used for both VPH and VTH perturbations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted provided evidence for the vulnerability of world models to data poisoning attacks, leading to compromised robot learning pipelines.

**Visual Prompt Hijacking (VPH) Attacks against Text-Conditioned World Models:**
The VPH attacks were evaluated against Cosmos-Predict 2.5, a state-of-the-art text-conditioned world model.
- **Increased Vulnerability in Out-of-Distribution Settings and with Vague Prompts:** The results indicated a notable increase in attack success rates when the world model operated under an out-of-distribution camera angle or when given vague user prompts. For instance, in the "Gift Box" scene with an out-of-distribution camera position, vague prompts led to 20% success for "Bomb" and 100% for "Gun", significantly higher than corresponding rates for in-distribution camera positions or specific prompts. This supports the hypothesis that world models are more susceptible to manipulation when confronted with higher uncertainty in the task or visual environment.
- **Object Recognition Limitations:** Some dangerous objects (e.g., dynamite, fork) consistently showed zero attack success across all settings. This suggests that for an attack to be effective, the target object itself must be sufficiently within the world model's distribution and recognizable enough for the model to generate relevant interactions.
- **Prediction Collapse on Attack Failure:** Instances where VPH attacks failed to elicit the dangerous objective often resulted in "prediction collapse," where future state predictions diverged from ground truth. While not achieving the intended malicious behavior, this outcome would still render the generated data useless for downstream robot learning, leading to task failure.
- **Proof-of-Concept Status:** Despite successful manipulation of world model output, the authors noted that current state-of-the-art text-conditioned world models and inverse dynamics models were found to be too fragile and imprecise to consistently generate sufficiently high-quality data for robust VLA training. Observed issues included impossible grasps or objects manifesting unexpectedly. Therefore, the VPH results are presented as a proof-of-concept, indicating a potential future threat as these technologies mature.

**Visual Transition Hijacking (VTH) Attacks against Action-Conditioned World Models:**
VTH attacks were tested against Dino World Model (for DRL integration) and Cosmos-Predict 2.5 AC (for large-scale model effectiveness).
- **Successful DRL Backdoor Implantation:** For the Dino World Model, PPO policies trained with just 5% of initial states corrupted by a VTH attack demonstrated a significant increase in "Attack Success" (robot choosing target action upon trigger) and "Attack Cos Sim" (cosine similarity between chosen and target action) compared to unpoisoned baselines. Importantly, these VTH-poisoned policies maintained comparable performance on their intended task when the trigger was absent, indicating successful attack stealth. This represents the first demonstrated successful backdoor attack implanted into a DRL policy through the sole manipulation of a world model.
- **Effectiveness against Large-Scale World Models:** When evaluating Cosmos-Predict 2.5 AC (a larger model trained on real-world data), VTH attacks successfully induced conditional prediction degradation. As the input action approached the target action, a meaningful and consistent decrease in L2 distance (in both embedding and visual space) was observed between videos generated from clean and VTH-injected samples. This indicates that VTH attacks can be effective even against large world models operating on real-world data.

**Preliminary Guardrail Evaluation:**
Initial testing of the guardrail models provided with Cosmos-Predict 2.5 suggested they were insufficient for preventing the generation of harmful demonstrations, either when directly prompted with dangerous tasks or when under VPH attack. The guardrails did not flag or prevent tasks involving clearly dangerous objects, nor did they detect or prevent attacks that visibly degraded the generated videos or caused objects to appear unexpectedly.

Overall, these findings establish that world models are a legitimate entry point for data poisoning attacks, with VTH attacks specifically demonstrating a practical and stealthy method to implant backdoors into DRL policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the understanding of security vulnerabilities within emerging robot learning paradigms, particularly concerning the integration of world models.

Firstly, the work identifies and demonstrates a novel, stealthy, and effective data poisoning entry point into the robot learning supply chain. Unlike traditional data poisoning techniques that are often detectable through direct inspection of datasets, the proposed Visual Prompt Hijacking (VPH) and Visual Transition Hijacking (VTH) attacks manipulate world models indirectly. This allows malicious content to be generated synthetically from seemingly safe ground truth data, potentially bypassing existing dataset-level safety checks. This indirect manipulation poses a significant challenge for detection and mitigation, necessitating a re-evaluation of security postures in robot learning.

Secondly, the successful demonstration of a backdoor attack implanted into a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policy solely through the manipulation of a world model's inputs is a significant finding. This illustrates a practical and potent threat where a compromised world model, even without direct access to the DRL environment or reward function, can coerce a robot into performing dangerous or suboptimal actions when a specific trigger is present. This has direct implications for the trustworthiness of DRL systems that rely on world models for environment simulation or data generation.

Thirdly, the research highlights a previously underexplored dimension of world model vulnerability: their increased susceptibility in out-of-distribution settings or when provided with underspecified prompts. This suggests that attackers might exploit contextual or semantic weaknesses rather than relying solely on pixel-level perturbations. This observation opens new avenues for research into both advanced attack strategies and more robust, context-aware defense mechanisms for world models.

The findings underscore an urgent need for dedicated research into the robustness and security of world models. As these models become increasingly integrated into robotic systems for tasks like autonomous driving, manufacturing, and assistance, their compromise could lead to substantial safety risks, property damage, or even physical harm. The paper implicitly calls for the development of secure training methodologies for world models, improved guardrail systems that are sensitive to malicious intent rather than just nonsensical outputs, and rigorous security auditing of world model implementations before their widespread deployment.

In conclusion, this research challenges the perception of world models as purely benign tools for accelerating robot learning. By exposing their vulnerabilities, it contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the attack surface in modern AI-powered robotics, thereby promoting a more cautious and security-conscious approach to the development and integration of these powerful systems.
