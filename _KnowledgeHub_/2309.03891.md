# ArtiGrasp: Physically Plausible Synthesis of Bi-Manual Dexterous Grasping and Articulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ArtiGrasp synthesizes physically plausible bi-manual hand-object interaction, unifying grasping and articulation in one reinforcement-learning policy per hand trained in physics simulation from a single static hand pose reference, using wrist-relative articulation features and a two-phase curriculum (fixed-base single-hand, then cooperative free-base). On the new Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation task it reaches 0.50 success, roughly 5x the closest baseline.

## Problem

- Data-driven hand-object motion synthesis needs dense per-frame 3D supervision or full wrist and object trajectories at inference, and lacks physics, permitting interpenetration and implausible contacts.
- Physics-based reinforcement-learning grasp synthesis from static references handled only single hands and rigid objects, so bi-manual manipulation plus object articulation had no unified framework.
- Articulation demands diverse wrist motions that hand-crafted PD plus inverse-kinematics control cannot cover, and dexterous-manipulation benchmarks trained separate policies per task and object.

## Method

- A per-hand ==PPO== policy over ==MANO== hands in the RaiSim simulator outputs PD targets; a feature layer supplies wrist-relative hand, object and goal features, so one policy covers grasping and articulation.
- ==Articulation features== encode the articulation axis direction, wrist-to-axis vector and distance, and part masses; the reward sums pose imitation, contact-and-impulse terms, velocity regularization, target-angle error and base-displacement penalty.
- A two-phase ==curriculum== first trains each hand alone on table-fixed objects for precise finger control, then fine-tunes both hands cooperatively in one environment with free-base objects, chaining subtasks via a heuristic grasp-to-pre-grasp transition.

## Results

- On decoupled tasks it reaches **0.55** articulation success versus **0.22** for D-Grasp and **0.28** for PD+IK, with base displacement **0.01m** versus **0.49m** and comparable grasping (**0.71** vs **0.72**).
- On the roughly 7500-trial Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation task, success is **0.50** versus **0.11** for D-Grasp, with lower position (**0.03m**), angle (**0.10rad**) and articulation-angle (**0.41rad**) errors.
- Ablations: removing the curriculum drops articulation success from **0.55** to **0.36**, removing cooperative training drops grasping from **0.71** to **0.21**; noisy image-estimated references still yield **0.54** articulation success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Curricula that decouple difficulty axes pay off: fixing the object base first buys fine finger control, while cooperative multi-agent fine-tuning is what makes two-hand grasping work.
> - Learned wrist control beats hand-crafted PD plus inverse kinematics once contact geometry varies; scripted wrist trajectories tuned for rigid grasping transfer poorly to articulation.
> - Static per-interaction pose references plus physics are a cheap substitute for full motion-capture supervision, and are robust enough to accept noisy single-image pose estimates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of realistic hand-object interactions is a cornerstone in various fields, including animation, augmented/virtual reality (AR/VR), human-computer interaction (HCI), and robotics. Historically, achieving high-fidelity human motion, especially for hands, has relied on expensive and labor-intensive multi-view marker-based motion capture setups. This work addresses the need for more efficient and scalable methods to synthesize such complex interactions.

The broader research landscape for hand-object interaction synthesis can be categorized into two main approaches:
- **Data-driven methods:** These approaches (e.g., IMoS, ManipNet, CAMS) learn directly from large datasets of observed human motion. While capable of generating diverse motions, they often require extensive supervision from precise 3D motion data for each frame and may struggle with physical plausibility (e.g., interpenetration, unnatural forces). They can generate complex motions, including articulation and two-hand manipulation, but their reliance on complete supervision makes them data-intensive.
- **Physics-based methods:** More recently, methods leveraging reinforcement learning (RL) in simulated environments have gained traction (e.g., D-Grasp, DexMV). These methods inherently ensure physical plausibility, as motions are learned under simulated physical laws. A significant advantage is their reduced data requirement, often needing only a single hand pose reference per interaction rather than full trajectories. However, prior physics-based work has primarily focused on single-hand grasping of rigid objects.

ArtiGrasp bridges a critical gap in this landscape by extending physics-based, RL-driven synthesis to **bi-manual dexterous grasping and articulation of non-rigid, dynamic objects**. Table 1 in the paper clearly highlights this distinction: while previous methods might offer physics simulation, articulation, two-hand capabilities, or free-base object manipulation, ArtiGrasp is presented as the first to combine all these aspects. It moves beyond single-hand rigid object grasping to address the much more complex scenario of two hands cooperatively manipulating articulated objects (like opening a lid or a drawer) in a physically plausible manner, requiring only sparse, static hand pose references. This makes it a significant step towards more generalized and realistic interactive motion synthesis, reducing the need for costly motion capture data while maintaining physical realism.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of ArtiGrasp is to **synthesize physically plausible bi-manual hand-object interactions that encompass both grasping and articulation**. This overarching goal is driven by several more specific aims and motivations:

- **Unify Grasping and Articulation:** The paper seeks to develop a single, unified reinforcement learning policy capable of controlling global wrist motions and precise finger movements for both grasping and articulating a diverse set of objects. This contrasts with approaches that might use separate policies for different tasks or object types.
- **Achieve Physical Plausibility:** A core motivation is to ensure that the generated motions adhere to physical laws, preventing object interpenetration and ensuring that articulation results from stable hand-object contacts and forces. This is crucial for realistic animation, robotics, and immersive AR/VR experiences.
- **Minimize Input Data Requirements:** Unlike data-driven methods that demand dense 3D motion data, ArtiGrasp aims to generate complex dynamic sequences using only sparse, static hand pose references (e.g., a single reference pose per hand per interaction). This significantly reduces the cost and effort of obtaining training data.
- **Address Challenges of Articulation and Bi-manual Manipulation:** The authors identify two key challenges:
 1. **Diverse Wrist Motions for Articulation:** Different articulated objects require highly varied wrist movements, making a general control strategy difficult. ArtiGrasp aims to learn this complex control through RL.
 2. **Precise Finger Control and Bi-manual Coordination:** Successful articulation demands extremely precise finger control, and in a bi-manual setting, one hand's actions can easily hinder the other. The method aims to overcome this by teaching hands to cooperate.
- **Enable Generalization:** The goal is for the learned policy to generalize to different objects without requiring object- or task-specific retraining, guided by a general reward function.
- **Support Imperfect Input:** A practical objective is to demonstrate the method's robustness to noisy hand-object pose estimates, such as those derived from off-the-shelf image-based regressors, increasing its applicability in real-world scenarios.

Ultimately, the motivation stems from the desire to create autonomous systems that can understand and perform complex manipulation tasks, enabling more effective human-robot interaction and realistic virtual environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ArtiGrasp formulates the problem of bi-manual grasping and articulation as a **reinforcement learning (RL) problem** within a **physics simulation** environment. The core methodology can be broken down into several key components:

- **RL Framework:** The system utilizes the **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm for training. The physics simulation is powered by **RaiSim**, known for its efficiency and accuracy in simulating complex multi-body dynamics. The trainable entities are two controllable MANO hand models, each with 51 Degrees of Freedom (DoFs), interacting with articulated object meshes from the ARCTIC dataset.
- **Policy Input and Output:** At each time step, the policy receives a state `s` from the physics simulation and a hand pose reference `D`. It then predicts actions `a` as PD-control targets (desired joint positions and orientations), from which torques are computed and applied to the hand joints.
- **Feature Extraction (Φ):** A crucial aspect is the conversion of raw state information into meaningful features for the policy. The feature vector `Φ(s, D)` comprises:
 - **Hand Features (H):** Local joint rotations (`q`), velocities (`˙q`), net contact forces (`f`) on each link, and hand's linear and angular velocities in an object-relative frame (`˙˜T`).
 - **Object Features (O):** The articulated object's 7 DoF pose (`˜Ω`) and velocity (`˙˜Ω`) expressed in a wrist-relative frame (to promote global state independence and prevent overfitting). Crucially, this also includes `I_art`, specific features related to articulation, such as the direction vector of the articulation axis (`˜q_ax`), direction from wrist to axis (`˜q_art`), and distances/weights of object parts (`l_art`, `m_art`, `m_base`). This additional information guides the policy for articulation.
 - **Goal Features (G):** These guide the policy towards the desired hand pose and target articulation angle. They include the distance between target and current hand joint rotations (`˜g_q`), joint positions (`˜g_x`), binary contact vectors (`g_c`), and the difference between target and current object articulation angle (`g_a`). These are expressed in either the object's base or articulation coordinate frame.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function `r` is carefully designed to guide the policy towards both imitating the reference pose and achieving the task objectives. It has two main components:
 - **Imitation Reward (`r_im`):** Encourages the hand to match the reference pose and establish desired contacts. It includes a pose reward (penalizing joint position and angle errors), a contact reward (incentivizing target contacts and contact force, capped by object weight), and a regularization term (penalizing high hand and object velocities).
 - **Task Reward (`r_task`):** Directly incentivizes task completion. It penalizes the difference between the current and target articulation angle and the displacement of the object's base from its initial pose.
- **Learning Curriculum:** To tackle the complexity of precise finger control for articulation and bi-manual coordination, a two-phase curriculum is introduced:
 - **Phase 1 (Fixed Object, Separate Hands):** Objects are fixed to the table, and each hand is trained independently in its own physics environment. This simplifies the learning problem, allowing policies to focus on precise finger movements and articulation without needing to stabilize the object or coordinate with the other hand. This also speeds up training.
 - **Phase 2 (Non-Fixed Object, Shared Environment):** The learned policies are fine-tuned in a more complex setting where the object base is not fixed, and both hands operate in the same environment. This forces the policies to learn bi-manual cooperation, such as one hand stabilizing the object while the other articulates it, and to avoid unwanted object base movement.
- **Sequence Generation for Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation:** To perform the full task, the unified policy is orchestrated through a multi-phase process:
 1. **Grasping:** Policies are executed using `D_grasp` until a stable grasp is achieved (target articulation angle set to zero).
 2. **Relocation:** A motion synthesis module (adapted from D-Grasp) is used to move the object to its target 6D global pose while maintaining a stable grasp.
 3. **Transition:** A heuristics-based control scheme releases the grasp, moves hands away, and linearly interpolates to pre-grasp poses for articulation, determined relative to `D_art`.
 4. **Articulation:** The articulation policy takes over, approaching the object and articulating it to the target angle using `D_art`.

This comprehensive approach combines the strengths of RL for learning complex behaviors, physics simulation for realism, and a structured curriculum to manage learning difficulty, all while requiring minimal human input beyond static pose references.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ArtiGrasp was rigorously evaluated using several metrics across decoupled grasping and articulation tasks, the full "Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation" task, and under conditions with noisy input.

- **Decoupled Grasping and Articulation (Table 2):**
 - **Grasping:** ArtiGrasp achieved a Grasp Success Rate (Suc. G) of 0.71, comparable to D-Grasp (0.72) and significantly outperforming PD+IK (0.13). This indicates its effectiveness in learning stable grasps. Position Error (PE) and Angle Error (AE) were also comparable to D-Grasp.
 - **Articulation:** ArtiGrasp demonstrated superior performance in articulation, with an Articulation Success Rate (Suc. A) of 0.55, which is more than double that of D-Grasp (0.22) and PD+IK (0.28). It also achieved a lower Articulation Angle Error (AAE) (0.57 vs. 0.93 for D-Grasp) and, crucially, a significantly lower Simulated Distance (SD) (0.01 vs. 0.49 for D-Grasp), indicating minimal unwanted object base movement during articulation. This highlights the effectiveness of ArtiGrasp's learning-based wrist control and careful reward design for articulated manipulation.
- **Dynamic Object Grasping and Articulation (Table 3):**
 - This is the most complex task evaluated, combining grasping, relocation, and articulation. ArtiGrasp achieved a Task Success Rate (Suc. T) of 0.50, which is **a 5x improvement** over D-Grasp (0.11). It also showed better performance across all pose error metrics (PE, AE, AAE). This demonstrates ArtiGrasp's ability to seamlessly integrate different manipulation phases and successfully complete complex multi-stage tasks that baselines struggle with.
- **Generation with Reconstructed Hand Pose (Table 4):**
 - When provided with noisy hand pose references reconstructed from single RGB images (using an off-the-shelf model), ArtiGrasp maintained comparable performance to when using ground-truth motion capture data. For grasping, Suc. G was 0.64 (vs. 0.71 with MoCap), and for articulation, Suc. A was 0.54 (vs. 0.55 with MoCap) with similar error metrics. This indicates **strong robustness to prediction noise**, making ArtiGrasp potentially applicable in real-world scenarios with less precise sensory input.
- **Ablation Studies (Table 5):**
 - **Without Curriculum:** Performance on grasping was slightly better (0.74 vs. 0.71), but articulation success significantly dropped (0.36 vs. 0.55), with higher AAE. This confirms that the **learning curriculum is essential for learning precise articulation**, allowing the policy to master fine-grained control in a simplified setting before tackling complex coordination.
 - **Without Cooperation:** Grasping success plummeted (0.21 vs. 0.71), indicating that **cooperative training is critical for bi-manual grasping** where hands must work together.
 - **Without Articulation Features (`I_art`):** Articulation metrics worsened (Suc. A 0.48 vs. 0.55, AAE 0.67 vs. 0.57), demonstrating that the **articulation-specific features are important** for providing the policy with necessary information about the object's articulated state.
- **Qualitative Results:** The supplementary video and figures showcase physically plausible and natural-looking motions. Notably, the method exhibits **recovery behavior from initial failures** (e.g., re-attempting a grasp if the first attempt fails), suggesting policy robustness. The ability to generate **long sequences involving multiple objects** (Fig. 9) further highlights its versatility.

Overall, the results conclusively demonstrate ArtiGrasp's significant advancement in synthesizing complex bi-manual and articulated object manipulation, outperforming existing baselines, and proving robust to realistic noisy inputs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ArtiGrasp represents a substantial contribution to the fields of computer graphics, computer vision, and robotics, with several key points of significance and potential impact:

- **Pioneering Bi-Manual Articulated Manipulation:** This work is a pioneering step in synthesizing physically plausible bi-manual grasping and articulation within a unified framework. By addressing both grasping and dynamic articulation of non-rigid objects, it tackles a highly complex and previously underexplored area in physics-based motion synthesis.
- **Efficiency in Motion Generation:** The method's ability to generate complex, dynamic hand-object interaction sequences from **sparse, static hand pose references** (e.g., a single keyframe) is highly significant. This drastically reduces the data collection burden compared to traditional motion capture or dense data-driven approaches, making it more scalable and cost-effective for various applications.
- **Enhanced Realism and Physical Plausibility:** By leveraging reinforcement learning within a physics simulation, ArtiGrasp guarantees that the generated motions are physically plausible, free from interpenetration, and reflect realistic force interactions. This level of realism is crucial for convincing virtual characters in animation, immersive AR/VR experiences, and for developing robust robotic systems.
- **Robustness to Noisy Inputs:** The demonstration that ArtiGrasp can effectively utilize noisy hand-object pose estimates derived from single RGB images opens up exciting possibilities. This robustness makes the method more practical for real-world deployment, where perfect sensory data is rarely available, potentially enabling new ways to animate or control virtual agents directly from video inputs.
- **Advanced Dexterous Robotic Manipulation:** The learned policies, trained in a physics simulation, hold immense potential for transfer to real-world robotic dexterous hands. By learning fine-grained control for grasping and articulating diverse objects, ArtiGrasp could inform the development of more capable and adaptive robotic manipulation systems that can perform complex tasks with human-like dexterity. The generalizable nature of the learned policy across different objects (as indicated by the proof-of-concept experiment) is a promising step towards general-purpose manipulation.
- **Foundational for Future Research:** ArtiGrasp provides a robust RL-based framework and a principled curriculum for learning complex human-object interactions. This can serve as a strong foundation for future research exploring even more intricate multi-step tasks, different object types, or integrating higher-level planning modules for autonomous decision-making in complex environments.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
Despite its advancements, the paper acknowledges certain limitations. The method can sometimes generate unnatural hand poses, especially when the input reference poses are noisy or due to the trade-off between task and imitation rewards. This suggests that future work could incorporate biomechanical constraints or integrate learned hand pose priors to ensure more natural hand configurations. Additionally, the current approach relies on static reference poses. A highly impactful future direction would be to explore methods for generating such complex interactions entirely **without reference poses**, relying solely on task objectives or high-level commands, which would further broaden the method's applicability. Lastly, while some generalization was shown, more diverse datasets would enable more thorough evaluation and improvement of the generalization capabilities.
