# Offline Reinforcement Learning as One Big Sequence Modeling Problem

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley propose the Trajectory Transformer, a new framework that views reinforcement learning as an autoregressive sequence modeling problem using a Transformer decoder. This approach unifies policies, value functions, and dynamics models into a single architecture, achieving competitive or state-of-the-art results across offline reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and goal-conditioned tasks on D4RL benchmarks.

## Problem

- Traditional reinforcement learning algorithms rely on decomposing problems into local subproblems, leading to separate components (policy, value function, dynamics model) that can be complex to coordinate.
- Model-based reinforcement learning often suffers from compounding errors over long prediction horizons, limiting its effectiveness in complex, long-horizon tasks.
- Offline reinforcement learning faces challenges with out-of-distribution actions and model exploitation when learning from fixed datasets, often requiring complex regularization techniques.

## Method

- The **Trajectory Transformer (TT)** frames reinforcement learning as an ==autoregressive sequence generation== problem, representing trajectories as sequences of ==discretized states, actions, and rewards== (including reward-to-go).
- It employs a ==Transformer decoder== architecture, trained via ==teacher-forcing== to maximize the likelihood of observed trajectories, implicitly learning the ==joint distribution== over all elements in a sequence.
- Planning and control are performed using ==beam search== on the trained Transformer, adapted for specific RL settings such as likelihood-maximizing for imitation, conditioning on future states for goal-reaching, and prioritizing higher-reward sequences for offline RL.

## Results

- The Trajectory Transformer generates **100-timestep** trajectories visually indistinguishable from ground-truth in complex environments like Humanoid and exhibits substantially better error compounding than conventional feedforward dynamics models.
- It achieves performance on par with or better than state-of-the-art model-free and model-based offline RL algorithms on D4RL locomotion benchmarks, with quantile discretization proving crucial for performance.
- Combining the Trajectory Transformer's planning capabilities with an external Q-function (IQL) yields state-of-the-art results on challenging sparse-reward, long-horizon AntMaze navigation tasks, outperforming IQL alone.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, high-capacity Transformer sequence model can implicitly capture and unify concepts typically found in separate RL components, such as policies, value functions, and dynamics models, by learning the joint distribution over trajectories.
> - Transformer architectures effectively model long-range dependencies in RL trajectories, leading to superior long-horizon prediction accuracy and mitigating error accumulation common in traditional model-based RL.
> - Modeling the joint distribution of states and actions intrinsically regularizes behavior in offline reinforcement learning, naturally preventing out-of-distribution actions without explicit conservatism mechanisms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning has traditionally relied on decomposing complex, long-horizon problems into simpler, local subproblems, heavily leveraging the **Markov property** and the **principle of optimality**. This leads to established algorithmic paradigms like dynamic programming (e.g., Q-learning, policy iteration) for model-free methods and single-step predictive models for model-based approaches. While effective, these methods often face challenges such as sample inefficiency, issues with out-of-distribution actions in offline settings, and the accumulation of errors over long prediction horizons in model-based scenarios.

Concurrently, the field of **natural language processing (NLP)** has seen a revolution with the advent of **high-capacity sequence models**, particularly the **Transformer architecture** (Vaswani et al., 2017) and its variants (e.g., GPT, BERT). These models excel at capturing long-range dependencies within sequences and have demonstrated unprecedented performance in tasks like language generation, translation, and text summarization.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these two fields, proposing a radical shift: to view the entire reinforcement learning problem as **one big sequence modeling problem**. Instead of estimating stationary policies or single-step dynamics, the goal becomes generating a sequence of actions that results in a sequence of high rewards, analogous to generating coherent and meaningful text.

This perspective directly challenges the traditional decompositionist view of RL. While prior works have explored using sequence models (like LSTMs or Transformers) to represent *components* within existing RL algorithms (e.g., recurrent policies, memory-augmented value functions), they still adhered to the fundamental RL algorithmic structures. The Trajectory Transformer, in contrast, aims to **replace as much of the RL pipeline as possible** with sequence modeling. This includes unifying concepts like policies, value functions, and dynamics models into a single autoregressive model that learns the joint distribution over trajectories of states, actions, and rewards.

The work is particularly relevant to **offline reinforcement learning**, a crucial subfield where agents learn from fixed, pre-collected datasets without further interaction with the environment. Offline RL often struggles with issues like **out-of-distribution (OOD) actions** and **model exploitation**, where policies might try actions not seen in the dataset, leading to poor performance with learned models. By modeling the joint distribution of states and actions, the Trajectory Transformer inherently biases generated actions towards those observed in the dataset, effectively providing a natural form of behavior regularization without explicit conservatism mechanisms or behavioral cloning constraints often required by other offline RL algorithms (e.g., BRAC, CQL, MOPO).

Furthermore, this work is part of a growing trend that explores the "return-conditioned" or "goal-conditioned" perspective in RL, where desired future outcomes (e.g., high reward, a specific final state) are provided as inputs to the model. A contemporaneous work, the **Decision Transformer (Chen et al., 2021)**, independently proposed a similar sequence modeling approach for RL focusing on reward conditioning. This parallel development underscores the growing recognition within the research community of the potential for Transformer-based sequence modeling to unify and simplify RL paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **investigate whether the powerful tools and architectures from contemporary sequence modeling, particularly Transformers and beam search, can provide effective and simplified solutions to various reinforcement learning problems.**

The authors are motivated by several key insights and challenges in current RL:

- **Simplification and Unification:** Traditional RL algorithms often involve separate components like actors (policies), critics (value functions), dynamics models, and explicit behavior policy estimation for offline settings. The authors aim to demonstrate that a single, high-capacity sequence model can implicitly capture all these aspects by simply modeling the joint distribution over states, actions, and rewards in a trajectory. This promises a more streamlined and conceptually simpler approach to RL.
- **Leveraging Scalability from NLP:** The success of large-scale language models (like GPT-3) in learning complex distributions from massive datasets suggests that similar architectures could benefit RL by scaling to larger datasets and more complex environments, potentially benefiting from the same unsupervised learning principles.
- **Addressing Long-Horizon Dependencies and Error Accumulation:** Standard model-based RL methods often struggle with compounding errors over long prediction horizons, limiting their practical applicability. Transformers, known for their ability to model long-range dependencies, are hypothesized to offer a more robust solution for predicting entire trajectories.
- **Mitigating Offline RL Challenges:** Offline RL inherently deals with a fixed dataset and the challenge of policy learning without further exploration. The authors propose that modeling joint state-action distributions can naturally prevent out-of-distribution actions and model exploitation, which are common pitfalls in offline settings and usually require complex regularization techniques.
- **Generalizability and Versatility:** The goal is to develop a single, flexible framework that can be adapted with minimal changes to solve a diverse range of problems, including imitation learning, goal-conditioned RL, and reward-maximizing offline RL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, dubbed the **Trajectory Transformer (TT)**, lies in framing reinforcement learning as an autoregressive sequence generation problem.

#### 3.1. Trajectory Representation and Discretization

- A trajectory `τ` is treated as an unstructured sequence of states (`s`), actions (`a`), and scalar rewards (`r`), concatenated sequentially over time: `(s1, a1, r1, s2, a2, r2, ..., sT, aT, rT)`.
- For continuous state and action spaces, a crucial step is **discretization**. Each dimension of states and actions is independently discretized into tokens. The paper explores two simple methods:
 - **Uniform Discretization:** Divides the continuous range of each dimension into fixed-width bins. This preserves Euclidean distance information.
 - **Quantile Discretization:** Ensures each token corresponds to an equal amount of empirical probability mass from the training data, ensuring all tokens are represented.
- Rewards and, notably, **reward-to-go** (discounted cumulative future reward, `Rt`) are also discretized and included in the sequence. `Rt` is used as a heuristic to guide planning towards higher returns.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture and Training

- The Trajectory Transformer uses a **Transformer decoder architecture**, mirroring the GPT architecture. It employs a "causal" attention mask, meaning predictions for a token can only attend to previously observed tokens in the sequence, mimicking the autoregressive nature of language models.
- The architecture is relatively small compared to large language models (four layers, four self-attention heads, 128-dimensional token embeddings).
- Training is performed using the standard **teacher-forcing procedure**, maximizing the likelihood of the observed trajectories from the dataset. The objective is to maximize the sum of log-probabilities of each token given all preceding tokens in the sequence. This implicitly learns the joint distribution `P(s_t, a_t, r_t | s_<t, a_<t, r_<t)`.

#### 3.3. Planning with Beam Search

The trained Trajectory Transformer is then used for control via **beam search**, a heuristic decoding scheme widely used in NLP for finding high-probability sequences. The standard beam search algorithm is adapted for different RL settings:

- **Imitation Learning:** The most direct application. Beam search is used to find trajectories `τ` with the highest probability `Pθ(τ | s_current)` under the learned data distribution. This effectively acts as a long-horizon model-based behavior cloning, generating actions that imitate the training data.
- **Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning:** The Transformer's autoregressive nature is leveraged by conditioning on a *future desired state* (`sT`). This is achieved by simply prepending `sT` to the input sequence, allowing all subsequent predictions to attend to it. The model then generates a trajectory that is likely to reach `sT`.
- **Offline Reinforcement Learning:** To maximize reward, the standard beam search (which maximizes log-likelihood) is modified. Instead of pure log-probabilities, the search prioritizes sequences based on a combination of their cumulative predicted rewards and the predicted reward-to-go. This essentially turns beam search into a planning algorithm that actively seeks high-reward trajectories. The joint modeling of states and actions inherently prevents the model from generating out-of-distribution actions that could lead to overly optimistic reward predictions.
- **Combination with Q-functions (for sparse rewards):** For tasks with sparse rewards where the Transformer's self-generated reward-to-go estimate might be insufficient (due to limited data or long horizons), the authors propose to replace this estimate with a more accurate Q-function derived from an existing off-policy RL algorithm (e.g., Implicit Q-Learning, IQL). This leverages the strengths of both approaches: the Transformer for long-horizon prediction and dynamic programming for accurate value estimation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the Trajectory Transformer (TT) demonstrates its versatility and strong performance across various RL settings.

- **Superior Long-Horizon Prediction:**
 - Qualitatively, TT generates 100-timestep trajectories that are "visually indistinguishable from the ground-truth trajectories" in complex environments like Humanoid, significantly outperforming conventional feedforward Gaussian dynamics models (e.g., from PETS), which show rapid error accumulation.
 - Quantitatively, TT exhibits "substantially better error compounding" than a feedforward model, maintaining significantly higher log-likelihoods over long prediction horizons.
 - An ablation study shows that the Transformer architecture itself and its autoregressive discretization scheme play a large role in its accuracy, even more so than long-range context in fully-observed environments. However, in partially-observed settings, long-horizon conditioning (full Transformer) becomes more critical.
 - Attention pattern analysis reveals two dominant behaviors: a Markovian pattern focusing on the immediately preceding transition, and a "striated" pattern where the model attends to specific dimensions across multiple prior timesteps. Interestingly, action predictions show strong dependencies on past actions, which may contribute to smoother action sequences.

- **Competitive Offline Reinforcement Learning Performance:**
 - On the D4RL locomotion (v2) benchmarks, TT achieves results "on par with or better than" state-of-the-art model-free (BRAC, CQL) and model-based (MBOP) offline RL algorithms, as well as the concurrently developed Decision Transformer (DT).
 - The choice of discretization matters: **Quantile discretization** proves crucial for environments with large state/action ranges (e.g., HalfCheetah-Medium-Expert), where uniform discretization struggles due to the resulting coarse bins.

- **State-of-the-Art in Sparse-Reward, Long-Horizon Tasks:**
 - For challenging sparse-reward AntMaze navigation tasks, where the Transformer's intrinsic reward-to-go estimate is insufficient, combining TT planning with a Q-function from Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) yields **state-of-the-art results**.
 - This hybrid approach (TT + Q) outperforms IQL alone, indicating that planning with a robust model as a search heuristic can be more effective than mere policy extraction from a Q-function. It also outperforms return-conditioning approaches like Decision Transformer, highlighting the benefits of explicit planning in temporally compositional tasks.

- **Effective Imitation Learning and Goal-Reaching:**
 - When used for imitation learning, TT with likelihood-maximizing beam search successfully reproduces expert behavior, achieving average normalized returns of 104% and 109% in Hopper and Walker2d, respectively, from expert datasets.
 - The goal-conditioned variant, using "anti-causal" conditioning on a future state, effectively solves goal-reaching tasks in a continuous 4-rooms environment and procedurally-generated MiniGrid maps without any explicit rewards or reward shaping, relying solely on goal relabeling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a significant conceptual contribution to the field of reinforcement learning by proposing a **unified and simplified framework** centered on sequence modeling. Its implications are far-reaching:

- **Paradigm Shift:** The paper effectively demonstrates that many seemingly disparate components of RL (policies, value functions, dynamics models) can be elegantly unified under a single autoregressive sequence model. This shifts the focus from designing complex inter-component algorithms to building powerful, general-purpose sequence models.
- **Leveraging AI Advances:** It successfully brings the immense power and scalability of Transformer architectures and sequence generation techniques, honed in NLP, directly to complex RL problems. This opens avenues for future cross-domain research, potentially allowing RL to benefit from architectural innovations and large-scale pre-training strategies from generative modeling.
- **Robustness in Offline RL:** The intrinsic behavior regularization provided by jointly modeling states and actions is a powerful and elegant solution to the critical challenge of out-of-distribution actions in offline RL. This approach avoids the need for explicit conservatism penalties or complex regularization mechanisms common in other methods.
- **Improved Model-Based RL:** The Trajectory Transformer's unprecedented accuracy in long-horizon dynamics prediction addresses a long-standing limitation in model-based RL, which has often been hampered by compounding model errors. This enhanced predictive capability could unlock more reliable and effective model-predictive control in the future.
- **Versatility and Generalization:** The ability of a single algorithmic paradigm (Trajectory Transformer + beam search) to effectively solve problems in imitation learning, goal-conditioned RL, and reward-maximizing offline RL demonstrates its impressive flexibility and potential for broader applicability.
- **Foundation for Hybrid Approaches:** The success of combining TT planning with external Q-functions for sparse-reward tasks highlights that the most impactful future directions might lie in intelligent combinations of sequence modeling with established dynamic programming techniques, rather than relying solely on one paradigm.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The paper acknowledges practical limitations, primarily the **computational cost** of Transformer inference and beam search, which makes real-time control unwieldy for most dynamic systems. This is a critical barrier for online RL applications. Future research could focus on computationally efficient Transformer architectures (e.g., sparse attention, linear Transformers) or faster decoding strategies. The discretization of continuous data also imposes a theoretical upper bound on precision, suggesting potential for architectures that inherently handle continuous inputs.

In conclusion, "Offline Reinforcement Learning as One Big Sequence Modeling Problem" offers a compelling and effective alternative to traditional RL methodologies, demonstrating that the future of RL may increasingly involve adopting techniques from the booming field of large-scale generative modeling. It simplifies the RL problem conceptually and achieves state-of-the-art results, paving the way for more unified and powerful RL systems.
