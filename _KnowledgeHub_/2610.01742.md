# World Motion Models: Flexible Sequence Modeling of SE(3) Trajectories

> [!summary] Summary
> World Motion Models (WMMs) represent any dynamic 3D scene, including humans, objects, cameras, robot states and actions, as sparse SE(3) pose trajectories. One flow-matching transformer is trained with independent per-token noise levels plus decoupled context tokens. At inference, choosing which tokens are clean turns the same network into a policy, forward dynamics model, motion planner, or retargeter, with state-of-the-art results across six robotics and vision tasks.

## Problem

- Existing motion generative models are fragmented by actor class, relying on morphology-specific parameterizations like SMPL, MANO, or URDFs that cannot share one prior across arbitrary entities.
- Video-based world models capture dynamics implicitly through pixel changes, entangling 3D motion with appearance, lighting, and viewpoint, while dense point-track approaches spend capacity on redundant tokens.
- Standard world and dynamics models are strictly causal, so policies, inverse dynamics, infilling, and retargeting need separate networks; Diffusion Forcing schedules noise mainly along time, not across entities.

## Method

- ==Sparse SE(3) token representation==: each entity's pose per timestep becomes a 9D token of three points (position plus scaled 6D rotation), paired with ==DINO== semantic and ==PointNet== geometry features.
- ==Per-token-noise flow matching==: every entity-timestep token gets an i.i.d. noise level, so one velocity loss covers all any-to-any conditionals; inference sets observed tokens to zero noise and targets to one.
- ==Context tokens== on a decoupled virtual timeline carry goals or paired retargeting exemplars, inside a transformer with per-token ==AdaLN==, ==register blocks==, and five ==factored time/entity attention== types scaling past 10K tokens.

## Results

- On Language Table, WMM reaches **87.2%** macro-average success versus **80.0%** for LCB and **76.8%** for ActionVLA; gradient-plus-sampling MPC raises block-to-block success from **90%** to **100%**.
- On TraceGen 3D trajectory prediction, WMM beats TraceGen on every metric (Droid MSE **0.151** vs **0.206**); a model trained on **128** tracks infers all **400** and outperforms the **400**-track variant.
- Human-to-G1 retargeting reaches **0.869** sequence success at **86.97** FPS versus PHUMA's **0.826** at **2.27** FPS; removing per-token noise drops policy success to **0%**, and plain DiT gives a ~**15x** MAE regression.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A physically grounded, appearance-free intermediate representation like SE(3) trajectories lets one prior span humans, robots, objects, and cameras without category-specific models.
> - Independent per-token noise turns conditioning into a masking choice, so policy, world model, inverse dynamics, and planner become one network rather than separately trained modules.
> - Guidance-based conditioning with synchronized noise fails where control needs high precision; subsampling sparse tokens during training can generalize to denser queries and reduce overfitting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A tabletop robot is told to push the blue cube toward the top right. It still has to turn that desired outcome into a sequence of movements. The approach studied here describes the moving objects and the robot's motion in one shared, pose-based format. A model is then asked to fill in whatever is missing, including a goal and candidate actions. The authors call these World Motion Models (WMMs). For the instruction "push the blue cube to the top right," the project page shows an imagined goal, a predicted action chunk and its last frame, an optimized action, and a rollout.

Forecasting a scene, producing a robot action, filling in a motion, and translating a human motion to a robot are usually treated as different modeling problems. With WMM's shared representation and conditioning masks, they become different uses of the same model. The authors frame this as a generative prior for "what was, is, and will be where." That aspiration is broader than the experimental evidence below.

### 2. One language for motion

WMMs encode moving entities as sequences of rigid poses. A pose says where an entity is and how it is oriented. The space of these rigid-body poses, position plus orientation, is called SE(3). Each token represents one entity at one time step, and a feature identifies what the entity is.

The paper assumes that many dynamic scenes can be approximated by a sparse set of such trajectories. Interpolation or skinning can recover denser deformation. The representation can therefore describe robot links, human joints, hands, objects, and scene points without a different representation for each category. It does not model general local scaling or shearing.

The second ingredient is the noise schedule. During training, each pose token receives its own noise level. At inference, known tokens are kept clean or nearly clean, and unknown ones are generated by denoising. The user's choice of which tokens are known and which are unknown selects the task. Context tokens use a separate, virtual timeline. This lets a goal state or a paired example guide a sequence without being an ordinary frame in that sequence.

The closest predecessor is [Diffusion Forcing (NeurIPS 2024)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2407.01392), which independently assigns noise levels to sequence tokens. WMM applies that idea across both time and heterogeneous entities represented by SE(3) poses. It also adds context tokens for non-causal anchors such as goals and paired motion exemplars.

A token holds a 9D pose, timestep, noise level, and feature. Robot-action, robot-state, scene-object, and human-joint sequences are entities over time (A). Token sequences corrupted at differing per-token noise levels are passed to a WMM to predict clean pose tokens (B). Future prediction/policy, action-conditioned prediction, motion planning/infilling, and retargeting/inverse dynamics appear as different clean/noisy token masks (C).

### 3. What the tests establish

On Language Table, a short history and a language instruction condition a prediction of future state and action. In the MPC variant, WMM also predicts a goal, and candidate actions are optimized against that goal. The metric is the macro-average success rate: the mean of success rates over five task families. Each family was evaluated with 50 rollouts, in an eight-block setup with a 200-step horizon. Under this protocol, WMM reaches **87.2%**, compared with **80.0%** for LCB. This is evidence on that benchmark only. The paper trains a separate WMM checkpoint for each benchmark.

The sharpest test is an ablation on the B2B task. It removes per-token noise and instead uses a shared noise timestep with guidance-based conditioning. WMM's B2B success rate is **0.860**, and the "no per-tk" variant scores **0.000**. The authors say policy mode requires very high accuracy. The result supports the importance of the proposed noise schedule in this policy configuration. It does not show that synchronized noise always fails, or that per-token noise is the sole cause of WMM's performance.

The paper also tests scene future prediction, human–object interaction, humanoid retargeting, and other applications. Each is evaluated with its own benchmark checkpoint.

### 4. One model, different questions

Goal state, past motion, action, and future state can all be posed as known or unknown parts of one pose-token field. Changing that partition changes what the same model is asked to infer. The main boundary is that WMM is not a single jointly trained model across all application areas. The current framework also needs geometry such as depth and estimated states, and it does not generalize to morphologies unseen during training.
