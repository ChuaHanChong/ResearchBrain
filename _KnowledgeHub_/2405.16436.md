# Provably Mitigating Overoptimization in RLHF: Your SFT Loss is Implicitly an Adversarial Regularizer

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO), an algorithm for aligning large language models that integrates a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss into the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective. RPO is theoretically proven to mitigate reward overoptimization by acting as an implicit adversarial regularizer, achieving improved in-distribution alignment and higher performance on benchmarks like MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0 compared to DPO.

## Problem

- Large language models fine-tuned with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) often suffer from reward overoptimization, where they exploit imperfections in the learned reward model to generate undesirable responses.
- Standard RLHF methods are prone to instability, sample inefficiency, and sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning, exacerbated by distributional shifts and reward uncertainty.
- Prior theoretical work on mitigating overoptimization has often been limited to simplified model classes, lacking direct applicability to large neural networks.

## Method

- Develops a ==maximin optimization framework== that models RLHF as an ==offline contextual bandit== problem, forcing the policy to be robust against an ==adversarially chosen reward model==.
- Derives **Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO)** by transforming the theoretical maximin objective into a practical loss function, which combines a ==preference optimization term== (similar to DPO) with a ==supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss==.
- The ==SFT loss== is shown to implicitly regularize the LLM by encouraging it to mimic high-quality, preferred responses from a baseline distribution, thereby preventing overoptimization towards unreliable reward signals.

## Results

- RPO effectively alleviates the continuous decrease in log probability of chosen responses during training, a symptom of overoptimization observed with DPO, for both Zephyr-7b-beta and Zephyr-7b-gemma models.
- Achieves improved in-data distribution alignment, with GPT-4 pairwise evaluations showing RPO consistently outperforming DPO (e.g., RPO (beta) wins **56%** vs. DPO (beta) **44%**).
- Demonstrates superior performance on standard LLM chat benchmarks, including MT-Bench (RPO (beta) **7.381** vs. DPO (beta) **7.278**) and AlpacaEval 2.0 across different LLM series and various tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Overoptimization in RLHF can be provably mitigated through a maximin objective that accounts for reward model uncertainty and distributional shifts by optimizing against an adversarial reward model.
> - The supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss, when integrated into the preference optimization objective, acts as an implicit adversarial regularizer, stabilizing training and improving alignment.
> - Rigorous theoretical guarantees for RPO's sample efficiency and ability to mitigate overoptimization are established under general function approximation settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses one of the most critical and pervasive challenges in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment: **overoptimization in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**. RLHF has become a cornerstone technique for training LLMs to be helpful, truthful, and harmless, moving beyond mere pre-training objectives to align with nuanced human preferences. Pioneering works like those from Christiano et al. (2017) and Ouyang et al. (2022) established the two-stage RLHF pipeline: first, training a reward model (RM) from human preference data, and then fine-tuning the LLM to maximize this learned reward using algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

However, this standard RLHF pipeline is notorious for its instability, sample inefficiency, and sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning, making it computationally expensive and challenging to deploy effectively. More fundamentally, it suffers from "reward overoptimization" or "reward hacking." This phenomenon occurs when the LLM optimizes too aggressively for an *imperfectly learned* reward model. Since the RM is estimated from finite human feedback, it inevitably contains errors and uncertainties. An overoptimized LLM can exploit these inaccuracies, generating responses that score highly on the learned RM but are actually undesirable or misaligned with true human preferences, especially for out-of-distribution (OOD) responses. This problem has been recognized in several works (Michaud et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023).

To address the limitations of PPO-based RLHF, a new class of "reward-model-free" methods emerged, most notably Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) by Rafailov et al. (2023). DPO reparameterizes the reward model in terms of the policy itself, allowing for direct optimization of the policy using a simple cross-entropy-like loss derived from human preferences, avoiding the explicit training of a reward model and the complexities of PPO. DPO has gained significant traction due to its simplicity and effectiveness.

The present paper builds upon and extends the DPO paradigm by specifically targeting the overoptimization issue. While DPO implicitly regularizes the policy with a KL-divergence term against a reference model, the authors argue this is insufficient to fully prevent overoptimization. Prior theoretical works on RLHF have often been limited, either by assuming simplified model classes (e.g., linear function approximations) or by proposing algorithms that are too complex to implement in the context of large neural networks. Other practical attempts at mitigating overoptimization, such as reward model ensembles or constrained RLHF, exist, but often lack strong theoretical backing.

This work distinguishes itself by providing a *principled theoretical framework* for understanding and mitigating overoptimization, and then deriving an *easily implementable algorithm* (RPO) that outperforms DPO. It bridges the gap between theoretical rigor (general function approximation, finite-sample guarantees) and practical applicability for LLMs, demonstrating how a seemingly heuristic addition (Supervised Fine-Tuning or SFT loss) can be theoretically justified as an adversarial regularizer. The work contributes to the ongoing effort to make LLM alignment more robust, reliable, and efficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to answer the critical question: **"How to mitigate reward overoptimization in RLHF in a principled and efficient manner for better alignment?"**

The authors are driven by the recognition that overoptimization is a fundamental bottleneck preventing successful and robust alignment of LLMs with human preferences. They identify the root causes of overoptimization in RLHF as:

- **Distributional Shift:** During fine-tuning, the LLM's response distribution can deviate significantly from the distribution of responses in the finite human preference dataset.
- **Reward Uncertainty:** For responses that are out-of-distribution or not well-covered by the training data, the learned reward model (being imperfect) inherently exhibits high uncertainty. This uncertainty makes the reward model misleading, as it can assign spuriously high rewards to undesirable OOD responses.

The motivation is to develop a method that can prevent the LLM from exploiting these misleadingly high proxy rewards. The authors propose to achieve this by:

1. **Principled Theoretical Formulation:** Developing a new theoretical algorithm that explicitly accounts for and counteracts the effects of reward model uncertainty and distributional shifts. This involves modeling the problem as an offline contextual bandit and devising an objective that forces the policy to be robust against an "adversarial" reward model.
2. **Practical Implementability:** Translating this complex theoretical objective into a surprisingly simple and easy-to-implement algorithm suitable for training LLMs. This addresses the common challenge where theoretically sound algorithms are often too complex or computationally demanding for real-world deployment.
3. **Provable Guarantees:** Providing rigorous theoretical guarantees for the proposed algorithm's sample efficiency and its ability to mitigate overoptimization, even under general function approximation settings.
4. **Empirical Validation:** Demonstrating the practical effectiveness of the new algorithm on real LLM alignment tasks, showing improved performance over existing state-of-the-art methods like DPO across various benchmarks.

By tackling overoptimization with a unified approach that spans theoretical foundations and practical application, the authors aim to enhance the reliability and generalization capabilities of aligned LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper's methodology proceeds in three distinct but interconnected stages: a theoretical algorithm, its transformation into an equivalent and implementable objective, and empirical validation.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Algorithm: Maximin Objective (Algorithm 1)

The authors model RLHF as an offline contextual bandit problem. Their core idea is to find a policy (LLM) that is robust against the uncertainties in the learned reward model. This leads to a **maximin optimization problem**:

$$ \hat{\pi} \in \operatorname{argmax}_{\pi \in \Pi} \min_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \left( \eta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x \sim d_0, \\ a_1 \sim \pi(\cdot|x), \\ a_0 \sim \pi_{\text{base}}(\cdot|x)}} \left[ r(x, a_1) - r(x, a_0) - \beta \cdot \operatorname{KL}\left(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right) \right] + L_{\mathcal{D}}(r) \right) $$

Here:
- $\pi$ is the policy (LLM) to be optimized.
- $r$ is a reward model chosen adversarially from a reward function class $\mathcal{R}$.
- $\eta, \beta > 0$ are hyperparameters.
- $d_0$ is the prompt distribution.
- $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a reference policy (the initial LLM before alignment).
- $\pi_{\text{base}}$ is a baseline policy (e.g., policy generating preferred responses in the dataset).
- $L_{\mathcal{D}}(r)$ is the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) loss (cross-entropy) for the Bradley-Terry preference model, learned from the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$.

The objective of the adversarial reward model $r$ is to minimize the sum of its MLE loss ($L_{\mathcal{D}}(r)$) and a *reward penalty term*. This penalty term, $\eta \cdot \mathbb{E}[\dots]$, incorporates the policy's expected reward difference relative to $\pi_{\text{base}}$, regularized by a KL divergence. By minimizing this sum, the adversarial $r$ is forced to penalize policies that achieve spuriously high rewards due to insufficient data coverage or inherent uncertainty. This mechanism prevents the policy $\pi$ from "overoptimizing" towards these unreliable reward signals. The term $\beta \cdot \operatorname{KL}(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x))$ acts as a regularization to keep the learned policy from diverging too far from the initial reference policy, a common practice in RLHF.

#### 3.2. Transformation to an Equivalent and Implementation-Friendly Objective (RPO)

The paper then shows that under mild regularity conditions on the reward model class $\mathcal{R}$ (e.g., compactness and a convex-like property), the maximin objective can be equivalently transformed into a minimax objective:

$$ \min_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \max_{\pi \in \Pi} \left( \eta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x \sim d_0, \\ a_1 \sim \pi(\cdot|x), \\ a_0 \sim \pi_{\text{base}}(\cdot|x)}} \left[ r(x, a_1) - r(x, a_0) - \beta \cdot \operatorname{KL}\left(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right) \right] + L_{\mathcal{D}}(r) \right) $$

The key insight for practical implementation comes from solving the *inner maximization problem* for $\pi$ given a fixed $r$. It is well-established that the policy maximizing a KL-regularized expected reward has a closed-form solution:
$$ \pi_r(\cdot|x) = \frac{1}{Z_r(x)} \cdot \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x) \cdot \exp\left( \beta^{-1} r(x, \cdot) \right) $$
where $Z_r(x)$ is a normalization constant. This relationship allows for a **reparameterization of the reward model $r$ in terms of its corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r$**, similar to how DPO operates.

Substituting this reparameterization back into the minimax objective, the authors derive the final objective for their practical algorithm, **Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO)**:

$$ \min_{\theta \in \Theta} \left( \underbrace{\eta\beta \cdot \mathbb{E}_{\substack{x \sim d_0, \\ a_0 \sim \pi_{\text{base}}(\cdot|x)}} \left[ -\log(\pi_{\theta}(a_0|x)) \right]}_{\text{Imitation (SFT) loss}} + \underbrace{L_{\mathcal{D}}\left( \beta \cdot \log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\cdot)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|\cdot)}\right) \right)}_{\text{Preference optimization loss}} \right) $$

This RPO objective is a weighted combination of two terms:
1. **Preference Optimization Loss:** This term is *identical* to the DPO objective, aligning the policy with human preferences.
2. **Imitation (SFT) Loss:** This new term explicitly regularizes the LLM ($\pi_{\theta}$) to mimic the responses from a "suitable baseline distribution" ($\pi_{\text{base}}$). In experiments, $\pi_{\text{base}}$ is chosen to be the distribution of *preferred responses* in the dataset.

The SFT loss in RPO acts as an **implicit adversarial regularizer**. Unlike the $\beta$-weighted KL regularization in DPO which primarily controls the scale of the gradient, the SFT loss in RPO modifies the *direction* of the gradient. By encouraging the LLM to stay close to high-quality, preferred responses, it prevents the model from straying into regions where the learned reward model is uncertain and prone to overoptimization.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Guarantees

The paper provides rigorous theoretical analysis:
- **Finite-Sample Suboptimality:** Theorem 5.3 establishes a finite-sample suboptimality gap for Algorithm 1 (maximin objective) of $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ under a "partial coverage" condition (Assumption 5.2). This means that with enough preference data $N$, the learned policy will be close to the optimal policy in terms of true human reward, provably mitigating overoptimization.
- **Equivalence and Generalization:** Theorem 5.6 formally proves the equivalence between the maximin and minimax objectives, thereby extending the sample complexity guarantees to the practical RPO algorithm. Corollary 5.8 further shows that RPO can generalize to new prompt distributions, provided these distributions are well-covered by the training prompt distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields compelling findings, both theoretically and empirically, demonstrating RPO's effectiveness in mitigating overoptimization and improving LLM alignment.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Provable Mitigation of Overoptimization:** The core theoretical finding is that the proposed maximin objective (Algorithm 1) provably mitigates reward overoptimization. By forcing the policy to optimize against an adversarially chosen reward model that simultaneously minimizes MLE loss and a reward penalty, the algorithm is robust to uncertainties arising from finite preference data and distributional shifts.
- **Finite-Sample Guarantees:** Algorithm 1 achieves a finite-sample suboptimality gap of $O(1/\sqrt{N})$ under a partial coverage condition (Assumption 5.2). This guarantee is established for general function approximations, which is a significant advancement over previous theoretical works often restricted to linear models.
- **Theoretical-Practical Bridge:** The formal equivalence (Theorem 5.6) between the theoretically motivated maximin objective and the practically implementable minimax objective (which simplifies to RPO) is a crucial contribution. This provides strong theoretical justification for the RPO algorithm.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** RPO is theoretically shown to generalize to new prompt distributions (Corollary 5.8), provided the training prompt distribution adequately covers the target distribution. This is empirically supported by PCA analysis of prompt embeddings (Figures 2 and 3).

#### 4.2. Empirical Findings

The authors evaluate RPO against DPO and reference models across two LLM series (Zephyr-7b-beta and Zephyr-7b-gemma) and various benchmarks.

- **Alleviation of Overoptimization (Log Probability Stability):** DPO is known to suffer from a significant and continuous decrease in the log probability of chosen responses during training, which is interpreted as a symptom of overoptimization. RPO effectively alleviates this issue, maintaining notably higher log probabilities on chosen responses throughout training for both beta and gemma series (Figures 1 and 4). This confirms RPO's ability to maintain trust in high-quality preferred responses.
- **Improved In-Data Distribution Alignment:** In pairwise GPT-4 evaluations on the test split of the training datasets (Ultrafeedback and Argilla-DPO-Mix-7K), RPO consistently outperforms DPO. For instance, RPO (beta) wins against DPO (beta) in 56% of cases, while DPO (beta) wins against RPO (beta) in 44% of cases, and similar results are shown for the gemma series (Table 1, Figures 5 and 6). This indicates RPO achieves better alignment within the observed data distribution.
- **Consistent Benchmark Performance Improvement:** RPO demonstrates superior performance on widely recognized LLM chat benchmarks:
 - **MT-Bench:** RPO (beta) achieves a score of 7.381, outperforming DPO (beta) (7.278) and the official Zephyr-beta-7b (7.200). Similarly, RPO (gemma) scores 7.916, surpassing DPO (gemma) (7.688) and official Zephyr-gemma-7b (7.719) (Table 2).
 - **AlpacaEval 2.0:** RPO shows higher win rates and Length-Control (LC) win rates compared to GPT-4, consistently outperforming DPO and official models in both beta and gemma series (Table 2).
- **Flexibility and Generalizability:** RPO functions as a "flexible plug-in module" that can be applied to different reference models and datasets without changing the base DPO structure. Its consistent performance across varied setups, including math, reasoning, and coding tasks (GSM8K, ARC, MBPP; Table 8), highlights its robust generalizability and potential for broader application.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a significant stride in the field of LLM alignment, offering contributions that span both theoretical understanding and practical methodology.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

- **Bridging Theory and Practice:** The most impactful theoretical contribution is the explicit derivation and proof of equivalence between a provably sample-efficient theoretical algorithm (maximin objective for mitigating overoptimization) and a simple, practical, DPO-like objective (RPO). This is a rare and highly valuable achievement in deep learning research, where theoretical guarantees often struggle to translate into practical, scalable methods for LLMs.
- **Understanding Overoptimization:** The paper provides a principled framework for understanding overoptimization in RLHF as a consequence of distributional shifts and reward uncertainty. By conceptualizing the SFT loss as an *implicit adversarial regularizer*, it offers a novel interpretation of how imitation learning can robustify preference optimization.
- **General Function Approximation:** Providing theoretical guarantees for general function approximation settings (rather than restrictive linear models) increases the relevance and applicability of the theory to real-world neural network-based LLMs.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact and Future Directions

- **Improved LLM Alignment Robustness:** RPO offers a straightforward and effective method to mitigate a critical failure mode of RLHF. By stabilizing training and preventing models from optimizing for spurious rewards, it leads to more reliable, safer, and genuinely preferred LLM responses. This directly translates to building more trustworthy AI assistants.
- **Enhanced DPO Effectiveness:** As a flexible "plug-in module" to DPO, RPO can easily be integrated into existing DPO pipelines. The empirical results demonstrate that RPO consistently outperforms vanilla DPO across diverse benchmarks and models, indicating a substantial improvement on an already popular and effective alignment algorithm. This means practitioners can achieve better alignment performance with minimal changes to their current workflows.
- **Reduced Training Instability:** By mitigating overoptimization, RPO can lead to more stable and predictable training dynamics, potentially reducing the extensive hyperparameter tuning and iterative adjustments often required in RLHF.
- **New Avenues for Research:** The work sheds light on the crucial interplay between preference optimization and supervised fine-tuning. It encourages further exploration of how different forms of imitation learning or baseline distributions can be theoretically justified and practically leveraged for robust LLM alignment. Future work could extend this framework to iterative RLHF settings, exploring how RPO could be combined with active data collection strategies to further boost LLM performance and sample efficiency. The insights could also inspire new methods for addressing other forms of alignment challenges, such as adversarial attacks or distributional robustness.

In conclusion, "Provably Mitigating Overoptimization in RLHF" makes a significant dual contribution: it advances the theoretical understanding of RLHF by providing strong guarantees for a robust alignment strategy and offers an immediately applicable and highly effective algorithm that addresses a key practical limitation in LLM training.
