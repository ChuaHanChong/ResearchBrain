# SPEAR: A Simulator for Photorealistic Embodied AI Research

> [!summary] Summary
> SPEAR introduces a simulator for photorealistic embodied AI research that significantly enhances programmability and rendering efficiency within Unreal Engine. The system exposes over 14,000 Unreal Engine functions to Python and achieves up to 21 times faster high-resolution image rendering compared to previous methods, while providing a comprehensive suite of ground truth data.

## Problem

- Existing UE-based simulators offer limited programmability (only hundreds of hand-crafted Python functions) and lack modularity, often requiring customized engine forks that complicate integration and third-party asset use.
- Significant communication overhead when transferring large data like high-resolution images from Unreal Engine to Python leads to substantial performance bottlenecks.
- Essential ground truth data, such as non-diffuse intrinsic image decompositions and physically based shading parameters, are often unavailable in existing UE-based simulators.

## Method

- **SPEAR** is implemented as a modular Unreal Engine plugin, leveraging UE's ==runtime reflection system== to dynamically expose a vast array of C++ functionality to Python.
- The simulator employs a ==client-server architecture== with ==asynchronous communication== and ==interprocess shared memory==, optimizing the transfer of high-resolution images and large data blocks.
- It introduces an expressive, ==transaction-based programming model== that allows for deterministic execution of complex graphs of Unreal Engine operations within a single frame.

## Results

- SPEAR exposes over **14,000** unique Unreal Engine functions and **53,000** properties to Python (an order-of-magnitude increase over existing UE-based simulators), and provides ground truth modalities unavailable elsewhere, including non-diffuse intrinsic image decompositions and PBR shading parameters.
- It achieves significantly enhanced rendering performance, with up to **73** frames per second for **1920x1080** images, demonstrating up to **21x** faster performance than UnrealCV+ and approximately **12x** faster than AirSim.
- Diverse example applications showcased capabilities like multi-agent control, programmatic manipulation of procedural content generation (PCG) systems, and real-time co-simulation with external physics engines such as MuJoCo.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging Unreal Engine's native reflection system enables the exposure of an order of magnitude more functionality with a minimal codebase, greatly expanding programmatic control.
> - Employing a dedicated C++ server thread, asynchronous communication, and shared memory significantly reduces data transfer latency, allowing the simulator to achieve near-native UE frame rates.
> - A modular plugin architecture facilitates seamless integration with any existing Unreal Engine application, enhancing generality and simplifying the incorporation of diverse assets and environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Interactive simulators have emerged as fundamental tools in the advancement of embodied artificial intelligence (AI), robotics, and computer vision. They are instrumental in training embodied agents, facilitating breakthroughs in areas such as reinforcement learning (e.g., competitive e-sports, drone racing), sensorimotor control, autonomous driving, dexterous manipulation, and quadruped locomotion. Concurrently, photorealistic synthetic datasets generated by these simulators are utilized for training and evaluating large-scale foundation models, as well as state-of-the-art methods in 3D and 4D reconstruction, segmentation, depth estimation, and controllable video synthesis.

The computer vision, robotics, and embodied AI communities increasingly leverage photorealistic simulators built upon industrial-grade game engines. Among these, Unreal Engine (UE) is recognized for its advanced real-time rendering capabilities, procedural content generation systems, established success in commercial game development, and its open-source nature. Other platforms include Unity, Panda3D, and proprietary solutions such as NVIDIA Isaac Sim. Beyond game engines, researchers have developed custom simulation stacks (e.g., MINOS, Habitat, AI2-THOR, iGibson) that often prioritize simulation speed over photorealism, typically for tasks like navigation or manipulation in specific environments. Commercial video games have also been repurposed as AI simulators, ranging from early Atari games to modern open-world titles. However, these often require custom scripting interfaces developed by game creators or rely on intrusive wrappers that limit access to the underlying game state.

Despite the advantages of Unreal Engine, existing UE-based simulators present several limitations. These include:
1. **Limited Programmability**: Current simulators expose a restricted number of hand-crafted Python functions, typically in the hundreds, which limits the scope of possible interactions.
2. **Performance Bottlenecks**: There is often significant communication overhead when transferring large data blocks, such as high-resolution images, from UE to Python. This can result in a substantial slowdown (e.g., 20–35 times slower for a 1920x1080 image compared to direct viewport rendering in UE).
3. **Lack of Modularity**: Many existing UE-based simulators are distributed as monolithic applications, sometimes requiring customized forks of the UE codebase. This design complicates their integration into existing projects and hinders the incorporation of third-party assets.
4. **Incomplete Ground Truth Data**: Some critical ground truth image modalities, such as non-diffuse intrinsic image decompositions, material IDs, and physically based shading parameters, are not available in existing UE-based simulators.

SPEAR aims to address these identified limitations by offering a simulator designed for enhanced generality, programmability, rendering speed, and comprehensive data provision within the Unreal Engine ecosystem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a simulator, named SPEAR (A Simulator for Photorealistic Embodied AI Research), that mitigates the limitations of current Unreal Engine-based simulators while leveraging UE's advanced capabilities for photorealistic rendering and content generation. The key motivations and specific objectives are:

1. **Enhance Programmability**: To provide an interface that exposes a substantially larger portion of Unreal Engine's native functionality to Python. The goal is to move beyond hundreds of hand-crafted functions to thousands of accessible functions, enabling more comprehensive and flexible programmatic control over UE applications. This aims to allow researchers to interact with UE systems (e.g., procedural content generation, physics, rendering) that are typically inaccessible in existing simulators.
2. **Improve Rendering and Data Transfer Efficiency**: To significantly accelerate the delivery of high-resolution photorealistic images and ground truth data from the Unreal Engine to a user's Python program. This involves reducing communication overhead and enabling near-native UE frame rates for rendering, facilitating faster training and data generation workflows.
3. **Increase Generality and Modularity**: To design SPEAR as a modular plugin architecture that can integrate with any existing Unreal Engine application, rather than as a standalone, monolithic fork of UE. This aims to simplify the process of incorporating SPEAR into diverse projects and allows for the easy integration of various third-party assets and environments.
4. **Provide Comprehensive Ground Truth Modalities**: To offer a camera sensor capable of rendering not only photorealistic beauty images but also a diverse set of ground truth image modalities critical for AI research. This includes depths, surface normals, instance and semantic IDs, non-diffuse intrinsic image decompositions, material IDs, and physically based shading parameters, some of which are unavailable in other UE-based simulators.
5. **Develop an Expressive Programming Model**: To introduce a high-level programming model that allows users to specify complex graphs of Unreal Engine work with arbitrary data dependencies. This model is intended to execute these graphs deterministically within a single UE frame, offering precise control over simulation dynamics and enabling complex agent behaviors.

The overall motivation stems from the recognition that photorealistic, interactive simulation environments are indispensable for driving progress in embodied AI and related fields. By addressing the current shortcomings of UE-based simulators, SPEAR aims to become a foundational tool that accelerates research by providing superior programmatic control, efficiency, and data richness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPEAR is implemented as a Python library designed to connect to and programmatically control any Unreal Engine (UE) application through a modular plugin architecture. The core of its methodology relies on exposing a comprehensive C++ interface that interacts directly with UE's runtime reflection system.

**Core Technical Insight:** The primary technical insight involves leveraging UE's reflection system to dynamically find classes, call functions, and manipulate variables on objects at runtime using string-based keys. This approach eliminates the need for hand-crafted wrapper code for each individual class, function, and variable, thereby enabling the exposure of a substantial portion of UE's underlying functionality with a comparatively modest codebase.

**System Architecture:**
SPEAR employs a client-server architecture.
- **Python Client**: This component is responsible for transforming user-defined operations, structured as transactions, into a stream of commands. These commands are then sent to the server. The Python client leverages `nanobind` for its C++ bindings, allowing server entry points to be called as if they were native C++ functions.
- **C++ Server**: Implemented as a plugin within the Unreal Engine application, the server runs on a dedicated "server thread" separate from the UE "game thread." This separation is crucial for supporting asynchronous operations without blocking the game thread. The server communicates with the client over a TCP/IP connection, allowing for distributed setups. `rpclib` is used for the C++ client-server interface, ensuring strongly typed arguments and return values.

**Programming Model:**
The SPEAR programming model is designed to be expressive, programmable, ergonomic, extensible, and efficient.
- **Transaction-based Execution**: Users define graphs of UE work within `begin_frame()` and `end_frame()` Python contexts. Work specified in `begin_frame` is guaranteed to execute at the start of a UE frame, and work in `end_frame` executes at the end of the same frame, all sequentially on the game thread.
- **Deterministic Stepping**: While not guaranteeing consecutive frame execution by default, users can achieve deterministic stepping by pausing the UE simulation, performing state mutations within a transaction, and then pausing again. Alternatively, `end_frame(single_step=True)` instructs the game thread to advance to the next frame's beginning and wait.
- **Direct Function and Variable Access**: Any UE function or variable exposed to the reflection system (via `UFUNCTION` or `UPROPERTY` annotations in C++) can be accessed directly from Python as if it were a native Python function or attribute. This includes nested variables within structs.
- **Extensibility**: New C++ functionality can be exposed to SPEAR simply by adding `UFUNCTION` or `UPROPERTY` annotations in C++ header files, even outside the SPEAR codebase. No additional registration or wrapper code is required.
- **Synchronous and Asynchronous Operations**: By default, UE operations are synchronous, blocking Python code until execution on the game thread is complete. For efficiency, asynchronous variants are provided (e.g., `async.GetComponentLocation`), which immediately return a future object. This object can be used later to retrieve the result without blocking the game thread unless the operation is still pending. The system gates Python thread progress to prevent it from getting too far ahead of the game thread.
- **Native Frame Rate Execution**: Asynchronous operations enable the UE application to execute user Python code at its native frame rate by preventing the game thread from being blocked by Python synchronization.

**Data Transfer and Performance Optimizations:**
- **JSON for Reflection-Visible Types**: The system leverages UE's ability to serialize and deserialize reflectable types to and from JSON. This enables Python dictionaries to serve as a universal representation for arguments, return values, and variable manipulation.
- **NumPy Interoperability (SpFunctions)**: For efficient transfer of large data blocks like high-resolution images, a custom dispatching system called `SpFunctions` is implemented. These functions, defined as special child components of UE objects, support NumPy arrays directly as arguments and return values, marshalling them efficiently.
- **Interprocess Shared Memory**: To further optimize data transfer, SPEAR integrates interprocess shared memory. This allows for zero-copy transfer of data, such as rendered images, directly from the GPU into a user's NumPy array, significantly reducing overhead. Python code can allocate shared memory regions and pass NumPy arrays backed by these regions to `SpFunctions`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SPEAR focused on demonstrating its expanded programmable functionality, enhanced rendering performance, and diverse application capabilities.

**Programmable Functionality:**
- SPEAR exposes over 14,000 unique Unreal Engine functions and 53,000 UE properties to Python. This represents an order-of-magnitude increase in programmable functionality compared to existing UE-based simulators. For instance, AirSim and CARLA do not expose underlying UE functions/variables, while UnrealCV+ exposes 747 UE functions and 8,721 UE variables.
- Despite this extensive exposure, SPEAR maintains a modest code footprint (27,193 lines of code) compared to AirSim (144,536 lines) and CARLA (150,502 lines). This is attributed to the reflection-based approach, which reduces the need for extensive hand-crafted wrappers.

**Rendering Performance:**
- **Comparison with UnrealCV+**: When rendering a 1920x1080 photorealistic beauty image, SPEAR achieved 73 frames per second (FPS) with 2 frames of rendering latency, asynchronous communication, and shared memory. This is reported to be an order of magnitude faster (9-21 times) than UnrealCV+, which achieved 3.5 FPS under similar conditions. Even with 0 frames rendering latency, SPEAR reached 56.2 FPS with asynchronous communication and shared memory, closely matching a standalone UE application performing comparable rendering work (56.5 FPS).
- **Comparison with AirSim and CARLA**: Under conditions where standalone rendering speeds of UE applications were matched across simulators (approx. 89-93 FPS), SPEAR demonstrated superior performance attributable to reduced communication overhead.
 - For 0 frames rendering latency, SPEAR achieved 32.3 FPS, which is approximately 12 times faster than AirSim's 2.6 FPS.
 - For 2 frames rendering latency, SPEAR achieved 37.1 FPS, approximately 10% faster than CARLA's 32.7 FPS.
- The results indicate that both asynchronous communication and the use of interprocess shared memory significantly contribute to SPEAR's performance gains. The customizable camera sensor also supports various ground truth image modalities not typically available in other UE-based simulators.

**Example Applications:**
The utility and flexibility of SPEAR were demonstrated through a diverse collection of example applications, highlighting capabilities that are challenging or impossible to implement in existing UE-based simulators due to limited programmatic access:
- **Multi-Agent Control**: Controlling multiple embodied agents (e.g., humans, cars, robots) with distinct action spaces across various Epic Games sample projects like CitySample, StackOBot, CropoutSample, and GameAnimationSample.
- **Environmental Manipulation**: Rendering photorealistic city-scale environments and programmatically manipulating Unreal Engine's procedural content generation (PCG) systems (e.g., translating rock structures, triggering dynamic environmental adjustments) and simulating time-of-day changes.
- **High-Fidelity Rendering**: Rendering synchronized multi-view images of detailed human characters in projects like MetaHumans.
- **Co-simulation**: Coordinating interactive co-simulation with external physics simulators, exemplified by integrating with MuJoCo for real-time physics updates and corresponding UE scene synchronization.
- **AI-Assisted Content Creation**: Demonstrating scene editing with natural language input via an AI coding assistant, which iteratively writes SPEAR programs to modify a UE scene.

These applications collectively illustrate SPEAR's ability to offer extensive programmatic control and high-performance data generation, expanding the scope of research possible within photorealistic simulation environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SPEAR's development addresses several critical limitations of existing photorealistic simulators built on Unreal Engine, positioning it as a potentially foundational tool for future research in embodied AI, robotics, and computer vision.

**Advancements in Embodied AI and Robotics Research:**
By providing an order-of-magnitude increase in programmable functionality and rendering speed, SPEAR enables researchers to exert finer-grained control over virtual environments. This can facilitate the development and training of more sophisticated embodied agents, including agile robots operating in complex, city-scale photorealistic environments. The comprehensive access to UE's systems allows for the creation of intricate interaction scenarios and the precise manipulation of environmental dynamics, which are crucial for learning robust behaviors.

**Enhanced Synthetic Data Generation:**
The simulator's ability to render high-resolution photorealistic images at high frame rates, coupled with a wide array of ground truth image modalities (including novel ones like non-diffuse intrinsic image decompositions and PBR shading parameters), significantly enriches the quality and diversity of synthetic datasets. This detailed data can be instrumental in training and evaluating large-scale foundation models, as well as state-of-the-art methods for 3D/4D reconstruction, segmentation, and depth estimation, potentially reducing reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world data collection.

**Bridge to Multimodal AI Models:**
The design of SPEAR, which allows programmatic control via Python and rapid photorealistic rendering, positions it as a bridge between internet-scale vision-and-language models and advanced virtual worlds in the Unreal Engine. This connection could foster new research avenues in AI-assisted content creation, personalized entertainment, and the development of virtual laboratories dedicated to studying spatial intelligence and world model dynamics.

**Overcoming Technical Bottlenecks:**
The modular plugin architecture, the reflection-based approach for exposing UE functionality, and the optimized data transfer mechanisms (NumPy interoperability, shared memory, asynchronous operations) directly address the issues of limited generality, programmability, and performance in previous UE-based simulators. This technical robustness reduces engineering overhead for researchers, allowing them to focus more on scientific inquiry rather than simulator development or optimization.

**Facilitating Complex Simulations:**
The expressive high-level programming model, which allows deterministic execution of complex graphs of UE work within a single frame, provides the precision necessary for intricate co-simulation applications, such as integrating external physics engines like MuJoCo. This capability enables more realistic and interactive simulations essential for various robotics and control tasks.

In summary, SPEAR represents an advancement in interactive photorealistic simulation, offering a more programmable, efficient, and versatile platform that can accelerate progress across multiple AI-related disciplines by providing enhanced tools for agent training, data generation, and complex scenario design.
