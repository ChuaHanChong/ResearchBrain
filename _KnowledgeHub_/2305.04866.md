# Causal Policy Gradient for Whole-Body Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A reinforcement learning framework called Causal MoMa automatically discovers causal dependencies between robot actions and reward terms in whole-body mobile manipulation tasks. This framework then leverages these dependencies within a causal policy gradient to reduce variance, yielding policies that demonstrate successful zero-shot transfer from simulation to real-world mobile manipulators and improved performance over baselines.

## Problem

- Whole-body mobile manipulation (MoMa) tasks involve high-dimensional action spaces and multi-objective reward functions, leading to unstable reinforcement learning (RL) training due to high variance.
- Existing RL approaches for MoMa often manually segregate locomotion and manipulation, hindering synergistic control and requiring extensive human domain knowledge.
- Traditional planning and control methods struggle with dynamic or uncertain environments and the direct optimization of complex, perceptual-driven tasks.

## Method

- A two-step reinforcement learning framework, Causal MoMa, is proposed: first, it automatically discovers causal dependencies, and second, it integrates them into policy learning.
- Action-reward causal discovery infers a binary bi-adjacency matrix using Conditional Mutual Information (CMI) from exploratory robot interactions.
- Policy learning, built on PPO, employs a modified policy gradient that multiplies per-reward-term advantages by the discovered causal matrix, selectively assigning credit to action dimensions to reduce variance.

## Results

- The causal discovery module accurately identified ground truth action-reward dependencies in a controlled Minigrid environment.
- Causal MoMa consistently achieved superior performance (e.g., 93.6% success rate for HSR vs. 74.3% for vanilla PPO) and higher rewards in realistic simulated environments like iGibson with various mobile manipulators.
- Learned policies successfully transferred zero-shot to a real-world Toyota HSR robot, demonstrating robust whole-body control and outperforming traditional planning and control methods in dynamic and unseen environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mobile manipulation tasks inherently possess a causal structure where specific robot action dimensions directly influence particular reward function terms.
> - Leveraging these precise causal relationships between actions and rewards can significantly reduce the variance of policy gradient estimators in reinforcement learning.
> - These causal dependencies can be automatically discovered from robot interaction data, eliminating the need for manual, human-engineered factorization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper introduces Causal MoMa, a novel reinforcement learning (RL) framework designed to address the challenges of mobile manipulation (MoMa) tasks in robotics. MoMa, which combines locomotion and interaction capabilities, is inherently difficult due to large action spaces and multi-objective task definitions. Causal MoMa proposes a two-step approach: first, it automatically discovers the causal dependencies between robot actions and reward function terms; second, it exploits these dependencies within a causal policy gradient method to reduce variance and improve training efficiency. The effectiveness of Causal MoMa is demonstrated across various simulated robots and validated through successful zero-shot transfer to a real-world mobile manipulator.

This research is situated at the intersection of mobile robotics, manipulation, and reinforcement learning, specifically addressing the challenging domain of whole-body mobile manipulation.

#### 1.1. Challenges in Mobile Manipulation (MoMa)

MoMa tasks require robots to simultaneously perform locomotion (navigation) and manipulation (interaction with objects). This presents several difficulties:
- **Large Action Spaces:** Combining the degrees of freedom (DoF) of a mobile base, an arm, and potentially a head results in a very high-dimensional action space.
- **Multi-Objective Nature:** MoMa tasks often involve multiple, sometimes conflicting, objectives, such as reaching a goal, avoiding collisions (base, arm, self-collision), maintaining orientation, and interacting with objects. These are typically represented by a composite reward function, which can complicate RL training.
- **Synergistic Control:** Traditional approaches often segregate these capabilities, either by sequential execution (navigate, then manipulate) or by manually partitioning the action space, which prevents true synergistic control of the whole body.

#### 1.2. Existing Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper discusses existing approaches and their shortcomings:
- **Motion Planning and Control:** These methods (e.g., RRT, MPC) often require accurate geometric models of the environment and robot, struggle with uncertainty, dynamic environments, and complex multi-objective optimization. They typically need human-defined task priorities and action dimensions per objective.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - **Hierarchical RL:** Many RL approaches for MoMa use hierarchical schemes, where low-level policies control specific parts of the robot (e.g., base, arm) separately. While efficient, this forfeits whole-body synergy.
 - **End-to-End RL:** Directly applying RL to the entire action space allows for synergistic control but often suffers from "insurmountable" complexity, especially with composite reward functions, leading to poor performance and stability issues. This is primarily due to high variance in policy gradient estimation, which scales quadratically with action space dimensionality.
 - **Factored RL (Manual):** Some prior work attempts to factorize the action space (e.g., base vs. arm) and assign them manually to sub-tasks, computing separate advantages. While offering some benefits, this still requires human domain knowledge and is limited by coarse factorization.

#### 1.3. Variance Reduction in RL

A fundamental problem in policy-based RL is the large variance of gradient estimators, which impedes stable and efficient learning.
- **Baselines:** Using a state-dependent value function (baseline) is a common, unbiased variance reduction technique.
- **Action-Dependent Baselines:** More advanced baselines have been proposed, but often introduce computational overhead or do not empirically show significant variance reduction over state-dependent ones.
- **Factored Policy Gradients (FPG):** Spooner et al. (2021) demonstrated that known causal relationships between action dimensions and reward terms can be leveraged to derive a low-variance, efficiently computable factored policy gradient. However, this relies on *a priori* known causal graphs, which are not always available or accurate.

Causal MoMa builds upon this landscape by aiming to overcome the limitations of manual factorization in MoMa and the need for pre-defined causal graphs in FPG. Its novelty lies in *automatically discovering* these causal dependencies and then seamlessly integrating them into a policy gradient framework to enable robust whole-body control for complex MoMa tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a new reinforcement learning framework, Causal MoMa, capable of effectively training policies for complex whole-body mobile manipulation tasks. The core motivation stems from the inherent difficulties of MoMa, particularly:

1. **Large Action Space and Multi-Objective Rewards:** MoMa tasks typically involve many degrees of freedom (base, arm, head) and multiple sub-objectives (e.g., reaching, collision avoidance, maintaining orientation). Existing end-to-end RL methods struggle with the resulting high-dimensional action spaces and composite reward functions, leading to high variance in policy gradients and unstable training.
2. **Limitations of Current Approaches:**
 - **Segregation:** Many methods manually segregate tasks into navigation and manipulation, preventing simultaneous, synergistic control of the robot's entire body. This requires significant human domain knowledge to partition the action space and match it to sub-objectives.
 - **Planning/Control Deficiencies:** Traditional planning and control methods often rely on accurate geometric models, struggle in dynamic or uncertain environments, and are difficult to extend to complex multi-objective scenarios or direct sensor inputs like images.
3. **Untapped Causal Structure:** The authors' key insight is that in MoMa, specific parts of the robot's action space (e.g., base movement) primarily affect certain reward terms (e.g., base collision), while other parts (e.g., arm movement) affect different reward terms (e.g., arm collision). This "strong correlation" or *causal dependency* between action dimensions and reward terms is often overlooked or manually approximated.

Therefore, the **key objectives** of Causal MoMa are:
- To **automatically discover** the direct causal dependencies between controllable degrees of freedom (action space dimensions) and task objectives (reward terms) in mobile manipulation tasks. This eliminates the need for manual, human-engineered factorization.
- To **exploit these discovered causal relationships** within a novel policy learning framework based on causal policy gradients.
- To **reduce the variance** of policy gradient estimation, thereby improving convergence speed and the overall performance of RL algorithms for MoMa.
- To enable **simultaneous and synergistic control** of the robot's whole body (base, arm, head) for complex MoMa tasks.
- To demonstrate **zero-shot transferability** of learned policies from simulation to real-world robots, highlighting the framework's practical utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Causal MoMa is a two-step reinforcement learning framework designed to train policies for mobile manipulation by leveraging the causal structure between action dimensions and reward terms.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is modeled as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process (MDP) (S, A, P, R, γ). The robot's action `a` is an `n`-dimensional vector. The reward function `R(s, a)` is assumed to be factored, meaning it's a linear sum of `m` reward terms: `R(s, a) = Σ_j=1^m r_j(s, a)`. The core insight is that each reward term `r_j` is typically only causally influenced by a subset of the action dimensions `a_i`.

#### 3.2. A. Action-Reward Causal Discovery

The first step is to autonomously infer the causal relationship between action dimensions and reward terms. This relationship is represented by a binary bi-adjacency `n × m` causal matrix `B`, where `B_ij = 1` if action dimension `a_i` causally affects reward term `r_j`, and `0` otherwise. This discovery phase relies on data collected from exploratory, random interactions with the environment.

The causal inference is based on four key assumptions:
- **A1: Causal Markov Condition:** Variables are conditionally independent of non-descendants given their direct causes.
- **A2: Faithfulness:** No conditional independence relations exist beyond those implied by the Markov property.
- **A3: Uncorrelated Action Dimensions during Exploration:** This is ensured by using a random policy for data collection, preventing spurious correlations between actions.
- **A4: Action-Reward Causality in Short-Horizon Transitions:** Causal dependencies are observable within a short time horizon `k` (e.g., 1 or few timesteps), implying that an action's effect on a reward is relatively immediate.

Based on Theorem 3.1, `a_i → r_j` if and only if `a_i` and `r_j` are conditionally dependent given the state `s` and all *other* action dimensions `a \ a_i`. This conditional dependence is measured using **Conditional Mutual Information (CMI)**.
`CMI(a_i, r_j) = E[log(p(r_j | {s, a}) / p(r_j | {s, a \ a_i}))]`
A causal edge `a_i → r_j` is established if `CMI(a_i, r_j)` exceeds a threshold `ϵ`.

To efficiently estimate CMI for all `n × m` pairs, a specialized neural network architecture is used. For each reward channel `r_j`, a single model predicts its value from either the full action vector (`p(r_j | {s, a})`) or a partial vector where `a_i` is masked (`p(r_j | {s, a \ a_i})`). This model uses individual feature mappings for each action dimension and the state, combines them via element-wise max pooling, and then passes them through a prediction network. This architecture allows the estimation of all necessary conditional probabilities with only `m` models, significantly reducing computational overhead compared to `n × m` models.

#### 3.3. B. Policy Learning

Once the causal matrix `B` is inferred, Causal MoMa integrates it into a modified policy gradient procedure to reduce variance. The policy gradient `∇_θ J(θ)` is redefined as:
`∇_θ J(θ) = ∇_θ log π_θ(a|s) ⋅ B ⋅ Â_π(s, a)`
where `Â_π(s, a)` is an `m`-dimensional vector representing the advantage function factored across reward terms (i.e., per-reward-term advantage). `∇_θ log π_θ(a|s)` is a matrix where each column corresponds to the log gradient of a particular action dimension's probability.

Theorem 3.2 proves that if the causal matrix `B` is correct, this modified gradient estimator is *unbiased*. Crucially, multiplying the per-reward-advantage with `B` effectively prunes irrelevant reward terms from the policy gradient update for each action dimension. This selective credit assignment significantly reduces gradient variance, stabilizing the training process without losing the agent's ability to coordinate all degrees of freedom.

The framework is implemented on top of the **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm. Key modifications include:
- A multi-dimensional value network `V_ϕ(s) ∈ R^m`, which predicts a value for each of the `m` reward terms.
- Per-reward-channel advantages `Â_π(s, a)` are calculated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).
- These are then transformed into per-action-dimension advantages `Â'_π(s, a) = B ⋅ Â_π(s, a)`.
- The policy network `π_θ(s)` is updated using these per-action-dimension advantages with the PPO policy objective, effectively updating each action dimension based only on its causally related reward terms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper evaluates Causal MoMa across three types of environments: a simplistic Minigrid simulator, realistic robot simulators (iGibson and Gazebo), and a real-world mobile manipulator, comparing it against various baselines.

#### 4.1. Minigrid Simulator Evaluation

- **Environment:** A modified Minigrid environment with 4 discrete action dimensions (2 for navigation, 2 for virtual arm manipulation) and 5 composite reward terms (navigation, obstacle avoidance, two virtual manipulation rewards). This environment allowed for a clear ground truth causal matrix.
- **Q1: Causal Matrix Accuracy:** Causal MoMa's discovered causal matrix (`B_cmi_minigrid`) **exactly matched the ground truth** causal matrix. This confirms the effectiveness of the causal discovery step in a controlled setting.
- **Q2: Performance Improvement:** Vanilla PPO failed to solve the task, converging to a local optimum where it avoided necessary "manipulation" tiles. Causal MoMa **significantly outperformed vanilla PPO**, achieving the highest reward. A hardcoded "arm-base" factored PPO performed almost as well as Causal MoMa, indicating that even approximate factorization can help in simpler domains. However, a randomly generated factorization was catastrophic. This highlights Causal MoMa's ability to automatically find the correct factorization, avoiding human error and enabling better performance.

#### 4.2. Realistic Robot Simulator Evaluation (iGibson and Gazebo)

- **iGibson Environment:** Used for training with two realistic mobile manipulators (Fetch and HSR) with continuous action spaces and different kinematics (Fetch: non-holonomic base, 7-DoF arm, Cartesian control; HSR: omnidirectional base, 5-DoF arm, joint control). The task involved reaching, maintaining end-effector orientation/height, and avoiding various collisions (base, arm, self), with 8 composite reward terms.
- **Q2 & Q3: Performance and Generality:** Causal MoMa consistently achieved **superior performance** (higher reward and success rates) compared to both vanilla PPO and FPPO with hardcoded arm-base separation for *both Fetch and HSR robots*. For instance, on HSR, Causal MoMa achieved 93.6% success vs. 74.3% for vanilla PPO. The larger performance gap observed with HSR (which had a sparser discovered causal matrix) supports the hypothesis that a more fine-grained, accurate causal factorization leads to greater variance reduction benefits. This demonstrates Causal MoMa's **generality across different robot morphologies and action spaces**.
- **Gazebo (Sim2Sim Transfer & Comparison to MPC):** Policies trained in iGibson were zero-shot transferred to Gazebo (a different simulator).
- **Q5: Comparison to Reactive Controllers:** Causal MoMa **outperformed SLQ-MPC** (a state-of-the-art model predictive control strategy), especially in dynamic scenarios. SLQ-MPC struggled with dynamic obstacles due to its reliance on explicit environment models and difficulty balancing multiple objectives without perceptual mechanisms. Causal MoMa's direct sensor-to-action mapping proved more robust in these conditions.

#### 4.3. Real-World Mobile Manipulator Evaluation

- **Robot & Environment:** Zero-shot transfer of the HSR policy learned in iGibson to a real Toyota HSR robot in an unseen household environment. The task involved reaching a moving goal with the end-effector while maintaining orientation, avoiding collisions, and keeping the goal in view.
- **Q4: Generalization to Real World:** Causal MoMa policies demonstrated **successful zero-shot transfer** and **robust performance** in the real world.
- **Q5: Comparison to Sampling-Based Planners:** Causal MoMa **outperformed CBiRRT2** (a sampling-based motion planner, both open-loop and replanning versions) in most scenarios, particularly with dynamic goals and dynamic obstacles. CBiRRT2 struggled with dynamic environments, model-real discrepancies, and accurately reaching goals due to open-loop execution. Causal MoMa's reactive policy, learned through RL, could adapt to unforeseen dynamics.
- **Limitations & Insights:** While generally superior, Causal MoMa showed a lower success rate in one specific static scenario (robot blocked by a long obstacle) compared to CBiRRT2. This is because CBiRRT2, given a privileged global map, could plan long paths around obstacles, a capability that Causal MoMa's purely reactive, local sensing-based policy sometimes lacked for complex global planning. Despite this, Causal MoMa maintained high accuracy in maintaining the desired end-effector orientation, even with dynamically changing orientation goals not seen during training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Significance and Potential Impact

The Causal MoMa framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic learning, particularly for complex mobile manipulation tasks.
- **Automated Causal Discovery:** By autonomously inferring causal dependencies, Causal MoMa eliminates the need for extensive human domain knowledge and manual engineering of action space factorization. This reduces the burden on roboticists and makes RL more accessible for complex, multi-modal control problems.
- **Enhanced RL Training:** The use of causal policy gradients provides an unbiased method for significant variance reduction in RL training. This leads to faster convergence, greater stability, and ultimately, better-performing policies for high-dimensional and multi-objective tasks.
- **Synergistic Whole-Body Control:** Causal MoMa successfully enables simultaneous and cooperative control of all robot degrees of freedom (base, arm, head), unlocking the full potential of mobile manipulators for intricate tasks that require coordinated movement.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The demonstrated zero-shot transfer from simulation to the real world, and the superior performance in dynamic and unseen environments, highlight the robustness and generalization capabilities of policies trained with Causal MoMa. This is a crucial step towards deploying learning-based robots in unstructured, real-world settings like households.
- **Bridging the Gap:** The work effectively bridges the gap between traditional model-based planning/control (which struggle with dynamics and lack direct sensor-to-action mapping) and end-to-end RL (which often suffer from variance and data inefficiency), offering a powerful hybrid approach.

The potential impact is substantial, paving the way for the development of more capable and autonomous household robots that can seamlessly integrate navigation and manipulation to perform complex chores and assistance tasks.

#### 5.2. Limitations

Despite its strengths, Causal MoMa has several limitations:
- **Short-Horizon Causality Assumption (A4):** The most critical limitation is the assumption that causal dependencies between actions and rewards can be observed within a short time horizon (`k` timesteps). This might not hold for very sparse or long-horizon reward functions where an action's effect might only manifest much later. The authors suggest that a hierarchical causal discovery procedure at different horizon lengths might address this.
- **Long-Trajectory Planning:** As a reactive, visuo-motor control policy, Causal MoMa may struggle with planning very long trajectories that require escaping local minima, especially when relying on partial local observations (e.g., LiDAR scans from behind a large obstacle). In such specific scenarios, global planners with privileged map information might still outperform it.
- **Direct Image Consumption:** The current policy does not directly consume raw image observations but rather transforms them into an end-effector goal position. While this accelerates training, it suggests an area for future improvement to enable more general visual understanding.
- **Exploration Overhead:** The causal discovery phase requires an initial period of exploratory data collection, which incurs a computational and data overhead before policy training truly begins.

In conclusion, Causal MoMa offers a principled and effective approach to tackle the complexities of whole-body mobile manipulation by automatically discovering and leveraging causal relationships in the robot's action-reward structure. While facing some inherent limitations common to reactive control and short-horizon causal inference, its demonstrated performance and transferability mark a significant step towards more autonomous and capable robotic agents.
