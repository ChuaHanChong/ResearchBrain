# Experience Replay with Random Reshuffling

> [!summary] Summary
> New methods, Random Reshuffling with Circular Buffer (RR-C) and Random Reshuffling by Masking (RR-M), adapt random reshuffling from supervised learning to address the dynamic nature of reinforcement learning's experience replay. These techniques reduce variance in how transitions are sampled, leading to improved performance on Atari-10 benchmarks for various deep RL algorithms.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning from sequential data suffers from temporal correlations, leading to unstable learning and reduced sample efficiency.
- Standard experience replay samples transitions with replacement, which incurs higher gradient variance compared to random reshuffling techniques used in supervised learning.
- Adapting random reshuffling to dynamic RL replay buffers, especially with non-uniform, dynamically updated priorities (as in prioritized experience replay), poses significant challenges.

## Method

- For uniform experience replay, **RR-C** (Random Reshuffling with a Circular Buffer) shuffles buffer indices at each "epoch" start and samples them sequentially, skipping invalid entries.
- For prioritized experience replay, **RR-M** (Random Reshuffling by Masking) temporarily masks the priorities of oversampled transitions to guide sampling towards under-sampled ones.
- Both methods guarantee ==without-replacement sampling== over a set period or against an ideal distribution, aiming to reduce variance in transition utilization.

## Results

- Numerical simulations confirmed both RR-C and RR-M substantially reduce variance in the number of times each transition is sampled compared to standard methods.
- RR-C demonstrated modest performance gains for DQN and C51 on Atari-10, significantly outperforming with-replacement sampling in **6 out of 10 games** for C51.
- RR-M improved DDQN+LAP performance on Atari-10 in **8 out of 10 games**, with significant improvement in one game, and showed additional benefits when integrated with stratified sampling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Random reshuffling, a technique known for variance reduction and faster convergence in supervised learning, can be effectively adapted to reinforcement learning's dynamic experience replay buffers.
> - The performance improvements observed come from applying without-replacement sampling at the buffer level across an "epoch," rather than merely preventing duplicates within a single mini-batch.
> - Systematically balancing the sampling frequency of transitions throughout the entire replay buffer leads to more robust and effective reinforcement learning agent training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents frequently learn from sequential data, which, by its very nature, introduces strong temporal correlations. These correlations can destabilize the learning process and reduce sample efficiency. Experience replay (Lin, 1992; Mnih et al., 2015) was introduced as a foundational technique to mitigate this problem. It stores past transitions (state, action, reward, next state) in a replay buffer and later samples them for training updates. By drawing mini-batches from this buffer, experience replay effectively decorrelates the training data, leading to more stable and efficient learning, particularly for off-policy algorithms like Deep Q-Networks (DQN).

The standard implementation of experience replay typically involves sampling transitions *with replacement* uniformly at random from the buffer. This approach treats the collected experiences as a re-usable dataset, akin to how data is handled in supervised learning (SL) when training on a fixed dataset. However, a crucial distinction exists: in many SL settings, particularly with Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), a technique called *random reshuffling (RR)* is commonly employed. RR shuffles the entire dataset at the beginning of each epoch and then processes each sample *exactly once* in a sequential manner. This *without-replacement* sampling strategy has theoretical advantages, often leading to faster convergence rates (e.g., O(1/K^2) for smooth and strongly convex functions) compared to sampling with replacement (O(1/K)) (Bottou, 2009; Nagaraj et al., 2019; Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2021). Empirical evidence in SL also supports RR's superior performance.

The present work identifies a significant gap: despite the proven benefits of random reshuffling in supervised learning, it has not been directly adopted in reinforcement learning's experience replay. The primary reasons for this omission are the dynamic nature of RL replay buffers (continuously adding new transitions and overwriting old ones) and the added complexity of prioritized experience replay, where transition priorities change non-stationarily. This paper aims to bridge this gap by proposing novel sampling methods that adapt the principles of random reshuffling to the unique challenges of RL's dynamic experience replay.

Beyond uniform experience replay, the paper also addresses *prioritized experience replay (PER)* (Schaul et al., 2016), a popular extension that samples important transitions (e.g., those with high temporal-difference error) more frequently. PER further complicates sampling by introducing non-uniform, dynamic probabilities. The proposed methods aim to bring the benefits of RR to this advanced setting as well.

The paper contextualizes its contribution against other related works. It distinguishes its approach from within-minibatch without-replacement sampling, which prevents duplicates within a single batch but doesn't guarantee overall balanced sampling across the buffer. It also contrasts with methods like Combined Experience Replay (CER) (Zhang & Sutton, 2017), which prioritizes recent transitions, and Corrected Uniform Experience Replay (CUER) (Yenicesu et al., 2024), which reduces the priority of sampled transitions. While these methods also aim for variance reduction, the current work specifically focuses on generalizing the epoch-level without-replacement guarantee of random reshuffling, aiming for a more fundamental and unbiased approach to balanced sampling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the established theoretical and empirical advantages of random reshuffling (RR) in supervised learning, particularly its ability to reduce the variance of gradient estimates and accelerate convergence. The authors postulate that a similar variance reduction could significantly benefit reinforcement learning by leading to more stable learning and improved sample efficiency, which are critical challenges in the field.

The key objectives of this paper are:

1. **Adapt Random Reshuffling for Uniform Experience Replay**: To develop a sampling method that integrates the principles of random reshuffling into the standard, uniform experience replay setting, specifically addressing the dynamic nature of the RL replay buffer (i.e., new transitions being added and old ones removed).
2. **Extend Random Reshuffling for Prioritized Experience Replay**: To design a complementary sampling method that adapts RR to the more complex prioritized experience replay, which involves non-stationary, non-uniform sampling probabilities.
3. **Analyze Properties of Proposed Methods**: To theoretically analyze the bias and variance properties of the new sampling methods, comparing them against traditional with-replacement sampling.
4. **Empirically Validate Effectiveness**: To empirically evaluate the performance of these RR-based sampling methods in deep reinforcement learning benchmarks, demonstrating their practical utility and potential for performance improvements.
5. **Provide Practical Implementations**: To ensure the proposed methods are straightforward to implement and can serve as drop-in replacements for existing sampling strategies in popular RL codebases, making them accessible to practitioners.

Ultimately, the goal is to bridge the conceptual and practical gap between data sampling strategies in supervised learning and reinforcement learning, leveraging a proven optimization technique to enhance RL agent training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper proposes two novel sampling methods, each tailored to a specific experience replay setting: Random Reshuffling with a Circular Buffer (RR-C) for uniform experience replay and Random Reshuffling by Masking (RR-M) for prioritized experience replay.

#### 3.1. RR-C: Uniform Experience Replay with Random Reshuffling

RR-C is designed for uniform experience replay, where all transitions are considered equally important. The core idea is to apply random reshuffling not to the transitions themselves (which are dynamic) but to the *indices* of the circular replay buffer.
- **Mechanism**: A shuffled list of indices, covering the entire capacity of the circular buffer, is generated at the beginning of an "epoch." During training, mini-batches are sampled by sequentially popping indices from this shuffled list. When the list is exhausted, a new shuffled list is generated, marking the start of a new epoch.
- **Handling Dynamics**: If a sampled index points to a location in the buffer that has not yet been filled or contains an overwritten (stale) transition, that index is simply skipped, and the next one in the list is drawn. This ensures that only valid, current transitions are sampled.
- **Properties**: This method guarantees that each buffer index is sampled exactly once per epoch, significantly reducing the variance in how often individual transitions are sampled.
- **Theoretical Analysis**: The paper theoretically demonstrates that RR-C is initially biased when the buffer is not full (due to skipping indices) but becomes *unbiased* for sufficiently old transitions (i.e., after the buffer is full and skipping is no longer an issue). Crucially, it proves that Var[X_RR-C] ≤ Var[X_UER] for sufficiently large ‘i’ (older transitions), indicating lower variance compared to uniform experience replay (UER) with replacement sampling.
- **Computational Efficiency**: RR-C is computationally efficient, requiring only a one-time shuffle of indices at the start of each epoch, adding minimal overhead.

#### 3.2. RR-M: Prioritized Experience Replay with Random Reshuffling

RR-M extends the principles of random reshuffling to the more complex setting of prioritized experience replay, where sampling probabilities are non-uniform and dynamically updated.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of explicitly creating a shuffled list of weighted transitions (which would be impractical given dynamic priorities and buffer content), RR-M maintains `actual_counts` (how many times each transition has been sampled) and `expected_counts` (how many times each transition *should* have been sampled based on its priority).
- **Dynamic Masking**: Before sampling a mini-batch, the method identifies "oversampled" transitions (where `actual_counts` > `expected_counts`). The priorities of these oversampled transitions are temporarily masked (set to a very small value) within the sum tree data structure used for prioritized sampling.
- **Sampling and Updating**: Mini-batches are then sampled *without replacement* from the sum tree according to these masked priorities. After sampling, the `actual_counts` of sampled transitions are incremented, and `expected_counts` for *all* transitions are updated proportionally to their current priorities and the batch size. When old transitions are overwritten in the circular buffer, their counts are reset.
- **Theoretical Analysis**: Due to its dynamic nature, RR-M's theoretical analysis is more complex. The paper shows that, under a simplified setting, the relative error between actual and expected sample counts vanishes asymptotically (lim k→∞ X_RR-M / E[X_PER] = 1). It also demonstrates that Var[X_RR-M] is bounded and thus eventually becomes lower than Var[X_PER], which grows linearly with the number of samples.
- **Computational Efficiency**: RR-M is more computationally intensive than RR-C, as it requires O(N) operations (N being buffer size) for updating expected counts and identifying oversampled transitions for each mini-batch. However, the authors note that these operations can be efficiently parallelized on GPUs using libraries like CuPy.

#### 3.3. Experimental Evaluation

- **Experience Replay Simulations**: Simple numerical simulations are conducted to visually demonstrate the variance reduction properties of RR-C and RR-M compared to standard sampling methods. These simulations track sample counts over 100 timesteps with a small buffer, illustrating the distribution of samples.
- **Deep RL Benchmarks**:
 - **Uniform ER**: RR-C is evaluated on the Atari-10 benchmark using DQN and C51 algorithms, comparing its performance against standard with-replacement sampling. The study also investigates if within-minibatch without-replacement sampling alone provides similar benefits.
 - **Prioritized ER**: RR-M is evaluated on the Atari-10 benchmark using DDQN+LAP (Double DQN with Loss-Adjusted Prioritized Experience Replay). Its performance is compared against both with-replacement sampling and stratified sampling, another common PER variant that aims for diverse sampling.
- **Metrics**: Performance is measured by the mean episodic returns over the last 100 episodes, averaged over 10 random seeds, along with statistical significance (p-values from Welch's t-test).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents compelling theoretical and empirical evidence supporting the benefits of integrating random reshuffling into experience replay.

#### 4.1. Experience Replay Simulations

- **Variance Reduction**: The numerical simulations clearly illustrate that both RR-C and RR-M significantly reduce the variance in the number of times each transition is sampled compared to traditional with-replacement (WR) and even within-minibatch without-replacement (WOR) sampling.
 - For uniform experience replay, RR-C ensures a much tighter distribution of sample counts, with each transition being sampled fewer times in total compared to WR and WOR, which show highly skewed distributions where some transitions are sampled many times and others very few.
 - Similarly, for prioritized experience replay, RR-M effectively leads to lower-variance sample counts without introducing noticeable bias, even with dynamic priorities. This empirically validates the theoretical analyses which suggest asymptotic lower variance and vanishing relative error.

#### 4.2. Deep RL with Uniform Experience Replay (RR-C)

- **Performance Improvements**: When applied to deep RL algorithms (DQN and C51) on the Atari-10 benchmark, RR-C consistently yielded "modest performance improvements" over standard with-replacement sampling.
 - For C51, RR-C significantly outperformed WR in 6 out of 10 games (Amidar, Frostbite, BattleZone, DoubleDunk, NameThisGame, Phoenix), with p-values indicating statistical significance.
 - For DQN, RR-C significantly outperformed WR in 2 out of 10 games (BattleZone, NameThisGame), and generally showed improved or comparable performance across others.
- **Buffer-level vs. Mini-batch-level Without-Replacement**: The experiments revealed that simply using *within-minibatch without-replacement* sampling (WOR) (which prevents duplicates in a single batch) did not offer significant advantages over WR for C51. This finding underscores that the benefits of RR-C arise from the *buffer-level* without-replacement sampling over an epoch, rather than just preventing immediate duplicates within a small batch from a large buffer.

#### 4.3. Deep RL with Prioritized Experience Replay (RR-M)

- **Performance Improvements**: For DDQN+LAP with prioritized experience replay, RR-M also showed beneficial, though less pronounced, results.
 - RR-M outperformed standard with-replacement sampling in 8 out of 10 games, with significant improvements in NameThisGame.
 - Comparing against *stratified sampling* (ST), another method used in PER to ensure diversity, RR-M combined with stratified sampling (RR-M+ST) still outperformed pure stratified sampling in 8 out of 10 games, with significant improvements in Amidar and Riverraid, and a significant decrease in Bowling. This suggests that the principles of RR-M provide additional benefits even when other diversity-enhancing sampling strategies are employed.

Overall, the empirical results confirm that explicitly managing the distribution of sampled transitions across the entire replay buffer through RR-based methods leads to more robust and often higher-performing RL agents.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning by systematically addressing a fundamental aspect of experience replay: the sampling strategy. By drawing parallels to the well-established benefits of random reshuffling in supervised learning, the authors have successfully adapted this powerful concept to the dynamic and complex environment of deep reinforcement learning.

The key significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Improved Stability and Sample Efficiency**: The primary impact is the potential for more stable and sample-efficient RL training. By reducing the variance in how often past experiences are replayed, the proposed RR-based methods ensure a more balanced and comprehensive utilization of the replay buffer. This can mitigate issues arising from over- or under-sampling critical transitions, leading to more robust learning curves and potentially faster convergence.
2. **Bridging the Gap between SL and RL Optimization**: The paper formally bridges a theoretical and practical gap between optimization strategies in supervised learning and reinforcement learning. It demonstrates that insights from one domain can be successfully translated and adapted to address challenges in another, opening avenues for further cross-pollination of ideas.
3. **Practical Drop-in Replacement**: Both RR-C and RR-M are designed to be straightforward to implement as "drop-in replacements" for existing sampling mechanisms. This ease of integration means RL practitioners can readily adopt these methods into their current codebases with minimal effort, potentially gaining performance improvements without extensive hyperparameter tuning or architectural changes. The provision of open-source code further facilitates this adoption.
4. **Foundation for Future Research**: This work provides a new baseline and direction for future research in experience replay. Investigating the interplay of RR-based sampling with other replay enhancements (e.g., advanced priority calculation methods, hindsight experience replay) or analyzing its impact on different classes of RL algorithms (e.g., policy gradient methods) could yield further insights. The theoretical analysis, while robust for RR-C, offers a simplified setting for RR-M, suggesting more complex theoretical investigations could be a fruitful area.
5. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Reliability**: In a field often plagued by sensitivity to hyperparameters and random seeds, methods that inherently reduce variance contribute to more reliable and reproducible research outcomes. The consistent, albeit modest, improvements across various games and algorithms attest to the robustness of the RR approach.

In conclusion, "Experience Replay with Random Reshuffling" introduces a valuable, principled approach to optimizing a core component of deep reinforcement learning. Its theoretical grounding, empirical validation, and practical implementability position it as an important advancement that can lead to more effective and efficient development of RL agents across a wide range of applications.
