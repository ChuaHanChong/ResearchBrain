# Imitation of Arm Gestures by the Semi-Humanoid Robot NICO

> [!summary] Summary
> A research team from Comenius University Bratislava developed an analytical, monocular RGB-based pipeline for arm gesture imitation on the NICO humanoid robot. The system achieves real-time performance, processing frames at an average of 590 FPS, and reconstructs joint angles with moderate accuracy (e.g., 10° MAE for shoulder pitch), while highlighting challenges in forearm rotation estimation, which improved by 25% with hand landmark integration.

## Problem

- Enabling robots to imitate human gestures for natural Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) is a fundamental challenge.
- Existing learning-based imitation methods often require extensive datasets, significant training time, and substantial computational resources.
- Prior analytical methods frequently rely on specialized or expensive hardware like depth cameras or are limited to simplified gesture sets.

## Method

- Developed **an analytical pipeline** that uses the ==MediaPipe== framework for real-time extraction of 3D human pose and hand landmarks from monocular RGB input.
- Computed human joint angles (e.g., wrist flexion, forearm rotation, elbow bend, shoulder pitch, shoulder roll) using ==derived geometric formulas== based on the extracted landmarks.
- Mapped the reconstructed angles linearly to the NICO robot's specific kinematic structure and motor ranges, executing these commands in real-time.

## Results

- The system achieved real-time processing performance, averaging **590 FPS** and a maximum of **108 FPS**.
- Demonstrated moderate reconstruction accuracy for shoulder pitch (approximately **10°** MAE), elbow bend (approximately **20°** MAE), and wrist bend (approximately **23°** MAE).
- The largest reconstruction error was observed for forearm rotation, with an MAE of **67.1°** using pose landmarks alone, which improved to **50.1°** with the addition of hand landmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Off-the-shelf RGB pose estimation and analytical geometry offer an efficient, interpretable method for real-time robotic gesture imitation without requiring extensive training or specialized depth sensors.
> - The integration of MediaPipe hand landmarks substantially reduces forearm rotation reconstruction error by approximately 25%, demonstrating the benefit of refined landmark inputs for specific joints.
> - The accuracy of joint angle estimation is most significantly influenced by the specific human pose, with complex forearm rotations and palm orientations posing the greatest challenges due to self-occlusion and ambiguities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI): enabling robots to imitate human gestures. Robotic imitation is a capability that allows robots to acquire skills through observation rather than through explicit programming, contributing to more natural and intuitive HRI.

Historically, robotic imitation has evolved through several paradigms. Early methods involved direct trajectory teaching, where a human manually guided a robot to record joint configurations. This was succeeded by inverse kinematics (IK) approaches, which computationally determined joint parameters to reproduce desired motions. More recently, the field has seen the emergence of learning-based methods, which frame imitation as a data-driven problem, often utilizing neural networks, self-supervised learning, and pose similarity metric learning. While these learning-based approaches demonstrate strong generalization capabilities, they typically necessitate large datasets, extensive training procedures, and significant computational resources, potentially limiting their real-time applicability in certain contexts.

In contrast, analytical and geometry-based approaches offer a computationally efficient and interpretable alternative. The paper positions itself within this analytical framework. The increasing success of RGB-based pose estimation frameworks, such as MediaPipe and OpenPose, has made vision-based human motion imitation more feasible by allowing robots to infer human body configuration directly from standard RGB images, thus reducing reliance on specialized sensing hardware like motion capture systems or wearable sensors.

Previous analytical geometry-based studies have explored similar concepts. For instance, some work used MediaPipe to extract hand landmarks for robotic hand control, computing joint angles via direct geometric relations. Other relevant research has utilized depth-camera input to derive joint angles for full arm motion imitation on robots like NAO. These prior studies demonstrated the effectiveness of analytical methods but often focused on simplified gesture sets or relied on more expensive, specialized hardware (e.g., depth sensors).

This present work aims to extend analytical geometry-based methods by applying them to a broader range of arm gestures, critically using only monocular RGB input, and adapting the formulations to the specific kinematics of the NICO semi-humanoid robot. It seeks to provide a computationally efficient and transparent imitation system that does not require a training phase, differentiating it from learning-based counterparts, and operates without specialized depth-sensing hardware, distinguishing it from some analytical approaches. The authors explicitly state that their approach does not require a training stage, uses only monocular RGB input, supports full arm and hand reconstruction, and is tailored to the kinematics of the NICO robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enhance seamless human-robot interaction (HRI) by enabling a semi-humanoid robot to imitate human arm gestures accurately and efficiently from visual input. The motivation for pursuing this capability stems from several factors:

1. **Enabling Natural HRI:** Robotic imitation is identified as a fundamental ability for natural HRI, allowing robots to acquire new skills through observation rather than explicit programming. This makes interaction more intuitive and accessible for human users.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Methods:**
 - **Learning-based approaches:** While powerful for generalization, these methods typically require extensive datasets, significant training time, and substantial computational resources, which can be prohibitive for real-time applications or systems with limited processing power.
 - **Analytical methods with specialized hardware:** Some existing analytical methods achieve high accuracy but rely on expensive and complex sensing modalities like depth cameras or motion capture systems, increasing system cost and complexity.
 - **Simplified gesture sets:** Many existing analytical approaches are limited to simplified gestures, suggesting a need for methods that can handle a broader range of arm movements.
3. **Leveraging Ubiquitous Technology:** The emergence of RGB-based pose estimation frameworks, specifically MediaPipe, provides a real-time, low-cost solution for extracting 2D and pseudo-3D human body and hand landmarks from monocular images. This technology offers a practical foundation for developing accessible and efficient imitation systems.
4. **Computational Efficiency and Interpretability:** The authors are motivated by the benefits of analytical and geometry-based approaches, which inherently offer computational efficiency and interpretability compared to opaque, data-intensive learning models. This makes the system's operation easier to understand, debug, and adapt.
5. **Tailoring to Specific Robotic Platforms:** The need to adapt imitation systems to the unique kinematic structures of different robots is a recurring challenge. This work specifically aims to tailor an imitation system to the NICO robot, which has a distinct joint configuration compared to other humanoid platforms like NAO, necessitating custom geometric formulations and angle mappings.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the research are:

- **Develop a robust system:** To create a system for imitating human arm gestures on the semi-humanoid robot NICO.
- **Utilize monocular RGB input:** To achieve this imitation using only a single RGB camera, avoiding the need for depth sensors or other specialized hardware.
- **Employ analytical geometry:** To reconstruct human joint angles from visual landmarks using derived geometric relations.
- **Integrate MediaPipe:** To leverage the MediaPipe pose-estimation framework for real-time extraction of 3D human body and hand landmarks.
- **Map to NICO kinematics:** To properly map the reconstructed human joint angles to NICO’s specific motor configuration and execute these commands.
- **Evaluate performance:** To conduct preliminary experiments to assess the method's ability to produce meaningful imitative motions across various arm gestures, participants, and viewpoints, while also identifying its limitations, particularly in complex poses and movements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents an analytical pipeline designed to convert monocular RGB input into robot motion, specifically focusing on the imitation of human arm gestures by the NICO semi-humanoid robot.

**Robot Platform:**
The target platform is NICO (Neuro-Inspired COmpanion), a child-sized semi-humanoid robot. NICO is equipped with six actuated degrees of freedom (DoF) in each arm, allowing for a range of upper-body motions. Its joints are driven by Dynamixel servo actuators, which offer programmable position control and feedback capabilities.

**Processing Pipeline:**
The proposed framework consists of several consecutive stages:
1. **3D Coordinates Extraction:** Human pose and hand landmarks are estimated from an RGB input frame using the MediaPipe framework.
2. **Joint Angle Reconstruction:** The extracted 3D landmark coordinates are processed by analytical geometric formulas to calculate the corresponding human joint angles.
3. **Angle Mapping to NICO:** The reconstructed joint angles are transformed into valid motor commands tailored to the NICO robot's kinematic structure and motor ranges.
4. **Motor Command Execution:** The mapped commands are sent to the NICO robot for execution.

**3D Coordinates Extraction:**
- **MediaPipe Usage:** The MediaPipe framework is employed for real-time extraction of 3D landmarks from RGB images. Specifically, the `pose_landmarker_heavy.task` model is used for full-body pose estimation (33 landmarks), chosen for its higher accuracy compared to lighter variants. The MediaPipe hand landmark model provides 3D coordinates for 21 hand keypoints.
- **Coordinate System:** The x and y coordinates are normalized to image dimensions, while the z-coordinate for pose landmarks is relative to the hips, and for hand landmarks, it is relative to the wrist (landmark 0 of the hand model).
- **Landmark Substitution:** When hand landmarks are available, the corresponding wrist and palm points from the hand detector are prioritized over the general pose landmarks (P15, P17, P19 for left hand; P16, P18, P20 for right hand). This substitution is applied to improve estimation of palm orientation, wrist flexion, and forearm rotation. The local hand landmark set is translated so its origin aligns with the corresponding wrist landmark from the pose model. No additional scaling or rotational alignment is required as both MediaPipe models operate within the same camera coordinate frame.
- **Scope:** The current implementation focuses on landmarks 11–24, which are relevant for arm motion.
- **Acknowledged Limitations:** The paper references prior work that highlights MediaPipe's accuracy dependency on viewing angle and increased error under occlusions, observations consistent with this study.

**Joint Angle Reconstruction:**
Calculations are detailed for the left arm, with the right arm processed similarly, accounting for vector product orientations.
- **Torso Alignment:** To compensate for the human subject's torso not being perfectly parallel to the camera plane, landmark coordinates are rotated around the vertical (yaw) axis based on the orientation of the shoulder line (derived from left and right shoulder landmarks). Compensation for torso pitch and roll is omitted, assuming an upright standing posture.
- **Wrist Flexion (NICO DoF 1):** Computed using the unsigned angle between the left palm plane normal vector (`V_LPN`, derived from landmarks P15, P17, P19) and the negative forearm vector (`-V_LF`, pointing from elbow P13 to wrist P15). The formula is `θ_wrist-bend = π/2 + angle(V_LPN, -V_LF)`.
- **Forearm Rotation (NICO DoF 2):**
 - **Reference Direction:** Defined as the component of the upper arm vector (`V_LA`, from shoulder P11 to elbow P13) orthogonal to `V_LF`. A fallback method using the cross product of `V_LA` and the shoulder line vector (`V_LRS`) is used if the arm is straight. Another fallback vector `(0, 1, 0)⊤` is used if `V_LA` and `V_LRS` are parallel.
 - **Palm Orientation:** The projection of the palm normal (`V_LPN`) onto the plane perpendicular to `V_LF`. A fallback vector is constructed from the wrist point and the midpoint of landmarks P17 and P19 if the palm is perpendicular to the forearm.
 - **Angle Calculation:** The forearm rotation angle is the signed angle between the palm orientation vector and the rotation base vector, measured around the forearm axis.
- **Elbow Bend (NICO DoF 3):** Calculated as the unsigned angle between the forearm vector (`V_LF`) and the upper arm vector (`V_LA`) using the dot product formula: `θ_elbow-bend = arccos((V_LF · V_LA) / (||V_LF|| · ||V_LA||))`.
- **Shoulder Pitch (NICO DoF 5):** The angle is derived from the components of `V_LA` using `atan2((v_x^2 + v_z^2)^1/2, v_y)`. This provides an angle in the range [0, π], distinguishing arm positions relative to the vertical axis.
- **Shoulder Roll (NICO DoF 4):** The angle is derived from the components of `V_LA` using `atan2(v_x, (v_z^2 + v_y^2)^1/2) + π/2`. This value is mirrored (subtracted from 2π) if `v_z > 0` (arm behind the body) to distinguish front and back arm configurations. The resulting angle is in the range [0, 2π).
- **Shoulder Yaw (NICO DoF 6):** This joint is fixed in a neutral position in the current implementation.

**Angle Mapping to NICO:**
Reconstructed analytical joint angles are linearly mapped to NICO’s specific motor ranges. For each joint, experimentally determined admissible analytical intervals `[θ_min, θ_max]` and corresponding motor positions `m(θ_min)` and `m(θ_max)` are used. The reconstructed angle `θ` is first clamped to its analytical interval and then linearly interpolated to the motor range. The final motor position is rounded to the nearest integer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted using a dedicated dataset involving six volunteers of varying heights (158–188 cm) who performed 11 predefined symmetric upper-limb poses. Each pose was recorded from three viewpoints (0°, 30°, and 45° relative to the camera) to assess the influence of perspective distortion and self-occlusion. A Google Pixel 9a front-facing RGB camera was used in a controlled environment. Reconstruction accuracy was quantified using the mean absolute error (MAE) between the reconstructed and predefined reference joint angles, with circular angular difference used for cyclic rotations.

**Reconstruction Accuracy for Different Joints:**
- **Moderate Accuracy:** The method achieved moderate reconstruction accuracy for several key joints:
 - Shoulder Pitch: Approximately 10° MAE.
 - Elbow Bend: Approximately 20° MAE.
 - Wrist Bend: Approximately 23° MAE. The accuracy of wrist bend was observed to decrease for more extreme flexion and extension, performing better when the wrist remained near its neutral configuration.
- **Lower Accuracy:**
 - Shoulder Roll: Reconstructed with lower accuracy, exhibiting a 34° MAE. This indicates sensitivity to body orientation and potential areas for improvement in geometric formulation or additional constraints.
 - Forearm Rotation: This joint presented the principal limitation, showing the largest reconstruction errors. Using only pose landmarks resulted in a 67.1° MAE. The incorporation of MediaPipe hand landmarks significantly improved this, reducing the error by approximately 25% to 50.1° MAE. Despite this improvement, the remaining error for forearm rotation was substantially higher than for other joints, suggesting limitations with reliable estimation from the available landmark configuration.

**Influence of Experimental Variables:**
- **Participant Height:** No clear relationship between participant body height (ranging from 158 cm to 188 cm) and reconstruction accuracy was observed. The MAE varied only slightly across participants (e.g., 30.1°–32.1° and 20.8°–23.7° for overall accuracy without forearm rotation and wrist flexion).
- **Viewpoint:** Within the evaluated range of viewing angles (0°–45°), the effect of viewpoint on accuracy was relatively small compared to the variability introduced by individual poses and reconstructed joints. The left arm generally exhibited a slightly higher MAE than the right arm, potentially due to reduced visibility from counter-clockwise turns.
- **Performed Pose:** The specific pose performed had the most substantial influence on estimation accuracy. Poses involving pronounced forearm rotation and palm orientation consistently yielded the largest errors, indicating that self-occlusion and ambiguities in hand orientation are dominant sources of reconstruction error.

**Computational Performance:**
The computational performance of the vision-based pipeline, which includes MediaPipe Pose and Hands estimation, joint angle reconstruction, and angle mapping, was evaluated.
- Average processing time per frame: 0.0017 seconds (approximately 590 frames per second).
- Maximum observed processing time per frame: 0.0093 seconds (approximately 108 frames per second).
This performance indicates that the proposed method is capable of real-time operation.

**Qualitative Observations:**
Qualitative examples of gesture imitation demonstrated the visual behavior of the system. While not quantitatively evaluated, these examples illustrated the robot's ability to reproduce various arm configurations based on human input.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper presents a significant contribution as a proof of concept for an analytical, monocular RGB-based gesture imitation pipeline for the semi-humanoid robot NICO. Its significance and potential impact can be understood in several dimensions:

**Contribution to Robotic Imitation and HRI:**
- **Accessible Imitation:** By demonstrating meaningful gesture imitation using only a standard RGB camera and pre-trained, readily available computer vision models (MediaPipe), the work lowers the barrier to entry for developing robotic imitation systems. This can make such capabilities more accessible for research and application in resource-constrained environments or for hobbyist robotics.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The analytical nature of the proposed method eliminates the need for extensive training data and computationally intensive learning phases. This inherent efficiency is critical for real-time human-robot interaction where low latency is essential. The reported processing times (average 590 FPS, worst-case 108 FPS) support its viability for real-time applications.
- **Interpretability:** Unlike black-box learning models, the geometry-based reconstruction provides an interpretable mapping from visual observations to robot motor commands. This transparency can facilitate debugging, understanding system behavior, and making targeted improvements.
- **NICO-Specific Adaptation:** The tailored angle-mapping procedure for NICO's kinematic structure provides a concrete example of how generic pose estimation outputs can be translated into specific robot commands, contributing to the growing body of work on robot-specific imitation strategies.

**Addressing Limitations of Current Approaches:**
- This research offers a viable alternative to learning-based imitation methods that often demand substantial computational resources and large, annotated datasets, thereby expanding the options for researchers and developers facing such constraints.
- It provides an RGB-only solution that contrasts with analytical approaches relying on specialized and often more expensive depth-sensing hardware, promoting a more economical approach to vision-based imitation.

**Identified Areas for Future Development:**
The findings highlight specific limitations, particularly in the accurate estimation of forearm rotation and shoulder roll, especially under self-occlusion or extreme wrist movements. These identified challenges offer clear directions for future research, including:
- Developing more robust geometric formulations or incorporating additional cues for challenging joint estimations.
- Integrating temporal smoothing to enhance motion fluidity and stability.
- Extending the system beyond static poses to dynamic gestures, which is crucial for more natural HRI.
- Validating the method on more diverse subjects and complex viewpoints to improve generalization.

**Potential Applications:**
The development of efficient and accessible gesture imitation systems has implications for various applications:
- **Social Robotics:** Enabling robots to mimic human gestures can significantly enhance their ability to engage in natural social interaction, contributing to their roles as companions, assistants, or educational tools.
- **Assistive Robotics:** Robots capable of imitating gestures could be used in assistive contexts, helping individuals with motor impairments by demonstrating tasks or learning movements from caregivers.
- **Teleoperation and Remote Control:** Facilitating intuitive control of robotic arms through natural human gestures, without the need for complex control interfaces.
- **Skill Transfer:** Providing a pathway for humans to teach robots new skills by demonstration, making robot programming more intuitive for non-expert users.

In conclusion, this work establishes a foundation for efficient and interpretable arm gesture imitation using widely available technology. While acknowledging current limitations, it opens avenues for further refinement towards more robust and universally applicable robotic imitation systems, thereby contributing to the advancement of seamless human-robot interaction.
