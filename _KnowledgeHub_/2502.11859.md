# Defining and Evaluating Visual Language Models' Basic Spatial Abilities: A Perspective from Psychometrics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University established a psychometric framework to define and evaluate Visual Language Models' fundamental spatial abilities, revealing that current VLMs achieve an average overall score of 24.95 compared to humans' 68.38 and suffer from constraints in dynamic simulation and precise metric encoding.

## Problem

- Existing research into AI spatial abilities is fragmented, often focusing on isolated tasks without a comprehensive theoretical foundation.
- Prior VLM evaluations lack clear benchmarks against human performance, making it difficult to systematically compare capabilities or align them with human cognitive frameworks.
- A systematic, psychometrically-grounded approach was needed to understand how VLMs' spatial intelligence is structured and where fundamental limitations lie.

## Method

- The study adopted a ==psychometric framework== from human spatial intelligence, defining ==five Basic Spatial Abilities (BSAs)== for VLMs: Spatial Perception, Spatial Relation, Spatial Orientation, Mental Rotation, and Spatial Visualization.
- **Nine** classic psychometric tests (e.g., Mental Rotation Test, Santa Barbara Solids Test) were selected and converted into image-based questions to evaluate these BSAs across **13** mainstream VLMs.
- Intervention strategies, including ==Chain-of-Thought prompting== and example-based ==few-shot learning==, were applied to assess their impact on VLM spatial reasoning.

## Results

- VLMs achieved an average overall spatial ability score of **24.95**, markedly lower than the human average of **68.38** across all psychometric tests.
- The Qwen series demonstrated superior performance (average **30.82**) compared to models from Google, OpenAI, and InternVL2, while smaller models often outperformed their larger counterparts within the same series.
- VLMs exhibited fundamental constraints including weak metric encoding, lack of dynamic simulation, poor cross-modal grounding, and over-reliance on static pre-training patterns, with limited improvements from Chain-of-Thought or few-shot prompting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Despite a significant performance gap, VLMs paradoxically mirror human performance hierarchies across spatial tasks, excelling in 2D orientation while struggling with 3D mental manipulation.
> - The weak correlation between different Basic Spatial Abilities in VLMs validates their independence, supporting the chosen psychometric decomposition.
> - Model size does not positively correlate with spatial ability, suggesting that architectural optimization and training methodologies are more critical than parameter count for spatial reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Artificial Intelligence has seen tremendous advancements, particularly with the emergence of large language models (LLMs) and, more recently, multimodal models like Visual Language Models (VLMs). These models have demonstrated impressive capabilities across a wide array of tasks, from natural language understanding and generation to image recognition and captioning. However, a significant frontier remains: endowing AI systems with human-like spatial intelligence, especially for embodied AI applications such as robotics, visual navigation, and virtual assistants that interact with the physical world.

Existing research into the spatial abilities of AI, particularly VLMs, has largely been fragmented. Many studies focus on isolated spatial tasks (e.g., identifying objects in 3D scenes, navigating simple environments) or evaluate text-based LLMs on abstract spatial relations derived from verbal descriptions. These approaches often lack a comprehensive theoretical foundation, making it difficult to systematically compare performance across different models or to align AI capabilities with human cognitive frameworks. Crucially, most prior work neglects to establish clear benchmarks against human performance, leaving the true gap in spatial intelligence largely unexplored.

This paper addresses these critical gaps by drawing upon the rich history of psychometric studies on human spatial intelligence. Human spatial intelligence, defined as the ability to mentally model and manipulate spatial environments, has been extensively studied within psychology and cognitive science. Spearheaded by theories like Gardner's Theory of Multiple Intelligences, it is understood as a hierarchical construct, comprising general intelligence, domain-specific spatial intelligence, and ultimately, several Basic Spatial Abilities (BSAs) such as Spatial Perception, Spatial Relation, Spatial Orientation, Mental Rotation, and Spatial Visualization. These BSAs are well-defined and measurable through standardized psychometric tests.

By adopting a psychometric framework and leveraging established human spatial tests, this research directly bridges the divide between cognitive science and artificial intelligence. It pioneers a systematic and theoretically grounded approach to evaluate VLMs' spatial reasoning, providing a much-needed comprehensive benchmark. This work is therefore positioned as a foundational step toward understanding not just *what* VLMs can do spatially, but *how* their spatial intelligence is structured, how it compares to humans, and where the fundamental limitations lie. It aims to inform the development of next-generation embodied AI that truly possesses human-like spatial cognition, moving beyond mere pattern recognition to genuine spatial understanding and manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations driving this research are rooted in the identified shortcomings of current VLM evaluation practices and the broader ambition to achieve human-level spatial intelligence in AI.

1. **Establish a Psychometric Framework for VLM Spatial Abilities:** The core objective is to pioneer a standardized, theoretically sound framework for defining and evaluating the Basic Spatial Abilities (BSAs) in VLMs. This framework is directly inspired by psychometric theories of human spatial intelligence, ensuring a robust and comparable basis for assessment.
2. **Define and Decompose Basic Spatial Abilities in VLMs:** The research aims to clearly define five specific BSAs—Spatial Perception, Spatial Relation, Spatial Orientation, Mental Rotation, and Spatial Visualization—as distinct yet interdependent facets of spatial intelligence relevant to VLMs. This decomposition provides a granular view of VLM capabilities.
3. **Systematically Benchmark Mainstream VLMs:** A key goal is to rigorously evaluate the defined BSAs across a wide array of mainstream VLMs (both open-source and commercial) using validated psychometric experiments. This benchmarking aims to quantify current VLM performance and identify specific strengths and weaknesses.
4. **Compare VLM Performance to Human Baselines:** A crucial motivation is to establish a direct comparison between VLM and human performance on these basic spatial tasks. This objective seeks to quantify the "gap" in spatial intelligence and understand if VLMs exhibit similar hierarchical performance patterns to humans.
5. **Investigate Factors Influencing VLM Spatial Abilities:** The study seeks to explore how architectural choices (e.g., model size, manufacturer), and intervention strategies (e.g., Chain-of-Thought prompting, few-shot learning) impact a VLM's spatial reasoning capabilities. This helps in understanding what contributes to or hinders performance.
6. **Identify Fundamental Constraints and Provide a Roadmap:** Ultimately, the motivation is to diagnose the underlying barriers preventing VLMs from achieving human-like spatial intelligence. By pinpointing these limitations, the research aims to provide a cognitive science-informed roadmap for future development in embodied AI, guiding architectural innovations and training methodologies.

In essence, the research is motivated by the critical need for a more scientific and comprehensive approach to assessing AI's spatial understanding, moving beyond task-specific evaluations to a foundational understanding of cognitive spatial abilities, much like how human intelligence is measured.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study is meticulously designed to provide a psychometrically grounded and comprehensive evaluation of Visual Language Models' (VLMs) basic spatial abilities.

#### 3.1. Definition and Composition of Basic Spatial Abilities (BSAs)

The study first establishes a theoretical foundation by adopting the hierarchical model of human spatial intelligence, focusing on five **Basic Spatial Abilities (BSAs)**:
- **Spatial Perception:** The ability to perceive horizontal and vertical orientations without interference.
- **Spatial Relation:** The ability to recognize relationships between parts of an entity.
- **Spatial Orientation:** The ability to navigate or enter a given spatial state.
- **Mental Rotation:** The ability to mentally rotate 3D objects.
- **Spatial Visualization:** The ability to mentally manipulate and transform 2D and 3D objects.

These definitions are based on established psychometric theories and existing VLM studies, ensuring both theoretical rigor and relevance to AI.

#### 3.2. Selection of Classic Psychometric Tests

To evaluate these BSAs, the researchers selected nine specific, classic psychometric tests. The criteria for selection included:
- Unambiguously demonstrating a certain BSA.
- Having complete questions, answers, and reproducible human experiment results.
- Being widely recognized and grounded in solid psychometric theoretical foundations.
- Utilizing as many classic tests as possible to authentically reflect BSAs, rather than arbitrarily deleting tests to balance weights.

The chosen tests include:
- **Spatial Perception:** MGMP Spatial Visualization Test (SVT).
- **Spatial Relation:** Net Cube Imagination Test (NCIT), Differential Aptitude Test: Space Relation (DAT:SR), R-Cube-Spatial Relation Test (R-Cube-SR).
- **Spatial Orientation:** Money Road-Map Test (MRMT).
- **Mental Rotation:** Mental Rotation Test (MRT), Purdue Spatial Visualization Tests: Visualization of Rotations (PSVT:R).
- **Spatial Visualization:** Santa Barbara Solids Test (SBST), R-Cube-Visualization Short Test (R-Cube-Vis).

These tests are primarily presented in multiple-choice or true/false formats, with questions provided as images. Human performance data from existing studies for these tests serve as benchmarks.

#### 3.3. Models and Experimental Settings

- **Models Tested:** The study evaluated 13 mainstream VLMs, comprising both open-source and commercial models.
 - **Commercial:** GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, GPT-4 Turbo (OpenAI); Gemini-1.5-pro, Gemini-1.5-flash, Gemini-1.5-flash-8b (Google).
 - **Open-source:** Qwen2-VL-72B, Qwen2-VL-7B (Qwen); InternVL2-Llama3, InternVL2-26B, InternVL2-8B (InternVL2); Llama-3.2-11B, Llama-3.2-90B (Llama).
- **API Usage:** To facilitate large-scale automatic testing, APIs from SiliconFlow, DeepInfra, OpenAI, and Google were utilized.
- **Data Processing:** A total of 312 questions were fed to each model. The distribution across BSAs was: Spatial Perception (10.26%), Spatial Relation (33.33%), Spatial Orientation (10.26%), Mental Rotation (17.31%), and Spatial Visualization (28.84%). For overall ability calculation, each BSA was weighted equally to avoid bias from question count.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Accuracy was the primary metric. Scores were calculated based on the number of correct options selected out of the total correct options for each item. The maximum score for each test and BSA was 100.
- **Prompting Strategy:** Models were given a zero-shot or few-shot prompt: "You are taking a spatial ability test. Please output the result in pure text format as: question number, answer." Each question (with options embedded in a single image) was presented separately. Original human experiment instructions were retained for comparability. Each test was repeated three times for every model to assess performance stability.

#### 3.4. Intervention Strategies

To further understand VLM limitations and potential improvements, two intervention strategies were tested on the SBST geometric cutting test using GPT-4o:
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:** A five-step reasoning protocol was designed (understanding 3D shape, analyzing plane, determining cross-section, matching options, giving answer).
- **Example-Based Training (Few-Shot):** The impact of providing 1, 3, 5, and 10 examples was evaluated, both with and without CoT.

This comprehensive methodological approach ensures that the evaluation is systematic, comparable to human performance, and designed to reveal fundamental insights into VLM spatial cognition.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, shedding light on the current state of Visual Language Models' (VLMs) basic spatial abilities compared to human performance and among different models.

#### 4.1. Significant Performance Gap Between VLMs and Humans

- **Overall Ability:** The average overall ability score for the 13 tested VLMs was a stark 24.95, significantly lower than the human average of 68.38. This indicates a substantial gap in basic spatial intelligence between current AI models and humans.
- **BSA-Specific Gaps:** Across all five BSAs, human performance consistently surpassed VLMs. The discrepancy was particularly pronounced in areas like Mental Rotation and Spatial Visualization.
- **Mirroring Human Hierarchies:** Despite the large performance gap, VLMs paradoxically mirrored the human performance hierarchy across the BSAs. Both humans and VLMs performed best in Spatial Orientation (2D tasks requiring simpler coordinate reasoning) and weakest in Mental Rotation (3D tasks demanding dynamic mental manipulation). This suggests a similar gradient of task complexity for both.

#### 4.2. Independence of Basic Spatial Abilities

- **Low Correlation:** A Pearson correlation analysis between the scores of different BSAs for the VLMs revealed that all correlation coefficients (r) were less than 0.4. This indicates very weak or no correlation between the abilities, implying that each identified BSA is sufficiently "basic" and independent, thus validating the chosen psychometric framework.

#### 4.3. VLM Performance Variations by Manufacturer and Size

- **Manufacturer Impact:** Models from different manufacturers exhibited noticeable performance differences. The **Qwen series** demonstrated clear superiority with an overall average score of 30.82, outperforming competitors. Mid-tier performers, including **Gemini, GPT, and Llama series**, clustered around an average of 25, while **InternVL2** significantly lagged at 19.6.
- **Divergent Strengths:** Performance in individual BSAs showed distinct patterns. For instance, Gemini-1.5-flash excelled in Spatial Relation (43.78) but was notably deficient in Mental Rotation (16.67) and completely failed in Spatial Orientation. Conversely, the Llama series showed strength in Spatial Orientation but underperformed in Spatial Visualization. These disparities are attributed to heterogeneous training data distributions and architectural biases.
- **Challenging Scaling Laws:** Contrary to the conventional expectation that larger models perform better, the study found no positive correlation between parameter count and BSA performance. Smaller models, such as **Qwen2-VL-7B** and **Gemini-1.5-flash-8B**, frequently outperformed their larger counterparts within the same series. This suggests that architectural optimization and training methodologies might be more crucial than sheer parameter size for spatial reasoning tasks.

#### 4.4. Limited Impact of Intervention Strategies (CoT and Example-Based Training)

- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** Implementing a five-step CoT protocol boosted baseline accuracy by 0.100, demonstrating its capacity to enhance visual-semantic alignment and multi-step reasoning.
- **Synergistic Effects:** When CoT was combined with a one-shot example, accuracy saw a further increase of 0.017.
- **Plateau in Few-Shot Learning:** Incremental example exposure improved accuracy initially (0.159 gain at 1-shot, 0.209 at 3-shots), but gains plateaued beyond 5 examples. This suggests that while examples help VLMs recognize recurring spatial patterns, they fail to address fundamental limitations in dynamic 3D mental simulation.

#### 4.5. Identified Constraints in VLMs’ Basic Spatial Abilities

The analysis pinpointed four fundamental constraints:
1. **Weak Metric Encoding:** VLMs struggle with subtle shape variations and misinterpret spatial relationships (e.g., interior/exterior boundaries), indicating a lack of precise geometric understanding in visual representations.
2. **Lack of Dynamic Simulation:** Even with CoT, VLMs exhibit shallow reasoning chains and cannot dynamically simulate 3D transformations like mental rotation trajectories, unlike human parietal lobe-driven mechanisms.
3. **Poor Cross-Modal Grounding:** Erratic behaviors, such as selecting multiple answers in single-choice tasks, reveal a critical gap between textual instruction parsing and visual feature extraction.
4. **Over-reliance on Static Pre-training Patterns:** VLMs demonstrate limited adaptability to novel spatial configurations, excessively relying on general patterns learned during pre-training.

These findings collectively highlight that current VLMs, despite their advancements, possess a rudimentary and often fragmented understanding of spatial concepts, far from human cognitive capabilities, and are constrained by inherent architectural limitations beyond mere data scarcity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant contribution to both the fields of Artificial Intelligence and cognitive science, with profound implications for the development of future AI systems, particularly in embodied AI.

#### 5.1. A Diagnostic Toolkit for Spatial Intelligence Evaluation

By establishing the first quantitative linkage between psychometric Basic Spatial Abilities (BSAs) and VLM capabilities, this study provides a much-needed diagnostic toolkit. Researchers can now systematically evaluate the specific spatial strengths and weaknesses of any VLM, moving beyond anecdotal observations or task-specific benchmarks. This standardized framework allows for direct comparisons between models and against human performance, offering a clearer picture of progress and remaining challenges.

#### 5.2. Methodological Foundations for Embodied AI Development

The paper lays critical methodological foundations for advancing embodied AI. For systems intended to navigate, interact with, and manipulate objects in 3D environments (e.g., robotics, virtual reality agents), a robust understanding of spatial relationships, orientation, and mental manipulation is paramount. By dissecting spatial intelligence into its fundamental components, the research provides a blueprint for designers of embodied AI to target specific abilities, track their improvement, and ensure a more holistic and human-like spatial cognition.

#### 5.3. Cognitive Science-Informed Roadmap for AI Advancement

The finding that VLMs, despite their limitations, mirror human performance hierarchies in spatial tasks (excelling in 2D orientation, struggling with 3D rotation) offers a crucial cognitive science-informed roadmap. This suggests a potential developmental pathway for AI, where strengthening basic 2D spatial reasoning might naturally scaffold more advanced 3D capabilities. This insight can guide the design of curriculum learning for AI, training models in a progression that mimics human cognitive development.

#### 5.4. Challenging Scaling Laws and Guiding Efficient Model Design

The unexpected finding that smaller models sometimes outperform larger counterparts in spatial reasoning tasks challenges the prevailing scaling laws in AI. This suggests that for spatial intelligence, architectural optimization and specialized training methodologies may be more impactful than simply increasing parameter count. This has significant implications for efficient model design, encouraging research into more compact, specialized architectures that offer better efficiency-performance tradeoffs, rather than solely pursuing ever-larger models.

#### 5.5. Identifying Fundamental Architectural Constraints

The study's identification of weak geometric priors, lack of dynamic simulation mechanisms, poor cross-modal grounding, and over-reliance on static patterns as fundamental constraints is invaluable. These are not merely data scarcity issues but point to inherent architectural deficiencies in current VLMs. This strongly advocates for a paradigm shift toward **hybrid architectures** that integrate neurosymbolic reasoning and biologically inspired spatial processing modules, akin to how humans use distinct neural pathways (dorsal and ventral streams) for spatial processing.

#### 5.6. Fostering Interdisciplinary Collaboration

By bringing psychometric theories into the realm of VLM evaluation, this work highlights the immense value of interdisciplinary collaboration between machine learning and cognitive science communities. Future progress in achieving human-like spatial intelligence in AI will depend on continued dialogue and joint efforts, leveraging insights from human cognition to inspire novel AI architectures and learning paradigms.

In summary, this research not only rigorously quantifies the current state of VLM spatial abilities but also provides a principled framework, critical insights, and a clear direction for the next generation of AI systems to genuinely understand and interact with the complex spatial world, moving closer to the goal of truly intelligent and embodied artificial agents.
