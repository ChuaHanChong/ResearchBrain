# Artificial Foveated Perception for Mitigating Shortcut Learning in Robotic Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Yale University and collaborators developed Artificial Foveated Perception (AFP) to address shortcut learning in robotic foundation models by predicting task-conditioned visual masks and using them to align policy attention during fine-tuning. This approach achieved substantially improved visual grounding, with Soft-IoU scores reaching up to 0.934, and enhanced out-of-distribution generalization and fine-tuning efficiency across various robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic foundation models (Vision-Language-Action, World Action Models) often exhibit slow adaptation and brittleness during fine-tuning, failing under minor environmental changes like distractors or new object placements.
- Policies suffer from shortcut learning, exploiting predictive but non-causal visual correlations in training data rather than focusing on true task-relevant visual evidence.
- This leads to poor generalization and robustness in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments, hindering reliable real-world robot deployment.

## Method

- An **Artificial Foveated Perception (AFP)** module was designed as a lightweight, policy-agnostic ==mask predictor== that generates continuous ==task-conditioned relevance masks== highlighting critical visual regions.
- During fine-tuning, these **AFP** masks provide an ==auxiliary grounding loss== to guide the policy's internal ==visual attention== towards task-relevant areas.
- A ==projected-gradient update== strategy is used for the auxiliary loss to prevent conflicts with the primary action learning, and the **AFP** module is entirely removed during inference to maintain computational efficiency.

## Results

- AFP significantly improved visual grounding, increasing Soft-IoU between policy attention and human-labeled masks from **0.170** to **0.934** (in-distribution) and reducing Earth Mover's Distance from **~4.0** to **~0.03** for π0.5
- Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization was substantially enhanced across all tested models; for example, π0.5 OOD success rates improved from **0.22** to **0.59** on a Kitchen task in simulation, and from **0.30** to **0.67** on real-world Drawer Assembly.
- Fine-tuning efficiency improved, with AFP-trained policies achieving **high success rates notably earlier** than directly fine-tuned policies, especially for complex or longer-horizon tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct fine-tuning of robotic foundation models often results in visually misgrounded policies that rely on spurious correlations, explaining their fragility in varied environments.
> - Explicitly guiding a policy's visual attention with task-conditioned relevance masks effectively mitigates shortcut learning, leading to better reliance on causal visual evidence.
> - An auxiliary grounding signal, applied through a projected-gradient approach, can enhance both out-of-distribution generalization and fine-tuning efficiency without modifying the core policy architecture or increasing inference-time overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robotic foundation models, including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs), have significantly expanded capabilities in multi-task execution, cross-embodiment transfer, and language-conditioned control. These models leverage representations from pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and World Models (WMs) to facilitate complex robotic behaviors. However, unlike Large Language Models (LLMs) that exhibit strong zero-shot inference, VLA and WAM models typically require task-specific fine-tuning for reliable deployment in new robotic environments. This fine-tuning process is often characterized by slow adaptation and brittleness, with policies degrading under minor environmental variations such as changes in object placement, lighting, or the presence of distractors.

This fragility is attributed, in part, to a phenomenon known as shortcut learning, where models exploit predictive but non-causal correlations present in the training distribution instead of relying on the true task-relevant visual evidence. Shortcut learning has been extensively documented in the broader computer vision and machine learning literature, with various mitigation strategies proposed, including worst-group loss minimization, invariant risk minimization, and techniques involving diffusion-based image editing or background masking. However, its specific manifestation and mitigation within the perception pipelines of robotic foundation models have remained comparatively underexplored.

Previous efforts to address limitations in robotic foundation models have focused on scaling training data through synthetic generation or augmentation, preserving pre-trained representations to prevent catastrophic forgetting, or employing structured representations and improved optimization procedures to accelerate training. This research introduces a complementary approach by directly targeting shortcut learning within the perceptual architecture. The work distinguishes itself by proposing a mechanism for explicitly aligning the policy's visual attention with task-relevant regions, thereby enhancing generalization and fine-tuning efficiency, a direct parallel to background masking strategies in general computer vision but applied specifically to the context of robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observation that despite substantial progress in robotic foundation models, their robust deployment in diverse real-world settings remains challenging. A core issue identified is the inability of fine-tuned policies to consistently differentiate between visual information that is causally relevant to a task and spurious scene-level correlations that merely happen to be predictive within the training data distribution.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

1. **Identify and Characterize Shortcut Learning in Robotic Foundation Models**: The authors posit that policies, during fine-tuning, often learn "shortcuts" by exploiting incidental scene context, background textures, lighting conditions, object co-occurrences, or camera-specific artifacts. These spurious correlations allow policies to achieve low training loss in-distribution but lead to failures when encountering out-of-distribution (OOD) perturbations. For instance, a policy trained to pick up a mug might inadvertently learn to rely on the surrounding tablecloth pattern rather than the mug itself, making it prone to failure if the tablecloth changes. The research aims to diagnose this visual misgrounding.

2. **Propose a Mechanism to Mitigate Shortcut Learning**: Given the identified failure mode, the central objective is to develop a lightweight and policy-agnostic module that can guide robotic foundation models to focus their perception on task-relevant visual evidence. This module should provide an explicit grounding signal to counteract the tendency of models to exploit non-causal features.

3. **Improve Generalization and Robustness to OOD Perturbations**: A key goal is to enhance the ability of fine-tuned robotic policies to perform reliably in environments that differ from their training settings. This involves improving robustness against variations in object placement, distractors, and other environmental changes that might trigger reliance on learned shortcuts.

4. **Enhance Fine-tuning Efficiency**: The auxiliary grounding signal is also expected to direct the policy's optimization toward causally relevant visual structures, potentially reducing the data and time required for effective fine-tuning, especially for tasks involving longer horizons or sparse relevant visual cues.

5. **Maintain Policy Architecture and Inference Efficiency**: The proposed solution, Artificial Foveated Perception (AFP), is designed to be integrated as an auxiliary training component, meaning it should not require modifications to the core policy architecture and should not be needed in the control loop during inference, thus preserving the operational efficiency of the deployed model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Artificial Foveated Perception (AFP), a lightweight and policy-agnostic module designed to improve the fine-tuning of robotic foundation models by mitigating shortcut learning. AFP operates by predicting task-conditioned masks that highlight relevant visual regions and then uses these masks as an auxiliary grounding signal during the fine-tuning process.

#### 3.1. Overview of Artificial Foveated Perception

AFP aims to dynamically filter visual input, emulating human foveated perception. Given an RGB observation image and a natural language task description, AFP predicts a continuous mask where pixel values range from 0 (non-salient) to 1 (task-relevant objects, robot end-effector, and action-critical regions). This mask is then used to supervise policy attention without modifying the core policy architecture or being present during inference.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture

AFP is implemented as a compact task-conditioned mask predictor, processing an observation sequence and a task description to output a sequence of dense relevance masks. Its architecture comprises two pathways:
- **Feature Path**: A MobileNetV3-Large backbone extracts a multi-scale feature pyramid from each RGB frame. These features are then projected to a shared hidden dimension, flattened into visual tokens, and processed by a deformable transformer encoder. The choice of a compact CNN aims to maintain AFP's lightweight nature.
- **Query Path**: This path utilizes a deformable DETR formulation. A set of learned region queries is decoded against the encoded visual memory using deformable cross-attention, yielding frame-wise decoder query states. These queries function as compact descriptors for task-relevant regions. Temporal consistency is enhanced through feature-level and query-level temporal modules adapted from VMFormer.
- **Language Conditioning and Mask Prediction**: A frozen CLIP text encoder extracts an ℓ2-normalized text embedding from the language task description. A lightweight FiLM adapter conditions the decoded queries by applying per-channel scale and shift parameters derived from the text embedding. Finally, a mask head fuses the encoded multi-scale features into a high-resolution feature map. This map is then matched with the language-conditioned queries to produce a score for each image location, which is then passed through a sigmoid function to generate the continuous task-relevance mask.

#### 3.3. Model Training

Training AFP requires a dataset of task-conditioned masks that represent graded visual relevance rather than discrete object segmentations. To address this, the authors developed a custom AFP Labeling Tool. This browser-based tool allows human annotators to provide positive and negative point prompts on keyframes within a robot trajectory. These prompts are used by SAM 2 to generate initial masks, which are then propagated through the entire video sequence using MatAnyone to produce continuous alpha masks. This process efficiently captures the robot, manipulated objects, uncertain boundaries, partial occlusions, and other action-critical regions over time. The dataset used for training AFP comprises 380,574 annotated images from various real-world and simulated manipulation datasets, including Open-X Embodiment, DROID, MimicGen, LIBERO, and custom scenes. AFP is trained as a supervised mask predictor on this dataset.

#### 3.4. Auxiliary Training Loss During Fine-tuning

The AFP masks are integrated into the fine-tuning process of robotic foundation models as an auxiliary grounding signal.
- **Attention Extraction**: During fine-tuning, the policy's internal visual attention over image tokens is extracted and averaged across trainable layers, heads, and query positions to yield a representative attention map.
- **Grounding Loss**: An L2 loss, denoted L_AFP, is calculated between the normalized policy attention distribution and the normalized AFP mask. This encourages the policy's visual attention to align with the task-relevant regions identified by AFP.
- **Total Optimization Objective**: The overall fine-tuning objective combines the standard action loss (L_act) with the AFP grounding loss: L = L_act + λL_AFP, where λ is a weighting hyperparameter.
- **Gradient Projection for Stability**: To prevent the auxiliary grounding objective from interfering with the primary action learning, especially in early training stages when policy attention might be misaligned, the authors employ a projected-gradient update strategy similar to PCGrad. If the action-loss gradient and AFP-loss gradient conflict (i.e., their dot product is negative), the AFP gradient is projected to remove its conflicting component with respect to the action gradient. This treats AFP as a directional constraint on gradients rather than an equally weighted task.
- **Inference-time Operation**: Crucially, AFP is only utilized during the fine-tuning phase. Once training is complete, the policy operates on the original observation stream without any input from or reliance on the AFP module, ensuring no additional computational overhead during deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Artificial Foveated Perception (AFP) involved empirical diagnosis of shortcut learning, extensive simulation experiments, and real-world robot deployments, covering a range of state-of-the-art robotic foundation models.

#### 4.1. Empirical Diagnosis of Shortcut Learning

- **Methodology**: Policy attention attribution was compared against human-labeled task-relevance masks using Soft-IoU (higher indicates better overlap) and Earth Mover's Distance (EMD, lower indicates better spatial alignment). This was performed on π$_{0.5}$ and later extended to SmolVLA, OpenVLA, and Motus.
- **Results (Direct Fine-tuning)**: Policies fine-tuned directly exhibited poor visual grounding. Qualitatively, attention maps often spread to background or distractor regions. Quantitatively, for π$_{0.5}$, direct fine-tuning yielded Soft-IoU scores of 0.170 (in-distribution, ID) and 0.159 (out-of-distribution, OOD), with high EMD values of 4.389 (ID) and 4.086 (OOD). Similar patterns of misgrounded attention were observed across other models (SmolVLA, OpenVLA, Motus).
- **Results (with AFP Auxiliary Loss)**: AFP significantly improved visual grounding. Soft-IoU scores for π$_{0.5}$ increased to 0.934 (ID) and 0.916 (OOD), while EMD values decreased to 0.035 (ID) and 0.038 (OOD). This improvement in grounding alignment was consistent across all tested foundation models.
- **Conclusion**: Direct fine-tuning often results in visually misgrounded policies that may rely on spurious correlations, and AFP successfully addresses this by explicitly aligning policy attention with task-relevant evidence.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

- **Methodology**: Eight MimicGen tasks were used, with 500 demonstration trajectories and 20,000 fine-tuning steps. OOD settings introduced random task-irrelevant mesh objects as distractors. Four robotic foundation models (SmolVLA, OpenVLA, π$_{0.5}$, Motus) were tested.
- **Results (OOD Generalization)**: AFP consistently improved OOD generalization across all models and tasks.
 - Direct fine-tuning often performed well in ID settings but experienced substantial performance degradation under OOD perturbations. For example, π$_{0.5}$ success rate on Kitchen D1 dropped from 0.82 (ID) to 0.22 (OOD) with direct fine-tuning.
 - AFP auxiliary loss mitigated this degradation significantly. For π$_{0.5}$, OOD success rates improved from 0.61 to 0.88 on Stack 3 D1, from 0.46 to 0.77 on Threading D1, and from 0.22 to 0.59 on Kitchen D1.
- **Results (ID Performance and Training Stability)**: AFP also improved ID performance in many tasks, indicating it functions beyond just OOD regularization. The projected-gradient update mechanism (PCGrad-style) was found to be critical for stable training, preventing the auxiliary grounding objective from conflicting with and destabilizing action learning.
- **Conclusion**: AFP auxiliary loss translates improved visual grounding into better policy performance, particularly under OOD conditions, by encouraging reliance on task-relevant visual evidence.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

- **Methodology**: Five real-world manipulation tasks were conducted using an i2RT YAM Arm. These included three FurnitureBench assembly tasks (Drawer, Lamp, Table Assembly), Cloth Folding, and Closet Storage. 300 demonstration episodes and 50,000 fine-tuning steps were used. OOD settings involved task-irrelevant distractors (unused furniture parts, unseen socks, opened cabinet door). π$_{0.5}$ was evaluated with 30 physical rollouts per setting.
- **Results (Qualitative Grounding)**: AFP-trained policies maintained task-relevant visual grounding in physical environments. Attention maps, though noisier due to real-world visual complexity, primarily focused on manipulated objects, robot-object interaction regions, and task-relevant workspace structures.
- **Results (Quantitative Success Rates)**: AFP auxiliary loss consistently improved success rates on every real-world task in both ID and OOD settings.
 - Notable gains under OOD perturbations included improvements from 0.30 to 0.67 on Drawer Assembly and from 0.20 to 0.53 on Lamp Assembly.
- **Conclusion**: The grounding benefits and improved robustness observed in simulation transferred effectively to physical robot control, supporting AFP as a practical mechanism for enhancing policy robustness in real-world scenarios.

#### 4.4. Training Efficiency

- **Methodology**: Comparison of ID success rate convergence curves for π$_{0.5}$ with and without AFP auxiliary loss across the eight MimicGen tasks. Task complexity was also correlated with average episode length.
- **Results**: AFP auxiliary loss improved fine-tuning efficiency, particularly for longer-horizon or visually complex tasks (e.g., Kitchen D1, Coffee Preparation D1, Three Piece Assembly D1). On these tasks, AFP-trained policies achieved high success rates substantially earlier than those fine-tuned directly. For simpler, shorter-horizon tasks, the convergence benefit was more modest.
- **Conclusion**: By directing optimization toward action-relevant visual evidence, AFP reduced the burden on the policy to discover relevant visual structures solely from action supervision, leading to improved sample efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research identifies shortcut learning as a significant visual grounding failure mode in robotic foundation models, which manifests as policy attention being misaligned with causally relevant visual evidence. This misalignment directly contributes to the brittleness and poor generalization observed in fine-tuned robotic policies when faced with environmental variations or out-of-distribution (OOD) distractors.

The introduction of Artificial Foveated Perception (AFP) offers a practical and effective solution to this identified problem. By training a lightweight, policy-agnostic module to predict task-conditioned relevance masks and utilizing these masks as an auxiliary attention supervision signal during fine-tuning, the research demonstrates several key benefits:
- **Improved Visual Grounding**: AFP explicitly guides the policy's visual attention towards task-relevant objects and interaction regions, moving away from reliance on spurious correlations. This is evidenced by significant increases in Soft-IoU and reductions in EMD between policy attention and human-labeled masks.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization**: Both simulation and real-world experiments show that AFP substantially improves policy success rates, particularly under OOD perturbations. This directly addresses the challenge of deploying robotic foundation models in diverse and unstructured environments.
- **Increased Fine-tuning Efficiency**: By providing a clearer signal for what visual information is relevant, AFP accelerates the learning process, allowing policies to achieve high success rates in fewer training steps, especially for more complex or longer-horizon tasks.
- **Non-Intrusive Integration**: A critical aspect of AFP's design is that it does not require modifications to the core policy architecture and is not needed during inference. This ensures that the benefits of improved grounding and generalization are gained without incurring additional computational overhead or complexity during robot operation.

The implications of this work are substantial for the field of robotic manipulation. By providing a practical mechanism to anchor robotic policies to causal visual evidence, AFP contributes to making robotic foundation models more robust, data-efficient, and scalable for real-world applications. This can accelerate the transition of advanced robotic capabilities from controlled laboratory settings to diverse industrial and service environments where adaptability is paramount.

**Limitations**:
A notable limitation of the current AFP approach is its reliance on human-labeled task-relevance annotations to train the AFP module. This annotation process incurs cost and introduces potential biases. Furthermore, AFP's performance may be constrained when task relevance is ambiguous, heavily occluded, or falls outside the distribution of the mask dataset.

**Potential Future Work**:
Future research directions can address these limitations by:
- **Scaling Annotation**: Expanding the size and diversity of the mask dataset through continued use of the provided annotation tool.
- **Self-Supervised Grounding**: Exploring methods to infer task relevance without explicit human annotation. This could involve leveraging interaction data, temporal consistency across trajectories, counterfactual scene changes, or policy failures to automatically identify and prioritize causal visual evidence.
