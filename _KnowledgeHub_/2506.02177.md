# Act Only When It Pays: Efficient Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning via Selective Rollouts

> [!summary] Summary
> GRESO efficiently trains large language models for reasoning by selectively filtering out uninformative prompts before costly rollouts, achieving comparable accuracy to state-of-the-art methods while reducing total training time by up to 2.0 times and rollouts by up to 3.35 times. This method addresses the major computational bottleneck in RL-based LLM fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLM reasoning relies on extensive and computationally expensive 'rollouts' to generate training data.
- A large proportion of prompts become 'uninformative' (zero-variance) during training, wasting significant computational resources during rollouts.
- Existing online data selection methods filter uninformative prompts only *after* costly rollouts, leading to substantial wasted computation and limiting efficient scaling.

## Method

- Introduces **GRESO** (GRPO with Efficient ==Selective Rollout==), an online, lightweight algorithm for pre-rollout filtering of uninformative prompts.
- Utilizes probabilistic pre-rollout filtering based on a prompt's historical reward dynamics and consecutive zero-variance occurrences.
- Employs a self-adjustable base exploration probability and an Adaptive Sampling Batch Size (ABS) to dynamically optimize sampling and maintain effective training batch ratios.

## Results

- Achieved accuracy comparable to state-of-the-art Dynamic Sampling across various models and math reasoning benchmarks.
- Reduced the total training time by up to **2.0** times and rollout generation time by up to **2.4** times.
- Required up to **3.35** times fewer rollouts compared to Dynamic Sampling, significantly improving computational efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Zero-variance prompts, which provide no learning signal, significantly increase in proportion (up to 80%) during later stages of RL training.
> - Prompt value exhibits strong temporal consistency; prompts uninformative in one epoch are highly likely (over 90%) to remain uninformative in subsequent epochs, enabling pre-rollout prediction.
> - Balancing exploitation (skipping uninformative prompts) with exploration (revisiting some previously uninformative prompts) is crucial for sustained performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly their application in complex reasoning tasks. Recent breakthroughs in LLM reasoning, often achieved through Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), rely heavily on generating extensive data during training—a process known as "rollout." Scaling these rollouts to sample more prompts has been shown to improve model performance by enabling the selection of higher-quality training data.

However, this performance gain comes at a substantial computational cost. The paper identifies rollout as a "major bottleneck" in RL training. Existing approaches, such as "Dynamic Sampling" (DS), attempt to improve training by oversampling and then filtering out "uninformative" or "zero-variance" prompts (prompts where all sampled responses yield identical rewards, thus providing no learning signal). While DS improves performance, it incurs significant overhead because the costly rollout generation is performed *before* filtering. This means a substantial portion of computational resources is wasted on generating responses for prompts that ultimately contribute nothing to the learning process.

The broader landscape of data selection for LLMs includes:
- **Static pruning methods:** These approaches attempt to select high-quality data *before* training. While they reduce dataset size, the paper notes a lack of conclusive evidence that they improve overall *RL training efficiency* end-to-end. Furthermore, they are static and do not adapt to the evolving value of data points during different training stages.
- **Online selection methods (post-rollout):** Exemplified by Dynamic Sampling, these methods perform selection during training but *after* the computationally expensive rollout phase, leading to the aforementioned wasted computation.

This paper positions itself by tackling the inefficiency of current online RL training methods for LLMs. It aims to bridge the gap by proposing a method that performs data quality estimation *before* rollout, significantly reducing the wasted computational effort. By making rollout scaling more efficient, the work contributes to making advanced LLM reasoning models more feasible to train and deploy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the observation that while scaling rollouts improves LLM reasoning performance, it introduces significant computational overhead due to a large proportion of "uninformative prompts." The authors highlight that in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), these are specifically "zero-variance prompts"—those where all generated responses yield the same reward, thus contributing no learning signal to the model. Existing methods, like Dynamic Sampling, address this by filtering out such prompts *after* the expensive rollout process, leading to substantial wasted computation.

The central research question driving this paper is: **"How can we perform more selective rollouts—focusing on sampling more valuable prompts—to make this rollout scaling more efficient?"**

To achieve this, the authors identify three key objectives for an ideal selective rollout algorithm:
1. **Online data selection:** The method should perform data selection during training, adapting to the current training state without requiring an auxiliary model trained offline.
2. **Model-based data value estimation:** The selection strategy must be dynamic, recognizing that the value of a data point changes throughout training and varies across different models.
3. **Low computational overhead:** The selection mechanism itself must be lightweight and introduce minimal additional cost to ensure overall efficiency gains.

Motivated by an empirical analysis revealing "strong temporal consistency" in prompt value (prompts uninformative in one epoch tend to remain uninformative in future epochs), the paper's overarching goal is to design an efficient online selective rollout strategy that predicts and skips these uninformative prompts *before* the rollout stage, thereby significantly reducing the computational burden of RL training for LLMs without compromising performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **GRESO (GRPO with Efficient Selective Rollout)**, an online, lightweight pre-rollout filtering algorithm designed to increase the efficiency of RL training for LLMs. GRESO is built upon key observations regarding the dynamics of uninformative prompts in GRPO training.

**Core Insights:**
1. **Impact of Zero-Variance Prompts:** In GRPO, prompts where all generated responses have identical rewards provide zero advantage and no learning signal. The ratio of these "zero-variance prompts" (or "uninformative prompts") in a batch significantly increases as training progresses, reaching up to 80% at later stages. This necessitates oversampling (as done in Dynamic Sampling) to maintain an effective training batch size, leading to high rollout costs.
2. **Temporal Consistency of Prompt Value:** Analysis reveals that prompts identified as zero-variance in one epoch are highly likely (over 90% probability) to remain zero-variance in subsequent epochs. Crucially, a smaller but significant portion (around 20%) can become effective again. This suggests that while aggressive pruning is possible, some degree of exploration is necessary to avoid discarding potentially valuable prompts permanently.

**GRESO's Components and Workflow:**

1. **Detection and Filtering with Reward Training Dynamics:**
 - Instead of filtering *after* rollout, GRESO aims to predict and filter zero-variance prompts *before* rollout.
 - Each prompt `x_i` is associated with a "training dynamics trace" `T_i`, which records the rewards obtained from its responses across previous epochs.
 - The goal is to predict if `x_i` will be a zero-variance prompt in the current epoch based on `T_i`.

2. **Probabilistic Pre-rollout Prompt Filtering:**
 - GRESO employs a probabilistic filtering strategy to balance exploitation (skipping uninformative prompts) and exploration (allowing some previously uninformative prompts to be re-sampled).
 - For each prompt `x_i`, a filtering probability `p_f(x_i)` is calculated: `p_f(x_i) = 1 - p_e * (I_i,j=1 for z_i consecutive rollouts)`.
 - `z_i` represents the number of *most recent consecutive* rollouts for `x_i` that resulted in zero-variance. A higher `z_i` (more consecutive uninformative instances) leads to a higher `p_f(x_i)`, making it more likely to be skipped.
 - `p_e` is the "base exploration probability," which controls the overall likelihood of a prompt being selected for rollout. This probabilistic nature ensures that even historically uninformative prompts have a chance to be revisited.

3. **Self-adjustable Base Exploration Probability:**
 - Recognizing that the optimal `p_e` can vary across models, datasets, and training stages, GRESO automates its adjustment.
 - It maintains two distinct base exploration probabilities: `p_e,easy` for "easy" zero-variance prompts and `p_e,hard` for "hard" ones. This allows for differential exploration, providing more chances for hard examples to become informative as the model improves.
 - At each training iteration, these probabilities are increased or decreased by a step size (Δp = 1%) based on whether the observed zero-variance percentage for easy and hard prompts is below or above a predefined target zero-variance percentage (e.g., 25% total, split 8.3% for easy and 16.7% for hard).

4. **Adaptive Sampling Batch Size (ABS):**
 - To further optimize rollout efficiency, GRESO dynamically adjusts the size of the rollout batch (`B_r`).
 - Instead of using a fixed large batch size (as in Dynamic Sampling), ABS calculates the minimum number of examples needed to fill the effective training batch, considering the current zero-variance ratio.
 - This prevents wasted computation when only a few more effective examples are required, allowing for smaller, more targeted rollouts.

**Overall Algorithm Flow (Simplified):**
In each training iteration, GRESO:
1. Samples prompts from the dataset.
2. Applies the probabilistic pre-rollout filter (based on historical dynamics and adaptive `p_e`) to these prompts, skipping those predicted to be uninformative.
3. Performs rollout generation only on the *selected* (potentially informative) prompts.
4. After rollout, it performs a final filtering to identify truly effective prompts (those with non-zero advantage) for the training batch.
5. Adjusts the next rollout batch size using ABS based on the number of effective prompts still needed.
6. Updates the `p_e,easy` and `p_e,hard` values based on the observed zero-variance ratios in the current iteration.
7. Finally, it performs the GRPO update on the collected effective training batch.

This comprehensive approach allows GRESO to significantly reduce the computational burden of rollouts by intelligently pre-filtering uninformative examples, while still ensuring sufficient exploration and adapting to training dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of GRESO was conducted on a broad range of math reasoning benchmarks and models, consistently demonstrating significant efficiency gains without compromising model performance.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Models Evaluated:** Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B.
- **Training Datasets:** DAPO+MATH (DM) and OPEN-R1 30k subset (OR1). DM combines integer solutions from DAPO with LaTeX-formatted solutions from MATH to improve generalization.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:** Six widely recognized and challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks: Math500, AIME24, AMC, Minerva Math, Gaokao, and Olympiad Bench.
- **Comparison Baseline:** Dynamic Sampling (DS), which represents a state-of-the-art method for utilizing effective data.
- **Hardware:** Experiments were run on 4xH100 GPUs (for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) and 8xH100 GPUs (for Qwen2.5-Math-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B).

**Key Quantitative Results:**
- **Comparable Accuracy with Significantly Fewer Rollouts:** Across all tested models and datasets, GRESO achieved accuracy levels comparable to Dynamic Sampling. For instance, Qwen2.5-Math-7B trained on the DM dataset achieved an average accuracy of 57.5% with GRESO, very close to DS's 57.8%. Crucially, GRESO accomplished this with a dramatic reduction in the number of required rollouts—up to **3.35 times fewer rollouts**. For Qwen2.5-Math-7B on DM, rollouts were reduced from 13.1 million (DS) to 6.3 million (GRESO). On OR1, it reduced from 11.4 million to 3.4 million.
- **Significant Wall-Clock Time Speedups:** The reduced rollouts directly translated into substantial savings in training time:
 - **Rollout Speedup:** GRESO achieved up to **2.4 times wall-clock time speedup in rollout generation**. For Qwen2.5-Math-7B on DM, rollout time decreased from 155.9 hours (DS) to 65.5 hours (GRESO).
 - **Total Training Time Speedup:** The overall training time was reduced by up to **2.0 times**. For Qwen2.5-Math-7B on DM, total training time dropped from 178.0 hours (DS) to 88.3 hours (GRESO). Similar improvements were observed for other models, with overall training speedups of 1.4x for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B.

**Analysis and Ablation Studies:**
- **Improved Effective Prompt Ratio:** GRESO consistently maintained a significantly higher effective prompt ratio (over 70%) during training compared to Dynamic Sampling (which dropped to around 20% in late stages). This directly explains the reduced rollout time per step.
- **More Effective Rollouts per Time Budget:** For the same total rollout time budget, GRESO generated considerably more effective rollouts than Dynamic Sampling, demonstrating its superior efficiency in utilizing computational resources.
- **Self-Adjusting Exploration:** The study of `p_e,hard` and `p_e,easy` dynamics showed that GRESO's self-adjustable mechanism effectively maintained the target zero-variance ratios for both easy and hard prompts. Notably, `p_e,hard` increased later in training, indicating GRESO's ability to adapt and explore harder examples more as the model's capabilities improved.
- **Selection Dynamics Case Study:** Qualitative analysis showed that very easy prompts were frequently skipped but occasionally sampled for minimal exploration. Moderately difficult prompts were increasingly skipped as the model improved. Some hard prompts became solvable over time, while others remained consistently unsolved.
- **Adaptive Batch Size (ABS) Contribution:** The ablation study confirmed that while ABS contributes to reducing the number of rollouts in earlier stages, GRESO's pre-rollout filtering is the primary driver of the consistent and significant reduction in samples per step throughout training.

In summary, GRESO demonstrably solves the problem of high rollout overhead in LLM RL training. It maintains high performance while delivering substantial computational savings, making the training process significantly more efficient and accessible.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of GRESO represents a significant advancement in the efficiency of Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models, particularly in reasoning tasks. By intelligently predicting and selectively skipping uninformative prompts *before* the computationally intensive rollout phase, GRESO directly addresses a major bottleneck in the LLM training pipeline.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to deliver substantial computational speedups (up to 2.4x in rollout and 2.0x in total training time) without any degradation in model accuracy. This is a crucial breakthrough, as previous methods often traded off between performance and efficiency. GRESO achieves the best of both worlds by optimizing the data selection process at its source.

The potential impacts of this research are multi-faceted:

- **Democratization of Advanced AI:** By significantly reducing the computational cost of RL-based fine-tuning, GRESO lowers the barrier to entry for training state-of-the-art LLM reasoning models. This makes advanced AI research and development more accessible to smaller research groups, academic institutions, and even individual researchers with limited computational budgets, fostering a more inclusive AI ecosystem.
- **Enhanced Sustainability:** The substantial reduction in compute cycles translates directly into lower energy consumption. This aligns with the growing demand for "greener AI" and promotes more environmentally responsible practices in the development of large-scale models.
- **Accelerated Research and Development:** With more efficient training processes, researchers can iterate faster, explore a wider range of hyperparameters, architectures, or training strategies, and work with larger datasets or more complex tasks within existing computational constraints. This can accelerate the pace of innovation in LLM research.
- **Improved Practical Deployability:** For industrial applications, the efficiency gains offered by GRESO make it more feasible to fine-tune and deploy LLMs for specific reasoning tasks, especially where rapid iteration and cost-effectiveness are critical.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The paper lays a solid foundation for future research in adaptive data selection for RL. The authors themselves acknowledge a limitation in that GRESO currently makes a binary decision (skip or not skip) and suggest extending it to a finer-grained scoring or ranking system for prompts based on their estimated training utility. Further exploration of optimal allocation schemes for easy/hard zero-variance prompts is also a promising avenue.

In conclusion, GRESO provides a highly practical and empirically validated solution to a significant efficiency challenge in LLM training. Its impact is poised to be felt across the academic, industrial, and societal spheres by making powerful LLM reasoning models more accessible, sustainable, and conducive to rapid innovation.
