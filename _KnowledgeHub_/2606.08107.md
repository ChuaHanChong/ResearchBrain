# Ego-Pi: VLA Fine-Tuning for Ego-Centric Human and Robot Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Ego-Pi fine-tunes π0.5 on interleaved human and humanoid dexterous-hand data, using action-token interleaving to fit bimanual hand actions into the pretrained action head, robot-centric human-to-robot joint mapping, and subtask-prediction auxiliary loss, achieving 90%+ success on tasks requiring semantics absent from robot data alone.

## Problem

- Existing VLAs target gripper-based, low-dimensional action spaces and cannot represent high-degree-of-freedom dexterous hand actions without altering pretrained action-head parameters.
- Inverse-kinematics retargeting from human hand poses to robot joints is unreliable for high-DOF hands, often producing self-colliding or unnatural robot hand poses.
- Prior human-robot co-training work targets in-distribution performance or scene generalization on the same tasks, not transfer of novel task semantics like sorting logic or skill composition.

## Method

- **Interleaved action tokens** split left- and right-hand 29-dim actions across two 32-dim action tokens per timestep, preserving π0.5's pretrained action projection layer.
- **Robot-centric alignment** converts ==MANO== human hand keypoints into per-link joint angles then maps them directly into the robot's native joint space, bypassing unreliable ==IK retargeting==.
- **Subtask-prediction auxiliary loss** trains the VLM to output an intermediate subtask string before the ==flow-matching== head predicts actions, plus occlusion-aware color-coded skeleton overlays for visual alignment.

## Results

- Co-training with human data raised tomato-sorting success from **40%** (robot-only) to **92%**, and packaging success from **10%** to **90%**.
- Boxing task needed subtask prediction to succeed: simple co-training reached only **27%**, while adding subtask prediction achieved **93%** with Inspire hands.
- Inspire hands outperformed Tesollo hands on boxing (**93%** vs **67%** with subtask prediction) and dropping wrist cameras at test time noticeably degraded grasp stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human ego-centric demonstrations can teach robots high-level task semantics (sorting rules, skill sequencing, ordering) without any robot demonstrations of the target task.
> - Complex, multi-step or bimanual tasks with prerequisite conditions benefit disproportionately from explicit subtask prediction over simple co-training alone.
> - Robot hand morphology closer to human proportions transfers cross-embodiment semantics more reliably than larger, dissimilar hand designs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot learning faces a significant challenge in data acquisition, contrasting with the abundance of internet-scale datasets available in fields like natural language processing and computer vision. Traditional robot interaction data collection is resource-intensive, requiring specialized physical hardware. To address this, a growing area of research explores leveraging human demonstration data, which is more readily scalable and broadly available, particularly with the proliferation of anthropomorphic robots and consumer-grade ego-centric data capture devices (e.g., Apple Vision Pro, Meta Ray-Ban glasses).

Prior work in this domain has investigated the co-training of human and robot data to improve policy performance and generalization. For instance, EgoMimic demonstrated that co-training with human demonstrations could enhance performance, though their focus was on gripper-based robots. PH2D reported similar benefits for robots equipped with fingers, but their models were trained from scratch, precluding the generalization advantages of foundation models. EgoVLA addressed the use of foundation models by fine-tuning on ego-centric human data but did not include real-world evaluations. Other approaches, such as Masquerade and Mirage, involve converting human demonstrations into robot demonstrations through computationally intensive rendering pipelines, primarily showing improved policy robustness in cluttered scenes rather than complex task-semantic transfer.

More recent concurrent research, like that from Physical Intelligence, has explored advanced generalization, such as sorting eggs by color based on human-specified rules, but utilized gripper-based robots with comparatively lower task performance. EgoScale investigated teaching novel tasks using human data combined with a single robot demonstration.

This work distinguishes itself by focusing on the transfer of high-level task semantics—including sorting logic, skill composition, and rule-based ordering—from ego-centric human data to dexterous humanoid robots. Unlike prior studies that predominantly aimed to improve in-distribution performance or generalize to novel scenes for *existing* tasks, or relied on simpler gripper mechanisms, this research specifically targets the ability of dexterous multi-fingered robots to infer and execute conceptually novel behaviors absent from their own training data, assuming no robot demonstrations for the target novel task itself. It also develops a method to adapt pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, originally designed for lower-dimensional gripper actions, for high-dimensional dexterous hand control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the data scarcity bottleneck in robot learning by investigating how to effectively leverage ego-centric human data for controlling dexterous humanoid robots. The core motivation stems from the observation that while robot hardware is becoming increasingly anthropomorphic and ego-centric data collection devices are becoming widespread, the methods for seamlessly transferring complex human skills and knowledge to robots, especially at a high semantic level, remain underdeveloped.

The specific objectives are:

1. **Investigate effective co-training strategies:** The authors aim to study optimal design choices for co-training a pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, specifically π0.5, using both human and robot data. This involves identifying how to integrate diverse data sources efficiently.
2. **Enable cross-embodiment task-semantic transfer:** A central goal is to determine if humanoid robots can acquire and execute novel task semantics and skills that are exclusively present in human demonstration data. This includes:
 - Learning abstract concepts like sorting logic (e.g., sorting objects by color).
 - Achieving skill composition, where a robot combines previously learned, isolated skills into a new, multi-step behavior.
 - Understanding and applying rule-based ordering in manipulation tasks (e.g., placing heavier items first).
3. **Adapt existing VLAs for dexterous bimanual control:** Current VLA models are predominantly designed for gripper-based robots with low-dimensional action spaces. The authors are motivated to overcome this limitation by developing mechanisms to adapt these models for high-dimensional, dexterous, five-finger bimanual robot hands without disrupting the pre-trained weights.
4. **Address challenges in human-robot action alignment:** Traditional inverse kinematics or optimization-based methods for mapping human hand poses to high-degree-of-freedom robot hands often yield unnatural or self-colliding configurations. The research aims to develop a robust action alignment method that directly maps human hand poses into the robot's joint space.
5. **Explore auxiliary learning mechanisms for enhanced transfer:** The researchers hypothesize that additional learning components, such as auxiliary losses for subtask generation, could further facilitate stronger task-semantic transfer between human and robot embodiments.

These objectives collectively aim to unlock a scalable paradigm for robot learning, moving beyond low-level motion imitation to the transfer of higher-level cognitive understanding and complex sequential reasoning, thereby making robot learning more accessible and versatile.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Ego-Pi, a framework designed to adapt pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for high-dimensional, ego-centric human and humanoid robot data, facilitating cross-embodiment task-semantic transfer.

#### 3.1. Foundation Model

The Ego-Pi framework is built upon the π0.5 model, a flow-matching VLA architecture. This model extends pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for robot control. It takes multi-modal inputs at time *t*, including a language instruction (ℓt), images from an ego-centric camera (I_ego_t), left and right wrist cameras (I_L_t, I_R_t), and the proprioceptive state (s_t). The model outputs an optional subtask language (ℓ̂t) and a short-horizon continuous action sequence (a_t:t+H). Training is performed through imitation learning, minimizing a flow-matching objective to match expert actions.

#### 3.2. Action Alignment between Human and Robot Hands

To enable effective behavior transfer, the research addresses the challenge of aligning human and robot action representations for dexterous hands. Instead of relying on inverse kinematics, which can produce problematic poses for high-dimensional robot hands (e.g., Tesollo with 20 active joints), a "robot-centric action representation" is adopted. Human hand keypoints (20 following MANO convention) are first converted into per-link joint angles, which are then mapped directly to the robot's joint space. This mapping accounts for kinematic and proportional differences between human and robot hands through per-joint additive offsets (δ_i) and multiplicative scaling factors (f_i). The robot hand's native joint angles are then used as the action representation. The overall policy action is a 29-dimensional vector comprising wrist position (p ∈ R^3), wrist orientation (r ∈ R^6), and robot hand joint angles (q ∈ R^20 for Tesollo, q ∈ R^6 for Inspire).

#### 3.3. Adapting Gripper-Based VLAs for Hand Control

The π0.5 model has a maximum action dimension of 32, which is insufficient for bimanual dexterous control (58 dimensions for two 29-dimensional hands). Instead of increasing the output dimension of the VLA's final projection layer, which led to higher training losses in experiments, Ego-Pi employs an "interleaved action sequence formulation." Left and right hand actions are emitted as separate tokens within the VLA's action sequence. This distributes the 58 action dimensions across two 32-dimensional action tokens, preserving the pre-trained architecture and weights. While this reduces the effective action horizon from H to H/2, it allows the model to converge effectively.

#### 3.4. Visual Alignment

To mitigate visual discrepancies between human and robot hands, guiding skeleton lines are overlaid onto input images (Figure 3). These lines are color-coded uniquely for each finger and rendered in an occlusion-aware manner, allowing the model to explicitly understand the correspondence and structure of fingers across embodiments.

#### 3.5. Auxiliary Losses: Subtask Generation

To enhance task-semantic transfer, an auxiliary loss for subtask generation is incorporated. The dataset is labeled with subtasks, and the VLM component of the VLA is trained to output a subtask string. This intermediate prediction is hypothesized to provide a cognitive "thinking" step before the action head predicts continuous actions, aiding in complex sequential tasks.

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

- **Robot Hardware:** Galaxea R1 Pro with a ZED mini head camera. End-effectors are Tesollo (20-joint) or Inspire (6-joint) hands, each with an Ardurocam wrist camera.
- **Data Collection:** Human and robot demonstrations were collected using Quest controllers mounted on Manus gloves. These provided 6D wrist poses and finger joint angles, which were mapped to the robot. A ZED mini camera was used for human data collection.
- **Training Ratio:** Co-training utilized a 50% human to 50% robot data ratio per batch, which was found not to be highly sensitive to policy performance.
- **Wrist Camera Handling:** As human data lacked wrist camera observations, robot wrist images were randomly dropped out 40% of the time during training to ensure consistent image input across embodiments.

#### 3.7. Experimental Tasks

Three benchmark tasks were designed to evaluate high-level task-semantic transfer:
1. **Tomato Sorting by Color:** Robot data teaches basic placement into one bowl; human data introduces sorting by color into two bowls.
2. **Boxing:** Robot data teaches isolated skills (open box, pick/place block); human data demonstrates sequential composition (open box, then place block inside).
3. **Packaging:** Robot data covers generic item placement; human data teaches a rule-based ordering (place heavier box first, then lighter doll).

These tasks allowed the systematic evaluation of simple co-training efficacy, the impact of design choices (subtask generation, visual augmentation), the role of robot hand morphology, and the criticality of wrist cameras.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate Ego-Pi's ability to transfer high-level task semantics from human data to humanoid robots across three distinct tasks.

#### 4.1. Efficacy of Simple Co-training with Human Data for High-Level Semantics

- **Tomato Sorting (Sorting Rule):** Simple co-training with human data achieved a 92% success rate, significantly outperforming the robot-only policy which managed 40% (random placement).
- **Packaging (Rule-Based Ordering):** Similarly, simple co-training yielded a 90% success rate, whereas the robot-only policy achieved only 10% (often picking the wrong item or attempting awkward placements).
- **Boxing (Sequential Skill Chaining):** In contrast, simple co-training alone was less effective for the boxing task, resulting in a 27% success rate. The policy frequently attempted to pick the block before opening the box or tried to perform both actions simultaneously, leading to collisions. The robot-only policy performed similarly poorly at 20%.
- **Conclusion:** Simple co-training proves highly effective for transferring certain high-level task semantics like sorting rules and rule-based ordering. However, for tasks requiring precise sequential skill composition, it was insufficient, suggesting a need for additional mechanisms.

#### 4.2. Impact of Additional Design Choices (Subtask Generation and Visual Augmentation)

- **Subtask Prediction:** This was identified as a critical component, especially for the boxing task. Incorporating subtask prediction increased the boxing task's success rate from 27% (simple co-training) to 93%. The policy exhibited improved sequencing, consistently attempting to open the box before placing the block. The authors suggest this enables the policy to "think before acting," which is crucial for tasks demanding cognitive understanding and prerequisite conditions.
- **Skeleton Overlays:** The visual alignment provided by skeleton overlays had a negligible impact on policy performance. No quantitative or qualitative improvements were observed when applied to the tomato sorting task.
- **Explanation:** The boxing task, being the only bimanual task and requiring a specific prerequisite (box open), likely benefited more from the structured reasoning provided by subtask prediction.

#### 4.3. Impact of Robot Hand Choice

- **Packaging Task:** Both Tesollo and Inspire hands showed similar performance qualitatively.
- **Boxing Task:** The Tesollo hand demonstrated lower performance compared to the Inspire hand. Even with subtask prediction, the Tesollo hand achieved only a 67% success rate (compared to Inspire's 93%). While the Tesollo hand correctly attempted to open the box first, it struggled with smooth transitions to the next subtask, exhibiting sensitivity to state input.
- **Hypothesis:** The Inspire hand, being closer in size and form factor to human hands, may facilitate better visual and kinematic transfer from human demonstrations.

#### 4.4. Criticality of Wrist Cameras

- Despite human data lacking wrist camera observations and the use of a 40% dropout rate for robot wrist images during training, the robot policy demonstrated a noticeable degradation in performance when wrist cameras were omitted at test time.
- For example, during tomato sorting, the robot struggled to achieve a stable grasp without wrist camera input.
- **Conclusion:** The robot policy relies on its wrist cameras for robust performance, indicating that these egocentric views provide important information for precise manipulation, even when human demonstrations do not include them.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards addressing the data scarcity problem in robot learning by demonstrating the feasibility of teaching high-level task semantics to dexterous humanoid robots directly from ego-centric human data. The Ego-Pi framework offers a practical recipe for achieving this, comprising several key technical contributions:

- **Robust Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The work demonstrates that dexterous humanoid robots can acquire novel sorting logic, sequential skill composition, and rule-based ordering concepts that are only present in human demonstrations, achieving high success rates (90% or higher for successful cases). This capability extends robot learning beyond simple imitation to the transfer of abstract task understanding.
- **Technical Innovations for Dexterous Control:** By introducing an interleaved action sequence formulation, the framework effectively adapts existing gripper-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for high-dimensional, bimanual dexterous hand control without costly architectural modifications or retraining of foundational layers. The robot-centric action alignment method further enables a more robust mapping of human hand poses to complex robot kinematics.
- **Enhanced Cognitive Abilities through Subtask Prediction:** The incorporation of subtask generation as an auxiliary loss significantly improves performance in complex, multi-step tasks requiring sequential reasoning and prerequisite fulfillment, such as the boxing task. This highlights the potential of structured cognitive guidance in robot learning.
- **Scalability for Robot Learning:** Leveraging readily available ego-centric human data, especially with the increasing adoption of anthropomorphic robots and wearable devices, offers a promising path to scale robot learning datasets, potentially accelerating the development of more versatile and capable robots.

**Potential Impact:**
The findings have the potential to broaden the applicability of robot learning by enabling robots to learn more complex, abstract tasks with less direct robot-specific training data. This could facilitate faster deployment of robots in diverse environments and for varied tasks, particularly in human-centric settings like homes or service industries where demonstrations from human operators are readily available. The proposed methods contribute to bridging the gap between human intuition and robot execution, moving closer to robots that can understand and perform tasks based on general instructions and observations of human behavior.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
The research acknowledges several limitations. The experiments focused on relatively short-horizon, simple pick-and-place-oriented tasks within a fixed-camera setup. Future work could extend these ideas to more challenging scenarios involving long-horizon, highly dexterous manipulation and mobile platforms. Additionally, while the work demonstrated the transfer of high-level task semantics, it assumed the robot already possessed the necessary low-level skills, using human data primarily to compose these existing behaviors. A key open challenge remains the direct transfer of low-level manipulation skills from human data to robots.
