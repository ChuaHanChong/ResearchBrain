# Dual Goal Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from the University of California, Berkeley introduces "dual goal representations" for Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL), defining goals by their optimal temporal distances from all other states. This representation, which theoretically offers sufficiency and noise invariance, consistently achieved the highest average performance across 13 state-based and 5 out of 7 pixel-based OGBench tasks, improving over baselines by up to 3x in some scenarios and exhibiting greater robustness to noisy goals.

## Problem

- Traditional GCRL methods often use raw state observations as goals, which can contain irrelevant or noisy information.
- Extraneous information in goal representations hinders policy learning efficiency, degrades performance, and limits generalization.
- Existing approaches lack robust mechanisms to effectively filter out irrelevant details from goal representations while retaining critical task-relevant information.

## Method

- A "dual goal representation" is proposed, characterizing a goal by the set of optimal ==temporal distances== from all other states, approximated by an inner product of state and goal embeddings (ψ(s)⊤φ(g)).
- Optimal temporal distances are learned using an offline goal-conditioned value learning algorithm, specifically Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), over a reward function that encourages reaching the goal.
- The learned dual goal representation (φ(g)) is then used as input to train various downstream offline GCRL policies (e.g., GCIVL, CRL, GCFBC) in a modular fashion.

## Results

- On 13 state-based OGBench environments, dual goal representations consistently achieved the highest average performance when combined with three distinct downstream GCRL algorithms, often significantly outperforming existing representation learning methods (e.g., **3x** improvement on `cube-double-play-v0` with GCIVL).
- For pixel-based tasks, the method demonstrated superior performance on 5 out of 7 tasks and showed greater robustness to exogenous Gaussian noise in evaluation goals compared to baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the inner product parameterization for approximating temporal distances generally outperformed a symmetric norm, and training a separate downstream policy achieved better results than attempting to directly extract a policy from the learned distance function.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dual goal representations are theoretically sufficient, retaining all necessary information to recover an optimal goal-conditioned policy.
> - The representations are robust to exogenous noise in observations, exhibiting invariance for goals corresponding to the same underlying latent state.
> - Defining goals through their temporal relationships to all other states intrinsically captures the dynamics relevant to navigation and control, providing a more structured and robust input for GCRL.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of reinforcement learning (RL), specifically focusing on Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL). GCRL aims to train a single multi-task policy capable of navigating an agent from any starting state to any target state within an environment, typically with the objective of minimal time. This framework is applicable to various real-world scenarios such as robotic navigation, object manipulation, and games.

A central challenge in GCRL is how to effectively represent goals. Traditional approaches often utilize the raw state observations from the environment directly as goal representations. However, these raw observations may contain irrelevant or noisy information that does not directly pertain to the underlying control problem. This extraneous information can hinder the learning process, degrade policy performance, and limit generalization capabilities, particularly to novel or out-of-distribution goals.

The paper contributes to the sub-field of representation learning for GCRL. Prior work in this area has explored various techniques, including metric learning approaches that define goals based on learned distances between states (e.g., Time-Contrastive Networks, VIP, METRA), contrastive learning methods (e.g., TRA), and self-predictive representations (e.g., BYOL-γ). This paper introduces a novel theoretical framework for goal representation, distinct from these prior methods, by defining goals through their temporal relationships to all other states. While related to general representation learning in RL, especially concepts like successor representations, this work focuses on learning representations of *optimal* temporal distances, which are independent of any specific policy. The proposed method is designed to be integrated orthogonally with existing offline GCRL algorithms, aiming to enhance their performance and generalizability by providing a more structured and robust goal input.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective goal representation for Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) that can improve policy learning efficiency, enhance goal-reaching performance, and enable robust generalization.

The motivation for this objective stems from limitations observed in current GCRL methodologies. Many existing approaches directly use the original state observations provided by the environment to represent goal states. While straightforward, these observations are not necessarily optimally structured for describing tasks or goals. They frequently contain extraneous information, such as uncontrollable background noise or irrelevant visual details, which can complicate the learning process for the policy network. Such superfluous information can act as a distractor, making it harder for the agent to identify and focus on the controllable aspects of the environment relevant to goal achievement.

To address these issues, the authors propose two desiderata for an ideal goal representation:
1. **Sufficiency:** The representation must contain adequate information to define and recover an optimal goal-conditioned policy. This ensures that no critical information for optimal goal-reaching is lost in the representation process.
2. **Noise Invariance:** The representation should be capable of disregarding information that is irrelevant to the goal-reaching task, such as exogenous noise or environmental features that do not affect the agent's ability to reach a target. This property aims to make the representation robust and focused on intrinsic environmental dynamics.

By satisfying these desiderata, the authors hypothesize that a well-designed goal representation can offer several benefits:
- **Accelerated Policy Learning:** Providing structured and relevant goal information can simplify the learning task for the policy network, leading to faster convergence during training.
- **Improved Goal-Reaching Performance:** A more informative and robust representation can translate to higher success rates and more efficient goal-reaching at test time.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** By filtering out irrelevant details and capturing the intrinsic dynamics, the representation could enable the agent to generalize more effectively to previously unseen goals or environments with variations in irrelevant aspects.

The core idea, inspired by mathematical concepts that characterize an object by its relations to other objects, is to represent a goal not by its inherent features, but by its temporal distances from all other possible states in the environment. This "relative point of view" is posited to intrinsically capture the dynamics relevant to navigation and control, thereby fulfilling the identified desiderata.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces "dual goal representations" and outlines a practical learning recipe for their implementation in offline GCRL.

**Core Concept: Dual Goal Representation (φ∨(g))**
A dual goal representation characterizes a state *g* by "the set of temporal distances from all other states." Formally, for a goal *g*, its dual representation φ∨(g) is defined as a functional (a function from the state space S to R) where φ∨(g)(s) = d∗(s, g), with d∗(s, g) being the optimal temporal distance (e.g., shortest path length in deterministic environments, or a transformed optimal value function) from state *s* to goal *g*. In a discrete state space, this corresponds to a vector of temporal distances from all possible states to the given goal.

**Theoretical Properties:** The authors present two theorems supporting the properties of dual goal representations:
1. **Sufficiency (Theorem 3.1):** It is theoretically demonstrated that the dual goal representation function φ∨ retains sufficient information to recover an optimal goal-conditioned policy. This implies that a policy can be constructed that takes a dual goal representation as input and achieves optimal goal-reaching performance.
2. **Noise Invariance (Theorem 3.2):** Under the Block Controlled Markov Process (BCMP) framework—which models environments with latent states and noisy observations—dual goal representations are shown to be invariant to exogenous noise in the observation space. Specifically, if two goal observations correspond to the same underlying latent state, their dual goal representations will be identical. This property suggests robustness to irrelevant perceptual variations.

**Practical Instantiation:** Implementing dual goal representations in practice involves two main challenges: approximating the functional form and learning the optimal temporal distance d∗.

1. **Approximating Functionals:**
 - The functional form φ∨(g) is approximated using a parameterized approach. The temporal distance d∗(s, g) is modeled as f(ψ(s), φ(g)), where ψ(s) is a state embedding, φ(g) is a goal embedding, and f is an aggregation function.
 - The output of the goal embedding network, φ(g), is then treated as the practical, finite-dimensional representation of the dual goal.
 - The aggregation function f is parameterized as an inner product: f(ψ(s), φ(g)) = ψ(s)⊤φ(g). This inner product form is noted for its universality in approximating two-variable functions.

2. **Approximating Temporal Distance (d∗):**
 - An existing offline goal-conditioned value learning algorithm is used to approximate d∗. Specifically, goal-conditioned Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) is employed.
 - IQL trains the parameterized value function V(s, g) = f(ψ(s), φ(g)) and a Q-function Q(s, a, g) using expectile losses.
 - The reward function used is r(s, g) = I(s = g) − 1 (where I is the indicator function), which results in f(ψ(s), φ(g)) approximating a transformed version of the optimal temporal distance function.

**Downstream Offline Goal-Conditioned RL (Algorithm 1):**
After the dual goal representation φ is learned, it is integrated into a downstream offline GCRL pipeline. A goal-conditioned policy π(a | s, φ(g)) is trained using this learned representation. The authors emphasize that this representation learning step is orthogonal to the choice of the downstream GCRL algorithm. For evaluation, dual goal representations are combined with three distinct offline GCRL algorithms: goal-conditioned IVL (GCIVL), Contrastive RL (CRL), and goal-conditioned Flow Behavioral Cloning (GCFBC).

**Experimental Setup:**
- **"Ideal" Dual Representations:** A proof-of-concept experiment on a discrete "Lights Out" puzzle uses analytically pre-computed optimal temporal distances to form dual representations, comparing them against original state representations with goal-conditioned DQN.
- **Practical Dual Goal Representations:** Evaluated on the OGBench task suite, comprising 13 state-based and 7 pixel-based robotic navigation and manipulation tasks.
 - **Baselines:** Original (raw state observations), VIB, VIP, TRA, and BYOL-γ.
 - **Evaluation Metrics:** Average success rates over multiple random seeds and evaluation epochs.
- **Ablation Studies:** Investigate robustness to noisy goals, compare the inner product aggregation function against a symmetric norm (metric) parameterization, and examine the efficacy of directly extracting a policy from the learned temporal distance function versus training a separate downstream policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of dual goal representations covered both theoretical proof-of-concept scenarios and practical application on a comprehensive benchmark.

**Results with "Ideal" Dual Representations:**
On discrete "Lights Out" puzzle tasks (4x5 and 4x6), the use of analytically derived dual representations, even when approximated by sampling 64 states, demonstrated improved training speed and goal-reaching performance compared to using original state representations with a goal-conditioned DQN. This initial experiment supports the underlying concept that structured temporal distance information can facilitate policy learning.

**Results on OGBench (Practical Instantiation):**

- **State-Based Tasks:**
 - Dual goal representations consistently achieved the highest average performance across the 13 state-based OGBench environments when combined with all three downstream GCRL algorithms (GCIVL, CRL, and GCFBC).
 - The method generally outperformed existing goal representation learning approaches (VIB, VIP, TRA, BYOL-γ) and the "Original" baseline, often by a notable margin. For instance, on `cube-double-play-v0` with GCIVL, dual representations improved performance by 3x compared to the original baseline (from 35% to 60%).
 - Dual goal representations exhibited less sensitivity to the choice of the downstream offline GCRL algorithm compared to alternative representation learning methods.

- **Pixel-Based Tasks:**
 - On the 7 pixel-based tasks, dual goal representations demonstrated superior performance on five tasks compared to other representation learning methods (or performed equally well), using GCIVL as the base algorithm.
 - **Negative Results on Visual Puzzle Tasks:** All representation learning methods, including dual goal representations, failed to achieve non-zero performance on `visual-puzzle-3x3-play-v0` and `visual-puzzle-4x4-play-v0`.
 - **Hypothesized Cause:** This failure was attributed to the "late fusion" approach typically used with representation learning methods for visual inputs (processing state and goal images separately before combining their representations), in contrast to "early fusion" (concatenating raw state and goal images and processing them together). An ablation study confirmed that vanilla GCIVL, when switched from early to late fusion, also experienced a significant performance drop on these visual puzzle tasks, supporting the hypothesis that the fusion strategy, rather than the representation itself, was the limiting factor in these specific pixel-based environments.

**Q&As / Ablation Studies:**

- **Robustness to Noise:** Experiments demonstrated that dual goal representations exhibited greater robustness to exogenous Gaussian noise added to evaluation goals across `antmaze-large`, `cube-double`, and `scene-play` environments, compared to using original representations. This empirical finding aligns with the theoretical property of noise invariance (Theorem 3.2).
- **Aggregation Function:** A comparison between the inner product parameterization (ψ(s)⊤φ(g)) and a symmetric norm (metric) parameterization (||φ(s) - φ(g)||²) for approximating the functional showed that the inner product generally resulted in better performance. This outcome is suggested to be due to the universality and higher expressiveness of the inner product function for representing goals.
- **Direct Policy Extraction:** Attempting to directly extract a goal-conditioned policy from the learned temporal distance function f(ψ(s), φ(g)) generally led to worse performance than training a separate, unconstrained downstream GCRL policy network (V_GCRL(s, φ(g))). This indicates that while the inner product parameterization is effective for learning an efficient goal *representation*, it might impose structural constraints that limit its expressiveness for directly determining optimal control actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of dual goal representations presents a novel theoretical framework for understanding and representing goals in reinforcement learning. Its significance lies in grounding goal representation in the intrinsic temporal dynamics of the environment, moving beyond direct observation-based representations.

The theoretical properties of sufficiency and noise invariance are notable contributions. Sufficiency ensures that the representation holds all necessary information for optimal policy recovery, while noise invariance provides a mechanism for robustness against irrelevant environmental variations. These properties are fundamental for developing more reliable and efficient GCRL systems.

Empirically, the practical instantiation of dual goal representations has demonstrated consistent performance improvements across a diverse and challenging benchmark (OGBench). The observed gains in goal-reaching success rates on a variety of state-based and pixel-based tasks, coupled with improved robustness to noisy goals, highlight the practical utility of the approach. The method's compatibility with multiple existing offline GCRL algorithms suggests its broad applicability and potential to serve as a general enhancement for GCRL frameworks.

The findings related to the aggregation function comparison also have implications for representation learning in RL, indicating that the choice of parameterization for relationships between states and goals can significantly affect performance. The observation that direct policy extraction from the temporal distance function is often suboptimal suggests a nuanced role for learned representations: they may be highly effective as inputs to more expressive downstream policy networks, rather than serving as direct controllers themselves.

Despite the identified challenge with "late fusion" in specific pixel-based environments, the overall results indicate a step forward in designing goal representations that are both theoretically sound and empirically effective. This work is likely to stimulate further research into state and goal representation learning in sequential decision-making, potentially leading to the development of GCRL agents with improved learning efficiency, generalization capabilities, and robustness in complex, high-dimensional environments.
