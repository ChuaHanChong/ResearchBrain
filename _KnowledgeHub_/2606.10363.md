# HiMem-WAM: Hierarchical Memory-Gated World Action Models for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> HiMem-WAM introduces a World Action Model for robotic manipulation that integrates hierarchical latent action learning with a memory-gated module. The framework achieves 97.7% success on LIBERO, 76.0% on LIBERO-PLUS (zero-shot), and significantly improves performance on memory-dependent tasks like RMBench, demonstrating enhanced robustness and generalization in real-world settings.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often exhibit limited robustness to deployment shifts and struggle to maintain relevant task history over long durations.
- Current World Action Models (WAMs) lack hierarchical abstraction for low-level motions and efficient mechanisms for retaining task-relevant memory across skill boundaries in long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- Memory-enabled policies frequently rely on dense history aggregation or fixed-length observation histories, which can be inefficient and insufficient for selectively storing critical task states.

## Method

- **HiMem-WAM** proposes a World Action Model that integrates ==hierarchical latent action== learning and a memory-gated module into a unified architecture.
- A three-stage training pipeline is employed: learning a latent action tokenizer, pretraining hierarchical latent actions (skills), and fine-tuning with the memory-gated module.
- A boundary-aware memory-gated module selectively writes compact task states at predicted skill transitions, enabling efficient, event-driven memory management without requiring test-time video generation or optical flow.

## Results

- HiMem-WAM achieved an average success rate of **97.7%** on the standard LIBERO benchmark and **76.0%** on the zero-shot LIBERO-PLUS benchmark, outperforming existing VLA and WAM baselines in robustness.
- The model demonstrated a total average success rate of **26.3%** on the RMBench memory-dependent tasks, a substantial improvement over baselines like ACT (**10.8%**), with **31.6%** on single-memory element tasks.
- Real-world robotic deployment showed improved generalization, with HiMem-WAM achieving gains of +**12.5%** on Medium and +**25.0%** on Hard tasks in standard settings, attributed to robust motion priors and effective memory management.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hierarchical latent actions effectively capture generalized task completion motions, providing robust motion priors that enhance performance under diverse deployment perturbations.
> - A memory-gated module, which selectively stores and retrieves task-relevant states at skill boundaries, improves the model's ability to track task history and adapt in memory-dependent manipulation scenarios.
> - The architecture's design ensures a causal inference pipeline at test time by utilizing future visual dynamics solely for training supervision, avoiding computational bottlenecks during deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied intelligence has seen advancements through Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which facilitate language-conditioned robotic manipulation by transferring semantic priors from large-scale vision-language pre-training. However, these VLA models often rely on direct end-to-end action prediction, which can exhibit limitations in robustness to deployment shifts (e.g., changes in lighting or camera view) and in maintaining relevant task history over extended durations.

World Action Models (WAMs) represent a complementary paradigm. They aim to learn action-relevant visual dynamics through methods such as future prediction, video generation, or latent dynamics modeling. WAMs have demonstrated capabilities in providing dynamics priors and robustness. Despite these strengths, existing WAM architectures have encountered challenges in efficient operation for long-horizon manipulation tasks.

Recent efforts to enhance long-horizon manipulation in WAMs include unified video-action architectures and verification-based adaptive execution. For instance, some models learn video generation and action prediction jointly or use causal sequences with KV-cache for persistent memory. Other approaches involve verifying imagined futures against real observations to adjust execution dynamically, aiming to reduce inference costs and improve success rates.

However, the authors identify two specific areas within existing WAMs that remain underexplored for long-horizon manipulation:
1. **Hierarchical abstraction of low-level motions into reusable skills:** This involves structuring tasks into more abstract, high-level components.
2. **Explicit retention of task-relevant memory across skill boundaries:** This addresses the need for robots to recall information that may no longer be visible (e.g., occluded object states, completed subtasks) or to adapt after partial task completion.

Current memory-enabled manipulation policies often employ dense history aggregation or fixed-length observation histories, which can be inefficient or insufficient for retaining task-relevant information over long horizons, particularly in memory-dependent scenarios like those highlighted by benchmarks such as RMBench.

HiMem-WAM contributes to this landscape by integrating hierarchical abstraction and boundary-aware memory into the WAM framework. It seeks to provide a structured approach to temporal abstraction and an event-driven mechanism for memory management, distinguishing it from approaches that rely on dense or fixed-window memory aggregation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance of World Action Models (WAMs) in long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks, specifically by addressing their limitations in task-relevant memory and structured temporal abstraction.

The motivation for HiMem-WAM stems from several observed challenges in current robotic manipulation systems:

1. **Struggle with Task-Relevant Memory in Long-Horizon Tasks:** Existing WAMs, despite their capabilities in learning visual dynamics, frequently encounter difficulties in retaining and utilizing task-relevant information over extended operational periods. This limitation hinders their ability to perform multi-step tasks where context from earlier stages is crucial for subsequent actions, especially when visual information about past states becomes occluded or unavailable.
2. **Lack of Hierarchical Abstraction:** Many existing robotic policies, including WAMs, often operate at a single level of temporal granularity, directly mapping observations to low-level actions. This flat representation can be inefficient for long-horizon tasks, which naturally decompose into a sequence of high-level skills, each comprising multiple low-level motions. A lack of such hierarchical abstraction can make policies less robust, difficult to interpret, and inefficient for learning and generalization.
3. **Inefficient Memory Management:** While some methods introduce memory components for long-horizon tasks, they often rely on dense history aggregation or fixed memory windows. This can lead to an accumulation of irrelevant information, increased computational overhead, and a failure to selectively store critical task states, particularly those associated with significant transitions or subtask completions. The ability to causally infer actions without computationally expensive processes like test-time video generation or optical flow estimation is also a motivating factor for efficient memory design.
4. **Robustness to Deployment Perturbations:** Direct end-to-end action prediction policies often show reduced robustness when deployed in environments with variations in lighting, camera views, or initial states that differ from training conditions. Learning robust motion priors that capture generalizable task completion patterns, rather than overfitting to specific visual trajectories, is essential for real-world applicability.

To address these motivations, HiMem-WAM establishes specific objectives:
- To develop a **hierarchical latent action framework** that learns both low-level motion-centric primitives and high-level skill latents. This aims to provide structured temporal abstraction, enabling reasoning at multiple levels and improving robustness under deployment perturbations by capturing transferable motion patterns.
- To design a **memory-gated module** that writes compact task states selectively at predicted skill transitions. This module is intended to facilitate the retention of occluded subtask states and enable adaptation after partial task completion, without requiring dense history aggregation or costly future prediction during inference.
- To integrate these components into a unified WAM architecture that allows for **causal inference** during test time, avoiding the need for future video generation or optical flow estimation, thereby maintaining efficiency.
- To validate the proposed architecture on established benchmarks and real-world tasks, demonstrating improvements in success rate, generalization, and memory-dependent manipulation compared to existing VLA and WAM baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HiMem-WAM is structured around a three-stage training pipeline designed to integrate hierarchical latent actions and a memory-gated module into a World Action Model framework. The policy at timestep *t* receives RGB observations, proprioception, a task instruction, and an external memory state to predict an action chunk. This policy is factored through a high-level skill latent and a low-level latent-action chunk. Critically, future visual dynamics are used solely as training supervision, ensuring that inference remains causal without requiring video generation or optical-flow estimation.

**Overview of the Three-Stage Training Pipeline:**

1. **Stage I: Latent Action Tokenizer Learning:**
 - **Objective:** To learn a compact, low-level motion space represented by latent actions ($z_l$) that can be derived from both action-labeled robot trajectories and action-free videos.
 - **Process:** For each transition, multi-view optical flow ($\Phi_t$) is computed using DPFlow. A variational tokenizer encodes a short-horizon context (current/next observations, proprioception, instruction, and optical flow) into a latent action $z_l$.
 - **Loss Function:** The tokenizer is trained to reconstruct visual motion (L1 loss on optical flow) and, when action labels are available, weakly align the latent space with real controls (MSE loss on auxiliary action prediction). A Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence term regularizes the latent space towards a standard normal distribution.
 - **Output:** After training, the tokenizer is frozen and applied offline to all trajectories and videos to generate a sequence of low-level latent actions ($Z_l$), which serve as pseudo-labels for subsequent stages.

2. **Stage II: Hierarchical Latent Action Pretraining:**
 - **Objective:** To discover high-level skill latents ($z_h$) by dynamically chunking the pre-computed low-level latent action sequences and to pretrain the planner and executor without external memory.
 - **Process:** The low-level latent actions ($Z_l$) are considered as the initial (stage 0) tokens. At each hierarchy stage, an encoder maps tokens to a feature, a boundary predictor identifies the start of new segments, and a segment pooling module summarizes variable-length segments into next-stage tokens. This process is repeated for a specified number of hierarchy stages (H) to yield the final high-level skill sequence ($Z_h$). The learned boundaries define skill transitions. These skill sequences are then unfolded back to the original control timeline to provide per-timestep skill targets ($\bar{z}_h$) and boundary labels ($\bar{b}_t$).
 - **Pretraining:** The planner and executor are pretrained in this stage. The planner predicts the current skill and a boundary score, while the executor predicts the low-level latent-action chunk. This stage explicitly does not use external memory, simplifying the initial learning of hierarchical structures.
 - **Loss Function:** The objective combines Mean-Squared Error (MSE) losses for predicting the high-level skill and the low-level latent action chunk, with a Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss for boundary prediction. Additional skill-discovery losses ensure next-latent prediction, motion preservation, and prevent degenerate boundary patterns.

3. **Stage III: Finetuning with Memory-Gated Module:**
 - **Objective:** To activate and fine-tune the full policy, including the memory-gated module, on action-labeled demonstrations.
 - **Memory-Gated Module:**
 - **State Encoding and Retrieval:** The current state is encoded. An attention mechanism retrieves a memory context ($c_m_t$) from an external memory bank ($M_t$), which stores compact skill-level tokens. A **read gate** ($\alpha_r_t$), controlled by a sigmoid function, determines how much of the retrieved memory context is injected into the current state representation ($\tilde{x}_t$).
 - **Planning and Execution:** A Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct planner, conditioned on the memory-adapted state ($\tilde{x}_t$) and the retrieved memory context, predicts the current high-level skill ($\hat{z}_h_t$) and a boundary score ($\hat{b}_t$). An executor then expands the predicted skill into a low-level latent-action chunk ($\hat{Z}_l_{t:t+K-1}$). An action decoder maps this chunk to executable robot controls ($\hat{a}_{t:t+K-1}$).
 - **Memory Writing:** A **write gate** ($\alpha_w_t$) (also sigmoid-controlled) decides when to store a new memory token ($\gamma_t$) in the memory bank. This token is formed from the current state, predicted skill, and pooled low-level latent chunk. Writing occurs only when $\alpha_w_t$ exceeds a predefined threshold, ensuring that new memories are stored only at predicted skill transitions (event-driven design). The memory bank is updated by appending $\gamma_t$ and compressing if it exceeds a budget.
 - **Loss Function:** The fine-tuning objective includes an action loss (MSE for deterministic, negative log-likelihood for stochastic policies), along with weighted losses for planner prediction, executor prediction, boundary prediction, and a specific gate loss. The gate loss uses BCE to supervise the write gate based on discovered skill-boundary labels and includes L1 regularization terms to encourage sparse memory reading and writing. Teacher-forced memory warmup can be used to stabilize training.

**Inference Pipeline:**
At test time, HiMem-WAM operates causally. It receives current observations, proprioception, instruction, and the current memory bank. It then performs the following sequence: reads memory, computes the memory-adapted state, predicts the high-level skill and boundary score using the planner, generates the low-level latent-action chunk via the executor, decodes actions, and conditionally writes new memory based on the write gate's activation. This process avoids test-time video generation or optical flow estimation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HiMem-WAM focused on its ability to improve robotic manipulation performance, particularly regarding latent actions as motion priors, the efficacy of the gated-memory module for long-horizon memory, and its deployment capabilities in real-world scenarios. Experiments were conducted on LIBERO, LIBERO-PLUS, RMBench simulation benchmarks, and a suite of real-world tasks.

**Q1: Comparison with Existing VLAs and WAMs on Standard Benchmarks**

- **LIBERO Benchmark:** HiMem-WAM achieved a total average success rate (SR) of 97.7%. This performance was comparable to Fast-WAM (97.6%) and slightly higher than AtomVLA (97.0%), positioning it among the top-performing models.
- **LIBERO-PLUS Benchmark (Zero-Shot):** In this benchmark, which assesses robustness under deployment perturbations, HiMem-WAM obtained an average SR of 76.0%. This result exceeded HoloBrain-0 (75.3%) and OpenVLA-OFT (71.4%), indicating its robustness under varying conditions without specific training on the perturbed environments.
- **RMBench Benchmark:** On RMBench, which specifically evaluates memory-dependent manipulation, HiMem-WAM achieved a total average SR of 26.3%. This represented a substantial improvement over baselines like ACT (10.8%) and X-VLA (9.8%). For tasks with Task Memory Complexity (TMC) M(1) (single memory element), HiMem-WAM reached 31.6% SR. For M(n) tasks (multiple memory elements), the SR was 19.8%, indicating a drop compared to M(1) tasks.

**Key Finding 1: Latent actions improve robustness by capturing transferable motion patterns.**

The results from Stage II latent action pretraining indicated a performance gain. Specifically, models with Stage II pretraining achieved an increase of +1.1% on the standard LIBERO benchmark and +3.8% on the LIBERO-PLUS (Zero-Shot) benchmark, compared to models without this stage. The larger gain observed under deployment perturbations on LIBERO-PLUS, which includes variations in camera, viewpoint, and observation noise, suggests that the learned latent actions effectively capture generalized task completion motions. This characteristic is posited to enhance robustness across diverse deployment conditions by providing a stable motion prior for action prediction, rather than overfitting to specific visual trajectories.

**Key Finding 2: Gated memory improves task state tracking, while stronger memory planning is still needed.**

HiMem-WAM's performance on RMBench, with a 26.3% total average SR and 31.6% on M(1) tasks, supports the effectiveness of the memory module. The read gate's ability to retrieve task history for conditioning the planner, combined with the write gate's mechanism to store compact key states at detected meaningful task transitions, was shown to facilitate target tracking, phase recognition, and overall memory-dependent manipulation. The Qwen3-VL-4B planner could leverage both current observations and past task states. However, the performance decline on M(n) tasks (19.8%) and the reported observation that it remains below specialized memory models (such as Mem-0, which uses a larger 8B planner and a more specialized memory planning design) suggest that further advancements in memory reasoning are required for tasks involving repeated trials and multi-step state updates.

**Q4: Real-world Deployment on Robotic Tasks**

HiMem-WAM was evaluated on a dual-arm platform performing 10 real-world manipulation tasks categorized into Easy, Medium, and Hard difficulty levels under two settings: Standard (ST) and Generalization (GE) with deployment-time perturbations.

**Key Finding 3: HiMem-WAM improves generalization in real-world robotic tasks.**

- On Easy tasks, HiMem-WAM performed comparably to the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline, both achieving high success rates.
- For Medium tasks, HiMem-WAM showed notable gains of +12.5% under ST and +10.0% under GE compared to $\pi_{0.5}$. This suggests that the latent action pretraining provides predictive motion priors that contribute to stabilizing real-world action prediction amidst variations in objects, lighting, and instructions.
- The most significant improvements were observed on Hard tasks, with gains of +25.0% under ST and +20.0% under GE compared to $\pi_{0.5}$. These tasks necessitate long-horizon execution and persistent task-state maintenance. The gated memory module was identified as crucial for enabling the policy to retain historical information and task states, mitigating "history forgetting" and allowing for continued long-horizon task execution.

**Key Finding 4: Stage II Latent Action Pretrain is effective across action representations.**

Analysis of real-world results under different action representations (Joint Position and End-Effector Pose) demonstrated that Stage II hierarchical latent action pretraining consistently improved SR in the ST setting. The gains were more pronounced with increasing task difficulty. On hard tasks, SR increased from 15.0% to 35.0% for Joint Position and from 10.0% to 30.0% for End-Effector Pose. This finding indicates that hierarchical latent actions provide beneficial motion priors and temporal structure, particularly for long-horizon manipulation. The larger gains for End-Effector Pose suggest that task-space commands, which inherently contain less robot-specific execution detail, benefit more from learned motion priors. However, Joint Position actions ultimately yielded a higher final SR, implying that they may retain more low-level action information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HiMem-WAM introduces an architecture for robotic manipulation that integrates hierarchical latent action learning with a memory-gated module into a World Action Model framework. The significance of this work resides in its approach to addressing known limitations in long-horizon robotic manipulation by providing both structured temporal abstraction and efficient, event-driven memory management.

The key contributions are:
1. **Structured Temporal Abstraction:** The hierarchical latent action framework, which decomposes tasks into low-level motion primitives and high-level skill latents, has been shown to improve policy robustness under various deployment perturbations. This suggests that learning motion priors separate from visual specifics enhances generalization.
2. **Efficient Memory Management:** The boundary-aware memory-gated module represents a method for selectively storing compact task states at meaningful skill transitions. This contrasts with dense history aggregation, offering a potentially more scalable and efficient way to retain relevant information over long horizons, especially for recalling occluded subtask states or adapting after partial task completion.
3. **Causal Inference at Test Time:** By leveraging future visual dynamics solely as training supervision and not requiring test-time video generation or optical flow estimation, HiMem-WAM maintains a fully causal inference pipeline. This design characteristic is important for real-time robotic control by avoiding computational bottlenecks during deployment.

The potential impact of HiMem-WAM on the field of robotic manipulation is multifaceted:
- **Enhanced Capabilities for Complex Tasks:** By enabling robots to effectively handle long-horizon, memory-dependent tasks with greater robustness, HiMem-WAM could facilitate the deployment of robotic systems in more intricate and dynamic real-world scenarios. This includes applications requiring multi-step procedures, interaction with occluded objects, or adaptation to changing task states.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** The demonstrated gains in performance under deployment perturbations and in generalization settings suggest that the framework contributes to more reliable robot behavior in non-ideal or varied environments, a critical step towards general-purpose robots.
- **Advancement in World Action Models:** HiMem-WAM extends the WAM paradigm by incorporating hierarchical reasoning and gated memory, offering a direction for future research in building more intelligent and context-aware embodied agents.
- **Foundation for Future Memory-Based Systems:** The event-driven memory update mechanism, tied to learned skill boundaries, provides a model for developing more intelligent memory architectures in robotics, moving beyond fixed-length histories or dense feature storage.

**Limitations:**
Despite its demonstrated capabilities, the paper acknowledges several limitations. The training pipeline is computationally intensive, involving multiple stages such as optical flow extraction, latent action learning, skill discovery, and memory policy training. This multi-stage design also introduces engineering complexity, with overall performance being contingent on the quality of each learned component (latent actions, skill boundaries, memory updates). Furthermore, the real-world evaluation was conducted on a specific dual-arm platform with 10 manipulation tasks. While these tasks covered various difficulty levels and perturbations, broader validation across a larger scale of tasks, diverse datasets, and different robot embodiments is necessary to fully assess the framework's scalability and generalizability.
