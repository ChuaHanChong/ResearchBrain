# Sliding Sensors: Configurable Confidence in State Estimation for Continuum Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Toronto developed a mechanically reconfigurable sensing system for continuum robots, allowing a sensor to actively translate along the robot's body. This approach enables dynamic shaping of state estimation confidence and demonstrates a 6.1-8.4% reduction in position RMSE for full-body shape estimation compared to a fixed sensor.

## Problem

- Confidence in continuum robot state estimates varies spatially, with lower confidence in areas without direct sensor measurements.
- Traditional fixed-placement sensors cannot adapt to dynamic task requirements where regions of interest along the robot's body change.
- Existing sensor designs for continuum robots lack active, controlled repositioning capabilities, relying instead on static placement or passive movement.

## Method

- Introduces a mechanically ==reconfigurable sensing== paradigm where a **sliding sensor** can be actively translated along the continuum robot's length using a lead screw system.
- Developed a proof-of-concept hardware system using a 5-DOF electromagnetic coil sensor housed within an inner tube that slides inside the robot's backbone.
- Employs a ==factor graph optimization== framework for state estimation, incorporating time-varying measurements from the sliding sensor and leveraging a ==continuous-time robot model==.

## Results

- Confidence ellipsoids in state estimates are smallest (highest confidence) in the immediate vicinity of the sliding sensor's current position, demonstrating reconfigurable confidence.
- Position RMSE for full-body shape estimation decreased by **6.1%** for C-shape and **8.4%** for S-shape robots when using a sliding sensor compared to a fixed tip sensor.
- Orientation RMSE improved by **3.2%** for C-shape and **3.8%** for S-shape robots, showcasing consistent accuracy gains across different robot poses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The spatial distribution of state estimation confidence can be actively reconfigured and directed to specific task-relevant regions by translating the sensor.
> - Integrating localized measurements from varying points along the robot over time, combined with a motion prior, enhances overall full-body shape estimation accuracy.
> - The concept of active estimation, where sensing configuration is dynamically controlled, offers a new approach to managing uncertainty in continuum robotics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Continuum robots are employed in diverse applications, particularly in environments characterized by uncertainty. For safe and effective operation within these contexts, precise state estimation, which involves determining the robot's shape and position, is critical. A foundational challenge in this domain is that the accuracy and confidence of state estimates are not uniform along the robot's body; they inherently vary based on the proximity and density of available measurements. Regions without direct sensor information typically exhibit lower confidence, as the state in these areas must be inferred using robot models.

Previous research in continuum robot state estimation has largely focused on the paradigm of optimal sensor placement. This involves strategizing the static locations of sensors to maximize estimation speed and accuracy, often under constraints regarding the number or types of sensors utilized. While these methods aim to improve overall estimation, they inherently lead to areas of reduced confidence, particularly at points distant from fixed measurement locations. The reliance on robot models to infer states between fixed sensors contributes to this increased uncertainty.

A significant limitation of fixed-placement sensor designs becomes apparent when the specific regions of interest or task-relevant measurement points change during an operation. Many robotic tasks require dynamic zones of focus, such as a robot navigating a cluttered environment and needing to detect contact at varying points along its length, or multiple continuum robots collaborating and requiring precise coordination at different interaction points. In such scenarios, a fixed sensor configuration may not provide optimal confidence where it is most needed at a given time. While some existing works have explored sensors that exhibit passive motion relative to the robot's backbone—for instance, tip-fixed sensors that move as the robot bends or extends—these designs do not allow for active, controlled repositioning of sensing elements.

This paper addresses this gap by introducing the concept of mechanically reconfigurable sensing, where a sensor's position can be actively adjusted along the robot's length. This capability is presented as a novel approach to actively shape and manage the spatial distribution of uncertainty in state estimation. By demonstrating the feasibility and benefits of an actuated, sliding sensor, the work moves beyond static sensor placement optimization and passive sensor movement, proposing a new direction for enhancing adaptability and targeted confidence in continuum robot state estimation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research presented in this paper is driven by a fundamental challenge in continuum robot operation and state estimation: the inherent spatial non-uniformity of estimation uncertainty. Confidence in a robot's estimated state is typically higher near measurement points and diminishes with increasing distance from them. While global estimation accuracy is a general goal, the specific operational requirements of continuum robots often prioritize achieving *sufficient* confidence in state estimates at *task-relevant locations*, which may change throughout an operation.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations:
- **Dynamic Task Requirements:** Many continuum robot applications involve dynamic scenarios where the areas of primary interest along the robot's body are not static. Examples include navigating cluttered environments where contact detection or avoidance might be critical at various, changing points, or multi-robot collaboration where interaction points shift. In such cases, a fixed sensor location cannot consistently provide the highest confidence where and when it is needed most.
- **Limitations of Fixed Sensors:** Traditional optimal sensor placement strategies for continuum robots focus on static configurations. These approaches lead to inherent regions of low confidence in areas without direct measurement, relying on potentially less accurate model-based inference. This static design prevents the robot from adapting its sensing capabilities to evolving task needs.
- **Absence of Actuated Sensing:** While some previous works have explored sensors with passive movement (e.g., tip-fixed sensors moving with robot deformation), the concept of an *actuated* sensor that can be actively translated along the robot's length to strategically reconfigure measurement points for state estimation has not been significantly explored.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of this research are:
1. **Introduce a Mechanically Reconfigurable Sensing Paradigm:** To conceptualize and demonstrate a system where sensor location can be actively changed along the continuum robot. This aims to enable the dynamic shaping of uncertainty profiles in state estimation, moving beyond static sensor placement.
2. **Demonstrate Feasibility of Longitudinal Sensor Translation:** To develop and present a proof-of-concept hardware design that physically implements the longitudinal translation of a sensor within the backbone of a continuum robot. This objective focuses on establishing the practical viability of such a mechanism.
3. **Show Reconfigurability of State Estimation Confidence:** To experimentally or via simulation demonstrate that by varying the sensor's location, the spatial distribution of confidence bounds in the robot's state estimate can be intentionally altered. The goal is to show that higher confidence can be directed to specific points along the robot by moving the sensor to those points.
4. **Quantify Error Reduction with Sliding Sensors:** To evaluate and quantify the improvement in full-body shape estimation accuracy when the sensor is actively moved back and forth over time, compared to a baseline configuration where the sensor remains fixed at a single location (e.g., the robot's tip). This objective seeks to provide empirical evidence for the benefits of an actuated sliding sensor strategy.

Through these objectives, the authors aim to introduce a novel approach to sensing in continuum robotics that enhances adaptability and targeted accuracy in state estimation, laying groundwork for more advanced, active estimation strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves both hardware development for a proof-of-concept system and the formulation of an estimation framework to process the data from the reconfigurable sensor.

#### 3.1. A. Hardware

The system is designed to allow a sensor to be linearly actuated within the backbone of a continuum robot. This core concept involves an inner tube housing the sensor, which slides within an outer tube that forms the robot's main structural element. The design is modular, allowing for substitution of different sensors or linear actuation methods, provided they fit within the robot's constraints and achieve the desired stroke length.

For the proof-of-concept implementation, specific components were selected:
- **Sensor:** A 5-DOF electromagnetic coil position sensor (Aurora v3, Northern Digital Inc., Canada) was used. This sensor has a diameter of 0.5 mm and a length of 8 mm, requiring an external magnetic field generator for operation. The 5-DOF measurement implies the sensor provides position (x, y, z) and two orientation angles, typically roll and pitch, omitting yaw.
- **Linear Actuator:** A lead screw system (Igus Inc., Germany) was chosen for linear actuation. This system offers a 60 mm stroke length, enabling the sensor to translate along a segment of the robot. The lead screw is driven by a Dynamixel XL430-W250 servo motor, which provides precise control over the sensor's position within the inner tube. The motor's control input is mapped directly to the desired sensor displacement.
- **Continuum Robot Backbone:** The robot itself is a tendon-driven mechanism with a total length of 70 cm. Its backbone consists of two concentric nitinol tubes: an inner tube with a 0.8 mm inner diameter (ID) and 1.2 mm outer diameter (OD), and an outer tube with a 1.8 mm ID and 2.0 mm OD. The sensor is housed within the inner tube, which slides within the outer tube.
- **Ground Truth Measurement:** To provide reference data for evaluating the system, optical trackers (Vicon Motion Systems Ltd., UK) were used. These trackers collected ground-truth pose measurements for both the continuum robot and the sliding sensor tube.
- **Operational Protocol:** During experimental trials, particularly for static robot poses, the sensor was programmed to oscillate. It performed three full cycles of movement across its maximum stroke length at a constant speed of 24 mm/s, collecting data as it moved.

#### 3.2. B. Estimation

The state estimation problem is framed as a factor graph optimization, a method commonly used for state estimation in robotics due to its ability to handle various types of measurements and priors.
- **Time-Varying Measurements:** A key aspect of the sliding sensor approach is that it introduces time-varying measurement factors. As the sensor translates, it provides direct measurements at different arclength coordinates along the robot at different times.
- **Prior Model:** The estimation methodology builds upon existing work in continuous-time state estimation for continuum robots, specifically referencing and. This prior model is selected for its capability to represent robot states continuously in both arclength (along the robot's body) and time.
- **Estimated State:** The state variables estimated include the robot's pose (position and orientation), strain, and velocity.
- **Sensor Measurement Model:** The 5-DOF pose sensor measurement is modeled using an SE(3) distance metric. The error `e_m(s, t)` is defined as the logarithmic map of the inverse of the measured transform `˜T_m` multiplied by the estimated robot transform `T(s, t)` at a given arclength `s` and time `t`.
 `e_m(s, t) = ln( ˜T_m^-1 T(s, t))∨`
 For the 5-DOF sensor, which does not provide yaw information, the yaw component is projected out from the error vector `e_m` by removing its last term, leveraging a small angle approximation. This modified error vector is then incorporated into the factor graph as a measurement factor, assuming a Gaussian noise model.

This combined hardware and software approach enables the researchers to physically demonstrate the concept of a sliding sensor and computationally evaluate its impact on state estimation confidence and accuracy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning the reconfigurability of confidence in state estimation and the impact of a sliding sensor on overall estimation accuracy.

#### 4.1. A. Confidence Reconfiguration

The paper demonstrates that the spatial distribution of confidence in the robot's estimated shape can be actively reconfigured by varying the sensor's location.
- **Visual Representation:** Figure 2 illustrates the robot position estimate mean with 3σ confidence ellipsoids for three different static robot shapes: straight, C-shape, and S-shape. For each shape, results are presented for two sensor configurations: the sensor fixed at the tip and the sensor translated 6 cm into the robot. The visual evidence indicates that the region of relatively higher confidence (represented by smaller confidence ellipsoids) shifts to correspond with the sensor's location. When the sensor is moved inward from the tip, the area of highest confidence also moves inward from the tip.
- **Simulated Data:** Figure 3 further supports this finding using data generated from a simulated continuum robot where the sensor can slide along the entire robot length. Multiple timesteps are shown as the sensor moves. The confidence ellipsoids are observed to be smallest, indicating higher confidence, in the immediate vicinity of the sensor's current position along the robot's backbone. This demonstrates the ability to dynamically "shape" the uncertainty profile, directing higher confidence to specific regions as the sensor slides.

#### 4.2. B. Shape Estimation Accuracy Improvement

The study quantifies the impact of using an oscillating (sliding) sensor configuration over time compared to a fixed sensor at the tip, showing improvements in full-body shape estimation accuracy.
- **Quantitative Comparison:** Table I presents a comparison of shape estimation errors for real-world data, contrasting a fixed tip sensor with an oscillating (sliding) sensor configuration for both C-shape and S-shape robot poses.
 - **Position RMSE Reduction:** For the C-shape robot, the position Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) decreased from 1.64 cm with a fixed sensor to 1.54 cm with a sliding sensor, representing a 6.1% reduction. For the S-shape robot, the position RMSE decreased from 1.78 cm to 1.63 cm, an 8.4% reduction.
 - **Orientation RMSE Reduction:** The orientation RMSE for the C-shape robot decreased from 0.0779 rad (fixed) to 0.0754 rad (sliding), a 3.2% reduction. For the S-shape robot, the orientation RMSE decreased from 0.2381 rad (fixed) to 0.2291 rad (sliding), a 3.8% reduction.
- **Hypothesized Mechanism:** The authors hypothesize that the observed improvements in accuracy are attributable to the integration of a motion prior within the estimation framework, which, when combined with the moving measurement data, effectively leverages temporal information. This is suggested to enable the estimator to produce more accurate results by continuously updating the state with localized, high-confidence measurements from varying points along the robot over time.
- **Future Considerations:** The paper posits that these accuracy improvements may become more pronounced when the sensor's sliding speed is fast relative to the robot's own motion, though this claim requires further investigation.

In summary, the results demonstrate the mechanical feasibility of sliding a sensor within a continuum robot, the capability to reconfigure state estimation confidence, and a measurable improvement in overall shape estimation accuracy when employing an active, oscillating sensor compared to a static sensor placement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel concept of mechanically reconfigurable sensing for continuum robots, addressing a fundamental limitation in current state estimation practices. The primary significance of this work lies in its demonstration of the feasibility and advantages of actively controlling sensor placement to influence the spatial distribution of state estimation uncertainty.

#### 5.1. A. Demonstrated Feasibility and New Paradigm

The work successfully demonstrates the mechanical feasibility of implementing a sliding sensor within a continuum robot's backbone. By showcasing a hardware prototype and experimental results, the paper validates the concept that a sensor can be actively translated along the robot's length. This capability establishes a new paradigm in continuum robotics, termed "active estimation," where the sensing configuration is not static but dynamically controlled to achieve specific estimation objectives, such as directing higher confidence to particular regions. This contrasts with traditional passive sensing or fixed sensor placement strategies.

#### 5.2. B. Reconfigurable Confidence and Improved Accuracy

A key impact is the observed ability to reconfigure confidence bounds in state estimates. This means that instead of having passively determined regions of high and low confidence, operators or autonomous systems can actively shift zones of high-confidence measurement to task-relevant locations. Furthermore, the demonstrated reduction in full-body shape estimation errors (up to 8.4% for position and 3.8% for orientation) when using a sliding sensor compared to a fixed tip sensor indicates a practical benefit in terms of overall state awareness. This improvement is attributed to the temporal leverage of moving measurements and the use of a motion prior in the estimation process.

#### 5.3. C. Foundation for Future Research and Applications

This work lays foundational groundwork for several advanced research directions and potential applications:
- **Belief-Aware Planning:** The concept of dynamically shaping uncertainty is crucial for "belief-aware planning." In such a framework, robot actuation and sensing configurations could be jointly optimized. For example, a robot might autonomously decide where to place its sensor based on the current task, environment uncertainty, and required confidence levels for specific actions (e.g., precise manipulation, collision avoidance).
- **Enhanced Autonomy in Complex Environments:** Continuum robots operating in highly uncertain or cluttered environments could leverage sliding sensors to achieve targeted high-confidence sensing. This could enable more robust contact detection, obstacle avoidance, or interaction with dynamic elements by focusing sensing resources where they are most critical at any given moment.
- **Overcoming Sensor Size Constraints:** While the current proof-of-concept uses a small electromagnetic sensor, the research motivates further exploration into alternative design solutions for active sensing. This includes developing mechanisms to overcome strict sensor size requirements, potentially integrating different types of sensors (e.g., optical fibers, miniature cameras) that can still be actuated within a robot's limited space.
- **Dynamic Robot Operation:** The current work primarily analyzes static and quasi-static robot configurations. Future research, as suggested by the authors, will explore the implications of sensor dynamics (such as sliding frequency) and its interaction with a dynamically moving robot. This will be critical for real-world applications where robots are continuously moving and interacting.

Overall, the paper introduces a significant conceptual and practical advancement in continuum robot state estimation, moving towards more intelligent and adaptive sensing systems that can actively manage their information gathering processes to enhance operational confidence and performance.
