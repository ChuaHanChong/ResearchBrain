# Combining model-predictive control and predictive reinforcement learning for stable quadrupedal robot locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates a hybrid control strategy for quadrupedal robots by integrating Model Predictive Control (MPC) with Rollout Q-Learning (RQL). The approach demonstrates that RQL improves control performance, particularly with short MPC prediction horizons, by lowering accumulated running costs and enhancing orientation stability, while maintaining computational efficiency.

## Problem

- Quadrupedal robot locomotion faces challenges due to complex dynamics, high degrees of freedom, and varying terrains.
- Traditional Model Predictive Control (MPC) is constrained by computational limits, which restrict prediction horizons and can lead to suboptimal long-term control decisions.
- Pure Reinforcement Learning (RL) typically requires extensive training data and does not inherently handle system constraints.

## Method

- A hybrid control approach combining ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) with Reinforcement Learning, specifically Rollout Q-Learning (RQL), is proposed.
- The standard MPC optimization problem is modified by replacing its terminal cost with a learned Q-function, which estimates the 'cost-to-go' from the terminal state.
- A neural network approximates the Q-function, trained using standard Q-learning to capture longer-term consequences of actions and extend the effective planning horizon.

## Results

- RQL with a short prediction horizon (N=2) achieves lower accumulated running costs and improved orientation control compared to standard MPC, particularly in roll stability.
- The performance gap between RQL and MPC diminishes as the prediction horizon increases, indicating that with sufficient horizon length, MPC can approximate the benefits of the learned Q-function.
- RQL with a short prediction horizon (N=2) provides superior performance to standard MPC with longer horizons (N=4 or N=5) while maintaining lower computational requirements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rollout Q-Learning (RQL) effectively extends the planning horizon of Model Predictive Control (MPC) by incorporating a learned Q-function as the terminal cost, without increasing computational complexity.
> - The benefits of RQL are most pronounced with short MPC prediction horizons, where it compensates for limited foresight by integrating learned knowledge about long-term consequences.
> - RQL improves orientation control, particularly for roll stability, indicating a better ability to maintain balance during locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Controlling quadrupedal robots for stable and efficient locomotion presents significant challenges due to their complex dynamics, multiple degrees of freedom, and the need to handle various terrains. Traditional control approaches often struggle to balance computational efficiency with long-term planning capabilities.

This paper investigates a novel hybrid approach that combines Model Predictive Control (MPC) with Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically using Rollout Q-Learning (RQL), to achieve stable and efficient quadrupedal robot locomotion. The research addresses a fundamental limitation in traditional MPC implementations: the computational constraints that restrict prediction horizons and lead to suboptimal control decisions.

### 2. Background

Control systems for legged robots typically fall into two categories: model-based approaches like MPC and learning-based methods like RL. Each has distinct advantages and limitations:

**Model Predictive Control (MPC)** optimizes control actions over a finite prediction horizon by solving an optimization problem at each time step. For a quadrupedal robot, the MPC formulation can be expressed as:

```
minimize J = Σ(i=0 to N-1) [l(x_i, u_i)] + l_f(x_N)
subject to x_{i+1} = f(x_i, u_i)
           g(x_i, u_i) ≤ 0
```

Where:
- J is the cost function to minimize
- x_i represents the robot state
- u_i represents the control inputs (ground reaction forces)
- l(x_i, u_i) is the running cost
- l_f(x_N) is the terminal cost
- f(x_i, u_i) represents the system dynamics
- g(x_i, u_i) ≤ 0 represents constraints like friction cone limits

While MPC excels at handling constraints and optimizing short-term behavior, its effectiveness is limited by computational constraints that restrict the prediction horizon.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)** learns control policies through experience, potentially capturing long-term dependencies without explicitly modeling the system. However, RL typically requires extensive training data and may not handle constraints naturally.

The proposed approach, **Rollout Q-Learning (RQL)**, aims to combine the strengths of both paradigms by using MPC as a base policy and augmenting it with a learned Q-function that captures longer-term consequences of actions.

### 3. Methodology

The research employs a simplified dynamic model of the Unitree A1 quadrupedal robot, treating it as a single rigid body with forces applied at the contact points. The model neglects leg dynamics for computational efficiency, focusing on the core body dynamics:

```
m·a = m·g + Σ f_i
I·ω̇ + ω × (I·ω) = Σ (r_i × f_i)
```

Where:
- m is the robot mass
- a is the linear acceleration
- g is gravity
- f_i are the ground reaction forces
- I is the inertia tensor
- ω is the angular velocity
- r_i are the position vectors from COM to foot contact points

This simplified model allows for efficient implementation of both MPC and RQL controllers while capturing the essential dynamics needed for stable locomotion.

### 4. System Architecture

The control architecture integrates MPC with RQL to enhance control performance. In standard MPC, the cost function typically includes:

1. A running cost that penalizes deviations from desired states and control effort
2. A terminal cost that accounts for the states beyond the prediction horizon

The key innovation in RQL is modifying this cost function by incorporating a learned Q-function as the terminal cost. The Q-function is trained to estimate the "cost-to-go" from terminal states, effectively extending the planning horizon without increasing computational complexity.

The modified MPC optimization problem becomes:

```
minimize J = Σ(i=0 to N-1) [l(x_i, u_i)] + Q(x_N, u_N)
subject to x_{i+1} = f(x_i, u_i)
           g(x_i, u_i) ≤ 0
```

Where Q(x_N, u_N) is the learned Q-function that replaces the standard terminal cost l_f(x_N).

### 5. Rollout Q-Learning Implementation

The RQL implementation uses a neural network to approximate the Q-function, taking the state and action as inputs. The Q-function is trained using standard Q-learning, where the update rule is:

```
Q(s_t, a_t) ← Q(s_t, a_t) + α[r_t+1 + γ·max_a Q(s_{t+1}, a) - Q(s_t, a_t)]
```

Where:
- α is the learning rate
- r_t+1 is the immediate reward (negative cost)
- γ is the discount factor
- max_a Q(s_{t+1}, a) is the maximum Q-value for the next state

For computational efficiency, the paper employs a relatively simple linear Q-function model, though more complex non-linear models could potentially yield better performance in future work.

The running cost used for both MPC and RQL includes terms that penalize:
1. Position errors (distance from desired position)
2. Orientation errors (deviation from desired orientation)
3. Control effort (magnitude of ground reaction forces)

### 6. Experimental Setup

The control algorithms were evaluated in a simulation environment using the Unitree A1 quadrupedal robot model. The simulation framework combines:

- **rcognita** for reinforcement learning implementation
- **ROS** for communication and coordination
- **Quad-SDK** for robot simulation and motion planning

The experiments compare the performance of standard MPC against RQL for varying prediction horizon lengths (N = 2, 3, 4, 5). Performance metrics include:

1. Position error in x, y, and z axes
2. Orientation error (roll, pitch, yaw)
3. Accumulated running cost
4. Computational efficiency

### 7. Results and Discussion

The experimental results reveal several important findings:

1. **RQL outperforms MPC with short prediction horizons:** With a horizon length of N=2, RQL demonstrates significantly lower running costs and better orientation control, particularly for roll stability. This highlights RQL's ability to effectively extend the planning horizon through the learned Q-function.

2. **Orientation control is significantly improved with RQL:** The data shows that RQL reduces orientation errors (particularly roll errors) compared to MPC. This is crucial for maintaining stability during locomotion, as orientation errors can lead to falls or inefficient movement.

3. **Performance converges as prediction horizon increases:** As shown in Figure 4, the performance gap between MPC and RQL diminishes with longer prediction horizons. This suggests that with sufficient prediction length, MPC can naturally capture most of the dynamics that RQL learns to approximate.

4. **Computational efficiency trade-offs:** RQL with a short prediction horizon (N=2) outperforms MPC with longer horizons (N=4 or N=5) while requiring less computation. This is particularly valuable for real-time control applications where computational resources are limited.

The key insight is that RQL effectively compensates for the limitations of short-horizon MPC by incorporating learned knowledge about long-term consequences, without the computational burden of explicitly simulating those future states.

### 8. Future Directions

Based on the findings, several promising research directions emerge:

1. **Exploration of non-linear Q-function models:** The current implementation uses a relatively simple linear Q-function model. More sophisticated neural network architectures could potentially capture more complex dynamics and further improve performance.

2. **Online learning and adaptation:** The current approach uses a fixed, pre-trained Q-function. Developing methods for online learning and adaptation could enable the robot to continuously improve and adapt to changing conditions or environments.

3. **Extension to uneven terrain and dynamic environments:** While the current implementation focuses on flat terrain, extending the approach to handle uneven terrain and dynamic obstacles would be a valuable next step.

4. **Hardware implementation and validation:** Validating the approach on physical quadrupedal robots would provide insights into real-world performance and identify practical challenges that may not be apparent in simulation.

5. **Incorporation of safety constraints:** Developing methods to ensure that learned behaviors respect safety constraints would be crucial for real-world deployment.

### 9. Conclusion

This research demonstrates that combining Model Predictive Control with Reinforcement Learning through Rollout Q-Learning provides a promising approach for quadrupedal robot locomotion. The hybrid approach effectively leverages the strengths of both paradigms, using MPC's ability to handle constraints and optimize immediate actions, while incorporating RL's ability to learn long-term value functions.

The key advantages of the RQL approach include:

1. Improved performance with short prediction horizons, reducing computational requirements
2. Better orientation control, particularly for roll stability
3. Reduced accumulated running costs, indicating more efficient locomotion
4. The ability to balance computational efficiency with long-term planning

These results suggest that hybrid approaches like RQL could play an important role in advancing the capabilities of legged robots, particularly in applications where computational resources are limited or where robots need to operate in complex, dynamic environments.

By addressing the fundamental limitation of prediction horizon length in MPC, this work contributes to the development of more efficient, stable, and robust control systems for quadrupedal robots and potentially other complex robotic systems.
