# FEAST: A Flexible Mealtime-Assistance System Towards In-the-Wild Personalization

> [!summary] Summary
> Cornell University researchers and collaborators developed FEAST, a flexible mealtime-assistance system enabling in-the-wild personalization for individuals with physical disabilities. The system utilizes Large Language Models for natural language customization, achieved high skill success rates, and demonstrated adaptability, transparency, and safety during in-home evaluations.

## Problem

- Individuals with physical disabilities require mealtime assistance systems that can adapt to their diverse and dynamic personal needs, a capability lacking in existing robotic solutions.
- Current robotic mealtime assistance systems often rely on fixed, manually programmed actions, leading to limited adaptability, obtrusive motions, and restricted workspace, hindering widespread adoption.
- The inability to self-feed leads to negative emotional impacts on care recipients and significantly contributes to the workload of human caregivers.

## Method

- A flexible mealtime-assistance system, **FEAST**, was designed based on insights from a formative user study with 21 care recipients, prioritizing adaptability, transparency, and safety.
- The system integrates a modular hardware design with a novel utensil orientation and autonomous tool-changing capabilities for feeding, drinking, and mouth-wiping tasks.
- An LLM-powered software framework allows natural language-based skill adaptation and personalized gesture synthesis, incorporating structured safety checks and explanations for transparency.

## Results

- FEAST addressed 36 out of 46 identified personalization requests from users, substantially exceeding the capabilities of existing state-of-the-art systems.
- A five-day in-home study with two community researchers showed high skill success rates for bite acquisition (**89.27%**) and bite transfer (**93.07%**), alongside a low cognitive workload (NASA-TLX average 7.41-22.22).
- An independent Occupational Therapist evaluation reported a 5/5 personalization success rate and a preference for the personalized system over default settings in all 10 scenarios tested.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective in-the-wild personalization for assistive robots hinges on explicitly designing for adaptability, transparency, and safety, validated through extensive user co-design.
> - Large Language Models can be integrated safely into physical human-robot interaction, enabling intuitive natural language customization and dynamic generation of personalized gestures.
> - Providing a flexible, user-driven robotic system for mealtime assistance significantly enhances care recipient independence and control, often surpassing the perceived autonomy with human caregivers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses the growing need for robotic assistance in daily living activities, specifically mealtime assistance, for individuals with physical disabilities. Millions globally require help with feeding, which can lead to negative emotional impacts such as shame and reduced self-esteem, while also contributing significantly to caregiver workload.

Prior research in robot mealtime assistance has focused on various sub-problems, including food manipulation (e.g., skewering, scooping, twirling), skill sequencing, and bite transfer (both outside-mouth and inside-mouth methods). Commercial systems like Obi and Neater Eater exist, but their adoption has been limited due to reliance on manually programmed, fixed actions and a lack of adaptability. Recent academic efforts, such as FLAIR, have improved robustness and autonomy in these areas by using sensors and vision-parameterized skills, often leveraging foundation models for skill sequencing.

However, a significant gap remains in personalizing these systems to the diverse and dynamic needs of individual care recipients. Existing systems often exhibit obtrusive motion, restrict workspace, or offer only limited, fixed customizations. The context of eating (e.g., social, watching TV), individual preferences, functional abilities, and the types of food consumed vary widely, necessitating a flexible and adaptable system.

This research distinguishes itself by focusing on "in-the-wild personalization," integrating a wide range of mealtime tasks (feeding, drinking, mouth wiping) with user-driven customization capabilities. It builds upon advancements in food acquisition (FLAIR) and bite transfer (Gallenberger et al., Feel the Bite) but adapts them to a novel utensil design that improves workspace and reduces occlusion. Furthermore, it incorporates recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) for Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), utilizing them for natural language-based skill adaptation and transparency, a relatively nascent area for physically assistive robots. The paper situates itself within the context of community-based participatory research, an approach that emphasizes co-design and in-depth evaluation with target users, which is increasingly recognized in assistive technology and robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate FEAST, a flexible mealtime-assistance system capable of in-the-wild personalization to meet the diverse and evolving needs of individual care recipients.

This overarching objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Addressing User Diversity and Dynamic Needs:** Individuals requiring mealtime assistance exhibit a wide range of functional abilities, behavioral preferences, and environmental contexts (e.g., social dining, watching TV). Current robot systems often adopt a "one-size-fits-all" approach, which is insufficient. For instance, some users may require inside-mouth bite transfer due to severe mobility limitations, while others prefer the robot to retract after bite transfer in social settings to maintain visual contact with companions. These variations necessitate a system that can adapt from meal to meal and as needs change over time.
- **Improving Quality of Life and Reducing Caregiver Burden:** The inability to self-feed is associated with negative emotional impacts on care recipients. Robot assistance can enhance their independence and quality of life while simultaneously alleviating the significant workload on human caregivers, for whom feeding is a time-consuming Activity of Daily Living (ADL).
- **Formative User Study Insights:** The research explicitly motivated its design by first conducting a formative user study with 21 care recipients. This study aimed to:
 - **Collect Specific Personalization Requests:** Identify concrete features and behaviors users would want to customize. This led to the identification of 46 distinct personalization request types across seven categories, directly informing the system's development priorities.
 - **Identify Key Tenets for Personalization:** From these user interactions, three core principles emerged as crucial for in-the-wild personalization in mealtime assistance:
 - **Adaptability:** The system must be able to change its behavior in response to user requests, not just during initial setup, but dynamically based on scenario, energy levels, and long-term changes in medical conditions.
 - **Transparency:** Users must understand and predict the robot's behavior, especially when it adapts, to foster trust and effective collaboration.
 - **Safety:** Any adaptations or changes in system behavior must be guaranteed to be safe, particularly given the close physical interaction with vulnerable users.
- **Leveraging Advanced Technologies for HRI:** The motivation also includes exploring how recent advancements in large language models can be effectively and safely integrated into assistive robotics to enable intuitive, natural language-based personalization and transparency, overcoming the limitations of traditional, rigid programming.
- **Demonstrating Real-World Applicability:** A critical motivation is to move beyond laboratory settings and demonstrate the system's effectiveness and usability in actual home environments, across realistic meals and diverse contexts, involving end-users in a participatory research paradigm. This includes evaluations with both community researchers and an independent Occupational Therapist to assess ecological validity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology, combining user-centered design, advanced robotics engineering, and contemporary artificial intelligence techniques.

#### 3.1. Formative User Study (Section III)

To inform the system's design, a formative study was conducted with 21 care recipients, including two community researchers (CRs) who later became co-authors.
- **Participants:** Diverse group (ages 20-62, 8 female, 11 male) with various medical conditions (e.g., Cerebral Palsy, Multiple Sclerosis, Spinal Cord Injury) and varying prior experience with assistive systems.
- **Materials:** Speculative videos demonstrating realistic mealtime scenarios with varying user functionality, behavior, and environment. These videos were used to prompt discussions.
- **Procedure:** Virtual, 90-minute sessions that included caregivers.
- **Outcomes:** The study yielded 46 specific personalization requests categorized across 7 areas (e.g., mealtime tasks, interaction, acquisition, transfer) and identified the three core tenets for personalization: adaptability, transparency, and safety.

#### 3.2. FEAST System Design (Section IV)

FEAST was developed using a community-based participatory research approach, involving iterative co-design and evaluation with the two CRs over several months, including in-person meetings at their homes.

#### 3.2.1. System Hardware (Section IV-A)

- **Robot Platform:** Kinova Gen3 7-DoF robot arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper.
- **Mounting:** Flexible, either on a ROVI wheelchair (powered by wheelchair battery) or a movable Vention stand (wall outlet).
- **Tool-Change Apparatus:** Modular design supporting three custom tools:
 - **Novel Feeding Utensil:** Integrated motors for wrist-like DOF (twirling, scooping), compliant silicone fork (detachable for metal option), 6-axis Nano25 ATI force/torque sensor, magnetic electrical connections for power/control.
 - **Adaptable Mug Handle:** Accommodates various cup shapes, features an ArUco marker for autonomous grasping.
 - **Custom Mouth-Wiping Tool:** Removable microfiber cloth for gentle cleaning.
 - Tools are mounted/dismounted autonomously by the robot gripper.
- **Novel Utensil Orientation and Camera Mount:** Tools positioned at a perpendicular angle (mimicking human wrist) to improve workspace and reduce user view obstruction. An RGB-D Intel RealSense Camera is mounted perpendicularly.
- **Compute and Networking:** Intel NUC for real-time control (approx. 1 kHz), Lenovo Legion Pro 7i laptop for primary compute (16GB RTX 4090 GPU), Nighthawk RAX43 router for communication.
- **Accessible Buttons:** Three buttons: one user-customizable for interactions, one user-accessible emergency stop, and one experimenter emergency stop.
- **Status LED:** Integrated into the camera mount to signal when user attention is needed.

#### 3.2.2. System Software (Section IV-B)

- **Skills as Parameterized Behavior Trees:** Skills are implemented as behavior trees, with exposed, human-readable node parameters (e.g., Speed, TimeToWaitBeforeAutocontinue) and predefined domains of possible values (e.g., {low, medium, high}, {true, false}).
- **Personalizing Skills from Natural Language (Adaptability, Safety, Transparency):**
 1. **Language to Structured Updates:** An LLM (GPT-4o) translates natural language requests into structured behavior tree updates. Static checks validate the output form and parameter names; re-prompting occurs if checks fail.
 2. **Safety Checks:** Node additions are restricted to "pause," "wait for gesture," and specific "retract" configurations. Parameter changes are validated against predefined safe domains. If unsafe, the update is rejected.
 3. **Update Results to Language:** An LLM summarizes the updates and outcomes in understandable language for the user, displayed on the interface.
- **Synthesizing New Gestures with LLMs:** Users provide a language label, description, and a few positive/negative examples (time series of head poses/facial keypoints). An LLM (GPT-4o) generates a Python function for gesture detection, including proposing and optimizing hyperparameters via grid search. The generated function is dynamically executed in a memory-controlled workspace for safety. An ablation study demonstrated improved F1-score with personalized parameters (0.9 vs 0.63).
- **Sequencing Skills with PDDL:** Each skill is associated with a PDDL operator. FastDownward (optimal task planner) sequences skills based on a known initial state and a propositional goal (e.g., "request a bite") from user input.
- **Skills Included:**
 - **PickTool/PlaceTool:** Grasping/returning utensil, mug (ArUco marker detection), or wiper.
 - **AcquireBite:** Builds on FLAIR, using a vision-parameterized skill library (skewering, scooping, twirling, dipping) and pre-acquisition actions (pushing, grouping, cutting). Force thresholds ensure safety. Vision-language foundation models identify plate contents, and an LLM sequences skills based on user preferences.
 - **TransferTool:** Default is outside-mouth transfer (Gallenberger et al.); configurable to inside-mouth transfer (Feel the Bite) using a task-space compliant controller and continuous head pose tracking. Uses head perception pipeline from Feel the Bite.
 - **EmulateTransfer:** Moves gripper to pre-mouth configuration for gesture recording/testing.
 - **Retract:** Moves robot to a safe, retracted position.

#### 3.2.3. User Interface (Section IV-C)

- **Web-based Interface:** Implemented using Vue.js and ROS Noetic, accessible on personal devices (tablets, phones).
- **Design:** Iteratively refined with CRs for large buttons, speech-to-text support.
- **Functionality:** Displays contextual pages, reflects system behavior, supports manual overrides (e.g., selecting specific bites, setting keypoints), and offers a Personalization Page for adaptability/transparency requests and a Gestures Page for adding custom gestures.
- **Transparency:** Displays dynamic text explaining current actions when switching pages.

#### 3.3. Default Mealtime-Assistance Procedure (Section IV-D)

Users initiate a meal by specifying food items and bite order. They can then select tasks (bite, sip, wipe). The system plans and executes actions, with opportunities for user confirmation or manual overrides. The Personalization Page allows for adaptability and transparency requests throughout the meal.

#### 3.4. Safety Adherence (Section V-C & Appendix C)

FEAST adheres to ISO 13842:2014 ("Safety Requirements for Personal Care Robots"). Key safety features include:
- **Pose Filtering:** Predefined zones for expected object/head positions; robot transitions to safety state if detections occur outside zones.
- **Active Collision Monitoring:** Compares joint torque feedback with model predictions to detect unexpected collisions when not using compliant control.
- **Compliant Control:** Used for inside-mouth transfer, providing physical interaction-aware control.
- **Safety-Centric Hardware Design:** Fork has a mechanical weak point to yield under excessive force; silicone fork tip, silicone straw, soft mouth wiper.
- **Emergency Stops:** Physical buttons for both user and experimenter.
- **Watchdog:** Continuously monitors sensors and emergency stops, triggering a safety state if issues arise.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded a range of findings across its formative study, system design, and evaluations:

#### 4.1. Formative User Study Outcomes (Section III)

- **Personalization Needs:** The study successfully identified 46 distinct personalization request types from care recipients, categorized into 7 areas (e.g., mealtime tasks, interaction, acquisition, transfer, hardware, miscellaneous). These requests highlighted the diversity of user preferences for interaction modalities, movement speeds, bite acquisition methods, and contextual adaptations.
- **Key Tenets:** The study established adaptability, transparency, and safety as the three fundamental tenets for developing an effective in-the-wild personalized mealtime assistance system.

#### 4.2. System Capabilities and Adherence to Tenets (Section V)

- **Adaptability:** FEAST demonstrates high adaptability, addressing 36 of the 46 personalization request types identified in the formative study. This significantly surpasses the capabilities of state-of-the-art baselines like Nanavati et al. (17 requests) and FLAIR (15 requests). The LLM-based personalized gesture synthesis achieved an F1-score of 0.9 ± 0.02, outperforming a non-personalized baseline (0.63 ± 0.09).
- **Transparency:** The system is designed to meet all five levels of the IEEE Transparency Standard. It provides an instruction manual (Level 1), demonstration videos and gesture testing (Level 2), LLM-summarized explanations of recent activity (Level 3) and "what if" queries (Level 4), and aims for continuous explanations of state changes (Level 5) via LLM summarization.
- **Safety:** FEAST adheres to the ISO 13842:2014 safety requirements for personal care robots through multiple hardware and software mechanisms, including pose filtering, active collision monitoring, compliant control, safety-centric hardware design, emergency stops, and a watchdog system.

#### 4.3. Five-Day In-the-Wild Evaluation (Section VI)

A five-day in-home user study with two community researchers (CR1 and CR2), involving six realistic meals across three distinct environmental contexts (personal, watching TV, social), yielded the following results:
- **Meal Completion:** Both CRs successfully completed their meals, either clearing their plate or eating until full.
- **Experimenter Interventions:** A low number of interventions were required (average 3 ± 2 per meal), primarily due to initial hardware issues (damaged ArUco marker) and network connectivity problems impacting the web interface.
- **Experimenter Explanations:** Users asked 20 questions across six meals (average 3.33 ± 2.69). This highlighted a need for more accessible in-situ transparency features within the system.
- **Skill Success Rates:** FEAST demonstrated high robustness across diverse food items:
 - Bite acquisition: 89.27% ± 9.24%
 - Bite transfer: 93.07% ± 7.70%
 - Drink acquisition: Improved to 100.0% ± 0.0% after initial hardware fix.
 - Drink and wipe transfers: 94.44% ± 13.61% and 100.0% ± 0.0% respectively.
- **Cognitive Workload (NASA-TLX):** The average cognitive workload was relatively low (CR1: 22.22 ± 10.02; CR2: 7.41 ± 5.79 on a 0-100 scale), compared to a baseline of 37. Users reported high success in achieving mealtime goals. Cognitive workload generally decreased with increased familiarity over multiple meals, though specific non-optimal user settings could temporarily increase it.
- **Comparison with Human Caregivers:** Both CRs reported that FEAST provided greater control and a stronger sense of independence compared to human caregivers. CR2 specifically noted less effort in conveying preferences to the robot.
- **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Personalization Tenets:** CRs rated FEAST highly (mean ≥ 4/5) for perceived usefulness, ease of use, attitude, intention, and enjoyment. Adaptability and safety also received high ratings. Transparency, while appreciated, was noted as needing improvement in accessibility.
- **Lessons Learned:** The study identified several practical insights: the significant variability in in-home scenarios necessitates personalized adaptation; transparency aids users in iteratively refining system behavior even when initial commands are imperfect; providing multiple interfaces is crucial for transparency; cognitive workload decreases with familiarity; and system flexibility, combined with user input, improves recovery from unpredictable real-world failures.

#### 4.4. Ecological Validity with an Occupational Therapist (Section VII)

An evaluation by an Occupational Therapist (OT) unfamiliar with FEAST further assessed its ecological validity:
- **Personalization Success and Ease:** The OT reported a 5/5 personalization success rate and 4.6 ± 0.16/5 for ease of personalization across 10 realistic scenarios.
- **Preference:** The OT preferred the personalized system over the default (no-personalization) system in all 10 scenarios.
- **Interactions:** The OT made 13 adaptability and 2 transparency requests, with minimal experimenter interventions (2) and explanations (1).
- **Feedback:** Positive feedback was received on the interface design, highlighting good visual contrast and the utility of speech-to-text.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents FEAST, a mealtime-assistance system that demonstrates the integration of complex robotic capabilities with user-centric personalization, indicating several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Enhanced Independence and Quality of Life:** By enabling individuals with diverse mobility limitations to personalize their mealtime experience, FEAST has the potential to significantly increase their independence and improve their quality of life, mitigating the emotional distress associated with relying on others for feeding. The user feedback indicating greater control and independence compared to human caregivers underscores this impact.
- **Reduced Caregiver Burden:** Automating aspects of mealtime assistance and allowing users to articulate preferences directly to the robot can reduce the physical and mental workload on human caregivers, for whom feeding is a demanding and time-consuming task.
- **Advancement in Personalization for Assistive Robotics:** The paper introduces a novel personalization framework explicitly built on the tenets of adaptability, transparency, and safety. This framework, leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for natural language interpretation, code synthesis for gestures, and structured behavior tree modifications, represents a significant step towards more flexible and user-driven assistive robots. It demonstrates how LLMs can be safely integrated into physical HRI, with built-in checks and explanations.
- **Demonstrated Real-World Applicability:** The extensive five-day in-the-wild evaluation in users' homes, across varied contexts and meal types, provides empirical evidence of FEAST's robustness and usability in realistic scenarios, moving beyond typical laboratory-based studies. The evaluation by an independent Occupational Therapist further validates its ecological relevance.
- **Comprehensive Task Integration:** FEAST is among the first systems to integrate multiple essential mealtime tasks—feeding, drinking, and mouth wiping—within a single, adaptable platform. This multi-modal assistance capability addresses a broader spectrum of user needs during meals.
- **Open-Sourced Contribution:** By open-sourcing its hardware and software components, the project contributes to the broader research community, facilitating further development and iteration on personalized assistive robotics.
- **Insights into HRI Design:** The "Lessons Learned" section offers valuable insights into designing flexible HRI systems, emphasizing the importance of diverse interaction methods, accessible transparency, and accommodating iterative user refinement, even in the face of system limitations.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as open-set food detection and LLM hallucination, it establishes a foundation for future research in these areas, positioning FEAST as a significant contribution to the field of assistive robotics and human-robot interaction.
