# What Makes Good Few-shot Examples for Vision-Language Models?

> [!summary] Summary
> The research demonstrates that few-shot learning with Vision-Language models is highly sensitive to the chosen examples, often exhibiting significant performance variability with random sampling. Researchers from Fudan University, Bytedance, and Sensetime introduce two example selection methods, Montecarlo and Representativeness, which consistently outperform random sampling and active learning strategies across various datasets and few-shot frameworks.

## Problem

- Few-shot learning performance of pre-trained Vision-Language (VL) models shows high variability due to the specific choice of training examples.
- Standard practice of random sampling for few-shot examples leads to suboptimal and inconsistent results.
- Existing instance selection methods designed for single-modality tasks do not effectively address the unique challenges of multi-modal VL settings.

## Method

- Propose ==Gaussian Monte Carlo (Montecarlo)== to select examples that are "unfamiliar" to the pre-trained VL model, based on robustness to Gaussian noise perturbation in embedding space.
- Propose ==Representativeness (REPRE)== to select examples closest to their class centroid, aiming for highly characteristic data points.
- Evaluate both proposed and existing ==Active Learning== methods (Entropy, Margin) across 11 diverse image recognition datasets and three few-shot VL frameworks (Linear Probe CLIP, CoOp, MaPLe).

## Results

- The proposed Montecarlo and Representativeness methods consistently achieve better accuracy compared to random sampling and Active Learning strategies across all **11** tested datasets and **3** few-shot learners.
- Montecarlo notably outperformed other methods when integrated with CoOp, while Representativeness showed superior performance with MaPLe and Linear Probe.
- Active Learning methods (Entropy, Margin) frequently underperformed random selection, indicating their limitations in few-shot Vision-Language contexts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The choice of few-shot examples significantly impacts Vision-Language model performance, challenging assumptions that random sampling is sufficient.
> - Traditional Active Learning methods based on uncertainty are generally ineffective in low-shot Vision-Language settings due to unreliable initial model predictions.
> - Effective few-shot examples for Vision-Language models are characterized by either their "unfamiliarity" to the pre-trained model or their "representativeness" of a class, rather than just model uncertainty.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical, yet previously overlooked, challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Vision-Language (VL) models, particularly concerning their application in few-shot learning scenarios. The broader research landscape has seen significant advancements in VL models such as CLIP and its derivatives (e.g., CoOp, MaPLe), which leverage large-scale pre-training on image-text pairs to achieve remarkable generalization performance on downstream tasks, even with limited labeled data.

Initially, much of the research effort in adapting these pre-trained VL models to specific downstream tasks focused on **prompt engineering and optimization**. This involved devising effective strategies to "prompt" the VL model, either through hand-crafted text templates (as in zero-shot CLIP) or by learning continuous prompt vectors (as pioneered by CoOp and further developed by MaPLe). These methods aimed to efficiently integrate downstream knowledge into the pre-trained model by subtly altering the input context or the model's internal representations.

However, the authors identify a crucial gap: while prompt optimization received extensive attention, the **selection of the actual few-shot training examples** themselves was largely overlooked. Previous studies often relied on simple random sampling of examples for fine-tuning, implicitly assuming that the specific choice of examples had a negligible or statistically averaged impact on performance. This paper empirically challenges this assumption, demonstrating that few-shot learning outcomes are highly sensitive to the chosen training examples, with random sampling leading to significant variability.

The work differentiates itself from related efforts in instance selection within single modalities like Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV). In NLP, methods like clustering-based selection or choosing examples closest to test instances have been explored for few-shot text generation or in-context learning (e.g., for GPT-style models). In CV, active learning techniques have been applied to populate support sets for meta-learning frameworks. Crucially, these prior works did not consider the complexities of **multi-modal learning environments** guided by linguistic context, nor did they focus on continuous prompt learning.

Therefore, this paper marks a significant **inaugural exploration** into how data selection profoundly affects few-shot training within the unique context of pre-trained vision-language models. By introducing novel selection methods tailored for VL models and empirically comparing them against both random selection and adapted active learning strategies, it redefines a critical aspect of efficient VL model deployment, shifting focus from merely *how* to adapt the model to *what data* to adapt it with.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from an empirical observation: despite the impressive advancements of few-shot learning with pre-trained vision-language (VL) models, the practical performance of these models is highly dependent on the specific choice of few-shot training examples. The authors provide compelling evidence (e.g., Table 1 showing a dramatic accuracy swing from 27.60% to 3.60% on FGVCAircraft with different random seeds for a 2-shot CoOp model) that relying on random sampling, a common practice, introduces significant variability and often results in suboptimal outcomes. This sensitivity highlights a critical, previously overlooked, facet of few-shot learning.

Given this motivation, the key objectives of the paper are:

1. **Systematically Analyze Performance Variability:** To meticulously examine how the selection of few-shot examples influences the performance of existing few-shot prompt learning approaches (e.g., CoOp, MaPLe). This involves moving beyond the assumption that random sampling is sufficient and understanding its inherent limitations.
2. **Evaluate Existing Active Learning (AL) Strategies:** To assess the effectiveness of well-established Active Learning techniques, specifically Entropy and Margin of Confidence, for instance selection within the specific context of few-shot VL model training. The hypothesis here is that AL, known for its strategic data selection, might offer improvements by pinpointing examples about which the model is most uncertain.
3. **Propose Novel Instance Selection Methods:** To devise and introduce new, more effective strategies specifically designed for the characteristics of pre-trained VL models, aiming to proactively identify and select the most informative examples for labeling. The authors propose two such methods: Gaussian Monte Carlo (Montecarlo) and Representativeness (REPRE).
4. **Demonstrate Model-Agnostic Enhancement:** To show that the proposed instance selection methods are versatile and can universally enhance a wide array of existing few-shot training methodologies for VL models, transcending specific prompt learning frameworks.
5. **Understand "What Makes Good Few-shot Examples":** Beyond just proposing methods, the ultimate underlying objective is to gain insights into the fundamental properties that make certain examples more valuable for few-shot learning in VL contexts, thereby informing future research and practical applications.

In essence, the paper seeks to move beyond the randomness of example selection and provide a principled approach to curate optimal training sets, thereby unlocking the full potential of pre-trained VL models in data-scarce environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors frame the problem as selecting the most valuable labeled data from an unlabeled pool to maximize prediction performance given a limited labeling budget (few-shot scenario). Their approach involves evaluating both existing Active Learning (AL) strategies and proposing novel methods tailored for Vision-Language (VL) models.

#### 3.1. Existing Active Learning (AL) Strategies

The study investigates two classic uncertainty-based AL methods:
- **Entropy:** This method selects examples where the model's prediction distribution exhibits the highest entropy, indicating maximal uncertainty. It is calculated as $E = \sum_{i=1}^N P_i \ln P_i$, where $P_i$ is the model's predicted probability for class $i$.
- **Margin of Confidence:** This strategy identifies examples where the difference between the two most confident prediction probabilities ($P_{max}$ and $P_{max-1}$) is minimal. A smaller margin suggests higher uncertainty.
For both methods, CLIP zero-shot scores are normalized to estimate prediction probabilities, applying these methods in a few-shot context.

#### 3.2. Proposed Novel Selection Methods

Recognizing the potential limitations of direct AL application in few-shot settings, the authors introduce two innovative methods:
- **Gaussian Monte Carlo (Montecarlo):** This method operates on the premise that examples the pre-trained VL model is "unfamiliar" with are the most informative. "Familiarity" is gauged by the model's robustness to Gaussian noise perturbation.
 1. **Noise Generation:** For each original image (anchor image), multiple Gaussian-noised versions are generated. Gaussian noise is added to each pixel, following a zero-mean normal distribution with a standard deviation of 0.1.
 2. **Distance Calculation:** The mean distance (using cosine similarity) between the representations of the original image and its Gaussian-noised counterparts in the embedding space is computed.
 3. **Selection:** Images with a *larger* average distance are considered less familiar to the pre-trained model (meaning the model's embedding changes significantly with noise), and thus more valuable. The top-K anchor images exhibiting the greatest such distance are selected. The assumption is that robust models should maintain close embeddings for perturbed familiar images.
- **Representativeness (REPRE):** This method aims to select examples that best represent their respective classes.
 1. **Class Centroid Computation:** For each class, a centroid is computed by averaging the embeddings of all examples belonging to that class in a validation set.
 2. **Distance to Centroid:** The distance (using cosine similarity) between each candidate example and its respective class centroid is calculated.
 3. **Selection:** Examples that are *closest* to their class centroid are considered most representative. The top-K most representative images are selected, aiming to capture the essential characteristics of each class with minimal examples.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** 11 diverse image recognition datasets are used, including ImageNet, Caltech101 (generic objects); OxfordPets, StanfordCars, Flowers102, Food101, FGVCAircraft (fine-grained); SUN397 (scene); UCF101 (action); DTD (texture); and EuroSAT (satellite imagery).
- **Few-Shot Settings:** Experiments are conducted with 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 shots (labeled examples per class).
- **Few-Shot Frameworks (Learners):** The data selection methods are integrated with three commonly used few-shot learners based on CLIP's ViT-B/16 backbone:
 - **Linear Probe CLIP:** A linear classifier trained on top of CLIP's visual encoder.
 - **CoOp:** Optimizes continuous prompt vectors in CLIP's language branch.
 - **MaPLe:** Jointly optimizes visual and text prompts.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Random:** Standard random selection of few-shot examples.
 - **Entropy & Margin:** The AL methods described above.
 - **Zero-shot CLIP:** Performance of the pre-trained CLIP model without any fine-tuning, serving as a weak baseline for comparison.
- **Evaluation:** All experiments are run with three different random seeds, and average results are reported to mitigate variability. Accuracy is the primary metric.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a rigorous empirical comparison of different example selection strategies across various datasets and few-shot learning paradigms, providing robust insights into what constitutes a "good" few-shot example.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive empirical study reveals several key findings regarding the effectiveness of few-shot example selection for Vision-Language models:

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Comparison to Baselines

- **General Trend:** As expected, increasing the number of training examples per class generally leads to an improvement in prediction accuracy across all methods and datasets, as more data allows for better decision boundary delineation.
- **Proposed Methods Outperform:** The two proposed methods, **Montecarlo** and **Representativeness (REPRE)**, consistently demonstrate strong competitive performance. They significantly surpass random selection as well as the Active Learning (AL)-based strategies (Entropy and Margin) across nearly all datasets and few-shot learners. This highlights the substantial benefit of strategic example selection over random sampling.

#### 4.2. Ineffectiveness of Active Learning (AL) Methods

- **Consistent Underperformance:** A striking and consistent finding is that Entropy and Margin of Confidence methods generally perform poorly, often yielding minimal improvement or even negative impacts compared to random selection. In many cases (e.g., ImageNet with CoOp), their performance falls significantly below that of Random, Montecarlo, and REPRE, and sometimes even below zero-shot CLIP.
- **Reasons for Failure (Analysis):** The authors attribute this subpar performance primarily to two factors:
 - **Dependence on Initial Model Performance:** AL methods rely on the current model's predictions to identify "uncertain" examples. In few-shot settings, the initial model is inherently weak and prone to inaccurate predictions, leading to a skewed understanding of informativeness.
 - **Noise Sensitivity:** In low-shot scenarios, the model's uncertainty (e.g., very close logit values across classes) might be more influenced by noise or spurious correlations, causing AL to select outliers or non-generalizable examples rather than truly informative ones.

#### 4.3. Performance Across Different Few-Shot Learners

- **CoOp Framework (Figure 2):**
 - **Montecarlo Dominance:** When paired with CoOp, the Montecarlo method emerges as the overall best performer. Its "unfamiliarity"-based selection strategy appears highly effective in comprehensively exploring the data space.
 - **Random vs. REPRE:** Random selection performs surprisingly well, often as the second-best method, with REPRE showing comparable effectiveness.
 - **AL Methods Fail:** Entropy and Margin consistently underperform in the CoOp framework.
 - **Dataset Specifics:** For simpler datasets (e.g., Caltech101, Flowers102), all methods quickly achieve high accuracy, but Montecarlo shows a distinct advantage at very low shots (1-2). For complex datasets (e.g., ImageNet, SUN397), overall accuracy remains lower, but Montecarlo still achieves the best or competitive results.

- **MaPLe and Linear Probe Frameworks (Figures 3 & 4):**
 - **REPRE Takes Lead:** In contrast to CoOp, **REPRE** emerges as the top-performing method for both MaPLe and Linear Probe, followed by Random. Montecarlo remains competitive with Random.
 - **AL Methods Consistent Failure:** The poor performance of Margin and Entropy remains consistent, underscoring their limitations across different few-shot frameworks.
 - **REPRE's Strength with Weak Learners:** Notably, within the "weaker" Linear Probe framework, REPRE is the only method capable of matching or surpassing zero-shot CLIP accuracy, particularly at extremely limited training examples (1 or 2 shots). This highlights REPRE's unique efficiency in leveraging minimal data by selecting highly representative examples.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Montecarlo's Varied Behavior

- **Dataset Generality Impact:** The paper provides an ablation study (Table 2) suggesting that Montecarlo's effectiveness is sensitive to the "generality" of the dataset, particularly when combined with MaPLe.
- **Higher Similarity = Better Montecarlo Performance:** Montecarlo performs better on datasets with higher cosine similarity among examples (i.e., less diverse or more specific datasets). This might be because Montecarlo, by focusing on unfamiliar examples, is more effective when the overall dataset has higher internal similarity, allowing it to pinpoint subtle nuances more effectively.
- **MaPLe's Role:** The authors hypothesize that this variance is due to MaPLe's unique approach of simultaneously tuning *both visual and text prompts*, whereas CoOp only adjusts the text prompt. The internal characteristics of image data thus have a different impact.

#### 4.5. What Makes Good Few-shot Examples? (Inferred Principles)

Based on their findings, the authors infer that good few-shot examples for VL models are characterized by:
1. **Decoupling from Model Prediction:** Effective selection methods should not heavily rely on the initial model's potentially inaccurate predictions (as AL methods do). Data-centric characteristics are more reliable.
2. **Unfamiliarity and Diversity:** Examples that introduce new or diverse concepts, or are "unfamiliar" to the pre-trained model (as identified by Montecarlo), are highly valuable for filling knowledge gaps.
3. **Representativeness:** Examples that are highly characteristic of their class, encapsulating its essential features (as identified by REPRE), are crucial for providing a comprehensive overview with limited data.
4. **Synergy with Frameworks:** The optimal selection method can depend on the specific few-shot learning framework used (e.g., Montecarlo for CoOp, REPRE for MaPLe/Linear Probe).

In summary, the research conclusively demonstrates that strategic example selection is vital for few-shot VL learning, with proposed methods significantly outperforming traditional and active learning approaches, while also providing valuable insights into the properties of effective few-shot data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the field of Vision-Language (VL) models and few-shot learning, impacting both fundamental understanding and practical applications.

1. **Fundamental Shift in Few-Shot Learning Paradigm:** By empirically demonstrating the substantial dependence of few-shot learning outcomes on example selection, the paper fundamentally challenges the previously common, unexamined practice of random sampling. It elevates instance selection from a secondary detail to a critical component of the few-shot learning pipeline for VL models. This encourages researchers to consider data curation as seriously as model architecture or prompt optimization.

2. **Novel and Effective Methodologies:** The introduction of Gaussian Monte Carlo (Montecarlo) and Representativeness (REPRE) provides concrete, robust, and empirically validated strategies for selecting high-quality few-shot examples. These methods are shown to significantly outperform both random selection and traditional active learning approaches, offering powerful tools for improving model performance in data-scarce environments. Their model-agnostic nature further broadens their applicability across various few-shot training methodologies.

3. **Critique of Active Learning in Few-Shot Contexts:** The consistent failure of classic uncertainty-based Active Learning (AL) methods (Entropy, Margin of Confidence) in few-shot VL settings is a crucial finding. It highlights that AL strategies, while effective in other contexts, cannot be naively applied to few-shot scenarios where initial model performance is inherently weak. This insight is vital for guiding future research in active learning, prompting the development of AL strategies specifically designed for low-data regimes and the unique challenges of multi-modal models.

4. **Practical Benefits for Real-World Deployment:**
 - **Reduced Annotation Costs:** By enabling more effective learning from fewer examples, the proposed methods can significantly reduce the need for extensive human annotation, which is often a major bottleneck and cost factor in developing AI systems.
 - **Improved Model Reliability:** Overcoming the high variability introduced by random sampling leads to more stable and predictable model performance, enhancing the reliability of VL models in downstream applications.
 - **Enhanced Performance in Resource-Constrained Settings:** For domains with inherently limited data (e.g., rare diseases, specialized industrial applications), these methods offer a pathway to leverage the power of pre-trained VL models effectively.

5. **Deeper Understanding of Informative Examples:** The analysis, particularly regarding the differing performance of Montecarlo with CoOp vs. MaPLe and the insights into why AL fails, contributes to a deeper theoretical understanding of "what makes good few-shot examples." It suggests that informative examples are those that are "unfamiliar" to the model or "representative" of the data distribution, rather than simply those the model is most "uncertain" about. It also highlights the complex interplay between data characteristics, selection strategy, and the specific few-shot learning framework used.

In conclusion, this paper not only presents innovative solutions to a critical practical problem in few-shot VL learning but also stimulates a re-evaluation of fundamental assumptions in the field. Its findings pave the way for more efficient, robust, and cost-effective deployment of powerful vision-language models across a diverse range of applications.
