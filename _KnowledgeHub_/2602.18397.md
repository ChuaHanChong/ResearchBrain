# How Fast Can I Run My VLA? Demystifying VLA Inference Performance with VLA-Perf

> [!summary] Summary
> NVIDIA Research developed VLA-Perf, an analytical, roofline-based performance model, to systematically study the inference performance of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models across various architectures and deployment systems. This work provides 15 key takeaways, guiding the design of future VLA models and systems to meet real-time robotics requirements, achieving up to 314 Hz for small models on datacenter GPUs and demonstrating significant performance advantages for server-side diffusion VLAs.

## Problem

- Deploying sophisticated Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on real-world robots is challenging due to stringent real-time inference constraints (e.g., 10-100 Hz).
- A comprehensive understanding of VLA inference performance is lacking, given the vast combinatorial space of model architectures, scaling factors, and inference system configurations.
- Prior work on VLA inference efficiency often targets specific model-system pairings, leading to a fragmented view without systematic guidance for design and deployment.

## Method

- Introduced **VLA-Perf**, an open-source, analytical, ==roofline-based performance model==, to efficiently analyze VLA inference latency across a wide range of model and system parameters.
- Modeled VLA inference as a sequence of components (==Vision Encoder==, ==VLM Backbone==, ==Action Expert==) and data transfers, calculating operator latencies based on ==compute or memory bounds==.
- Validated VLA-Perf against a ==Triton-based== implementation of the **π0** model, demonstrating **73.3% to 82.6%** fidelity to real-world performance for coarse-grained analysis.

## Results

- Datacenter GPUs (A100, H100, B100) achieve **61-314 Hz** inference for models like π0, exceeding typical camera frame rates, while edge GPUs (Jetson Thor) are limited to **19 Hz**.
- Datacenter GPUs (B100) can support real-time inference (**9.6 Hz**) for VLA models an order of magnitude larger (e.g., **81B parameters**) and long-context VLAs with up to **1,000** past timesteps (**11.7 Hz**).
- Diffusion-based VLA inference is **1-2 orders of magnitude** faster (e.g., **102x** for default π0) than vanilla autoregressive VLA inference when action chunking is used, while action chunk size itself has a negligible impact on performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action prediction in VLAs is primarily memory-bound, while vision and VLM inference are often compute-bound on powerful GPUs, highlighting distinct bottlenecks.
> - Server-side inference, even with consumer GPUs and wireless networks, generally outperforms on-device solutions, underscoring the importance of powerful external compute.
> - Diffusion-based VLA inference is significantly faster than vanilla autoregressive methods for action chunking, and asynchronous inference can substantially boost effective throughput for server-side deployments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied AI, enabling physical agents to perceive, reason, and act in the real world, is rapidly advancing, with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models at its forefront. These models integrate visual perception and language understanding to generate actions, demonstrating impressive capabilities in general-purpose manipulation tasks (e.g., RT series, OpenVLA, π0, GR00T). However, deploying these sophisticated VLA models on real-world robots introduces a critical challenge: meeting strict real-time inference constraints. Robots must react to dynamic environments with low latency, typically requiring inference frequencies from 10 Hz to over 100 Hz.

The broader research landscape has seen various efforts to improve VLA inference efficiency. These include model-level optimizations like adopting smaller models, quantization, layer skipping, reducing denoising steps in diffusion models, and employing asynchronous inference or dual-system designs. On the system side, advancements in hardware (from edge to datacenter GPUs) and software optimizations (e.g., CUDA graphs, operator fusion) also contribute to efficiency.

Despite these individual advancements, a significant research gap exists: a comprehensive understanding of the *VLA inference performance landscape*. This landscape is characterized by a vast combinatorial space of potential model architectures (e.g., scale, context, architectural choices) and inference systems (e.g., accelerator type, deployment location, network configuration). Prior work often targets specific model-system pairings, leading to a fragmented view. The sheer number of possible combinations makes exhaustive empirical evaluation prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.

This paper addresses this gap by providing the first systematic study of VLA inference performance. It draws parallels with similar challenges faced in Large Language Model (LLM) inference, where analytical performance models have proven effective. By developing `VLA-Perf`, an analytical modeling tool, the authors aim to move beyond ad-hoc optimizations to provide a principled, holistic understanding of the interplay between VLA model design and deployment strategies. This work is crucial for enabling the practical deployment of next-generation VLA models in real-world robotics, pushing embodied AI from lab demonstrations to robust, real-time applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The fundamental research question guiding this study is: **"How should we design future VLA models and systems to support real-time inference?"**

The motivation stems from the stringent real-time requirements of embodied AI, where VLA models must provide actions within tens of milliseconds to match camera frame rates (24-60 Hz). An acceptable inference frequency is defined as 10 Hz, with 100 Hz considered high-performance. The current fragmented understanding of VLA inference performance, due to the enormous number of possible model-system combinations, makes it difficult for researchers and practitioners to make informed design and deployment decisions.

To comprehensively address the central question, the authors break it down into two primary sets of objectives:

1. **From the perspective of VLA models:**
 - To understand how model scaling (size) impacts real-time inference capabilities.
 - To assess the practical feasibility of long-context VLAs that incorporate thousands of past visual frames.
 - To compare the inference performance of different action expert architectures (autoregressive vs. diffusion-based).
 - To analyze the influence of configurable parameters such as the number of denoising steps (for diffusion models) and action chunk size.
 - To quantify the performance gains achievable through asynchronous inference and dual-system VLA pipelines.

2. **From a systems deployment perspective:**
 - To determine the optimal inference execution location: on-device, on edge servers, or in the cloud.
 - To evaluate the necessary hardware capabilities (different GPU types) to meet real-time performance.
 - To assess the criticality of network performance in server-side inference systems.
 - To identify specific combinations of models and systems required to support VLA inference at target rates (10 Hz up to 100 Hz).

To achieve these objectives, the authors introduce `VLA-Perf`, an analytical performance model designed to efficiently analyze the vast combinatorial space. The ultimate goal is to distill 15 key takeaways from their comprehensive evaluation, providing practical and actionable guidance for the design of future VLA models and their supporting inference systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology adopted in this paper is a **modeling-based approach to performance analysis**, specifically using an **analytical, roofline-based performance model** called `VLA-Perf`. This approach is chosen to overcome the prohibitive cost and time of empirical evaluation across the nearly unbounded combinatorial space of VLA models and inference systems.

**VLA-Perf Overview:**
`VLA-Perf` abstracts VLA inference as a sequence of model components (Vision Encoder, VLM Backbone, Action Expert) interleaved with data transfers. It considers that depending on the placement of these components (on the robot or a server), data is exchanged either locally or over a network. Each model component is further broken down into a sequence of operators (e.g., FC layers, attention blocks).

**Input Parameters:**
`VLA-Perf` is highly configurable, taking detailed parameters for both models and systems:
- **Model side:** Vision encoder, VLM backbone, action expert choice; input/output sequence lengths; number of denoising steps; action chunk size; action dimensionality.
- **System side:** Accelerator specifications (peak FLOP/s, memory bandwidth, memory capacity); network configurations (upload/download bandwidth, base latency, efficiency).

**Latency Calculation (Roofline Model):**
The end-to-end inference latency ($T_{total}$) is modeled as the sum of all model inference latencies ($T_m$) and data movement latencies ($T_d$):
$T_{total} = \sum_{m \in M} T_m + \sum_{d \in D} T_d$

For each model component, its latency ($T_m$) is the sum of its constituent operator latencies ($T_o$). Each operator's latency is calculated using a roofline model:
$T_o = \max \left( \frac{\text{FLOPs}_o}{\text{FLOP/s}_h}, \frac{\text{Bytes}_o}{\text{MemBW}_h} \right)$
This formula determines whether an operator's execution is compute-bound (limited by FLOP/s) or memory-bound (limited by memory bandwidth).

Network-based data movement latency ($T_{net\_d}$) is modeled as:
$T_{net\_d} = \text{NetLat} + \frac{\text{Bytes}_d}{\text{NetBW}}$

**Assumptions and Fidelity:**
- Local data movement on the same accelerator is assumed negligible.
- Each model component runs on a single accelerator (modern GPUs offer sufficient memory).
- `VLA-Perf` estimates the *upper bound* of achievable performance by assuming optimistic software implementations.
- **Validation:** The model's fidelity is validated against an optimized Triton-based implementation of the π0 model on an RTX 4090, showing 73.3% to 82.6% of real-world performance. The authors argue that this level of fidelity is sufficient for providing valuable coarse-grained insights into the VLA performance landscape, even if it doesn't capture all hardware/software overheads.

**Evaluation Setup:**
- **Base Model:** π0 architecture (400M SigLIP vision encoder, 2B Gemma VLM, 300M diffusion action expert).
- **Robot Context:** Bimanual manipulation with three 224x224 cameras, 14 DoF action space, resulting in 800 total input tokens (768 visual + 32 language). Default action chunk size 50, 10 denoising steps.
- **Hardware:** NVIDIA Jetson Thor (edge), RTX 4090 (consumer), A100, H100, and B100 (datacenter GPUs). (See Table 10 for detailed specs).
- **Inference Systems/Placement:** On-device, edge server, and cloud server configurations.
- **Network Configurations:** Wired (Ethernet 1G, 10G), Wireless (WiFi 6, WiFi 7, 4G, 5G), and Cloud-specific (Slow Cloud, Fast Cloud). (See Table 7 for detailed specs).
- **Performance Metrics:** End-to-end latency (milliseconds) and throughput (Hertz) for batch size one.

This comprehensive approach allows the authors to systematically investigate various model and system knobs, providing a structured understanding of VLA inference performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study distills 15 key takeaways from its comprehensive evaluation, offering practical guidance for VLA design and deployment:

**Baseline Performance (π0 model):**
- **Takeaway 1:** Datacenter GPUs (A100, H100, B100) achieve inference frequencies (61-314 Hz) comparable to or exceeding camera frame rates for small VLA models like π0. Edge GPUs (Jetson Thor) remain performance-limited (19 Hz).
- **Takeaway 2:** Action prediction is primarily memory-bound across all hardware, while vision and VLM inference are compute-bound on most GPUs. Jetson Thor, with its lower-bandwidth LPDDR memory, sees even vision encoder and VLM inference become memory-bound.

**Model Design Aspects:**
- **Takeaway 3:** Inference latency for each VLA component scales approximately linearly with increasing model sizes.
- **Takeaway 4:** While edge and consumer GPUs struggle with larger models (e.g., RTX 4090 runs out of memory for >16.7B parameters), datacenter GPUs (B100) can support real-time inference (9.6 Hz) for VLA models more than an order of magnitude larger (e.g., 81B parameters).
- **Takeaway 5 (Long-Context):** Datacenter GPUs can support real-time long-context VLA inference with up to 1,000 past timesteps (11.7 Hz). Edge and consumer GPUs are limited to roughly 100 timesteps for real-time operation. Beyond 1,000 steps, KV cache size becomes a prohibitive memory and latency factor.
- **Takeaway 6 (Denoising Steps & Action Chunk Size):** The number of denoising steps significantly impacts action expert and end-to-end VLA latency (e.g., 5x steps leads to 2.15x VLA latency). Conversely, action chunk size has a negligible effect on performance, as the action expert remains memory-bound regardless of chunk size.
- **Takeaway 7 (Diffusion vs. Autoregressive with Chunking):** Diffusion-based VLA inference is one to two orders of magnitude faster than vanilla autoregressive VLA inference when action chunking is employed (e.g., 102x faster for default π0).
- **Takeaway 8 (Autoregressive Competitiveness):** Autoregressive VLAs are competitive only when generating a small number of action tokens (e.g., low DoF without chunking) or when parallel decoding is enabled. However, parallel decoding can become compute-bound for larger action chunk sizes, making diffusion more stable.

**System Deployment Aspects:**
- **Takeaway 9 (On-Device vs. Server-Side):** Server-side inference, even with consumer-grade GPUs (RTX 4090) and wireless networks (WiFi), significantly outperforms on-device inference (Jetson Thor) in most scenarios. On-device becomes preferable only under extremely poor network conditions (e.g., 4G cellular or distant cloud servers).
- **Takeaway 10 (Device-Server Collaboration):** Collaboratively splitting VLA inference between device and server is generally slower than both device-only and server-only solutions. The overhead of transferring KV cache between devices, especially over wireless networks, often negates any potential benefits.
- **Takeaway 11 (Asynchronous Inference):** Asynchrony between robot execution and inference can significantly improve system throughput for server-side inference, particularly under slow wireless network connections (speedups of 2.6x to 7.3x). While it doesn't reduce end-to-end latency, it allows for higher effective action frequencies by overlapping computation and network transmission.
- **Takeaway 12 (Dual-System Pipelines):** Asynchronous inference in dual-system VLA pipelines (System 1 for fast action, System 2 for slower VLM reasoning) can improve action prediction performance. Performance gains are strongly dependent on hardware capability and network latency, ranging from moderate (1.3-1.4x on Jetson Thor) to substantial (2.2x on B100 with fast Ethernet) but diminish with slow networks.

**Achieving Performance Targets (10 Hz & 100 Hz):**
- **Takeaway 13 (On-Device):** The most advanced edge GPUs (Jetson Thor) can achieve 10 Hz inference for π0. However, reaching 100 Hz requires substantial model-level adjustments (e.g., reducing model size, fewer diffusion steps, quantization).
- **Takeaway 14 (Edge-Server):** 10 Hz inference is achievable with consumer GPUs (RTX 4090) even with wireless networks (4G). Achieving 100 Hz, however, demands datacenter-class GPUs (B100) and faster networks (wired Ethernet or high-quality WiFi 7).
- **Takeaway 15 (Cloud-Server):** 10 Hz is feasible with good networking conditions. For 100 Hz, synchronous cloud inference is often limited by network latency (>10 ms per transfer), making asynchronous inference generally necessary for high-frequency operation. Asynchronous execution can restore acceptable performance (50.5 Hz) even under poor network conditions (e.g., 4G+slow cloud).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper presents the first comprehensive and systematic study of VLA inference performance, addressing a critical bottleneck in the real-world deployment of embodied AI. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Foundational Performance Analysis Tool:** The introduction of `VLA-Perf` is a major contribution. By providing an open-source, analytical, roofline-based performance model, the authors offer a powerful tool that enables researchers and engineers to rapidly explore a vast combinatorial space of VLA models and inference systems. This eliminates the need for expensive and time-consuming empirical setups for initial performance assessments, significantly accelerating the design and optimization cycle for future VLA systems.

2. **Practical Guidance for VLA Design:** The 15 key takeaways provide concrete, actionable insights for VLA model developers. They clarify how architectural choices (diffusion vs. autoregressive), scaling strategies, context management, and hyperparameter tuning (denoising steps, action chunk size) directly influence real-time inference capabilities. This guidance can lead to the creation of more efficient VLA models that are intrinsically designed with deployability in mind, rather than treating performance as a post-hoc optimization.

3. **Informed System Deployment Strategies:** The study offers invaluable insights for system architects responsible for deploying VLA models. It rigorously evaluates the trade-offs between on-device, edge-server, and cloud-server deployments, highlighting the critical roles of hardware capability and network performance. This guidance will help in selecting appropriate hardware accelerators (from edge to datacenter GPUs), designing robust network infrastructures, and making informed decisions about synchronous versus asynchronous inference strategies for different operational environments.

4. **Identification of Key Bottlenecks and Future Research Directions:** The paper clearly identifies memory bandwidth as a dominant bottleneck for action prediction and network latency as a major impediment for server-side and cloud-based deployments, especially for synchronous inference. These findings point towards crucial areas for future hardware-software co-design research, such as developing more memory-efficient VLA architectures, specialized accelerators for memory-bound workloads, and network-aware inference scheduling. The paper also acknowledges its limitations, opening avenues for future work in other embodied AI domains (e.g., autonomous driving, drones) and integrating robot execution and sensor latency into a more complete end-to-end system analysis.

In essence, this work serves as a vital blueprint for bridging the gap between impressive VLA model capabilities and their practical realization in real-world robotic systems. By demystifying the complex interplay of model architecture, hardware, and network, it empowers the embodied AI community to build more performant, reliable, and deployable intelligent agents.
