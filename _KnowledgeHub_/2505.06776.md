# FALCON: Learning Force-Adaptive Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed FALCON, a deep reinforcement learning framework that allows humanoid robots to perform robust loco-manipulation while adapting to significant 3D end-effector forces. It demonstrated up to a 2x improvement in upper-body tracking accuracy in simulation and successfully enabled physical robots to pull carts with 100N force and transport payloads up to 1.2kg per hand.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face challenges in achieving robust and precise whole-body control when subjected to significant, dynamic, and multi-directional 3D end-effector forces.
- Current deep reinforcement learning approaches for humanoids are either kinematically limited in force compensation or suffer from training inefficiency and entangled control objectives across the whole body.
- Real-world humanoid tasks involving forceful interaction, such as opening heavy doors or carrying variable payloads, demand dynamic adaptation to unknown forces without compromising stability or precision.

## Method

![[2505.06776-method.png]]
*Overview of FALCON. (a) Two agents with different sub-tasks are jointly trained with shared whole-body proprioception. During training, we apply 3D external forces bounded by upperbody joint torque limits on the end-effectors; (b) FALCON is deployed with either teleoperation or an autonomy pipeline including FoundationPose for pose estimation and motion planning*

- A dual-agent ==reinforcement learning== framework that decomposes whole-body control into specialized lower-body locomotion and upper-body manipulation agents, jointly trained with shared proprioceptive observations.
- A novel torque-limit-aware 3D force curriculum that calculates and progressively applies physically feasible external forces on end-effectors, respecting joint torque limits.
- Asymmetric actor-critic training, utilizing privileged information for critics, combined with extensive domain randomization to enhance training efficiency, robustness, and sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- FALCON achieved superior performance in simulation, reducing upper-body tracking errors to 0.37 under large forces, a ~**2x** improvement over the best baseline's 0.60 error.
- Policies trained in simulation successfully deployed on Unitree G1 and Booster T1 humanoids, performing tasks such as transporting up to 1.2kg payloads per hand and pulling carts with up to 100N longitudinal force.
- The framework exhibited faster training convergence and more stable exploration compared to monolithic whole-body RL, resulting in robust loco-manipulation with lower tracking errors and better postural stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing humanoid whole-body control into specialized yet jointly trained lower and upper-body reinforcement learning agents significantly improves training efficiency and coordinated force adaptation.
> - A curriculum that proactively accounts for and respects robot joint torque limits during force application is vital for learning policies that are both performant and safe for real-world deployment.
> - Shared whole-body proprioceptive observations enable specialized agents to develop mutual awareness, leading to robust whole-body compensation and stability even under substantial external forces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical and persistent challenge in humanoid robotics: achieving robust and precise whole-body control under significant, dynamic, and multi-directional 3D end-effector (EE) force interaction. Humanoid robots have made impressive strides in both locomotion and manipulation independently, but integrating these capabilities into "forceful loco-manipulation" tasks remains an open problem. Such tasks, ranging from opening heavy doors to transporting variable payloads or pulling carts, demand dynamic adaptation to unknown forces without compromising stability or precision.

Prior approaches to humanoid whole-body control largely fall into two main paradigms, each with significant limitations that FALCON aims to overcome:

1. **Lower-RL-Upper-IK (Reinforcement Learning for Lower Body, Inverse Kinematics for Upper Body):** This paradigm applies RL for stable locomotion of the lower body, while kinematic solvers (e.g., Inverse Kinematics, or IK) handle the upper body. While sample-efficient, these methods typically lack whole-body dynamics modeling necessary for forceful interactions. They suffer from delayed or insufficient force compensation in the upper body and limited whole-body coordination, making them unsuitable for tasks involving significant external forces.
2. **Monolithic-Whole-body-RL (Single Reinforcement Learning Policy for All Degrees of Freedom):** This approach attempts to learn control for all degrees of freedom (DoFs) simultaneously using a single policy. While offering potential for more expressive and coordinated behaviors, it faces severe challenges in training efficiency. The large action space and the often weakly correlated objectives of locomotion and manipulation lead to sample inefficiency, prolonged exploration, and a tendency for policies to overfit or exhibit behavioral dominance of either the upper or lower body.

Furthermore, while force adaptation has seen advances in quadrupedal robots (often with mounted arms), humanoids present unique and greater challenges. Their inherent instability (bipedal stance), higher complexity (more DoFs, coupled upper and lower body dynamics), and stricter joint torque limits (especially in wrist joints) make robust force adaptation significantly more difficult compared to quadrupeds with their typically centralized mass and greater stability margins. Existing model-based humanoid force control methods often require prior knowledge of object properties (mass, CoM) or predefined force trajectories, limiting their adaptability to unknown disturbances.

FALCON positions itself as a novel solution that transcends these limitations. By proposing a dual-agent RL architecture that jointly trains specialized lower- and upper-body controllers with shared observations, and critically, a *torque-limit-aware 3D force curriculum*, it directly addresses the shortcomings of existing paradigms. It seeks to combine the efficiency benefits of decomposition with the coordination benefits of whole-body learning, tailored specifically for the complexities of forceful humanoid interaction. This work contributes significantly to moving humanoid robots closer to practical deployment in dynamic, force-rich environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient reinforcement learning (RL) framework that enables humanoid robots to perform a diverse set of force-adaptive loco-manipulation tasks. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Unlocking Humanoid Potential:** Humanoid robots hold immense transformative potential for a wide range of applications, including daily service tasks (e.g., household assistance), industrial automation (e.g., warehouse logistics), and disaster response. However, this potential remains largely untapped because many real-world tasks involve significant physical interaction and forceful manipulation, which current humanoid systems struggle to achieve reliably.
2. **Addressing the Force Interaction Gap:** Many crucial tasks, such as opening stiff doors (a challenge highlighted in the DARPA Robotics Challenge), transporting heavy or unevenly weighted payloads, or pulling loaded carts, inherently require robust adaptation to unknown, dynamic, and multi-directional forces exerted on the robot's end-effectors. Traditional and existing RL methods are often limited to lightweight manipulation or struggle to maintain stability and precision under substantial external forces, creating a significant performance gap.
3. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing RL Paradigms:** As detailed in the broader research landscape, current RL approaches for humanoids (Lower-RL-Upper-IK and Monolithic-Whole-body-RL) exhibit critical shortcomings when faced with forceful interaction. Lower-RL-Upper-IK lacks dynamic compensation, while Monolithic-Whole-body-RL suffers from inefficient exploration and the risk of sub-optimal whole-body coordination. The motivation is to devise a more effective learning architecture that circumvents these issues.
4. **Ensuring Safety and Feasibility in Real-World Deployment:** Humanoid robots have strict physical constraints, particularly joint torque limits. Ignoring these limits during training with large external forces can lead to unsafe behaviors, damage to the robot, or failure in real-world deployment. A key motivation is to integrate these physical constraints into the learning process to ensure that learned policies are not only performant but also safe and feasible for physical robots.
5. **Achieving Cross-Platform Generalizability:** Developing robot-specific policies is time-consuming and resource-intensive. A significant motivation is to create a learning framework that can generalize across different humanoid platforms with varying morphologies and actuation systems, allowing for broader applicability and reduced development overhead.
6. **Enabling Autonomous Operation:** Beyond teleoperation, the ultimate goal for many humanoid applications is autonomous operation. The research is motivated by the need to develop policies that can be integrated into broader autonomous pipelines for complex tasks like logistics, where robots need to perceive, interact, and navigate their environment intelligently.

In essence, the authors are motivated to push the boundaries of humanoid capabilities by enabling them to robustly and precisely interact with the physical world under significant forces, paving the way for their broader adoption in practical, demanding applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FALCON (Force-Adaptive Humanoid Loco-Manipulation) proposes a dual-agent reinforcement learning (RL) framework designed to tackle the challenges of forceful humanoid loco-manipulation. Its core methodology revolves around a strategic decomposition of whole-body control, joint training, and a novel force curriculum.

#### 3.1. Dual-Agent Learning Framework

The central innovation is the decomposition of the complex whole-body control problem into two specialized, yet cooperatively trained, agents:
- **Lower-Body Locomotion Agent:** This agent is responsible for ensuring stable and robust locomotion of the robot's base and legs. Its policy ($\pi_l$) learns to generate lower-body actions ($a_l$) conditioned on whole-body proprioception and locomotion-specific goals (e.g., desired root linear/angular velocities, stance indicators, root height, waist yaw). Its objective is optimized via a locomotion-specific reward function ($R_l$).
- **Upper-Body Manipulation Agent:** This agent focuses on precise end-effector (EE) position tracking and implicitly adapting to external forces acting on the arms and hands. Its policy ($\pi_u$) learns to generate upper-body actions ($a_u$) conditioned on the same whole-body proprioception and manipulation-specific goals (e.g., target upper-body joint configurations). Its objective is optimized via a manipulation-specific reward function ($R_u$).

**Joint Training with Shared Observations:** Crucially, unlike completely decoupled architectures, these two agents are *jointly trained* within the same simulation environment. They share identical whole-body proprioceptive inputs ($s_p$) and commands. This shared observation mechanism is vital for enabling mutual awareness between the agents, allowing them to learn coordinated responses to external forces that affect the robot's full-body dynamics. The combined action from both agents, $a_t = [a_l; a_u]$, is then sent to a joint-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller.

**Asymmetric Actor-Critic Training:** To enhance training efficiency, an asymmetric actor-critic setup is employed. While the actors (policies $\pi_l, \pi_u$) only observe proprioceptive history during both training and deployment (reflecting partial observability in the real world), the critics (value functions $V_l, V_u$) have access to privileged information, such as root linear velocities and true EE forces, during training. This privileged information helps the critics accurately estimate state values, guiding policy optimization more effectively without requiring force sensors during real-world deployment. Both agents are optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

**Reward Design and Domain Randomization:** The reward functions ($R_l, R_u$) are tailored for their respective subtasks, incorporating specific terms for velocity tracking, height tracking, joint tracking, and various penalties for undesired behaviors (e.g., hip position, negative knee joints, unstable stance, ankle roll, torso orientation) that are crucial for robust sim-to-real transfer. Extensive domain randomization (e.g., varying friction, link mass, base mass, PD gains, control delays, and external pushes) is also applied during training to improve policy robustness and generalizability to real-world uncertainties.

#### 3.2. Torque-Limit-Aware 3D Force Curriculum

A key component enabling robust force adaptation is a carefully designed 3D force curriculum that progressively increases the magnitude of external forces during training while respecting the physical limits of the humanoid robot. This curriculum adheres to three principles:

1. **Torque-Aware Force Computation:** Before applying any external force, FALCON estimates the maximum and minimum admissible forces that can be exerted on each end-effector along Cartesian axes (x, y, z) without violating joint torque limits ($\tau_{lim}$). This is achieved by analyzing the end-effector Jacobian ($J_{EE}$), joint torque limits, and gravity compensation torques ($\tau_g$). The approach ensures that sampled forces are always physically feasible, preventing unrealistic training scenarios and promoting safer real-world deployment. The forces are sampled uniformly within this estimated, feasible range, with their relative ratios determined by a Dirichlet Distribution.
2. **Progressive Force Curriculum:** To facilitate gradual learning of force adaptation, the computed feasible EE forces are scaled by a global factor ($\alpha_g$), which progressively increases from 0 to 1 over the course of training. This allows the policy to first learn basic loco-manipulation skills in low-force regimes and then gradually adapt to larger disturbances. A low-pass filter is also applied to reduce force jitter.
3. **Position Randomization of Applied Force:** To enhance robustness against variations in contact points on the end-effectors, the exact point of force application is randomized along the EE link (from the wrist yaw to the distal segment). This forces the policy to learn to implicitly compensate for forces applied at different points, effectively improving its generalization without needing explicit force estimation or sensing in the real world.

The methodology combines a modular learning architecture with intelligent curriculum design, allowing for efficient, safe, and robust learning of complex force-adaptive behaviors for humanoid robots.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating FALCON's superior performance, training efficiency, and generalizability.

#### 4.1. Superior Loco-Manipulation Performance (Q1)

- **Reduced Tracking Errors:** In IsaacGym simulations on the Unitree Humanoid G1, FALCON consistently achieved the lowest tracking errors for both upper-body manipulation (E_upper_tracking) and root velocity (E_root_tracking) across all force levels (No-Force, Middle-Force, Large-Force).
 - Under Large-Force (αg = 1.0), FALCON reduced upper-body tracking error to 0.37, significantly outperforming the best baseline Lower-RL-Upper-IK variant (Upper-PID-Force-Curr. at 0.60) and Monolithic-Whole-body-RL-with-Force-Curr. (0.73). This represents approximately a 2x improvement in upper-body accuracy over prior methods.
 - Root velocity tracking errors remained robustly low (0.45 under Large-Force), indicating stable locomotion even under significant disturbances.
- **Effect of Force Curriculum:** While a force curriculum generally improved performance across all methods, FALCON benefited most, highlighting the effectiveness of its dual-agent structure in leveraging this training strategy.

#### 4.2. Enhanced Training Efficiency and Exploration (Q2)

- **Faster Convergence:** FALCON demonstrated faster and smoother decay of action noise standard deviation for both upper and lower body actions, indicating more efficient and stable exploration compared to Monolithic-Whole-body-RL (M-WB-RL). M-WB-RL suffered from prolonged noise due to entangled control objectives.
- **Improved Postural Stability:** FALCON achieved lower tracking errors in both upper-body joints and base angular velocity, with reward terms that fluctuated less. In contrast, M-WB-RL exhibited larger torso and ankle penalties, leading to unnatural bending and misalignment of the center of mass (CoM) due to excessive whole-body compensation, further validating FALCON's more stable learning process.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Torque-Limit-Aware 3D Force Curriculum

- **Feasible Force Scaling:** The torque-limit-aware curriculum enabled the force scale (αg) to progress effectively during training, reaching desired levels without premature saturation due to frequent joint torque limit violations.
- **Balanced Learning:** Compared to a curriculum that samples random forces without torque feasibility checks, the torque-limit-aware approach resulted in significantly better upper-body tracking errors, particularly under large forces. This indicates that respecting physical limits during training prevents the policy from overfitting to the locomotion objective and allows it to learn effective force compensation for the upper body.
- **Robustness to Force Range:** The results showed that FALCON's performance was robust even when trained with a wider, yet still torque-limit-aware, force clipping range, further emphasizing the curriculum's strength.

#### 4.4. Cross-Platform Generalizability and Real-World Deployment (Q3)

- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies trained purely in simulation using FALCON were successfully deployed on two different physical humanoid platforms: Unitree G1 and Booster T1. This was achieved with minimal (or no) reward or force curriculum modifications, showcasing strong cross-platform generalization.
- **Diverse Forceful Tasks:** FALCON enabled humanoids to perform challenging real-world tasks:
 - **Transporting Payloads:** Handling 0-20N vertical forces while maintaining stable locomotion and precise upper-body tracking.
 - **Cart-Pulling:** Resisting up to 100N longitudinal forces during walking.
 - **Door-Opening:** Adapting to 0-40N 3D forces while in a stance.
- **Real-World Tracking Accuracy:** In real-world payload transportation tasks (1.2kg per hand), FALCON achieved significantly lower tracking errors (E_upper_tracking: 0.39, E_root_tracking: 0.42) compared to Upper-PD-Force-Curr. (1.81, 0.40) and M-WB-RL-Force-Curr. (0.81, 0.58), confirming its superior performance in physical environments.
- **Autonomous Operation:** FALCON was successfully integrated into an autonomous pipeline for "tote logistics" on the Unitree G1, demonstrating capabilities like walking, picking up a tote of unknown weight (using FoundationPose for object pose estimation), and transporting it to a designated drop-off area.
- **Hardware Limits Acknowledged:** The authors transparently noted a limitation where real-world motors (e.g., Unitree G1 wrist joints) struggled with sustained high torques for heavy payload transport due to overheating, despite the policy's ability to handle the forces in simulation. This highlights a crucial gap between simulated torque limits and real-world thermal constraints.

In summary, FALCON represents a significant advancement in humanoid control, delivering robust, efficient, and generalizable force-adaptive loco-manipulation capabilities that push the boundaries for real-world humanoid applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FALCON represents a substantial leap forward in humanoid robotics, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Robust Forceful Interaction:** The most immediate impact of FALCON is its ability to enable humanoid robots to perform tasks requiring significant and dynamic 3D end-effector force interaction. This moves humanoids beyond lightweight manipulation and purely locomotor tasks into a realm where they can physically engage with their environment in meaningful ways, like opening heavy doors, carrying diverse payloads, or pushing/pulling objects. This capability is foundational for humanoids to operate effectively in unstructured, human-centric environments.

2. **Advancing RL for Humanoid Whole-Body Control:** FALCON introduces a novel and efficient dual-agent reinforcement learning paradigm for humanoid whole-body control. By strategically decomposing the learning problem into specialized but jointly trained lower- and upper-body agents, it sidesteps the limitations of both monolithic (inefficient exploration, entanglement) and strictly decoupled (lack of coordination, no force compensation) approaches. This hybrid architecture could serve as a blueprint for future RL-based humanoid control systems, providing a more scalable and robust learning framework.

3. **Enhancing Safety and Reliability:** The development of a torque-limit-aware 3D force curriculum is a crucial contribution to robot safety and reliability. By explicitly accounting for physical joint torque constraints during training, FALCON ensures that learned policies are feasible and less prone to causing self-damage or unsafe behaviors in real-world deployment. This meticulous approach to training in constrained environments is vital for the adoption of humanoids in industry and daily life.

4. **Promoting Cross-Platform Generalization:** The successful deployment of policies trained with FALCON on multiple physically different humanoid platforms (Unitree G1 and Booster T1) with minimal tuning demonstrates strong cross-platform generalizability. This significantly reduces the development overhead for deploying new humanoid robots or adapting existing ones to new tasks. It suggests that FALCON learns fundamental force-adaptive principles rather than robot-specific heuristics, accelerating the pace of humanoid robot development and deployment.

5. **Opening New Application Domains:** The demonstrated tasks—transporting payloads, cart-pulling, and door-opening—are highly relevant to various real-world applications:
 - **Logistics and Warehousing:** The autonomous tote logistics demonstration highlights FALCON's potential for automating material handling, packaging, and order fulfillment in warehouses, where humanoids could navigate dynamic environments and interact with objects of varying weights.
 - **Service Robotics:** Humanoids capable of forceful interaction could assist in homes or workplaces, performing tasks like opening doors, moving furniture, or helping individuals with mobility challenges.
 - **Industrial Automation:** In manufacturing, humanoids could perform tasks requiring force exertion, such as assembly, tool operation, or part manipulation, especially in areas inaccessible or unsafe for humans.

6. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The paper's identified limitations—such as the focus solely on end-effector forces (neglecting multi-contact interactions) and external torques—point to exciting avenues for future research. FALCON provides a strong foundation upon which more complex force-adaptive and multi-contact interaction capabilities can be built, pushing the boundaries of humanoid intelligence and physicality.

In conclusion, FALCON signifies a pivotal step towards realizing the full potential of humanoid robots by endowing them with the critical ability to robustly and intelligently interact with the physical world through forceful loco-manipulation. Its robust methodology and impressive real-world performance promise to accelerate the transition of humanoids from research labs to practical, impactful applications.
