# Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction in Simulation to Real-World Transfer for In-hand Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Oklahoma State University, GAC R&D Center Silicon Valley, and Colorado School of Mines developed Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR) with a Deep Random Generator (DRG) to enable Deep Reinforcement Learning policies to gradually adapt from rich simulated sensor data to limited real-world sensing. This approach allows the actor policy to learn from comprehensive information initially and then systematically reduce its reliance on hard-to-extract features, achieving up to a 58% success rate in real-world in-hand object rotation tasks with an Allegro hand, outperforming baselines.

## Problem

- Dexterous in-hand manipulation requires complex DRL policies, often trained via Sim2Real transfer, but high-fidelity simulated sensing (e.g., numerous tactile sensors) is expensive and impractical to replicate in physical robots.
- Existing Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) methods use a fixed, one-step reduction of features from simulation to reality, leading to a non-adaptable actor-critic feature gap and suboptimal policy performance, requiring compromises between real-world setup effort and learning efficiency.
- Relying on high-dimensional inputs like images in DRL models increases computational burden, data requirements, and vulnerability to real-world environmental variations, further complicating Sim2Real transfer.

## Method

- **Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR):** A method to generate a stepwise feature reduction curriculum, which evaluates the importance of target features (e.g., tactile sensors) during early training using online metrics (e.g., activation number) to prioritize less critical features for removal.
- **Deep Random Generator (DRG):** A deep random neural network that replaces signals from reduced features with randomly sampled values from a normal distribution and reinitializes its weights each training epoch. This prevents the DRL model from forming new dependencies on constant values or retaining old dependencies on removed features.
- **Gradual Adaptation:** The actor policy starts training with a rich feature space (including hard-to-extract features) and progressively adapts to a more limited, real-world-accessible feature space through the CSR-defined curriculum, with DRG handling the feature removal process stably.

## Results

- The 2-step CSR + DRG configuration consistently achieved the highest task performance during training and exhibited the highest real-world success rate of **58%** on an Allegro hand block rotation task at a 10Hz control frequency, outperforming baseline AAC and alternative curriculum methods.
- CSR configurations without DRG (replacing features with zeros) performed considerably worse, struggling to recover after feature reduction steps, underscoring DRG's importance for stable training and dependency removal.
- The proposed CSR + DRG method achieved a higher number of successful tasks within a 30-second period compared to both OpenAI's and NYU-RL's related in-hand manipulation benchmarks, demonstrating its efficiency and high performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradual, curriculum-based reduction of features from the actor's observation space significantly improves learning efficiency and final task performance in Sim2Real transfer compared to abrupt, one-step reductions.
> - Using a Deep Random Generator to replace reduced feature signals with random noise is critical for maintaining training stability and effectively decoupling policy dependencies, as directly replacing them with zeros leads to severe performance degradation.
> - Feature importance can be effectively determined through online metrics, such as tactile sensor activation numbers, allowing for a principled and adaptive sequence of feature removal in the curriculum design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous in-hand manipulation is a fundamental capability for robots across various applications, including human-robot interaction, intelligent manufacturing, telemanipulation, and assisted living. However, this domain presents significant challenges due to the high degrees of freedom (DoFs) in control space and the complex interactions between the robot, object, and environment. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising approach to address these challenges, enabling robots to acquire control policies through exploration and exploitation within their environment.

A key technique for training DRL policies in robotics is Simulation-to-Real (Sim2Real) transfer. Sim2Real leverages the benefits of simulated environments, such as ease of customization, accelerated training through parallelization, and the provision of explicit, rich information (e.g., detailed tactile, depth, and thermal sensing) that is difficult or expensive to obtain in the real world. This rich information in simulation can expand the feature space available to a DRL policy, potentially enhancing task performance.

However, the reliance on such rich information in simulation introduces a significant practical challenge: replicating this extensive sensing capability in a physical robot is often difficult and costly. For example, a single BioTac tactile sensor for an Allegro hand costs approximately $15k, while advanced simulation platforms like OpenAI Gym can model hundreds of tactile sensors across an entire robotic hand, a setup currently impractical in reality. Furthermore, using high-dimensional inputs like images in DRL models necessitates additional computational layers for information extraction, increasing data requirements and training burdens. These high-dimensional data are also susceptible to real-world environmental variations (e.g., lighting, color, camera setup), which can compromise policy performance upon transfer.

To mitigate the observation gap between simulation and reality while still benefiting from rich simulated data, the Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) method has been widely adopted in Sim2Real research. In AAC, a critic observes explicit, rich information to facilitate learning, while the actor, which is ultimately deployed on the physical robot, observes only ambiguous or easily accessible information. This approach aims to reduce the implementation effort in the real world by transferring only the actor. Previous applications of AAC span various tasks, including robot arm control, deformable object manipulation, and soft robot locomotion, as well as dexterous in-hand manipulation with hands like the Shadow hand for tasks such as Rubik's Cube solving or block rotation.

Despite its advantages, current AAC implementations for Sim2Real typically employ an empirically defined, one-step feature reduction. This fixed "curtail" reduces features from the rich simulation space to the limited real-world accessible space in a single, predetermined step. This approach introduces a "non-adaptable actor-critic feature gap" and can lead to suboptimal actor performance. If the feature reduction is too small, it may still be challenging to set up the physical system. Conversely, if the reduction is too large, it can hinder training efficiency and performance. Consequently, researchers often must compromise between the effort required for feature extraction in the real world and the learning performance achieved in simulation. This existing limitation prevents the full maximization of benefits from the rich information available in simulation. The current work directly addresses this deficiency by proposing a curriculum-based, gradual feature reduction strategy to enhance adaptation and performance in Sim2Real transfer for in-hand manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the identified limitations within existing Sim2Real transfer methodologies, particularly in the context of dexterous in-hand manipulation using Deep Reinforcement Learning. While rich feature spaces in simulation offer substantial benefits for policy learning, the practical challenges and high costs associated with replicating these sensory capabilities in real-world robots remain a bottleneck. The current Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) approach, which uses a fixed, one-step empirical reduction of features from simulation to reality, necessitates a compromise between the effort of setting up a physical system and the learning efficiency and performance. This often results in a non-adaptable actor-critic feature gap and suboptimal policy performance, as the actor struggles to fully leverage the initial rich information or adapt smoothly to the significantly reduced real-world observation space.

Given this motivation, the core rationale underlying this work is to enable the actor policy to commence training with the same rich feature space available to the critic, and then systematically reduce the "hard-to-extract" features in a step-by-step manner. This gradual adaptation strategy is hypothesized to facilitate higher training performance initially by capitalizing on the comprehensive information in simulation, followed by a smoother and more effective adaptation to the more constrained feature space relevant for real-world deployment.

To realize this overarching goal, the research identifies two primary questions that need to be addressed:
1. **How to systematically decide which features to reduce at each step of the curriculum?** This question concerns the creation of an intelligent, performance-aware sequence for feature removal.
2. **How to perform this feature reduction during training without compromising the stability or efficiency of the learning process?** This question targets the mechanism by which features are effectively decoupled from the policy without introducing undesirable side effects.

Based on these motivations and questions, the key objectives and contributions of this work are explicitly outlined as follows:
- **Development of Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR):** To address the first question, a CSR method is developed. This method is designed to generate a stepwise feature reduction curriculum. It evaluates the importance of target features during the early training period, utilizing expert-defined or online metrics related to task performance. This curriculum specifies the sequence and timing of feature removal, aiming to enable the control policy to gradually adapt to a limited feature space while maintaining performance.
- **Development of Deep Random Generator (DRG):** To address the second question, a DRG method is developed. This method uses a deep random neural network to generate random signals that replace the signals from the reduced features. The objective of DRG is to eliminate existing dependencies between the policy's output and the features being removed, while simultaneously preventing the creation of new, unwanted dependencies on constant or zero values.
- **Integration of Low-Dimensional Input:** The work aims to incorporate low-dimensional inputs to reduce the overall model size and ease implementation efforts in the real world. This is achieved by explicitly improving data and learning efficiency, leveraging the benefits of a more focused feature set for the final actor policy.
- **Empirical Validation and Real-World Transfer:** The proposed CSR and DRG methods are validated extensively. This involves training the policies on the Nvidia Isaac Gym robotics simulation platform for an in-hand manipulation task using an Allegro robotic hand. Subsequently, the trained policy is transferred and tested on a physical Allegro hand in real-world experiments across multiple control scenarios, demonstrating the practical applicability and effectiveness of the approach.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the gap between information-rich simulations and sensing-constrained real-world robotics by providing a principled, adaptive mechanism for feature reduction that optimizes both learning performance and practical deployability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops a novel approach combining Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR) with a Deep Random Generator (DRG) to improve Sim2Real transfer for in-hand manipulation tasks.

**A. Modeling and Representation**

The in-hand manipulation task is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), characterized by a tuple [𝐴, 𝑆, 𝑇, 𝑅, 𝛾], representing the set of actions, states, state transition probability, reward function, and discount factor, respectively. In this work, the environment is assumed to be deterministic. A policy 𝜋(𝑠) dictates actions for a given state, and the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is employed to learn this policy. The policy 𝜋𝜃 is approximated by a Deep Neural Network (DNN), with 𝜃 representing its parameters. The objective function for PPO is given by 𝐿 = 𝐸̂𝑎𝑡, 𝑥𝑡∈𝐷[𝑙𝑜𝑔𝜋𝜃 (𝑎𝑡|𝑥𝑡)𝐴̂𝑡], where 𝐴̂𝑡 is the advantage function estimator, and 𝐷 is the set of past transitions.

The observable state space, 𝑥 ∈ 𝑆, encompasses various components:
- **Robot information:** Joint positions, velocities, and torques.
- **Object information:** Cartesian position and rotation (represented by a quaternion), linear and angular velocities.
- **Target information:** Target position for manipulation.
- **Tactile information:** This is explicitly identified as the target feature set for reduction in this work.

Both action and state spaces are normalized to a range of -1 to 1 to facilitate stable training.

**B. Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR)**

CSR is introduced to manage the gradual reduction of features for the actor during training. The core idea is that while both the actor and critic initially benefit from explicit, rich observation (including joint, object, and tactile information) in simulation, only the "easy-to-get" features (e.g., joint encoders, camera-tracked object position) should remain for the actor in the real world. "Hard-to-get" features, specifically tactile information in this study, are progressively removed.

The process of generating this curriculum involves:
1. **Feature Importance Evaluation:** The method identifies which features are less critical for task performance during early training. Instead of directly measuring impact by removal, which is computationally expensive, the work proposes empirically defined metrics based on expert interpretation. For instance, tactile sensors on fingertips might be deemed more important than those on the palm. In this specific research, an *online metric* based on the **activation number for each tactile sensor** is utilized: sensors with higher activation numbers are considered more important, indicating greater reliance by the policy during object interaction.
2. **Curriculum Generation:** Features are ranked based on their importance measure, 𝑔(𝑓). A curriculum 𝐶 = {𝑐1, … , 𝑐𝑚} is then generated, where each step 𝑐𝑚 specifies a set of features to be reduced, starting with the least important ones. The number of steps (𝑚) and features removed per step (𝑖) are empirically defined in this work.
3. **Trigger Mechanism:** Each curriculum step is activated dynamically during training. A performance threshold 𝜏, determined by the cumulative episode reward, triggers the reduction. If the episode reward surpasses 𝜏, CSR removes features according to the importance ranking.

**C. Deep Random Generator (DRG) for Decremental Feature Curriculum Learning**

After CSR selects features for reduction, the DRG addresses the challenge of removing these features without destabilizing the DRL model or compromising learned dependencies. The concern is that directly replacing feature signals with a constant value (e.g., zeros) might not completely sever input-output dependencies or could inadvertently create new, undesirable dependencies on that constant value, leading to performance degradation.

DRG is designed to counteract these issues through a two-step signal randomization process:
1. **Feature Signal Randomization:** The signals from the reduced features (𝑓) are replaced by randomly sampled values from a normal distribution: 𝑓~𝒩(𝛿, 𝜎2), where 𝛿 is the mean and 𝜎 is the standard deviation.
2. **Deep Random Layer Processing:** The newly randomized observation, 𝑥̂𝑡 = 𝔽(𝑥𝑡, 𝜑), which combines remaining original signals and the newly randomized signals, is then passed through a "deep random layer." The weights (𝜑) of this layer are reinitialized at the beginning of every training epoch using a Xavier normal distribution. This initialization strategy helps maintain the variance of the input observation (𝑥) and the randomized input (𝑥̂), preventing the DRL model from forming new dependencies on the randomized signals and effectively "nullifying" the influence of the removed features. The modified DRL objective function becomes 𝐿′ = 𝐸̂𝑎𝑡, 𝑥𝑡∈𝐷[𝑙𝑜𝑔𝜋𝜃 (𝑎𝑡|𝔽(𝑥𝑡, 𝜑))𝐴̂𝑡].

**D. Experiments**

**Task Design:**
- The system is evaluated on an in-hand manipulation task: rotating a block around its Z-axis on the palm of an Allegro robotic hand to reach a randomly generated target pose.
- The Allegro hand is a 4-finger, 16-DoF robot equipped with 13 tactile sensors (4 per finger, 1 on the palm).
- The Nvidia Isaac Gym platform is used for simulation, incorporating domain randomization.
- The initial actor observation space consists of 75 features, including joint positions, velocities, torques, object pose and velocities, target pose, and tactile information. The goal is to reduce 13 tactile sensor features, ending with 62 features for the actor.
- For real-world deployment, joint information is extracted from a ROS node. Object position and rotation are tracked by a Logitech C930e webcam (30Hz) using an Aruco code attached to the object, with a Kalman filter for denoising.
- A goal is considered achieved if the rotation difference is less than 0.1 rad during training and 0.4 rad during testing.
- Simulation runs at 60Hz, while the actor controls the Allegro hand at 30Hz to match the physical hand's speed limit.
- Training hardware: Intel 12900K, Nvidia RTX3080ti, 64 GB RAM. Hyperparameters are based on Isaac Gym defaults, with 8192 environments, 20000 total epochs, and a mini-batch size of 65536.

**Evaluation Metrics and Curriculum Configurations:**
- The curriculum trigger threshold 𝜏 is set to 2500 (cumulative episode reward).
- Four configurations are compared:
 1. **CSR (3 steps) + DRG:** Reduces 4, then 4, then 3 tactile features.
 2. **CSR (2 steps) + DRG:** Reduces 7, then 6 tactile features.
 3. **CSR (2 steps):** Same as (2), but features are replaced with zeros instead of DRG.
 4. **Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC):** Baseline, actor starts with no tactile features (one-step curtail).
- All configurations target the same final actor feature space (62 features) for fair comparison.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Learning efficiency:** Assesses training performance over a specified period.
 - **Task performance:** Measured by success rate over 100 trials in both simulation and real-world, with randomly generated initial and target poses. An episode length of 10 seconds is used, with continuous target generation upon success.
 - **Manipulation behavior:** Analyzed by varying control frequencies (30Hz, 10Hz, 5Hz) in the real world, counting successful rotations over 30 seconds, and comparing with existing literature.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted both in simulation and with a physical robot hand yielded several findings regarding the proposed Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR) and Deep Random Generator (DRG) methods.

**A. Training Process (Learning Efficiency)**

- **Overall Performance:** The 2-step CSR + DRG configuration consistently achieved the highest task performance during the designed training period, indicating the effectiveness of the proposed method in improving learning efficiency.
- **Comparison of CSR Steps:** Both 2-step and 3-step CSR + DRG methods exhibited similar initial learning efficiency and triggered their first feature reduction steps at comparable times. After the initial reduction, the 2-step CSR + DRG experienced a more significant, albeit temporary, performance drop due to reducing a larger number of features simultaneously. However, it subsequently recovered and triggered the second curriculum step around the same time as the 3-step CSR + DRG. This suggests that the feature importance measure accurately identified and removed less critical features, allowing the policy to adapt effectively. In later training stages, the 3-step CSR + DRG showed higher performance impact at its second and third reduction steps, possibly indicating that the policy had developed stronger dependencies on those features.
- **Effectiveness of DRG:** The 2-step CSR configuration without DRG (replacing features with zeros) demonstrated considerably poorer performance. It struggled to recover after the first curriculum step and experienced a substantial performance drop after the second step. This finding underscores the importance of DRG in completely removing dependencies on reduced features and preventing the creation of new ones, thus maintaining training stability and performance.
- **Baseline AAC Performance:** The baseline Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC), which starts training with the actor already having a reduced feature space (one-step curtailment), exhibited a lower initial performance and slower learning progression. Its final performance at the end of training was notably lower compared to the CSR + DRG methods. This suggests that allowing the actor to initially learn from a rich feature space, followed by gradual reduction, is more beneficial than an immediate, extensive reduction.
- **Feature Importance Observations:** A consistent pattern observed across all curriculum-based methods was that tactile features located closer to the lower phalanx of the fingers were reduced first. This implies that the learned manipulation policy predominantly relied on the tactile information from the fingertips, rendering the lower phalanx sensors less critical and therefore prioritized for early reduction.

**B. Task Performance (Success Rate)**

- **Simulation vs. Real-World Performance:** Across all configurations, a notable drop in success rate was observed when transferring policies from simulation to the real world. For instance, the 2-step CSR + DRG, which achieved a 96% success rate in simulation at 30Hz, saw its performance drop to 32% when tested at the same frequency on the physical robot. This discrepancy highlights the inherent challenges of Sim2Real transfer, including modeling inaccuracies between simulated and physical environments.
- **Optimal Real-World Control Frequency:** The control frequency significantly impacted real-world task performance. The 2-step CSR + DRG achieved its highest real-world success rate (58%) at a reduced control frequency of 10Hz. Performance decreased when operating at the original 30Hz (32% success) and further declined at 5Hz (38% success). This pattern was consistent across all evaluated configurations. Analysis of joint following ability revealed that 10Hz allowed the robot to best match target positions. At 30Hz, the servo motors' speed limitations prevented effective tracking of fast commands, while at 5Hz, the robot struggled to reach targets when the command position was too far from the current state within the allocated time.
- **Method Comparison:**
 - CSR (2 steps) + DRG consistently achieved the highest success rates in both simulation and real-world tests across all control frequencies.
 - CSR (3 steps) + DRG performed second best, with its performance slightly impacted by the final curriculum step.
 - CSR (2 steps) without DRG converged to a suboptimal policy, leading to poor performance in all tests, further confirming DRG's necessity.
 - The baseline AAC performed comparably to CSR (2 steps) without DRG, demonstrating lower overall task performance.

**C. Comparison with Literature**

The performance of CSR (2 steps) + DRG was compared against similar in-hand manipulation tasks from OpenAI and NYU-RL, using the average number of successful tasks within a 30-second period.
- **OpenAI:** Utilized AAC with a one-step curtail and image-based input, trained with extensive data on supercomputers for a more complex task (XYZ axis rotation with a 24-DoF Shadow Hand).
- **NYU-RL:** Employed Demonstration Augmented Policy Gradient with human demonstrations, using low-dimensional input similar to this work.
- The results indicated that CSR (2 steps) + DRG achieved a higher number of successful tasks compared to both OpenAI and NYU-RL. While OpenAI's task was arguably more difficult, its reliance on image-based input and extensive training resources contrasts with the low-dimensional input and more efficient training of CSR + DRG. NYU-RL's approach, learning from slowed-down human demonstrations for stability, resulted in fewer successes. This comparison suggests the promise of the proposed CSR + DRG method for efficient and high-performing dexterous manipulation.

**D. Manipulation Behavior Analysis between Simulation and Real World**

Analyzing the manipulation behaviors, particularly with the 10Hz CSR (2 steps) + DRG configuration, revealed differences between clockwise and counterclockwise rotations:
- **Clockwise Rotation:** The robot tended to use the thumb, index, and middle fingers or the index, middle, and ring fingers for rotation. This pattern was generally more challenging in the real world.
- **Counterclockwise Rotation:** The index and middle fingers performed periodic rotation movements, while the thumb and ring finger stabilized the object. This pattern was observed to be more stable and resulted in better performance in the real world.
- **Influence of Robot Hand Structure:** The Allegro hand used in the experiments is a right hand, which naturally showed superior performance in counterclockwise rotation. Clockwise rotation, often relying on impulses from edge fingers (thumb or ring), resulted in less stable object manipulation compared to the more controlled adjustments observed in counterclockwise rotation. This finding suggests that intrinsic robot hand structures and resulting manipulation behaviors significantly influence performance and Sim2Real transfer success, warranting further investigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method that addresses a notable challenge in the field of learning-based robotics, specifically in the context of Simulation-to-Real (Sim2Real) transfer for dexterous in-hand manipulation tasks. By proposing Curriculum-based Sensing Reduction (CSR) combined with a Deep Random Generator (DRG), the work offers a principled approach to navigate the trade-off between leveraging rich, idealized sensory information available in simulation and adapting to the limited, accessible features of real-world robotic systems.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Sim2Real Transfer Efficiency and Performance:** The proposed CSR + DRG framework demonstrably improves both the learning efficiency and the ultimate task performance of DRL policies in Sim2Real scenarios. By enabling the actor to initially train with the full, rich feature space and then gradually adapt to a reduced set, the method facilitates more robust learning and avoids the performance penalties associated with abrupt or sub-optimally defined feature curtailments inherent in conventional Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) approaches. This leads to higher success rates in physical robot tasks.
- **Reduced Real-World Implementation Burden:** A core impact is the alleviation of the costly and complex burden of replicating high-fidelity sensing systems in physical robots. By systematically reducing dependence on hard-to-extract features like numerous tactile sensors, the method allows for effective policy deployment on simpler, more cost-effective hardware configurations, making advanced robotic manipulation more accessible.
- **Improved Training Stability with Feature Reduction:** The Deep Random Generator (DRG) is a key innovation that ensures stability during the feature reduction process. By replacing reduced feature signals with random noise rather than fixed values (like zeros), DRG effectively decouples the policy's output from the removed inputs without inadvertently creating new, undesirable dependencies. This addresses a critical technical challenge in adaptive feature-space learning.
- **Guidance for Sensor Design and Deployment:** The finding that feature importance (e.g., fingertips over lower phalanx tactile sensors) can be dynamically determined and leveraged for curriculum generation provides valuable insights for future robotic sensor design and placement strategies. It suggests that resources can be optimally allocated to the most impactful sensors, rather than uniformly distributing them.
- **Broader Applicability in DRL Robotics:** While validated on an in-hand manipulation task with an Allegro hand, the core principles of curriculum-based sensing reduction and robust feature replacement could be transferable to other complex DRL robotics applications where rich simulation data contrasts with real-world sensing limitations (e.g., complex assembly, navigation in unstructured environments, human-robot collaboration).
- **Foundational for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future research, including the development of more sophisticated, automatic curriculum generation methods that do not rely on empirically defined steps or initial expert input. Further investigation into fine-tuning robot controllers to better match policy commands (e.g., optimizing acceleration curves) and deeper analysis of how robot morphology and manipulation behaviors influence Sim2Real transfer performance are also highlighted as important directions.

In summary, this research contributes a practical and effective methodology for bridging the simulation-to-reality gap in DRL for dexterous manipulation. It moves beyond fixed feature reduction strategies to enable adaptive learning that is both high-performing and pragmatically deployable on physical robots, thereby advancing the capabilities of autonomous systems in real-world environments.
