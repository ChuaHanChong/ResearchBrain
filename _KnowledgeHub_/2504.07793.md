# Revisiting Likelihood-Based Out-of-Distribution Detection by Modeling Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Linköping University developed a method to re-evaluate likelihood-based out-of-distribution (OOD) detection by applying score-based diffusion models to learned representation spaces from pre-trained encoders. This approach demonstrates that these methods can achieve state-of-the-art performance, with the class-conditional variant reaching an average AUROC of 96.35% and FPR95 of 17.06% on the ImageNet benchmark, challenging the previous perception of their ineffectiveness.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks exhibit unpredictable behavior on out-of-distribution (OOD) data, posing reliability and safety concerns in real-world applications.
- Traditional explicit likelihood-based generative models often failed at OOD detection in raw image space, assigning higher likelihoods to OOD data, leading the community to prioritize post-hoc methods.
- Many current state-of-the-art OOD detection methods rely on supervised training and labeled in-distribution data, limiting their applicability in label-scarce domains or with self-supervised foundation models.

## Method

- Introduces the **Representation Diffusion Model (RDM)**, which estimates data density using a ==score-based diffusion model== trained on learned representations from pre-trained image encoders, rather than raw pixel data.
- Leverages the precise ==likelihood computation== capabilities of score-based diffusion models via the ==probability flow ODE== formulation.
- Develops a class-conditional variant, **ConRDM**, which utilizes in-distribution labels to guide the diffusion model, further enhancing OOD detection performance by modeling class-specific likelihoods.

## Results

- The Representation Diffusion Model (RDM) achieved competitive label-free OOD detection, with an average AUROC of **93.16%** and FPR95 of **32.17%** on the ImageNet benchmark when paired with a DINOv2 encoder.
- ConRDM, the class-conditional variant, surpassed all benchmarked methods with supervised ViT encoders, achieving an average AUROC of **96.35%** and FPR95 of **17.06%** on the ImageNet OOD benchmark.
- The method demonstrated computational efficiency, with training taking approximately **12 minutes** on an RTX4090 GPU and likelihood estimation achieving a throughput of **1500 representations/sec**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The historical failures of likelihood-based OOD detection were not due to an inherent flaw in the likelihood principle but rather from applying it in raw pixel space, where low-level features obscure semantic shifts.
> - Likelihood functions effectively as an OOD score when applied to semantically clustered representation spaces generated by modern pre-trained encoders.
> - More accurate likelihood estimation in the representation space, as provided by score-based diffusion models, directly correlates with improved OOD detection performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is a critical area in machine learning, particularly for enhancing the reliability and safety of deep learning systems deployed in real-world, safety-critical applications. Deep neural networks often perform unpredictably or poorly when presented with data that deviates from their training distribution. OOD detection aims to identify such "unseen" data points, preventing unreliable inferences and potential negative consequences.

Historically, OOD detection was conceptualized around identifying data with low likelihood under a statistical model trained on in-distribution (ID) data. Explicit likelihood-based deep generative models (DGMs), such as autoregressive models and normalizing flows, appeared theoretically suitable for this task. However, practical applications demonstrated a significant challenge: these models often assigned higher likelihoods to OOD images than to ID images, a phenomenon observed with datasets like FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, MNIST, and SVHN. This counter-intuitive behavior led to a general perception that likelihood-based OOD detection was fundamentally flawed, particularly in the raw image space.

Due to these limitations, the OOD detection community largely shifted its focus to post-hoc methods. These methods typically derive OOD scores from pre-trained classifiers, often utilizing features from the penultimate layer, logits, or softmax probabilities. Examples include MSP, ODIN, Mahalanobis distance, Energy, ReAct, ViM, and Generalized Entropy. While widely adopted, a common dependency of these post-hoc methods is the requirement for supervised training and label information of the in-distribution data. This dependency poses a significant constraint in scenarios where acquiring annotations is expensive or infeasible, such as in medical imaging (e.g., histopathology data) or other fine-grained categories requiring expert knowledge. Furthermore, many of these methods are incompatible with self-supervised models, which are gaining prominence as foundational models for various vision tasks.

This research revisits likelihood-based OOD detection, positioning itself as an effort to challenge the prevailing view of its ineffectiveness. It seeks to bridge the gap by demonstrating that likelihood-based methods can perform comparably to, or even surpass, state-of-the-art post-hoc methods, particularly when applied in a semantically meaningful representation space rather than the raw pixel space. By leveraging modern, expressive generative models (score-based diffusion models) and advanced pre-trained encoders (both supervised and self-supervised), the work aims to re-establish likelihood as a viable and robust approach for OOD detection, especially in contexts where label information is unavailable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to reinvestigate likelihood-based out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and demonstrate its effectiveness by applying it in the representation spaces of modern pre-trained image encoders. The authors contend that the historical failures of likelihood-based methods are not due to an inherent flaw in the concept of likelihood itself, but rather stem from specific properties of the image space that hinder accurate OOD detection.

The motivations underpinning this research are multi-faceted:

- **Addressing the Perceived Flaw of Likelihood-Based Methods:** The work aims to demonstrate that likelihood is not inherently flawed as an OOD detection score. Previous research indicated that explicit likelihood-based generative models often assigned higher likelihoods to OOD data than to in-distribution (ID) samples, especially when operating directly on image pixels. The authors hypothesize that this issue arises because likelihood estimation in image space is heavily influenced by low-level features and background statistics, which do not necessarily correlate with semantic content. Images with distinct semantic meanings can be visually similar due to low-level features, while semantically similar images might appear distant in pixel space due to minor transformations. This makes it challenging for models to learn meaningful semantic boundaries based solely on image-level likelihood.
- **Leveraging Semantically Clustered Representation Spaces:** The central hypothesis is that likelihood functions effectively within semantically clustered representation spaces. Pre-trained image encoders, particularly those from modern deep learning architectures, are designed to transform raw pixel data into a lower-dimensional embedding where semantic similarities and differences are more pronounced. By performing likelihood estimation in this representation space, the model can potentially capture semantic shifts between ID and OOD data more accurately, circumventing the issues observed in pixel space.
- **Utilizing Advanced Likelihood Estimators:** To achieve accurate likelihood estimation in the representation space, the research leverages score-based diffusion models. These models are noted for their high expressiveness and strong performance in density estimation, offering a significant advantage over earlier generative models (e.g., autoregressive models or normalizing flows) that often struggled to provide sufficiently good likelihood estimates for effective OOD detection. The probability flow formulation of diffusion models allows for precise likelihood computation.
- **Enabling OOD Detection without Labeled Data:** Many current state-of-the-art OOD detection methods are post-hoc approaches that rely on supervised classifiers and, consequently, on the availability of labeled ID data. This dependency limits their applicability in scenarios where labels are expensive or impossible to obtain, such as in certain medical imaging domains or when utilizing self-supervised foundational models. A key motivation of this work is to develop a likelihood-based method that operates effectively with representations from self-supervised encoders, thereby expanding OOD detection capabilities to label-free settings.
- **Improving Computational Efficiency:** Estimating likelihood in a lower-dimensional representation space is generally more computationally efficient than processing high-dimensional raw image data. This aspect contributes to the practicality and scalability of the proposed method for large-scale benchmarks.
- **Guidance with Labeled Data (Optional Enhancement):** While aiming for label-free capabilities, the research also seeks to explore how existing ID class labels, when available, can further enhance the OOD detection performance of the likelihood model through class-conditional training (guidance) of the diffusion model.

In summary, the authors are motivated to demonstrate that by combining sophisticated pre-trained encoders with advanced likelihood estimators in a semantically rich representation space, likelihood-based OOD detection can be a highly competitive and versatile approach, overcoming its historical limitations and addressing critical needs in AI safety and trustworthy machine learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Representation Diffusion Model (RDM), re-frames likelihood-based OOD detection by estimating data density in the learned representation space of pre-trained encoders, rather than directly in the image space.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The work delineates two main setups for OOD detection:
- **OOD detection with ID labels:** This setup assumes a predefined set of semantic categories ($Y_{ID}$) for in-distribution data ($D_{ID}$). OOD data ($D_{OOD}$) belongs to semantic groups outside $Y_{ID}$. The goal is both accurate ID classification and identification of OOD inputs during inference.
- **OOD detection without ID labels:** In scenarios where annotated ID data is unavailable, a density model $p_{\theta}(x)$ is trained to approximate the true distribution of training inputs $p(x)$ using only $x \sim X_{ID}$. Inputs with sufficiently low density under $p_{\theta}(x)$ are classified as OOD.

#### 3.2. Pre-trained Encoders for Representation Extraction

Given an image dataset $\{x_i\}$, representations $\{z_i\}$ are calculated using a pre-trained encoder $E$, such that $z = E(x)$. The same encoder is used for both ID and OOD data, but only ID representations are utilized for training the likelihood estimator. The study employs a diverse set of encoders:
- **Supervised Encoders:** BiT-S-R101x1, RepVGG-b3, ResNet-50d, Swin-B, ViT-B/16, DeiT-B/16, all pre-trained on ImageNet-21K or ImageNet-1K with cross-entropy loss.
- **Self-Supervised Encoders:** MAE-B/16, DINO-B/16, DINOv2-B/14 (pre-trained on ImageNet-1K or LVD-142M), and specialized encoders for histopathology data: Pathology-SSL (ViT-S/16, trained on TCGA&TULIP) and Uni (ViT-L/16, trained on Mass-100K).

#### 3.3. OOD Detection with Representation Likelihood Estimation

The core of the method involves using a score-based diffusion model as the likelihood estimator. Unlike models that provide only a lower bound on likelihood (e.g., VAEs) or cannot compute it (e.g., GANs), score-based diffusion models, via the probability flow Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) formulation and instantaneous change of variables formula, allow for the exact or highly accurate computation of likelihood.
- **Representation Diffusion Model (RDM):** The diffusion model is trained exclusively on ID representations. The training objective approximates the score function $\nabla_z \log p_t(z)$, where $p_t(z)$ is the marginal distribution of $z$ at time $t$. The model is parameterized by a time-dependent Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with 12 residual blocks.
- **Likelihood Computation:** The representation likelihood $p_0(z)$ is computed using Equation (2):
 $\log p_0(z(0)) = \log p_1(z(1)) + \int_0^1 \nabla \cdot f_{\theta}(z(t), t)dt$
 where $f_{\theta}(z,t)$ is a learned drift function incorporating the approximated score function. Hutchinson’s trace estimator is used to approximate the divergence term $\nabla \cdot f_{\theta}(z(t), t)$.
- **OOD Decision:** After training, the likelihood $\log p_0(z)$ is calculated for both ID and OOD test representations. An input image $x$ is classified as OOD if its representation's likelihood $\log p_0(z)$ falls below a predefined threshold $\lambda$, otherwise it is classified as ID. The expectation is that $\log p_0(z_{ID}) > \log p_0(z_{OOD})$.

#### 3.4. Leveraging Labeled In-Distribution Data (ConRDM)

When ID labels are available, the method is extended to a class-conditional Representation Diffusion Model (ConRDM).
- **Conditional Likelihood:** The model learns a class-conditioned diffusion score function $s_{\theta}(z, c, t)$ to estimate $p(z|c)$, where $c$ is the class label.
- **Class Conditioning:** Inspired by classifier-free guidance, the class $c$ (obtained from the encoder's classifier prediction, $c = \text{arg max } Wz + b$) is provided as an additional input to the neural network parameterizing the score function. This allows the diffusion model to assign higher likelihoods to regions corresponding to high-confidence ID classes in the representation space.

#### 3.5. Experimental Settings

- **Datasets:** Large-scale OOD detection benchmark with ImageNet-1K as ID, and OpenImage-O, Texture, iNaturalist, ImageNet-O as OOD datasets. A histopathology benchmark using PatchCamelyon (PCam) with non-tumor images as ID and tumor images as OOD.
- **Metrics:** Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) and False Positive Rate at 95% True Positive Rate (FPR95).
- **RDM Training Details:** Trained with AdamW optimizer, batch size 4096, learning rate 2e-3, cosine annealing, gradient clipping. Default sub-VP SDE formulation.
- **Likelihood Estimation Details:** RK45 ODE numerical integrators (torchdiffeq) with `atol=1e-5` and `rtol=1e-5`, Skilling-Hutchinson trace estimator for divergence.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Training takes approximately 12 minutes on an RTX4090 GPU, with likelihood estimation throughput of 1500 representations per second.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of likelihood-based OOD detection when applied to learned representation spaces, challenging previous conclusions drawn from its performance in pixel space.

#### 4.1. Motivating Observations

Initial experiments confirmed that a diffusion model trained directly on CIFAR-10 images assigned higher negative log-likelihood (lower likelihood) to OOD SVHN images, indicating its limitations in image space OOD detection. In contrast, when the same diffusion model was trained on representations extracted from a ResNet18 encoder (pre-trained on CIFAR-10), the negative log-likelihood distribution showed better separation between ID CIFAR-10 and OOD SVHN, suggesting enhanced effectiveness in the representation space.

#### 4.2. Evaluation with Self-Supervised Encoders

For scenarios without ID label information, the Representation Diffusion Model (RDM) was evaluated against label-free methods like KNN and Residual (without offset) using MAE, DINO, and DINOv2 encoders on the ImageNet benchmark.
- **ImageNet Benchmark (Table 2):** RDM demonstrated competitive performance. When paired with DINOv2, which was the highest-performing encoder, RDM achieved an average AUROC of 93.16% and FPR95 of 32.17%, comparable to KNN (AUROC 93.10%, FPR95 30.50%) and Residual (AUROC 92.19%, FPR95 35.26%). The results indicated that the choice of encoder had a more significant impact on OOD detection performance than the specific OOD detection method itself within this group.
- **PCam Histopathology Benchmark (Table 3):** On the PatchCamelyon dataset for unsupervised tumor detection, RDM showed comparable or superior performance to KNN and Residual. With the Uni encoder, RDM achieved the highest AUROC (89.38%) and lowest FPR95 (45.25%). The study noted that RDM was not sensitive to hyperparameters, unlike KNN (which requires calibration for optimal `k`) or Residual (which can be sensitive to the number of principal components).

#### 4.3. Evaluation with Supervised Encoders

The study further evaluated RDM and its conditional variant, ConRDM, with supervised encoders, comparing them against a comprehensive set of state-of-the-art OOD detection methods.
- **OOD Detection with ViT Encoder (Table 4):**
 - **Without Label Information (RDM):** RDM consistently outperformed other label-free methods. It achieved an average AUROC of 95.18% and FPR95 of 22.83% across the four OOD datasets, surpassing Residual (AUROC 92.71%, FPR95 31.19%) by a notable margin. Specific improvements were observed in challenging datasets like ImageNet-O.
 - **With Label Information (ConRDM):** ConRDM, leveraging class-conditional training, demonstrated superior performance to all other benchmarked methods. It achieved the highest average AUROC of 96.35% and the lowest average FPR95 of 17.06%, outperforming even methods like ViM (AUROC 96.23%, FPR95 18.07%) and Mahalanobis (AUROC 96.02%, FPR95 19.45%).
- **OOD Detection with Alternative Encoders (Table 5):** When evaluated with other supervised encoders (Swin, DeiT, RepVGG, ResNet50d, BiT), likelihood-based methods (RDM and ConRDM) generally performed comparably to state-of-the-art OOD detection approaches. ConRDM showed a slight improvement over RDM when labels were used. However, their performance was observed to be slightly lower on CNN-based encoders (RepVGG, ResNet50d, BiT), which typically have higher dimensional and potentially more asymmetrical representation spaces, possibly requiring greater network expressiveness for optimal likelihood estimation.

#### 4.4. Representation Space Analysis

An analysis of the relationship between likelihood estimation accuracy and OOD detection performance (Figure 3) demonstrated that more precise likelihood estimation (indicated by lower average bits/dim on the ID validation dataset) correlated with better OOD detection. This finding empirically contrasts with some prior observations in pixel space where better likelihood estimation did not always lead to improved OOD detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of OOD detection and broader AI safety.

- **Rehabilitation of Likelihood-Based OOD Detection:** The work fundamentally challenges the prevalent skepticism regarding the efficacy of likelihood-based methods for OOD detection. By demonstrating that these methods can achieve state-of-the-art performance when applied to semantically rich representation spaces, the study suggests that the historical failures were not due to an inherent flaw in the likelihood principle but rather a mismatch between the method and the domain (raw pixel space). This could lead to a renewed interest and investigation into likelihood-based approaches.
- **Enhanced Reliability for AI Systems:** Improving OOD detection capabilities directly contributes to the reliability and robustness of deep learning systems. In safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous driving, medical diagnosis), reliably identifying inputs outside the training distribution is crucial to prevent erroneous and potentially dangerous decisions. The proposed method offers a robust mechanism for flagging such uncertain inputs.
- **Practicality for Label-Free Scenarios:** A major impact lies in the method's effectiveness with self-supervised encoders and in settings where labeled data is scarce or unavailable. This is particularly relevant for fields like medical imaging (e.g., histopathology), where expert annotations are expensive and time-consuming. By providing a competitive label-free OOD detection strategy, the research expands the applicability of OOD detection to a wider range of real-world problems and emerging foundational models.
- **Advancement with Foundational Models:** The demonstrated performance gains when leveraging modern pre-trained encoders, especially transformer-based models like ViT and DINOv2, indicate that the approach is well-suited to capitalize on the powerful representations learned by these foundational models. This integration allows OOD detection to benefit from the generalizable features extracted by large-scale pre-training.
- **Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** Operating in lower-dimensional representation spaces, as opposed to high-dimensional pixel space, results in more computationally efficient likelihood estimation. This aspect is important for deploying OOD detection systems in environments with computational constraints or for scaling to very large datasets.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The findings open new avenues for research. Future work could focus on:
 - **Improving Likelihood Estimators:** Exploring other advanced generative models that allow for precise likelihood estimation to potentially further enhance performance.
 - **Optimizing Representations:** Investigating methods to fine-tune encoders specifically to promote characteristics that are beneficial for OOD detection performance within the representation space.
 - **Understanding Representation Space Properties:** Further theoretical and empirical analysis of why certain representation spaces are more amenable to likelihood-based OOD detection than others.

In conclusion, this research significantly contributes to trustworthy machine learning by re-establishing likelihood-based methods as a powerful and practical strategy for OOD detection, particularly when integrated with modern representation learning techniques.
