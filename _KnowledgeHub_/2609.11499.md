# Recursive Code World Models: Building Complex Worlds through Recursive Scene Programs

> [!summary] Summary
> Recursive Code World Models (RCWM) reconstruct complex 3D worlds as executable Three.js scene programs from a single image. A recursive solver establishes the whole scene, gives unresolved parts their own complete solves using matched reference crops and inherited camera projection, then revisits the assembled parent to fix cross-part errors. It beats three code-based baselines on PSNR and LPIPS.

## Problem

- Code world models give an executable representation but do not specify how to construct a complex world containing more structure than one reconstruction trajectory can resolve.
- Whole-scene reconstruction with repeated reviews overlooks small structures such as windows, signs and ornaments, which need focused inspection at finer scales.
- Refining parts independently creates conflicts, such as overlapping signs or bridges that no longer meet banks, which only appear when refined parts are inspected together.

## Method

- ==Recursive Scene Program==: executable compositional code organized as ==nested subworlds==, with explicit child references, editable parameters and shared dependencies, composed into a parent via a ==Compose operation== and runnable in Three.js.
- ==Recursive solver==: every node runs the same ==establish-whole, recurse-on-unresolved-parts, revisit-whole cycle==, driven by a ==frozen vision-language coding agent== that compares renders to references and decides descent and return.
- ==Matched reference crop== and ==camera crop==: each child inherits the parent's camera pose with a narrowed frustum and magnification, giving consistent local comparisons, while ==parent revisitation== repairs boundaries, relations and shared errors.

## Results

- On ten references, five whole scenes and five crops, RCWM gets the **highest PSNR and lowest LPIPS on every one** against SEIG, VIGA and img2threejs, and the best SSIM on **nine**.
- On city-full, RCWM scores PSNR **18.2**, LPIPS **0.158** and edge F1 **0.94**, versus img2threejs **15.5**, **0.243** and **0.91**, SEIG **14.5** and VIGA **9.5** PSNR.
- In ablation on medieval-village, free-depth recursion (three levels, **33** nodes) beats fixed two-level recursion: PSNR **19.0** versus **16.8** and local SSIM **0.60** versus **0.52**. On school-block, gains were similar, single runs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex construction with a frozen model benefits from applying one complete solve loop recursively at every scale, rather than a fixed scene-then-object pipeline with final cleanup.
> - Returning to the parent after local refinement is essential, because independently edited parts conflict at boundaries and shared geometry, and only the assembled whole exposes these errors.
> - Sharing camera projection across levels lets local crops stay geometrically comparable to the whole. Evidence is preliminary: two-scene ablations, single runs, one base model, and convergence is not guaranteed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This preprint addresses image-to-3D reconstruction through executable code rather than through meshes, implicit neural fields, or image-only generation. Its central premise is that a scene represented as source code can remain editable, compositional, inspectable, and usable for downstream rendering or simulation. The work therefore lies at the intersection of computer vision, inverse graphics, procedural modeling, vision-language agents, and recursive inference.

Earlier work on executable scene generation established that language or vision-language models can produce Blender or Three.js programs describing geometry, materials, lighting, and spatial relationships. Systems such as SceneCraft, VIGA, Thinking in Blender, and img2threejs use iterative program generation and rendering to compare a synthesized scene with a visual target. Procedural world-generation systems such as Infinigen and WorldClaw similarly demonstrate how code can organize large scenes, although their primary settings involve procedural generation, textual specifications, or open-ended world construction rather than reconstruction from one fixed image.

RCWM focuses on a specific problem within this landscape: reconstructing a visually complex world from a single reference image while preserving both global scene organization and fine local detail. The paper argues that existing code-generation pipelines do not sufficiently specify how reconstruction should be organized across scales. A model may establish a plausible whole scene but miss small structures, or it may refine local components independently and introduce conflicts when those components are recombined. RCWM contributes a recursive construction strategy intended to address this tension.

The approach is also related to recursive language-model inference, in which a model calls itself on selected subproblems. The distinction claimed by the authors is that RCWM applies recursion to the complete reconstruction cycle rather than only to decomposition or planning. Every subworld is expected to establish its own context, refine its parts, and revisit the assembled result before returning to its parent.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to reconstruct a complex 3D world as an executable scene program from a single reference image. The output should reproduce the visible layout, geometry, appearance, occlusions, and spatial relationships of the reference while remaining editable and renderable from additional viewpoints.

The motivation arises from the multiscale nature of complex environments. A town, for example, contains terrain and streets, buildings, facades, windows, signs, roofs, and smaller architectural or natural elements. These structures have dependencies across scales. A building must be placed correctly relative to a street; a facade must fit the building; a sign must not overlap a window incorrectly; and a bridge or shoreline must meet adjacent terrain. A single flat reconstruction process may not allocate enough focused attention to each scale.

The paper’s central design claim is that each selected part should be treated as a subworld rather than as an isolated asset. A subworld should inherit the parent’s coordinate conventions and camera projection, receive focused visual evidence, and undergo the same complete construction loop as the root scene. After the local reconstruction returns, the parent should inspect the whole again to identify boundary errors, collisions, incorrect spatial relationships, or shared causes of error.

The authors therefore pursue three related objectives:

1. **Representation:** Define a Recursive Scene Program (RSP) that organizes an executable world into nested subworld programs with explicit references and editable parameters.
2. **Construction:** Define a recursive solver that applies a global–local–global process at every level of the scene hierarchy.
3. **Evaluation:** Compare the resulting reconstructions with existing image-to-scene-program methods and measure the effects of recursion, construction order, recursive depth, and parent-level revisitation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Recursive Scene Program Representation

An RSP consists of source procedures, parameters, component references, and shared dependencies. The root program represents the entire world, while child programs represent objects, assemblies, or continuous regions such as terrain. Parent programs retain local construction logic and placement rules while referring to child programs through explicit identifiers.

The complete program is executed through a Three.js compiler. Execution produces a 3D scene, and a rendering operation produces an image from a specified camera. This code-based representation makes dimensions, materials, geometry, and placement directly editable. It also permits child programs to be replaced or refined without discarding the parent’s remaining structure.

#### 3.2. Recursive Solver

RCWM uses a frozen vision-language coding agent as the solver. The same solver function is invoked at every level of the hierarchy. Each call receives:

- The current component’s executable code.
- A reference image or crop.
- A matched camera configuration.
- An inherited parent context.
- A remaining inference budget.

The solver follows three stages.

##### 3.2.1. Stage 1: Establish the Whole

The agent renders the current scene or subworld in its inherited context and compares the render with the corresponding reference. It first revises layout, shared structures, component interfaces, and other scene-level properties. For example, a building-level call establishes the facade arrangement before delegating individual windows or signs.

The root call also calibrates the camera. The root controls camera revisions, and affected child views are refreshed if the camera or shared scene dependencies change.

##### 3.2.2. Stage 2: Prepare and Recursively Construct Parts

The agent identifies unresolved visual subproblems that require their own reconstruction loops. It chooses crop windows, magnification levels, and budgets for selected children. Each child receives a crop from the parent reference and a corresponding camera viewport.

The camera transformation preserves the parent camera pose while modifying the intrinsic matrix and viewport to match the crop. This allows local comparisons to retain the parent’s projection, scale, and spatial alignment. Overlapping crops can be used to inspect object boundaries and relationships between neighboring components.

The child begins from its existing referenced program and is placed back into a snapshot of the parent context. It then invokes the same solver. Consequently, a facade can call the solver on a window band, and that window band can call it on individual windows or signs if further subdivision is justified.

##### 3.2.3. Stage 3: Compose, Refine, and Return

After child calls return, the parent integrates the returned subprograms and renders the assembled whole. The agent then reviews the combined result for placement, occlusion, contact boundaries, continuity, and spatial relationships. It may revise the parent program directly or reopen affected child tasks.

This return phase is a defining part of the method. Local child calls work against inherited but temporarily fixed contexts, so conflicts may become visible only after multiple children are composed. Parent revisitation is intended to identify these emergent errors.

The process continues until the agent finds no new actionable discrepancy or the inference budget is exhausted. The implementation allows up to five recursion levels in the main experiments, although observed scenes often use fewer.

#### 3.3. Visual Inspection and Completion

The released solver instruction directs the agent to compare reference and rendered views side by side at matched magnification. It also asks for rotated views to identify flat or billboard-like geometry and to inspect thickness and continuity. Hidden or occluded geometry is completed using object knowledge and style assumptions, since it cannot be determined uniquely from one image.

The method relies on the agent’s visual judgment during construction rather than using evaluation metrics as an optimization objective. The metrics are calculated after reconstruction.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design

The evaluation uses five whole-scene references and five local crops. The scenes include city-full, snow-village, island-harbor, medieval-village, japan-island, and several crops from city and valley-village examples. The source images are used without the original prompts, terrain, or assets.

RCWM is compared with SEIG, VIGA, and img2threejs. All methods use the same reported base model, OpenAI’s gpt-6-astra, with the same reasoning-effort setting. Performance is measured using PSNR, SSIM, Canny edge F1, LPIPS, and CLIP image-embedding cosine similarity.

The ablation compares five workflows: flat reconstruction with zoom, local-to-global construction, global-to-local construction without post-composition refinement, fixed two-level recursion, and free-depth recursion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Comparison With Baselines

RCWM obtains the highest PSNR and lowest LPIPS for every reference listed in Table 1. On whole scenes, its PSNR ranges from 17.4 to 19.3, compared with 9.5–16.6 for the baselines. Its LPIPS ranges from 0.158 to 0.237, while baseline values are generally between 0.272 and 0.670.

For example, on **medieval-village**, RCWM achieves 18.7 PSNR, 0.71 SSIM, 0.89 edge F1, 0.200 LPIPS, and 0.93 CLIP similarity. The strongest baseline values are lower in PSNR and edge F1 and higher in LPIPS. On **japan-island**, RCWM achieves 19.3 PSNR and 0.201 LPIPS, compared with 16.6 PSNR and 0.302 LPIPS for img2threejs, the strongest baseline on several measures.

The gains also occur on local crops. On **park-lake**, RCWM reaches 23.3 PSNR, 0.83 SSIM, 0.99 edge F1, and 0.075 LPIPS. On **school-block**, it reaches 16.4 PSNR and 0.56 SSIM, compared with 12.8 PSNR and 0.26 SSIM for img2threejs. These results support the paper’s claim that recursive local attention improves detail reconstruction while retaining scene-level alignment.

RCWM has the highest SSIM on nine of the ten references. The exception is the valley-village crop, where VIGA reaches 0.41 SSIM and RCWM reaches 0.38. This case also has lower overall scores for all methods, suggesting that the crop or scene structure remains difficult even with recursion.

Additional rendered views demonstrate that the output is an executable 3D program rather than a single-view image approximation. However, the paper notes that crop-based scenes contain only content visible in the reference, so unseen regions may be incomplete when viewed from behind.

#### 4.2. Ablation Findings

The ablation results indicate that construction organization matters. On **school-block**, fixed two-level recursion achieves 16.8 PSNR, 0.55 SSIM, and 0.57 local SSIM, while flat-plus-zoom achieves 13.3 PSNR, 0.28 SSIM, and 0.27 local SSIM. The recursive result is also better than global-to-local construction without final joint refinement.

On **medieval-village**, free-depth recursion performs best, reaching 19.0 PSNR, 0.73 SSIM, 0.60 local SSIM, and 0.187 LPIPS. The fixed two-level version reaches 16.8 PSNR, 0.67 SSIM, 0.52 local SSIM, and 0.252 LPIPS. In this case, free-depth recursion expands to three levels and 33 nodes.

The results suggest that deeper recursion can improve both fine-scale and whole-frame fidelity when the scene contains sufficiently nested structure. However, the effect is not uniform. On school-block, free-depth recursion uses only two levels and performs slightly below fixed two-level recursion. Thus, recursion depth is task-dependent rather than automatically beneficial.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It frames complex scene reconstruction as a recursive construction problem rather than merely a program-generation problem. The RSP representation provides a way to preserve editable structure, while the recursive solver provides an organizational principle for allocating visual reasoning across scales.

Potential applications include procedural asset creation, editable scene reconstruction, simulation-oriented world modeling, game and virtual-environment construction, and vision-language systems that must manipulate structured 3D environments. The explicit program hierarchy could also support later edits, component reuse, alternative camera views, and integration with simulation systems.

The findings should nevertheless be interpreted within the study’s limitations. The evaluation uses a small set of references, mostly stylized scenes, single runs, one reported base model, and limited ablations. Single-image reconstruction cannot resolve hidden geometry, physical scale, or unseen continuations uniquely. The method relies on visual judgment by the coding agent, and repeated recursive calls can require substantial computation. The paper does not establish guaranteed convergence or robustness across different models, styles, image resolutions, or real-world photographs.

Overall, RCWM presents recursive global–local–global construction as a framework for improving executable 3D reconstruction. Its empirical results indicate improvements over the evaluated baselines, while the ablations provide evidence that parent revisitation and, in some cases, deeper recursive calls contribute to reconstruction quality. Further validation would require larger benchmarks, repeated trials, human assessment of spatial relationships, computational-cost analysis, and evaluation on scenes with substantial real-world geometric and appearance variation.
