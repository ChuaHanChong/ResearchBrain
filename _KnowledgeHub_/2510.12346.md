# PolygMap: A Perceptive Locomotion Framework for Humanoid Robot Stair Climbing

> [!summary] Summary
> The PolygMap framework enables humanoid robots to climb stairs in unknown environments by constructing a real-time polygonal semantic map, achieved through multi-sensor fusion of LiDAR, RGB-D data, and IMUs for robust state estimation and foothold generation. Experiments demonstrated real-time performance on embedded hardware, with successful stair climbing in diverse indoor and outdoor settings and footstep errors as low as 11.4-12.1 mm for the Double Step gait.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with stair climbing due to reliance on reactive control, leading to conservative gaits and high footstep uncertainty, insufficient for sustained navigation in complex, varied conditions.
- Stair environments pose perception challenges like small treads, sharp edges, low texture, and light absorption, which compromise depth measurements and stable plane detection, exacerbated by robot body vibrations and sensor noise.
- The coupling between robot odometry and environmental mapping leads to accumulating local drift over extended movements, degrading the spatio-temporal consistency of perceived step boundaries crucial for precise footstep planning.

## Method

- Developed **PolygMap**, a perception-based locomotion framework that builds a real-time polygonal semantic map of staircases, leveraging multi-sensor fusion (LiDAR, RGB-D camera, IMU) for accurate plane segmentation and visual odometry.
- Implemented a multi-sensor fusion-based state estimation module using a Kalman filter to integrate proprioceptive sensing with LIO, yielding precise, smooth, and low-drift robot odometry.
- Designed a foothold generation module that constructs polygonal maps via anisotropic diffusion filtering and RANSAC, followed by layered erosion and candidate selection to identify optimal, safe, and stable foothold regions for bipedal gait planning.

## Results

- The multi-sensor fusion method successfully generated accurate and smooth state estimates, significantly reducing high-frequency noise and cumulative drift compared to individual proprioceptive or LIO odometry.
- Gazebo simulations showed efficient climbing (average **3.4**-**3.7** seconds per step) and high accuracy, with a maximum footstep error of **12.4** mm, while maintaining real-time plane detection frequencies of 20-29 Hz.
- Real-world experiments demonstrated robust stair climbing in indoor and outdoor environments, with the Double Step gait achieving footstep errors of **11.4**-**12.1** mm and foot trajectory tracking errors within 40 mm, though the Single Step gait showed larger errors (up to **33.4** mm) and increased collision risk in complex outdoor scenes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing LiDAR-based odometry (LIO) with proprioceptive observations through a Kalman filter significantly improves both the global accuracy and smoothness of robot state estimation, effectively suppressing high-frequency noise and cumulative drift.
> - Explicitly generating polygonal semantic maps of stair treads and applying layered erosion robustly handles perception noise and environmental variations, enabling precise and safe foothold selection for humanoid robots.
> - Real-time whole-body motion planning can be achieved on embedded hardware (NVIDIA Orin NX), demonstrating the practical feasibility of tightly coupled perception-planning-control loops for complex locomotion tasks like stair climbing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are increasingly being developed for deployment in real-world scenarios such as building inspections, emergency responses, and industrial collaboration. While locomotion on flat surfaces has seen substantial advancements, navigating complex terrains, particularly stairs, remains a significant challenge. Stairs introduce specific difficulties including varied step heights, narrow contact surfaces, and the inherent risk of falling, which demand advanced capabilities in perception, planning, and control.

Traditional approaches to humanoid stair climbing often rely on powerful self-balancing mechanisms and high-bandwidth tracking stabilizers. These methods typically address geometric disturbances reactively, characterized as a "brute-force" approach. Such strategies frequently result in conservative gaits, exhibit considerable footstep uncertainty, and struggle to ensure reliable and sustained climbing on long staircases, under diverse illumination conditions, or with degraded tread surfaces due to their lack of explicit environmental awareness.

Perception-based locomotion offers an alternative by enabling robots to acquire pre-contact information about traversable planes, step edges, and potential obstacles. This prior knowledge can explicitly constrain footstep planning and mitigate unforeseen risks. However, applying perception in stair environments presents several challenges:
- Stair treads are typically small with sharp edges. Depth measurements can be compromised by factors like low texture, black absorptive materials, and viewpoint changes, leading to unstable normal estimation and fragmented planar representations.
- Body vibrations during bipedal walking and camera rolling-shutter effects can amplify noise and cause sensing data dropouts, making single-frame plane fitting unreliable.
- The coupling between odometry and mapping can induce local drift, accumulating errors in multi-frame fusion and degrading the spatio-temporal consistency of perceived step boundaries and traversable polygons.
- The entire perception-planning-control loop must operate in real-time, necessitating computationally efficient geometric extraction and map representations that can be rapidly integrated into control systems.

Previous research in planar semantic mapping has evolved from dense grid and elevation maps to bounded planar or polyhedral representations directly applicable to footstep planning. Techniques like CAPE and GPU-based pipelines have aimed for high-frequency geometric feature extraction. For stair-specific environments, some work has integrated perception with multi-contact motion generation. The general trend in this area is moving towards structured, spatio-temporally consistent models of feasible foothold regions that can be tightly coupled with footstep planning and whole-body control.

In perceptive locomotion, early efforts separated footstep planning from walking pattern generation. The 2015 DARPA Robotics Challenge saw the adoption of multi-sensor fusion for on-board perceptive footstep planning, as demonstrated by the MIT team. More recently, elevation maps and methods integrating planar region measurements with kinematic-inertial state estimation have gained prominence, particularly in quadruped locomotion. Advances such as nonlinear Model Predictive Control (MPC) incorporating traversability classification and signed distance field preprocessing, and learning-based methods utilizing attention-based map encoding, aim to directly integrate environmental geometry into the online optimization of contact sequences and whole-body motions.

This paper's PolygMap framework positions itself within this landscape by focusing on the robust generation of stair-adaptive traversable regions despite perception noise, state uncertainty, and real-time computational constraints. It specifically aims to efficiently couple these perceptual representations with whole-body motion generation, contributing to the development of reliable stair climbing for humanoid robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, perception-based locomotion framework that enables humanoid robots to climb stairs accurately and reliably in unknown environments, thereby emulating human-like walking behavior.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in current humanoid robot locomotion systems when encountering stairs:
- **Limitations of existing reactive control:** Many current humanoid systems lack an explicit understanding of the environment's geometry, relying instead on high-bandwidth control to react to disturbances. This often leads to conservative gaits and considerable uncertainty in foot placement, which is insufficient for sustained and reliable navigation on complex structures like stairs, especially under varying conditions.
- **Specific difficulties of stair environments for perception:** Stairs are particularly challenging for robot perception systems. Their small treads and sharp edges can compromise depth measurements due to factors such as low texture or light absorption, leading to fragmented or inaccurate plane detection. Furthermore, robot body vibration and camera sensor effects (e.g., rolling shutter) introduce noise that complicates stable plane fitting from single frames.
- **Accumulation of errors in mapping and odometry:** Over extended movements, the coupling between robot odometry and environmental mapping can lead to local drift. This drift accumulates, negatively affecting the spatio-temporal consistency of perceived step boundaries and traversable regions, which is critical for precise footstep planning.
- **Real-time computational demands:** For practical implementation, the entire perception-planning-control loop must operate in real-time. This necessitates computationally efficient geometric extraction and map representations that can be quickly processed and integrated into motion controllers.

To address these motivations, the PolygMap framework sets forth the following specific objectives:
- To develop a perception-planning framework that integrates LiDAR data with robot forward kinematics to achieve real-time online footstep planning for stair climbing tasks.
- To implement a precise localization system using LiDAR and fuse it with visual RGB-D data to accurately extract staircase planes and generate safe and stable foothold regions.
- To design a foot trajectory planner capable of generating collision-free trajectories based on these identified safe regions, ensuring the robot's stability and safety during climbing.
- To validate the proposed framework thoroughly through both simulation and real-world experiments in diverse indoor and outdoor stair environments, assessing its efficiency, robustness, and accuracy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PolygMap framework is designed as a perception-based locomotion system for humanoid robot stair climbing. Its core approach involves constructing a real-time polygonal semantic map of the staircase and then leveraging these polygonal plane segments for footstep planning. This is achieved through a multi-sensor fusion strategy that integrates data from LiDAR, an RGB-D camera, and Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) for plane segmentation and visual odometry. The system is implemented on a NVIDIA Orin NX platform, which facilitates whole-body motion planning outputs at a frequency of 20-30 Hz.

The system's architecture integrates joint recorders, depth sensing, and a LiDAR-Inertial Odometry (LIO) estimator. Robot pose is derived by fusing forward kinematics with the LIO, while depth images contribute to the polygonal map for foothold generation. Based on these generated footholds, footstep and whole-body motion planning are executed to enable stable stair climbing.

**A. Multi-Sensor Fusion based State Estimation**
This component focuses on achieving a smooth and accurate robot pose estimation by integrating proprioceptive sensing with LIO.
- **Forward Kinematics (FK):** A linear Kalman filter is employed to integrate multi-modal data from the IMU, joint encoders, and foot contact states. This filter provides a unified estimation of the robot's base position, velocity, and orientation, alongside the positions of eight contact points (four per foot). The robot's state vector includes CoM position, CoM velocity, and the positions of all contact points. The state-space equation models the system dynamics, with the control input being the linear acceleration of the robot's CoM. The observation vector includes the position and velocity of foot contact points relative to the robot's CoM, as well as their heights. This process estimates the robot's torso state in real-time, resulting in smooth and low-drift odometry.
- **Odometry Fusion:** A loosely coupled scheme integrates pose estimates from LIO into the Kalman filter framework. The LIO odometry is obtained from a laser SLAM algorithm, specifically Point-LIO. The LIO pose is transformed from the LiDAR frame to the robot's CoM frame using pre-known extrinsic parameters. A complementary filtering approach is then applied to fuse the position estimates, combining the robot's proprioceptive odometry with the LIO-derived position using a complementary coefficient `α`. Attitude fusion is performed using the exponential map form. This fusion process yields a precise and smooth robot odometry estimation, enhancing the overall system's robustness and accuracy.

**B. Foothold Generation**
This section details the process of constructing a polygonal map of the stair environment and extracting optimal foothold candidates.
- **Polygon Map Construction:**
 - The fused odometry and RGB-D depth image are utilized to extract a polygonal representation of the staircases.
 - Surface normals for each pixel are calculated directly from the depth image and camera intrinsics using differential vectors and their cross product.
 - An anisotropic diffusion filter is applied to depth images to reduce noise while preserving edge features. This filter adaptively adjusts its thermal conductivity based on gradient magnitude.
 - The RANSAC algorithm is then used within the extracted polygonal areas to iteratively fit planes and obtain their optimal parameters, forming the basis of the polygonal map.
- **Optimal Foothold Extraction:**
 - The system receives a set of detected polygons, each defined by a set of vertices. For 2D convex hull calculation, the height of each vertex is temporarily set to zero.
 - The 2D convex hull of each polygon is computed, defining its outer boundary in the horizontal (XY) plane.
 - A dense Point Cloud Data (PCD) is generated within the bounding box of each convex hull using a uniform grid resolution. Only points inside the convex hull are kept, and their height is assigned the average value of the original polygon's vertices, creating a `P_total` dataset.
 - **Range Filtering:** The `P_total` is transformed from the world frame to the robot's body frame (base link). Points outside a specified XY distance (`g_range`) from the robot's base are filtered out, resulting in `P_filtered`.
 - **Height Feature Extraction:** From `P_filtered`, the maximum height point of each grid cell is extracted to form `P_max`, representing the highest points for each XY grid.
 - Points higher than a threshold (`z_foot + g_z`, where `z_foot` is the minimum sole height and `g_z` is a safety margin) are removed, yielding `P_max_filtered`.
 - **Layered Erosion:** `P_max_filtered` is divided into height layers. An erosion operation (`N_erosion`) is performed on each layer to filter out isolated points and sharp edge noise. The eroded PCD from all layers are merged to form `P_eroded`.
 - **Candidate Selection:** The primary foothold candidate (`p*`) is chosen from `P_eroded` as the point closest to the robot's base that satisfies a minimum height condition. A second candidate (`p**`) is considered for the next step, reflecting obstacle height distribution. The relative direction of `p*` to the robot's base orientation is also calculated.
 - An additional erosion process is applied to the PCD layer near the sole height for enhanced climb locomotion safety. Finally, optimal foothold points are generated around these candidates, providing both spatial position and orientation for footstep planning.

**C. Footstep Planning**
This module is responsible for generating bipedal gait trajectories based on the real-time robot pose estimation and the selected footholds.
- **Foot Pose Generation:** Given the estimated robot torso pose (position and yaw orientation), the foot position (`p_f`) for each step is generated by offsetting the torso pose (`p_t`) with a predefined left/right foot offset (`b_f`) and rotating it according to the torso's yaw.
- **Foot Trajectory Interpolation:** To ensure smooth gait, the foot trajectory along the vertical (Z) direction during lift-off and landing phases is interpolated using a sinusoidal curve, defined by maximum step height (`z_max`), lift-off time (`t_lift`), landing time (`t_land`), and reference landing height (`z_0`). The horizontal (X) trajectory is linearly interpolated.
- **Collision Avoidance:** A rotating rectangle model represents the occupied space of the foot. The system checks for intersections between the current step's foot rectangle and the previous foot's position to detect trajectory conflicts, prompting adjustments to the input torso path if necessary.
- **Discretization:** The entire gait trajectory is discretized at fixed time intervals (`∆t`). This process ensures torso-foot coordination, smooth foot lifting, and collision-safe bipedal gait planning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed PolygMap framework was evaluated through a series of experiments: sensor fusion analysis, Gazebo simulation, and real-world trials in both indoor and outdoor environments. The robot used for experiments was a KUAVO humanoid, 166 cm tall, 55 kg in weight, with 28 degrees of freedom and foot dimensions of 26 × 9.6 cm. It was equipped with a 3D LiDAR, an RGB-D camera, and an IMU. All computations were executed on an NVIDIA Orin NX, achieving real-time whole-body motion planning at 20-30 Hz. Evaluation metrics included the continuity of stair detection, the number of consecutive steps climbed, and the horizontal and vertical errors between planned and executed footholds.

**A. Sensor Fusion Experiment Analysis**
A comparative analysis of three odometry estimations (proprioceptive, LIO, and fused) during robot walking was conducted.
- **Proprioceptive odometry (odom base):** Exhibited significant cumulative drift over time, particularly along the x- and z-axes, rendering it unsuitable for extended navigation requiring precise localization.
- **LiDAR-based odometry (odom lidar):** Provided higher absolute positioning accuracy but suffered from pronounced high-frequency oscillations, especially in the y-axis direction, making it challenging for direct use in the control module.
- **Fused odometry (odom fused):** Successfully integrated the advantages of both. It maintained the global accuracy of LIO while using proprioceptive observations to constrain and smooth the state estimation. This resulted in significantly more stable trajectories across all three axes, effectively suppressing high-frequency noise and reducing cumulative drift.
These results indicate that the multi-sensor fusion method generated accurate and smooth state estimates, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent perception and planning modules.

**B. Gazebo Simulation Results**
Five simulation trials were conducted in Gazebo, three on a 10-level staircase and two on a 4-level staircase.
- **Efficiency:** The maximum climbing duration was 35 seconds for 10 steps, with an average time of approximately 3.4–3.7 seconds per step. This suggests efficient motion while maintaining stability.
- **Accuracy:** The maximum footstep error from detection to execution was 12.4 mm. This error is considerably smaller than the available tread width, satisfying the required safety margin between the robot's foot size and the stair tread.
- **Real-time performance:** The plane detection frequency ranged from 20 to 29 Hz, indicating real-time operation of the perception module.
The simulations demonstrated smooth climbing and accurate foot placements, confirming the effectiveness and stability of the integrated perception-planning-control loop under ideal conditions.

**C. Real-World Stair Climbing Evaluations**
Five real-world experiments were conducted in both indoor and outdoor environments, using two gait strategies: Double Step (DS) and Single Step (SS).
- **DS (Double Step) Gait (Indoor):** This strategy involves both feet stepping onto the same stair before proceeding to the next. Trials using DS gait for 4 steps required 9.6–10.2 seconds. This gait yielded smooth and steady motion with smaller footstep errors, averaging around 11.4–12.1 mm. The planning for each step occurs after the robot achieves a stable stance.
- **SS (Single Step) Gait (Indoor/Outdoor):** This gait involves continuous alternate stepping onto successive stairs, leading to a faster forward pace. Indoor SS trials for 4 steps took 7.7 seconds. Outdoor SS trials for 5-6 steps took 9.8–11.2 seconds. However, SS gait resulted in generally larger footstep errors, with the maximum reaching 33.4 mm. This larger error approaches the safety margin for the robot's foot size and stair tread. The increase in error is attributed to online planning while walking, which can prevent the robot from consistently obtaining stable base observations.
- **Robustness in challenging environments:** In outdoor scenarios, despite ground unevenness and varying illumination, the plane detection frequency remained within 20–23 Hz, meeting real-time planning requirements.
- **Indoor Experiment Example (Fig. 6):** An indoor trial on a standard staircase (13 cm step height, 28 cm tread width – challenging for a 26 cm foot) using the DS gait successfully completed 4 steps. The process involved sequential environment perception, stair detection via multi-sensor fusion, foothold candidate generation, whole-body trajectory planning, and execution. Visualization showed accurately detected stair planes and planned foothold regions.
- **Trajectory Tracking (Fig. 7):** The 3D trajectories of the robot's base and feet demonstrated successful climbing with foot trajectories highly consistent with planned regions, showing smooth motion and no noticeable drift. Foot trajectory tracking performance along the x- and z-axes revealed that actual trajectories closely followed planned curves, with maximum tracking errors remaining within 40 mm. Error curves exhibited a periodic pattern, with peak errors occurring just before the initiation of each footstep trajectory and during swing phases, indicating that the controller actively corrects deviations.
- **Outdoor Experiment Observations (Fig. 8):** The robot successfully climbed 5-6 steps in two outdoor scenes. While it completed climbing on a standard outdoor stair, a scene with a protruding edge combined with the larger foothold error of the SS gait sometimes led to the robot's toe colliding with the edge. This indicated a potential failure rate for outdoor long staircase climbing, particularly with the SS pattern, possibly due to state estimation inaccuracies, outdoor lighting conditions, or actuator performance limitations.

Overall, the real-world experiments demonstrated that the proposed method maintains good robustness, especially with the DS gait. The closed-loop error from perception to execution was effectively suppressed, ensuring high-precision foot trajectory tracking and stable locomotion on uneven surfaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PolygMap framework represents a step in enhancing humanoid robot autonomy by addressing the complex challenge of stair climbing in unknown and varied environments. This research contributes to the field in several ways:

- **Advancement in Perceptive Locomotion:** The work demonstrates a functional integration of multi-sensor perception, semantic mapping, and real-time motion planning for dynamic tasks like stair climbing. This moves beyond reactive control strategies by providing the robot with an explicit understanding of its environment, which is crucial for safe and efficient navigation in human-centric spaces.
- **Robust State Estimation and Environmental Mapping:** The proposed multi-sensor fusion approach, combining LiDAR, RGB-D data, and proprioceptive sensing via a Kalman filter, addresses key challenges of noise, drift, and fragmentation in stair perception. The resulting accurate and smooth state estimation, coupled with the real-time polygonal semantic map, provides a reliable foundation for subsequent planning.
- **Real-time Performance on Embedded Hardware:** The deployment and validation on an NVIDIA Orin NX, achieving 20-30 Hz whole-body motion planning output, indicates the practical feasibility and computational efficiency of the framework. This is a critical factor for deploying humanoid robots in real-world scenarios where rapid decision-making and response are essential.
- **Improved Safety and Reliability:** By explicitly generating feasible foothold regions and planning collision-free foot trajectories based on a detailed environmental map, the framework aims to enhance the robot's stability and reduce the risk of falls compared to systems lacking such perceptual awareness. The quantitative results on footstep error and trajectory tracking demonstrate a level of precision that supports reliable operation.
- **Foundation for Complex Terrain Navigation:** While specifically focused on stair climbing, the principles of robust semantic mapping and perceptive footstep planning can potentially be extended to other challenging terrains, such as rough ground, slopes, or obstacle fields. This work thus contributes to the broader goal of enabling humanoid robots to operate autonomously in diverse and unstructured environments.
- **Contribution to the Robotics Community:** The commitment to open-sourcing the code indicates a potential contribution to the wider robotics research community, enabling other researchers to build upon and further develop these methods.

The noted limitations, particularly regarding the Single Step (SS) gait in complex outdoor environments with protruding edges, highlight areas for future investigation, such as improving robustness under extreme lighting variations or enhancing actuator control to prevent collisions. Despite these, PolygMap advances the capabilities of humanoid robots in navigating a common yet demanding man-made structure, thereby moving closer to their broader deployment in various service and industrial applications.
