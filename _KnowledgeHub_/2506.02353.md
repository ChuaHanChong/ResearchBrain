# SAVOR: Skill Affordance Learning from Visuo-Haptic Perception for Robot-Assisted Bite Acquisition

> [!summary] Summary
> The SAVOR framework enables robot-assisted feeding systems to reliably acquire food by learning skill affordances from visuo-haptic perception, dynamically estimating food properties, and adapting manipulation strategies. It achieved an 87.3% success rate within three attempts and 51.5% average attempt efficiency, surpassing state-of-the-art baselines like FLAIR and VLM-only methods across diverse and unseen food items.

## Problem

- Robot-assisted bite acquisition struggles with the diverse and temporally variable physical properties of food items (e.g., softness, moisture, viscosity).
- Existing methods, often relying on fixed categories or vision-only perception, fail to bridge the gap between visual appearance and crucial physical interaction mechanics.
- Current systems lack robust adaptability to handle different utensils and generalize effectively to novel or dynamically changing food items.

## Method

- **SAVOR** employs a two-stage framework: an offline phase to calibrate ==tool affordances== and train a multimodal neural network (**SAVOR-Net**), and an online phase for real-time state estimation and adaptive skill selection.
- A ==VLM== provides initial, commonsense-based estimates of food properties, which **SAVOR-Net** refines using time-series ==visuo-haptic feedback== (RGB-D, force-torque, end-effector pose) after each manipulation attempt.
- The system's high-level planner, also a ==VLM==, selects the optimal manipulation skill by reasoning about both the learned ==tool affordances== and the dynamically estimated ==food affordances==.

## Results

- SAVOR achieved an Average Attempt Efficiency of **51.5%** and a Success Rate within three attempts (SR3) of **87.3%**, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines like FLAIR (**38.2%** AE, **73.4%** SR3) and VLM-only methods (**43.8%** AE, **81.0%** SR3).
- The integration of visuo-haptic perception was crucial, with SAVOR's combined approach (**51.5%** Average AE) significantly surpassing vision-only (**27.1%** Average AE) and haptics-only (**40.1%** Average AE) variants.
- SAVOR demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, achieving higher SR3 (p < 0.05) than FLAIR in **70%** of 20 tested food items, including 10 items completely unseen during training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating both vision and haptics is critical for accurate food property estimation, as each modality complements the other in resolving ambiguities (e.g., visually similar foods with different textures, or haptically similar items with distinct appearances).
> - Skill affordances for bite acquisition emerge dynamically from the interaction between a tool's capabilities and a food item's physical properties, enabling adaptive manipulation beyond static, category-based approaches.
> - Combining foundation models for initial commonsense reasoning with physically-grounded, online visuo-haptic sensing and a specialized neural network allows for robust adaptation and generalization to novel food items and changing conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of **robot-assisted feeding** is a crucial area within **assistive robotics**, aiming to enhance the independence and quality of life for individuals with mobility impairments. Bite acquisition, the act of picking up food with a utensil, is a foundational yet highly challenging aspect of this task. The complexity arises from several factors:
1. **Diverse and Temporally Variable Food Properties:** Food items exhibit a wide range of physical characteristics (e.g., softness, moisture, viscosity, fragility) that can change over time (e.g., cooling steak becoming firmer, hot rice solidifying).
2. **Varied Utensil-Food Interactions:** Different utensils (forks, spoons, knives) interact differently with various foods, and even the same utensil might perform differently based on its material (e.g., plastic vs. metal fork).

Previous research in robot manipulation and feeding has explored several avenues:
- **Category-Based Approaches:** Methods like FLAIR categorize food (e.g., "meat," "fruit") and assign a predefined manipulation skill. While simple, this approach often fails when food properties deviate from the stereotype (e.g., a raw, firm avocado vs. a ripe, soft one) or when properties change.
- **Vision-Only Systems:** While successful in many robotic tasks like semantic grasping, vision alone often lacks the fine-grained understanding of physical interaction necessary for contact-rich tasks like food manipulation. Visually similar foods can have vastly different mechanical properties.
- **Haptic-Only Systems:** Research has long recognized the importance of haptic feedback in food manipulation. However, haptics alone can be ambiguous, as different food items (e.g., tofu and nuts) might produce similar force signatures under certain interactions.
- **Foundation Models (VLMs) in Robotics:** Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have shown promise in high-level planning and reasoning in robotics. These models can leverage commonsense knowledge for initial task understanding and skill sequencing. However, their reliance on visual priors and lack of direct physical interaction data limit their accuracy in dynamic, contact-rich scenarios.

SAVOR's contribution lies in advancing this landscape by explicitly tackling the dynamic and interactive nature of bite acquisition. It moves beyond static, category-based reasoning and single-modality perception by integrating:
1. **Multi-modal Visuo-Haptic Perception:** Combining time-series visual and haptic data to infer nuanced food physical properties.
2. **Affordance-Based Reasoning:** Formulating the problem in terms of "skill affordances" that emerge from the interaction between tool capabilities ("tool affordances") and food properties ("food affordances").
3. **Adaptive Learning:** Employing a framework that starts with commonsense priors (from VLMs) but dynamically refines them through online interaction feedback.

This work represents a significant step towards more robust, generalizable, and intelligent robot-assisted feeding systems that can truly adapt to the unpredictable real-world variations of food.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the SAVOR research is to significantly improve the **reliability and adaptability of robot-assisted bite acquisition**. This is driven by the recognition that current approaches often fall short in real-world scenarios due to the inherent complexity of food-utensil interactions.

The key motivations behind the SAVOR framework are:
- **Addressing Temporal Variability of Food Properties:** Food characteristics are not static. A piece of steak cools and becomes firmer, or rice hardens. Existing category-based methods cannot account for these dynamic changes, leading to failures. SAVOR aims to perceive and adapt to these changes in real-time.
- **Handling Diverse Food Items and Utensils:** Food items present a vast spectrum of physical properties (e.g., soft tofu vs. crunchy nuts), and different utensils have varying capabilities (e.g., a plastic fork vs. a metal fork). A robust system must reason about these variations to select appropriate manipulation skills.
- **Bridging the Gap between Visual Appearance and Physical Interaction:** While vision provides initial cues, it can be misleading. Foods that look similar might have vastly different physical properties, and subtle changes in texture or firmness are often imperceptible visually but crucial for successful manipulation. Haptic feedback is essential to resolve these ambiguities.
- **Developing a Physically Grounded Understanding of Skills:** Instead of simply associating a food category with a skill, SAVOR seeks to understand *why* a particular skill (e.g., skewering, scooping) is appropriate based on the physical interaction mechanics between the utensil and the food. This leads to more intelligent and generalizable skill selection.
- **Enhancing User Independence and Dignity:** The ultimate motivation for robot-assisted feeding is to help individuals regain control over a fundamental daily activity. Failures in bite acquisition can be frustrating and counterproductive to this goal. A more reliable system contributes directly to improving the quality of life for users.

To achieve these motivations, the paper sets out the following key objectives:
1. To develop an algorithm that learns **food affordances** based on physical properties (softness, moisture, viscosity), leveraging both commonsense priors and dynamic refinement via online visuo-haptic perception.
2. To create a method that characterizes **tool affordances** and integrates them with food affordances to model **skill affordances** for adaptive skill selection across diverse food items and tools.
3. To comprehensively evaluate the proposed SAVOR approach against state-of-the-art methods in varied real-world meal scenarios, demonstrating improved bite acquisition success and generalization.
4. To contribute a novel dataset of robot-assisted bite acquisition trials, providing synchronized visual and haptic signals across diverse foods and manipulation skills, to support future research in this domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SAVOR proposes a novel, two-stage framework for robust robot-assisted bite acquisition: a **pre-deployment (offline) phase** for learning fundamental affordances and a **during deployment (online) phase** for real-time state estimation and adaptive skill selection. The core idea is that skill affordances (how suitable a skill is) arise from combining **tool affordances** (what a utensil can do) and **food affordances** (what the food allows).

#### 3.1. Pre-Deployment: Offline Learning

This phase focuses on establishing a foundational understanding of tool capabilities and training a perception model for food properties.

1. **Offline Tool Calibration:**
 - **Goal:** To implicitly represent the functional capabilities of different utensils.
 - **Process:** The robot systematically executes various manipulation skills (e.g., skewering, scooping, cutting, pushing, dipping, twirling) on a small, diverse set of known food items using specific utensils (e.g., plastic fork, metal fork).
 - **Data Collection:** For each trial, human annotators record the utensil type, food type, its physical properties (shape, size, softness, moisture, viscosity), and the outcome (success/failure) of the skill.
 - **Output:** This forms a "calibration dataset" summarized in natural language (e.g., "Plastic fork: Nuts, Softness 1, Skewer 0/5 success, Scoop 3/5 success..."). This dataset serves as an implicit model of tool affordances, which is later provided to a VLM planner.

2. **Offline Training SAVOR-Net:**
 - **Goal:** To train a neural network capable of predicting key food physical properties from raw visuo-haptic sensor data.
 - **Target Properties:** Softness, moisture, and viscosity, each discretized into 5 Likert-scale levels (1-5). Shape and size are inferred visually by a VLM.
 - **Input to SAVOR-Net:** Time-series observations consisting of RGB-D images (I_t), force-torque readings (F_t), and end-effector poses (P_t).
 - **Architecture (SAVOR-Net):**
 - Employs separate encoders for each modality: an RGB encoder (pre-trained ResNet50 + MLP), a depth encoder (4-layer CNN + MLP), a haptics encoder (2-layer MLP), and a pose encoder (2-layer MLP).
 - Each encoder outputs a 128-dimensional vector.
 - These four vectors are concatenated into a unified multimodal representation.
 - This representation is then fed into a 2-layer LSTM (hidden size 512) to process the time-series data.
 - A final 3-layer MLP processes the LSTM output to produce log probabilities for each of the three target food properties.
 - **Training:** SAVOR-Net is pre-trained on an existing human-robot food manipulation dataset and then fine-tuned on a newly collected robot-centric dataset of 300 examples across 20 food items, encompassing various manipulation skills.

#### 3.2. During Deployment: Online State Estimation and Planning

This phase details how the robot operates in a real-world feeding scenario, adapting its behavior based on sensory feedback.

1. **State Estimation (Food Affordances):** This process continually estimates and refines the physical properties of the target food item.
 - **Initializing State Estimates:**
 - Given an initial RGB-D image (I_0) of a new plate of food, a VLM (GPT-4V) is prompted to identify semantic labels for food items.
 - GroundingDINO uses these labels to generate segmentation masks for each item.
 - The VLM is then prompted again with the segmented images and in-context examples to infer initial estimates of physical properties (shape, size, softness, moisture, viscosity) based on commonsense reasoning.
 - **Refining State Estimates with SAVOR-Net:**
 - After each attempted manipulation skill, new time-series visuo-haptic data (I_t, F_t, P_t) is collected.
 - This data is fed to the trained SAVOR-Net, which outputs log probabilities for softness, moisture, and viscosity.
 - If the confidence (log probability) for a property exceeds a predefined threshold (θ_th), the corresponding food property estimate is updated. This is particularly crucial if an initial skill attempt fails.
 - **Generating State Estimates for New Targets:** When the robot moves to a new food item on the same plate, and no direct interaction data is available yet, the VLM is prompted with the segmented image of the new item, the history of past attempts on the plate, and previous food property estimates. The VLM leverages this context to infer initial properties for the new target, which are then refined through subsequent interaction.

2. **Planning (Skill Selection):**
 - **Goal:** To select the most appropriate manipulation skill from a predefined library given the current understanding of food and tool affordances.
 - **Planner:** A VLM (GPT-4V) serves as the high-level planner.
 - **Prompting Strategy:** The VLM is prompted with a comprehensive context, including:
 - The **tool calibration dataset** (representing tool affordances).
 - Brief descriptions of each available manipulation skill (skewer, scoop, twirl, dip, cut, push).
 - A natural-language history of past acquisition attempts on the current food item.
 - The segmented image of the target food item.
 - The **dynamically estimated physical properties** (shape, size, softness, moisture, viscosity) of the target food (representing food affordances).
 - Based on this rich context, the VLM reasons and outputs the chosen skill. This decision is physically grounded in the estimated properties and calibrated tool capabilities, rather than just food categories.

**Hardware:** The system is implemented on a Kinova Gen3 robot arm equipped with a motorized feeding utensil (interchangeable plastic/metal fork), an Intel RealSense D435 camera mounted on the wrist for visual perception, and a Nano25 F/T sensor for haptic feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SAVOR's performance was rigorously evaluated across 20 single food items and 10 "in-the-wild" meal plates, using metrics of Average Attempt Efficiency (AE) and Success Rate (SR1, SR2, SR3 for 1, 2, and 3 attempts respectively).

1. **Contribution of Tool Calibration (Q1):**
 - Tool calibration significantly improved success rates, showing an **18% increase in SR2 and a 13% increase in SR3 (p < 0.05)**.
 - Without calibration, the robot frequently selected inappropriate skills, such as attempting to skewer a firm steak with a plastic fork or skewering soft tofu with a metal fork. Calibration enabled the system to align skill selection with the specific utensil's physical capabilities.

2. **Contribution of Visuo-Haptic Perception (Q2):**
 - **SAVOR (combined visuo-haptic)** achieved the highest performance with an **Average AE of 51.5% and an SR3 of 87.3%**.
 - The **vision-only variant** performed the worst (27.1% Average AE), struggling with visually similar but physically different items (e.g., carrots, watermelon, strawberries) and frequently misestimating properties, leading to repeated failures.
 - The **haptics-only variant** performed better than vision-only (40.1% Average AE) but still fell short of SAVOR. It faced ambiguities when foods shared similar force signatures (e.g., tofu and nuts), where visual cues are critical for disambiguation.
 - These results strongly confirm that **integrating both vision and haptics** is crucial for accurate food property estimation and robust bite acquisition. SAVOR's ability to significantly improve performance from SR1 to SR2 (from 53.2% to 77.2%) demonstrates its effective learning and adaptation from initial interactions.

3. **Evaluating the Overall Framework (Q3):**
 - SAVOR outperformed all baselines and state-of-the-art methods:
 - **FLAIR (SOTA category-based):** 38.2% Average AE, 73.4% SR3. Failed on atypical foods (e.g., raw avocado) due to fixed category-skill mapping.
 - **VLM w/o history:** 43.8% Average AE, 81.0% SR3. Improved over FLAIR by inferring properties visually but struggled with ambiguities or dynamic changes (e.g., cooling food).
 - **SayCan:** 36.2% Average AE, 73.4% SR3. Often oscillated between skills, suggesting a lack of robust physical understanding.
 - **End2End:** 42.7% Average AE, 81.8% SR3. Generalized poorly without explicit physical property representations.
 - SAVOR's performance gain from SR1 to SR2/SR3 was due to **informed adaptation** based on learned physical properties and interaction feedback, rather than just repeated attempts. The qualitative example of switching from skewering to scooping for a firming nugget effectively illustrates this adaptive behavior.

4. **Generalization to Unseen Food Items (Q4):**
 - SAVOR demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, achieving higher SR3 (p < 0.05) than FLAIR in 70% of the 20 tested food items, including 10 items completely unseen during training (e.g., raw avocado, cookie, chicken).
 - This highlights a key advantage of SAVOR: its property-based reasoning allows it to handle novel foods more effectively than methods relying on predefined categories.

5. **Performance on Challenging Foods:** SAVOR achieved high average attempt efficiency for crumbly foods like crackers (70%) and soft foods like tofu (95%), demonstrating its ability to adapt to diverse food textures through interaction feedback.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SAVOR research marks a significant advancement in the field of robot-assisted feeding and, more broadly, in robotics dealing with complex, deformable objects and dynamic interactions. Its primary significance lies in the paradigm shift from static, category-based skill selection to a **dynamic, physically-grounded, and adaptive approach based on visuo-haptic perception of food and tool affordances.**

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

1. **Robust and Adaptive Bite Acquisition:** By explicitly modeling skill affordances as a combination of tool and food properties, and critically, by refining food property estimates through online visuo-haptic feedback, SAVOR achieves significantly higher success rates and greater adaptability than prior state-of-the-art methods. This directly translates to more reliable and effective robot-assisted feeding for users.
2. **Physically Grounded Affordance Learning:** The work emphasizes that affordances are not static but emerge dynamically through interaction. This insight is crucial for developing intelligent robots that can reason about and manipulate objects in the real world, where properties are rarely constant or perfectly known a priori.
3. **Advancement in Multi-modal Perception:** The paper strongly demonstrates the necessity and efficacy of combining time-series vision and haptics for understanding complex physical interactions. It shows how each modality complements the other, resolving ambiguities that neither could tackle alone, particularly regarding temporal variations in food properties.
4. **Effective Integration of Foundation Models:** SAVOR cleverly leverages the commonsense reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models for initial property estimation and high-level planning, while grounding these abstract inferences in concrete physical interactions through specialized neural networks (SAVOR-Net) and empirical tool calibration. This provides a blueprint for integrating symbolic reasoning with sub-symbolic, sensory-driven learning.
5. **Enhanced Generalization:** By reasoning about underlying physical properties rather than superficial categories, SAVOR exhibits strong generalization to unseen food items, a critical capability for real-world deployment where a robot will encounter an infinite variety of foods.
6. **New Research Dataset:** The creation and proposed release of a new dataset with varied manipulation skills and synchronized visuo-haptic signals is a valuable contribution to the research community, enabling future work in learning complex food manipulation strategies.

**Potential Broader Impact:**

- **Improved Quality of Life:** The most direct impact is on individuals with mobility impairments, offering greater independence and dignity during meals. More reliable robotic feeding reduces frustration and improves the overall experience.
- **Foundation for Other Dexterous Manipulation Tasks:** The principles of learning skill affordances from visuo-haptic perception and dynamically adapting to object properties can be extended to other challenging manipulation tasks involving deformable, fragile, or multi-property objects beyond food (e.g., medical robotics, industrial assembly, domestic chores).
- **Advancement in Human-Robot Interaction:** By making robots more capable and reliable in assistive tasks, SAVOR contributes to building trust and acceptance in human-robot collaboration, paving the way for more ubiquitous and beneficial robotic assistants.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as treating food items as monolithic entities and using open-loop skill execution, these open avenues for future research further solidify SAVOR's position as a foundational work. The explicit focus on physical properties and interaction-driven learning is a critical step towards truly intelligent and adaptive manipulation systems.
