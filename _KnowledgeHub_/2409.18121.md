# Robot See Robot Do: Imitating Articulated Object Manipulation with Monocular 4D Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> Robot See Robot Do (RSRD) imitates articulated object manipulation from one monocular human demo plus a static multi-view object scan. Its 4D Differentiable Part Models (4D-DPM) recover part motion via differentiable rendering of DINO feature fields, then a bimanual robot plans grasps to reproduce object-level motion, reaching 60% end-to-end success across 90 trials with no task-specific training.

## Problem

- Monocular articulated-object tracking is underconstrained, causing degenerate solutions, and prior monocular methods need known kinematic chains or category-specific priors.
- Prior joint/part reconstruction methods require RGB-D video or multi-view observations at multiple states, limiting them to controlled capture setups rather than single casual videos.
- Single-demonstration imitation methods still need extensive in-domain robot data or paired human-robot data to bridge the morphology gap between hand and gripper.

## Method

- **4D Differentiable Part Models (4D-DPM)**: a ==GARField==-segmented ==3D Gaussian Splat== embedded with ==DINO feature fields==, optimized via ==analysis-by-synthesis differentiable rendering== to recover per-timestep ==SE(3)== part poses from monocular video.
- Regularized part-motion optimization combining a ==DINO feature MSE loss== with a ==mono-depth ranking loss== and an ==as-rigid-as-possible (ARAP)== boundary-gaussian penalty plus ==temporal Laplacian smoothing== for stable 3D trajectories.
- Hand-guided part selection and part-centric grasp/trajectory planning: ==HaMeR== hand detection biases which parts to grasp, analytic ==antipodal grasp sampling== covers every part, and a sparse ==Levenberg-Marquardt== planner with ==cuRobo== finds collision-free bimanual trajectories.

## Results

- On a bimanual YuMi robot across **9** objects and **90** trials, RSRD achieved **94%** pose-initialization, **87%** trajectory-planning average success, and **60%** total end-to-end task success.
- 4D-DPM reached **7.5mm** average point-distance tracking error versus ground truth, beating ablations without ARAP (**+2.1mm** worse) and without depth regularization, and vastly outperforming photometric-only tracking (**56.7mm** average).
- Per-stage physical trial success rates were **84/90** for registration, **77/84** for trajectory planning, **63/77** for grasping, and **54/63** for motion execution, with no task-specific fine-tuning or data collection used.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object-centric part trajectories, not hand-motion copying, let a demonstration transfer across a large morphology gap between a human hand and a parallel-jaw gripper.
> - Distilled features (DINO) from large pretrained vision models give a far more robust differentiable-rendering optimization target than raw photometric loss for 3D tracking.
> - Simple geometric priors (ARAP rigidity, depth ranking) meaningfully stabilize monocular 4D reconstruction without needing labeled part datasets or category-specific training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Teaching robots to perform complex manipulation tasks has traditionally required extensive programming, large datasets, or specialized training for each new task. The "Robot See Robot Do" (RSRD) research by Kerr et al. from UC Berkeley presents a fundamentally different approach: enabling robots to learn articulated object manipulation by simply watching a single human demonstration captured with a standard monocular camera.

The core innovation lies in focusing on the object's motion rather than the human's hand movements. By reconstructing and tracking the 3D motion of object parts from a single demonstration video, the system allows robots to achieve the same manipulation outcome using their own movement strategies. This object-centric approach bridges the morphological gap between human hands and robotic grippers, making the learning process more generalizable and practical.

### 2. The Challenge of Robot Imitation Learning

Traditional approaches to robot imitation learning face several significant challenges. Most methods require extensive datasets of paired human-robot demonstrations, where the same task is performed by both humans and robots. This data collection process is time-consuming and often impractical for real-world deployment.

Another major challenge is the morphological difference between human hands and robot end-effectors. Direct copying of human hand trajectories often fails because robots have different kinematic constraints, workspace limitations, and grasping capabilities. Previous work has attempted to address this by learning mappings between human and robot motions, but these approaches typically require task-specific training and struggle to generalize to new objects or scenarios.

The RSRD approach sidesteps these issues by focusing on the motion of the manipulated object rather than the manipulator itself. This shift in perspective allows the robot to understand the intended outcome of the demonstration without being constrained by the specific way humans achieve that outcome.

### 3. Technical Approach

The RSRD system operates in two distinct phases: a "See" phase that reconstructs 4D object motion from a demonstration video, and a "Do" phase that executes the learned manipulation on a robot.

The "See" phase begins with capturing a multi-view scan of the articulated object using a standard smartphone. This scan is used to construct a 3D model using Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), a recent advancement in 3D scene representation that provides high-quality reconstructions with efficient rendering capabilities.

The 3DGS representation is then segmented into movable parts using GARField, a clustering method that groups 3D Gaussians into discrete object components. Each part is embedded with DINO features - high-dimensional visual features extracted from a self-supervised vision transformer that capture semantic and geometric properties of the object.

During the demonstration, a human manipulates the object while being recorded with a single RGB camera. The system then tracks the motion of each object part by optimizing the 3D transformations that best explain the observed visual changes in the monocular video.

### 4. 4D Differentiable Part Models

The core technical contribution is the development of 4D Differentiable Part Models (4D-DPM), which enables recovery of 3D part motion from monocular video observations. The method formulates motion estimation as an analysis-by-synthesis optimization problem.

For each frame of the demonstration video, the system renders the current estimate of the 3D object configuration and compares it to the observed image. The comparison uses both DINO features and monocular depth estimates. The 3D part poses are then optimized to minimize the difference between rendered and observed features.

A key component is the As-Rigid-As-Possible (ARAP) regularization term:

```
L_ARAP = Σ ||d_i - d_i'||_2
```

where `d_i` represents distances between points in the initial object state and `d_i'` represents the corresponding distances in the current frame. This regularization ensures that object parts maintain their shape while allowing for articulated motion.

The optimization also incorporates geometric constraints that leverage the fact that articulated objects have predictable motion patterns. These constraints help resolve ambiguities that arise from the under-constrained nature of monocular 3D reconstruction.

### 5. Robot Execution Pipeline

The "Do" phase translates the recovered object motion into executable robot actions. The process begins with object pose registration, where the robot identifies the same object in its workspace and determines its current pose relative to the demonstration.

The system then plans a bimanual trajectory that will induce the same 3D motion on the object as observed in the demonstration. Rather than copying human grasps, the robot generates its own candidate grasps for each object part, with a bias toward contact points that align with those observed in the human demonstration.

Trajectory planning considers the robot's kinematic constraints and uses collision avoidance to ensure safe execution. The system employs cuRobo for efficient collision checking and motion planning in the robot's configuration space.

An important aspect of the execution pipeline is its focus on outcome rather than process. The robot is not constrained to follow the exact temporal sequence of the human demonstration but instead aims to achieve the same final object configuration through potentially different intermediate states.

### 6. Experimental Results

The researchers conducted extensive experiments to validate their approach across multiple dimensions. The 4D-DPM tracking method achieved an average distance error of 7.5mm when compared to ground truth part poses, demonstrating accurate 3D motion recovery from monocular video.

Physical robot experiments were performed using a bimanual YuMi robot across 90 trials involving 9 different articulated objects. The system achieved the following success rates:

- Initial Pose Registration: 94%
- Trajectory Planning: 87%
- Initial Grasps: 83%
- Motion Execution: 85%
- End-to-End Success Rate: 60%

The 60% end-to-end success rate represents a significant achievement for a system that requires no task-specific training, fine-tuning, or data collection. The experiments covered a diverse range of objects including toolboxes, sunglasses, toy guns, teddy bears, boxes, and wooden mannequins.

Ablation studies confirmed the importance of key design choices. The ARAP regularization was crucial for preventing unrealistic object deformations during tracking. DINO features proved more effective than other visual features for establishing correspondences across different viewpoints and object configurations.

### 7. Limitations and Future Work

The current system has several limitations that present opportunities for future research. The method assumes that the object's initial configuration in the robot workspace matches that in the demonstration video. This constraint could be relaxed by developing more sophisticated pose registration techniques or by learning to adapt trajectories to different starting configurations.

The part segmentation process currently requires some manual intervention, though the researchers note this could be automated using emerging segmentation models. The system also struggles with objects that have complex backgrounds, significant self-occlusions, or high degrees of symmetry that make part tracking ambiguous.

Another limitation is the focus on prehensile manipulation - tasks that involve grasping and moving object parts. Many real-world manipulation tasks involve non-prehensile actions like pushing, sliding, or leveraging, which are not addressed by the current approach.

The researchers suggest several directions for future work, including extending the method to handle few-shot demonstrations rather than single examples, improving robustness to environmental variations, and incorporating more sophisticated reasoning about object affordances and manipulation strategies.

### 8. Significance and Impact

The RSRD research represents a significant step toward more intuitive robot programming paradigms. By enabling robots to learn from simple visual demonstrations without requiring extensive datasets or task-specific training, the work addresses a major barrier to widespread robot deployment.

The object-centric approach to imitation learning has broader implications beyond this specific application. By focusing on manipulation outcomes rather than manipulator motions, the research opens new possibilities for transfer learning across different robot platforms and embodiments.

The technical contributions, particularly the 4D-DPM method for monocular articulated object tracking, advance the state of the art in computer vision and could find applications in augmented reality, motion capture, and other domains requiring understanding of object motion from visual input.

From a practical perspective, the work points toward a future where robots can be taught new tasks through natural demonstration rather than complex programming. This capability could significantly expand the accessibility of robotic automation to users without extensive technical expertise, potentially accelerating the adoption of robots in manufacturing, healthcare, and service applications.

The research also demonstrates the power of combining recent advances in neural rendering, self-supervised learning, and optimization-based tracking. This interdisciplinary approach showcases how different areas of machine learning and computer vision can be integrated to solve complex robotics challenges.
