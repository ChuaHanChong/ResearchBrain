# JEV-as-a-Judge: Accept When Confident, Escalate When Unsure

> [!summary] Summary
> JEV-as-a-Judge studies a decision-only hosted judge (TypeSafe JEV) that returns a typed verdict plus label probabilities instead of a generated rationale. Against sixteen generative and reward-model judges with blinded human adjudication, it lands within three points of a state-of-the-art LLM judge on ordinary preference and evidence-grounded factuality at 0.36% of its fee, and its confidence drives a cascade retaining 99% of that judge's accuracy at lower cost.

## Problem

- Generative LLM judges spend reasoning tokens on every item, so fees and latency scale with the volume of responses and model revisions being evaluated.
- Models asked to verbalize their own confidence are often overconfident on wrong answers, so prior judges lack a trustworthy signal for when further scrutiny is needed.
- Existing cascade and routing work targets generation cost or learned routers, leaving no measured operating profile for a hosted decision-only judge under frozen thresholds and real fees.

## Method

- Decision-only judging via **TypeSafe JEV**'s ==typed interface==: Choice, Noul and Score requests return a verdict plus label probabilities, with confidence taken as the maximum label probability q.
- A ==matched evaluation protocol==: seventeen judge configurations on a frozen 1,312-item suite, a shared decision-and-probability output contract without rationale, 894 order/repeat/paraphrase diagnostics, and blinded human adjudication of disagreements.
- A frozen ==two-order confidence cascade==: each pair is judged in both candidate orders, aligned probabilities averaged, and items below a pilot-fitted threshold escalated to a stronger fallback judge.

## Results

- JEV reaches **92.2%** on RewardBench and **87.5%** on HaluEval against GPT-6's **93.5%** and **86.7%**, at **$0.044** per 1,000 judgments versus **$12.18**, with **0.152s** median latency.
- Hard workloads separate them: JudgeBench **78.6%** versus **93.1%** and RM-Bench style-adversarial pairs **74.8%** versus **94.6%**; blinded adjudication confirms the gap, siding with GPT-6 on **57 of 69** disputed items.
- The frozen two-order cascade at threshold **0.9** accepts **53.7%** of 510 held-out pairs and scores **92.5%** against GPT-6's **93.1%** while using **56.8%** of its fee.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A usable verdict, a correct decision and reliable uncertainty are three separate properties; measure each one rather than inferring calibration from a typed probability output.
> - Confidence routes well where a weak first stage is uncertain where it errs, but fails when it is confidently misled by elaborately written wrong answers.
> - Escalation thresholds and temperature scaling do not transfer across workloads or fallbacks; fit them on a local selection set and re-check on held-out items.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the growing use of large language models (LLMs) as automated evaluators. Traditional natural-language-processing evaluation relied on fixed metrics such as BLEU, which are effective for constrained tasks but poorly capture qualities such as factuality, helpfulness, instruction following, reasoning quality, and preference between open-ended responses. Human assessment remains more flexible but is expensive, slow, and difficult to apply repeatedly during model development.

The emergence of “LLM-as-a-judge” systems has provided a scalable alternative. Generative LLM judges can inspect prompts and candidate answers, apply natural-language rubrics, and produce rankings or scores. Prior work has demonstrated their usefulness but has also identified position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference, sensitivity to rubric wording, and overconfident incorrect judgments. Reasoning-oriented models may improve difficult evaluations, but they can incur substantially greater latency and token costs.

This paper studies a decision-only judging system called **JEV**, provided through the TypeSafe service. Unlike a conventional generative judge, JEV directly returns a typed decision and a probability distribution over the allowed labels, without generating a rationale. The central research question is operational rather than architectural: can a low-cost decision-only judge handle routine evaluations and use its confidence signal to identify cases that should be sent to a more capable and expensive evaluator?

The study therefore connects several research areas: automated preference evaluation, reward-model benchmarking, confidence calibration, selective classification, and model cascades. Its primary contribution is an empirical analysis of the joint relationship between accuracy, uncertainty, cost, latency, and escalation behavior. It does not propose a new routing algorithm or expose JEV’s internal model, since the service is proprietary.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper has four main objectives.

First, it evaluates whether JEV can provide acceptable accuracy on common judging tasks. These include pairwise preference comparisons, evidence-grounded factuality assessment, final-answer adjudication, and correctness judgments involving multiple-choice, mathematical, coding, and reasoning responses. The authors emphasize that a decision-only output format does not make the underlying evaluation simple: the judge may still need to understand long free-form answers and apply a semantic rubric.

Second, the study examines where inexpensive judging fails. The authors distinguish routine preference and evidence-supported factuality from more demanding cases requiring verification of a derivation, assessment of technical reasoning, or resistance to a persuasive but incorrect answer. This distinction is important because average accuracy across mixed benchmarks can obscure workload-specific weaknesses.

Third, the paper evaluates the quality of JEV’s probability outputs. A confidence score is useful only if it correlates with correctness and can identify risky decisions. The authors therefore measure calibration, negative log-likelihood, Brier score, expected calibration error, and the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve for error detection. They also test whether confidence remains useful under style adversaries, reference-free prose, candidate-order reversal, and rubric paraphrasing.

Fourth, the authors assess the economic and operational value of a cascade. In the proposed policy, JEV’s confident decisions are accepted, while uncertain cases are escalated to a stronger LLM judge. The goal is to retain most of the stronger model’s accuracy while paying for it only on a subset of examples.

### 3. Methodology and Experimental Approach

#### 3.1. Evaluation Tasks and Data

The core evaluation uses three public benchmarks. **RewardBench** contributes 400 pairwise preference examples across chat, difficult chat, safety, and reasoning. **JudgeBench** contributes 350 pairs spanning knowledge, reasoning, mathematics, and coding, with an emphasis on objective correctness. **HaluEval** contributes 240 evidence-grounded factuality judgments involving questions, reference answers, hallucinated answers, and supplied evidence.

The authors also evaluate 150 existing model replies requiring extraction and adjudication of a final multiple-choice or numeric commitment. Additional controls include 108 reference-correct GSM8K conversational trajectories and 64 synthetic evidence judgments. Follow-up experiments use 100 four-way selections from RewardBench 2 and 80 source prompts from RM-Bench, with multiple style variants of preferred and rejected answers.

The evaluation was divided into a 642-item pilot and a 670-item extension. The pilot was split into selection and test portions. Temperatures for calibration and thresholds for routing were fitted only on the selection data. The extension was frozen before inspection of pilot accuracy and was not used for fitting. However, the authors acknowledge that the expanded multi-model comparison was exploratory because some models were added after initial results were known.

#### 3.2. Judges and Output Contracts

The principal system is **JEV 1.13**, a hosted TypeSafe service. The experiments primarily use its Choice interface, which returns a decision and probabilities over specified labels. Confidence is represented by the maximum label probability,

\[
q = \max_k p_k.
\]

JEV is compared with a broad set of generative judges, including several GPT, Claude, Gemini, Qwen, and GPT-OSS configurations, as well as local Qwen models, PairRM, and the modern Skywork-Reward-V2 reward model. Generative judges receive the same task instructions and structured inputs as JEV and are required to return a verdict and label probabilities without a rationale. Their probabilities are verbalized estimates rather than native classifier probabilities.

All outputs are schema-validated. Invalid judgments count as errors for accuracy purposes, while probability metrics are calculated only on valid outputs. The study distinguishes provider failures, contract violations, and transport failures rather than treating them as reasoning errors.

#### 3.3. Metrics, Human Review, and Operations

The authors report accuracy, macro-F1, multiclass Brier score, clipped negative log-likelihood, ten-bin expected calibration error, and error-detection AUROC. Paired differences use 2,000 source-cluster bootstrap resamples, preserving related answers and presentation variants.

To investigate label noise, a member of the research team conducted blinded human adjudication on all base-order items where JEV and GPT-6 disagreed in correctness. This covered 183 items. The annotator did not see benchmark labels or judge outputs. However, the paper notes that the adjudication was not fully independent: one human completed the main pass, an LLM pass was used to identify disagreements between annotation passes, and an author resolved remaining differences.

Latency and cost were measured on a separate 120-decision panel. The panel included 40 items from each principal public task and used a persistent client, controlled pacing, and parallel workers. The authors report outcome latency rather than intrinsic model inference time. Fees use provider-reported usage, cache discounts, reasoning-token charges, and conservative reservations when usage data were missing.

For pairwise cascades, both candidate orders were evaluated and probabilities were aligned to semantic response identity before averaging. Thresholds were selected on pilot data and then evaluated on held-out extension pairs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Accuracy and Workload Dependence

JEV performed competitively on routine evaluations. On RewardBench, it achieved **92.2%**, compared with **93.5% for GPT-6**. On HaluEval, JEV achieved **87.5%**, slightly above GPT-6’s **86.7%** under the supplied labels. On the existing final-answer adjudication set, JEV scored **94.0%**, compared with **96.7% for GPT-6**.

Human adjudication generally favored GPT-6 on disagreements, but the difference was relatively small for ordinary preference and evidence-grounded factuality. On RewardBench disagreements, the human adjudicator favored GPT-6 on 17 cases and JEV on 5, producing a human-adjusted task difference of approximately three percentage points. On HaluEval, the human favored GPT-6 on 8 of 10 disagreements. The adjudication also identified substantial label noise in HaluEval: 24 of 26 cases where both judges disagreed with the supplied label appeared unsupported by the evidence as labeled.

JEV’s main weakness was difficult correctness. On JudgeBench it achieved **78.6%**, whereas GPT-6 and GPT-5.6 achieved approximately **93.1%**. The gap was particularly large in reasoning, coding, and mathematics. Human review supported this result: GPT-6 was favored on 57 of 69 disputed items, with the largest differences in reasoning and coding.

The style-adversarial RM-Bench results showed another limitation. JEV scored **84.0%** when preferred and rejected answers had matched styles, but only **74.8%** when the rejected answer was more elaborately written. GPT-6 remained near 94% in both conditions. Thus, JEV was sensitive not simply to preference difficulty but to persuasive presentation that conflicted with semantic quality.

On four-way RewardBench 2 selection, JEV scored **73.0%**, compared with **75.0% for GPT-6** and **79.0% for Skywork**, although the sample was small. On document-grounded summaries, JEV achieved **71.2%**, close to GPT-5.4’s 72.5%. For reference-free prose, all tested judges were near chance: JEV achieved 52.5%, GPT-4.1 mini 53.8%, and GPT-5.4 55.0%. These systems also remained highly confident despite poor agreement, showing that confidence did not solve unsupported factuality assessment without evidence.

#### 4.2. Stability, Calibration, and Confidence

JEV showed no decision changes across 96 repeated requests and changed four decisions across 48 rubric paraphrases. Candidate-order reversal changed 3.25% of RewardBench decisions and 11.14% of JudgeBench decisions. Its first-position selection rate was close to 50%, suggesting that reversal errors reflected instability rather than a simple preference for the first response.

JEV’s confidence was informative but not universally reliable. Error-detection AUROC was **0.869 on RewardBench**, **0.745 on JudgeBench**, and **0.863 on HaluEval**. Accuracy increased with confidence: across the three base tasks, JEV was correct on 47.7% of decisions with confidence below 0.6, but on 99.1% of decisions with confidence equal to 1. However, confidence was less useful on style-adversarial pairs, where JEV could remain highly confident while selecting an elaborately written wrong answer. On reference-free prose, its error-detection AUROC was only 0.518.

Temperature scaling also transferred unevenly. A temperature fitted on pilot data improved negative log-likelihood on HaluEval but worsened it on RewardBench and JudgeBench. This indicates that probability calibration must be validated separately for each workload.

#### 4.3. Cost and Cascade Performance

JEV’s median latency was **0.152 seconds**, compared with 1.885 seconds for GPT-6. Its measured cost was approximately **$0.044 per 1,000 judgments**, compared with **$12.182 for GPT-6**, or about 0.36% of the comparator’s fee on the matched workload.

A single-order cascade accepting JEV decisions at confidence at least 0.9 and escalating the rest achieved **91.3% accuracy**, compared with 91.7% for GPT-6 alone, while escalating 34% of cases and using approximately 47% of GPT-6’s fee. In the frozen two-order evaluation, the GPT-6 cascade accepted 53.7% of pairs, achieved 92.5% versus GPT-6’s 93.1%, and used 56.8% of the fallback fee, or 62.2% under the conservative cost estimate. This corresponds to retaining approximately 99% of the stronger judge’s accuracy at substantially lower cost.

The cascade was more effective on RewardBench than on JudgeBench because JEV and GPT-6 made complementary errors. It was ineffective on reference-free prose and less dependable on style-adversarial inputs, where JEV’s confidence could be misleading.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main implication is that automated evaluation should be treated as a resource-allocation problem rather than a choice between one weak and one strong judge. A low-cost decision-only judge can serve as a first pass for ordinary preference, evidence-grounded factuality, and final-answer checks. A stronger evaluator can then be reserved for low-confidence or demonstrably difficult cases.

The findings also separate three properties that are often conflated: producing a valid output, making a correct judgment, and reporting useful uncertainty. JEV was highly reliable at satisfying the output contract and was inexpensive, but its correctness varied considerably by workload. Its confidence supported escalation in some settings but could not be treated as a certificate of correctness.

Potential applications include large-scale model benchmarking, response filtering, dataset quality control, and iterative evaluation during model development. The approach could reduce evaluation expenditure where routine cases dominate. However, deployment should include local validation, candidate-order balancing, workload-specific calibration, and targeted testing against style and reasoning failures. The study does not support using JEV—or any tested automated judge—as the sole arbiter of consequential decisions, particularly when evidence is unavailable or correctness depends on multi-step technical reasoning.
