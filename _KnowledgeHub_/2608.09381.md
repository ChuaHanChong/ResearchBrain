# JEPA-WAM: Learning Vision-Language-Action Policies with Joint-Embedding World Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> JEPA-WAM is a latent world action model built in a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 space. A shared Qwen2.5-0.5B predictor predicts a spatially structured joint current-future transition target while emitting dedicated tokens that condition a flow-matching DiT action expert, so transition supervision directly shapes the action backbone. It reaches 79.2% on LIBERO-Plus without robot-policy pretraining; a pretrained instantiation reaches 86.3%.

## Problem

- Video-generation world action models predict future frames explicitly, but iterative future prediction imposes substantial deployment cost for real-time robot control.
- Latent world models reuse generator features optimized for frame synthesis rather than state change, or compress futures into few tokens, losing fine-grained spatial structure.
- Policy integration stays weak: feeding predicted futures as extra context adds redundant state information, while separate dynamics modules only weakly shape the representations that generate actions.

## Method

- Joint current-future target: current and future observations are stacked temporally and jointly encoded by a frozen ==V-JEPA 2.1== encoder, giving a dense 24x24 patch target of their temporal relation.
- Shared predictor: a ==Qwen2.5-0.5B== backbone consumes projected V-JEPA tokens plus the instruction; visual-position hidden states pass through an MLP head trained by patch-wise ==cosine distance== to the target.
- 64 dedicated action placeholders read out conditioning for a 16-layer ==DiT== ==flow-matching== expert, trained jointly as action loss plus 0.5 times transition loss; the transition branch is dropped at deployment.

## Results

- LIBERO-Plus: **79.2%** average, best without robot-policy pretraining (ResVLA **77.1%**); adding the objective to a pretrained VLA lifts **84.5%** to **86.3%**, best overall, with in-distribution LIBERO at **96.7%**.
- RoboTwin 2.0: **79.9%** Clean and **36.9%** Random versus **73.9/8.3** for the strongest non-pretrained baseline; real-world bimanual OOD score **54.2%** against **22.5%**, and **84.7%** for the pretrained instantiation.
- Ablations: joint target **79.2%** beats future-only **77.3%** and endpoint-difference **70.9%**; convolutional alignment **74.7%**, layer-16 supervision **76.5%**, full-hidden conditioning **73.1%**; inference runs at **85 ms** (**11.76 Hz**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Encoding both temporal endpoints together beats predicting or subtracting them: joint encoding decodes temporal gaps at 67.2% versus 47.0%, retaining within-interval trajectory structure differencing loses.
> - Auxiliary predictive losses transfer best when they supervise the final shared backbone and actions are read from dedicated placeholder tokens, avoiding interference between the two objectives.
> - Caution: task-shared, language-independent transition targets may underperform when identical observations imply different futures per instruction; dense patch alignment beats pooled or convolutionally mixed targets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot control policies based on vision-language-action (VLA) models have demonstrated capabilities across a range of manipulation tasks. However, these policies typically model state transitions implicitly, which can limit their robustness when encountering variations or shifts in distribution in the environment. This limitation has motivated the development of World Action Models (WAMs), which explicitly incorporate future state prediction alongside action generation.

Traditional WAMs often rely on video generation to predict future observations. While effective, this approach introduces substantial computational costs during deployment due to the iterative nature of future frame generation. This constraint has led to the emergence of *latent WAMs*, which aim to retain the benefits of predictive world modeling by operating within learned representation spaces, thereby avoiding explicit pixel-level future observation generation.

Existing latent WAMs face two primary design challenges:
1. **Defining the predictive target:** Some methods reuse intermediate features from pretrained video generators, which are optimized for iterative video generation rather than explicit state change representation. Others compress future observations into a small number of latent tokens or subgoals, potentially sacrificing fine-grained spatial structure.
2. **Integrating predictive supervision with action generation:** Current approaches either feed predicted future representations as additional context to the action module, which may introduce redundant information, or employ separate prediction objectives or latent dynamics modules, which might only weakly influence the core representations used for action generation.

This research introduces JEPA-WAM, a latent WAM designed to address these limitations. It aims to develop a system that learns a spatially structured representation of observed transitions and directly uses predictive supervision to shape the policy backbone responsible for conditioning action generation. By building upon the pretrained V-JEPA representation space, which is known for producing temporally consistent representations, JEPA-WAM seeks to improve robustness and generalization under distribution shifts without incurring the deployment costs associated with explicit video generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are rooted in overcoming the identified limitations of existing VLA policies and latent WAMs, specifically concerning robustness under distribution shifts and efficient deployment. The key motivations include:

1. **Enhancing Robustness through Explicit Transition Modeling:** VLA policies often infer state transitions implicitly. The motivation is to explicitly model future states to improve robustness, particularly in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. However, this must be achieved without the computational burden of video-generation WAMs.

2. **Developing an Effective Latent Predictive Target:** Existing latent WAMs either rely on intermediate features from video generators (which may not be optimal for representing state changes) or compress future observations into low-dimensional tokens (which can lose fine-grained spatial detail). A key motivation is to devise a predictive target that is both efficient and rich in information. This led to the objective of creating a *spatially structured joint current–future target* within a pretrained V-JEPA representation space. This target aims to capture the temporal relationship between current and future observations, emphasizing stable and changing regions, and evolving local object and spatial relations, rather than merely reconstructing a future state in isolation.

3. **Achieving Direct and Integrated Policy Supervision:** Many latent WAMs integrate predictive supervision indirectly, either as additional context or through separate modules, potentially resulting in weak influence on the core action-generating representations. The motivation is to directly leverage transition supervision to shape the same policy backbone that generates actions. This objective led to the design of a *shared predictor* that is responsible for both latent transition modeling and the extraction of representations for action conditioning, ensuring that the learned temporal visual patterns directly benefit action generation.

4. **Demonstrating Generalization Capabilities:** A central objective is to validate that the proposed approach, by explicitly modeling transitions and directly shaping the action backbone, can achieve strong out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization across diverse visual and spatial shifts, while maintaining competitive in-distribution (ID) performance.

5. **Applicability to Pretrained Policies:** Beyond developing a new model, the research aims to demonstrate that the proposed transition supervision methodology can also be effectively applied to enhance the performance of existing pretrained VLA policies, improving their robustness without altering their original perception and action pathways.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more robust, generalizable, and deployable robot manipulation policies by addressing the shortcomings in how implicit and explicit world models handle state transitions and integrate predictive learning with action generation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

JEPA-WAM is designed as a latent world action model that leverages a pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) representation space and a shared predictor to couple latent transition modeling with continuous action generation. The methodology centers on defining a specific predictive target and integrating its learning directly into the action policy's backbone.

#### 3.1. V-JEPA Representation Space and Joint Current–Future Target

- **Foundation:** The system utilizes a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder ($E_J$) as its core visual representation space. This encoder provides dense, patch-level, spatially structured visual tokens, meaning their organization within each camera view and across multiple views is preserved.
- **Current Visual Representation:** For a given time step $t$, observations from multiple camera views ($O^v_t$) are independently encoded by $E_J$. The resulting visual tokens are concatenated to form the current visual representation $Z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{vis} \times d_J}$, where $N_{vis}$ is the total number of visual tokens and $d_J$ is the V-JEPA feature dimension.
- **Joint Current–Future Target:** During training, each current observation $O_t$ is paired with a future observation $O_{t+\delta}$ (where $\delta$ is a temporal offset). For each camera view, $O^v_t$ and $O^v_{t+\delta}$ are stacked along the temporal dimension and jointly encoded by the *frozen* V-JEPA encoder. This process yields the joint current–future target $Y_{t,t+\delta}$. A stop-gradient operation is applied to $Y_{t,t+\delta}$. Because V-JEPA 2.1's video tokenizer groups two frames into one temporal tubelet, this two-frame joint input produces the same spatial token grid as a single image, ensuring that $Y_{t,t+\delta}$ and $Z_t$ share the same camera and spatial-token ordering. This joint target is designed to capture the temporal relation between the two observations (e.g., stable vs. changing regions, local object and spatial relations) rather than solely the future state.

#### 3.2. Shared Predictor for Transition Prediction and Action Generation

- **Predictor Architecture:** A shared predictor $F_\theta$, initialized from Qwen2.5-0.5B, processes visual information and task instructions.
- **Visual Interface Alignment:** A lightweight visual projector ($P_{vis}$) maps the frozen V-JEPA representations ($Z_t$) into the input space of the Qwen predictor. The V-JEPA encoder remains frozen, while $P_{vis}$ and the Qwen backbone (via LoRA adapters during robot-policy training) are finetuned.
- **Shared Processing:** The projected visual tokens from $P_{vis}(Z_t)$, the task instruction $\ell$, and dedicated action placeholder tokens ($P_{act}$) are fed into $F_\theta$. This produces two main outputs:
 1. **Visual Token Hidden States ($Q^t_{wm}$):** These are the hidden states corresponding to the visual-token positions and are used for transition prediction. They retain the fixed camera and spatial-token ordering of $Z_t$.
 2. **Dedicated Action Representations ($C_t$):** These are derived from the dedicated action placeholder tokens and are used for action generation.
- **Latent Transition Prediction:**
 - **Prediction Head ($G_\phi$):** The visual token hidden states $Q^t_{wm}$ are passed through a lightweight prediction head $G_\phi$ (an MLP) to generate $\hat{Y}_{t,t+\delta}$, a prediction of the joint current–future target.
 - **Loss Function ($L_{wm}$):** The model is optimized using a mean patch-level cosine distance between $\hat{Y}_{t,t+\delta}$ and the ground-truth $Y_{t,t+\delta}$. This loss directly updates the shared predictor $F_\theta$ and $G_\phi$, ensuring the backbone learns patch-level temporal patterns.
- **Action Prediction and Generation:**
 - **Action Expert ($A_\psi$):** A DiT-based flow-matching model serves as the action expert. It receives the proprioceptive state ($s_t$), learnable future tokens, and the dedicated action representations $C_t$.
 - **Loss Function ($L_{act}$):** The action expert is trained with a conditional flow matching objective (e.g., velocity prediction), aiming to predict a continuous action chunk $a$ given noisy actions, flow time, and context.

#### 3.3. Joint Training and Deployment

- **Joint Optimization:** During policy training, JEPA-WAM optimizes a combined loss function: $L = L_{act} + \lambda_{wm}L_{wm}$, where $\lambda_{wm}$ is a weighting factor. Both losses contribute to updating the shared predictor $F_\theta$.
- **Deployment:** For inference, the target branch, including the V-JEPA target encoder and the prediction head $G_\phi$, is removed. The deployed policy consists solely of the current-frame V-JEPA encoder, visual projector, shared predictor, and action expert, making it efficient for real-time operation.

#### 3.4. Transfer to Pretrained VLA Policies

- **Auxiliary Branch Integration:** The same joint current–future transition target formulation can be applied to pretrained VLA policies (e.g., $\pi_{0.5}$) without modifying their original perception or action pathways.
- **Future Tokens and Alignment:** A set of learnable *future tokens* is introduced into the VLM prefix of the pretrained policy. Their output hidden states ($R_t$) are reshaped into a coarse 2D feature map, projected, and spatially upsampled to match the resolution and feature dimension of the V-JEPA joint current–future target $Y_{t,t+\delta}$.
- **Supervision:** An auxiliary transition loss $L_{wm}$ is applied to this predicted target. Crucially, action tokens are masked from attending to these newly introduced future tokens, ensuring that the original action pathway of the pretrained policy remains unchanged while its backbone is influenced by the transition supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated JEPA-WAM across various simulation benchmarks and real-world manipulation tasks, focusing on in-distribution (ID) performance and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, as well as an analysis of its design choices.

#### 4.1. Generalization in Simulation

- **LIBERO (In-Distribution):** JEPA-WAM achieved an average success rate of 96.7%, demonstrating competitive performance compared to other methods without large-scale robot-policy pretraining. When integrated with the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ policy, the approach (labeled $\pi_{0.5}$ + JEPA Obj.) further improved its average success rate from 96.9% to 97.8%, indicating that transition modeling can benefit strong existing VLA policies.
- **LIBERO-Plus (Out-of-Distribution):** This benchmark tests generalization under various visual and spatial shifts without OOD fine-tuning. JEPA-WAM achieved 79.2% average success, which was the highest among methods not utilizing large-scale robot-policy pretraining. The $\pi_{0.5}$ + JEPA Obj. instantiation showed an improvement from 84.5% to 86.3% over the base $\pi_{0.5}$, achieving the best overall performance. These results suggest robust scene-level generalization.
- **RoboTwin 2.0 (Clean/Random):** On this benchmark involving randomized bimanual manipulation, JEPA-WAM achieved 79.9% on Clean settings and 36.9% on Random settings. This substantially surpassed other methods without robot-policy pretraining and demonstrated comparable Random performance to the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$. The $\pi_{0.5}$ + JEPA Obj. variant further improved the Clean performance of $\pi_{0.5}$ from 75.4% to 84.6%, while maintaining similar Random performance (37.5% vs. 37.2%).

#### 4.2. Design Analysis (Ablation Studies on LIBERO-Plus)

Controlled ablations were conducted to understand the contribution of key design elements:

- **Visual Representation:** Comparing V-JEPA with DINOv2+SigLIP (both without transition prediction), the V-JEPA-only variant improved average success from 73.2% to 77.0%. This indicates that the V-JEPA latent space itself provides a strong foundation for OOD policy learning, particularly under camera, lighting, and noise perturbations.
- **Transition Target Formulation:**
 - Replacing the *joint current–future target* with a *future-only target* resulted in 77.3% success (vs. 79.2% for joint).
 - Using an *endpoint-difference representation* (explicitly subtracting independently encoded current and future observations) achieved 70.9%. This suggests that jointly encoding both temporal endpoints is more effective than representing only the future or their explicit difference.
 - Further probe analyses showed that the joint target makes temporal separation more accessible, generalizes better to unseen temporal gaps, and better predicts within-interval trajectory structure even after removing direct endpoint displacement.
- **Preservation of Spatial Structure:** An iREPA-style convolutional transformation applied to the joint target (74.7%) performed worse than preserving the original patch-level representation (79.2%), suggesting the importance of fine-grained spatial correspondence for transition supervision.
- **Interaction with Action Generation:**
 - Applying transition supervision to an intermediate layer (Lower-16 variant, 76.5%) was less effective than supervising the final shared predictor (79.2%).
 - Conditioning the action expert on all last hidden states (Full-hidden variant, 73.1%) was less effective than using dedicated action placeholders (79.2%), indicating that specialized action readouts help prevent interference between transition and action objectives.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

Experiments on an AgileX COBOT Magic bimanual platform with five manipulation tasks evaluated performance under both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) settings (changes in background or object configurations).

- **JEPA-WAM:** Achieved an average score of 59.8% ID and 54.2% OOD, demonstrating strong generalization under scene appearance and object configuration changes, consistent with simulation results. This was an improvement over the $\pi_0$ baseline (51.8% ID, 22.5% OOD).
- **$\pi_{0.5}$ + JEPA Obj.:** Improved the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ from 77.5% to 90.3% ID and from 72.5% to 84.7% OOD. This further confirms that the proposed transition supervision benefits both ID and OOD performance of pretrained VLA policies in real-world settings.
- **Inference Efficiency:** JEPA-WAM demonstrated an inference latency of 85 ms (11.76 Hz) on RoboTwin, which is an efficient rate for deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces JEPA-WAM, a novel approach to learning robust robot control policies by integrating latent transition modeling into the vision-language-action (VLA) framework. This work offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** JEPA-WAM demonstrates improved out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization across diverse visual and spatial shifts in both simulation and real-world environments. This suggests a step towards more reliable robot agents that can operate effectively in varied and unpredictable settings, a critical challenge in robotics.

2. **Efficient World Modeling:** By adopting a latent world action model built in the V-JEPA representation space, JEPA-WAM avoids the high computational costs associated with explicit future-frame generation in traditional WAMs. This makes the approach more practical and deployable for real-world robotic systems, where inference efficiency is a key concern.

3. **Innovative Predictive Target Design:** The introduction of a spatially structured joint current–future target is a notable innovation. This target captures the temporal relationship between observations, emphasizing localized changes and evolving object/spatial relations at a fine-grained, patch level, rather than solely predicting a future state. This design choice has been shown to be more effective than alternative target formulations (e.g., future-only or endpoint-difference representations).

4. **Direct Integration of World Modeling with Policy Learning:** The methodology effectively couples latent transition prediction with continuous action generation through a shared predictor. This direct supervision of the policy backbone ensures that the learned temporal visual patterns directly inform and benefit action generation, leading to more robust action conditioning. This addresses a limitation of prior methods where world modeling and action generation were more loosely connected.

5. **Extensibility to Pretrained Policies:** The ability to instantiate the same transition supervision mechanism within existing pretrained VLA policies (e.g., $\pi_{0.5}$) without modifying their core perception or action pathways is a significant practical advantage. This allows for leveraging pre-existing knowledge in powerful foundation models while enhancing their robustness and generalization, potentially accelerating the development of capable robotic systems.

The findings collectively indicate that explicitly modeling task-shared visual temporal structure, particularly in a spatially structured, patch-level manner and directly integrating this supervision into the action-generating backbone, can yield substantial benefits for robot control. The research provides a foundation for developing more capable and general-purpose robot manipulation policies.

A recognized limitation of the current JEPA-WAM design is its focus on learning general visual temporal structures independent of language. Future work exploring language-conditioned or multimodal transition targets could enhance its expressiveness for tasks where instructions significantly alter how observations relate to future states.
