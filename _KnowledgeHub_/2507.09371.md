# Constrained Style Learning from Imperfect Demonstrations under Task Optimality

> [!summary] Summary
> ConsMimic, a framework for robot learning from ETH Zurich, proposes a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) approach to integrate stylistic behaviors from imperfect demonstrations while ensuring near-optimal task performance. It achieved more natural and energy-efficient locomotion on a quadruped robot, reducing mechanical work by 14.5% compared to policies trained solely for the task.

## Problem

- Integrating fine-grained stylistic behaviors into robotic control through Reinforcement Learning (RL) is challenging due to the difficulty of crafting explicit reward functions for such nuances.
- Learning from Demonstration (LfD) methods often struggle with imperfect, incomplete, or misaligned demonstrations, as they tend to prioritize stylistic imitation at the expense of task performance.
- Existing approaches rely on manual tuning of fixed reward weights to balance style and task, which is labor-intensive, lacks guarantees of near-optimal task performance, and struggles to adapt dynamically.

## Method

- **ConsMimic** formulates the problem as a ==Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP)==, maximizing a style-imitation objective under explicit constraints that ensure the agent maintains near-optimal task performance.
- An adaptively adjustable ==Lagrangian multiplier== is introduced to dynamically balance the importance of style imitation versus task performance, adjusting emphasis based on whether task optimality thresholds are met.
- A novel ==symmetry-augmented style reward== formulation embeds robot-specific symmetry transformations into ==adversarial imitation learning==, counteracting mode collapse and reinforcing symmetric patterns in learned policies.

## Results

- ConsMimic consistently maintained task performance comparable to task-only baselines while achieving substantially better imitation quality across diverse robotic platforms and tasks.
- The symmetry-augmented reward significantly improved learned motion symmetry, with GR1 locomotion tasks showing improvements in symmetry scores (e.g., GR1-Flat increased from **0.779 to 0.814**).
- Real-world validation on an ANYmal-D quadruped demonstrated that ConsMimic policies reduced mechanical work by **14.5%** (1143 J vs 1337 J) and increased foot-air time by **32%** (0.37 s vs 0.28 s) compared to task-only policies, indicating improved efficiency and dynamism.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Framing style learning as a CMDP with explicit task optimality constraints provides a principled mechanism to reconcile the often-conflicting goals of stylistic imitation and robust task completion.
> - Dynamically adjusting the trade-off between style and task objectives using an adaptive Lagrangian multiplier allows for more robust and effective learning from imperfect or misaligned demonstrations.
> - Injecting robot-specific symmetry into the style reward, particularly through adversarial imitation learning, effectively mitigates mode collapse and promotes the generalization of symmetric and robust motion patterns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen significant advancements through Reinforcement Learning (RL), which has enabled robust and adaptive control for systems such as legged robots in real-world scenarios. However, existing RL methods often encounter difficulties when integrating fine-grained stylistic behaviors, such as agile locomotion or expressive motions, primarily because crafting explicit reward functions for such high-dimensional and nuanced styles is inherently complex.

Learning from Demonstration (LfD) has emerged as a complementary approach to embed stylistic imitation into robotic behaviors. Traditional LfD techniques include motion-clip tracking and adversarial imitation learning. These methods typically assume the availability of high-quality reference motions that are perfectly aligned with the desired task objectives. In practical applications, however, demonstrations are frequently imperfect; they may be incomplete, contain noise, or originate from biological counterparts with different morphologies or environments. For example, human motion data collected on flat terrain might not generalize effectively to tasks requiring navigation over complex, uneven terrains, leading to suboptimal task performance if strictly imitated. Prior work using adversarial imitation frameworks, such as Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) and its successors, have combined motion imitation with task execution effectively, but these approaches often implicitly assume that demonstration data will either enhance or at least not impede task performance. This assumption frequently does not hold when policies need to generalize across diverse conditions or when demonstrations are limited or misaligned.

Current approaches to managing the trade-off between stylistic fidelity and task performance often involve manual tuning of fixed weights for different reward components or the design of customized curricula. These methods are labor-intensive, lack explicit guarantees of near-optimal task performance, and can lead to policies that prioritize style at the expense of task completion when demonstrations are imperfect.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing a principled framework that draws inspiration from the domain of safe reinforcement learning, specifically Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs). CMDPs are commonly used to enforce safety constraints in RL. By adapting this formulation, the authors aim to "safely" learn from imperfect demonstrations, allowing robots to acquire stylistic behaviors without compromising their ability to achieve task objectives. The research contributes a method to reconcile the conflict that arises when task requirements diverge from the stylistic cues embedded in imperfect demonstrations, thereby enhancing the utility and robustness of LfD in real-world robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges of integrating natural, stylized behaviors into robotic control, particularly through Learning from Demonstration (LfD), while ensuring robust task performance. While LfD offers a promising avenue for acquiring complex motions that are difficult to specify via explicit reward functions, practical demonstrations are often imperfect, incomplete, or misaligned with the specific task at hand.

The paper identifies a critical problem: existing LfD methods tend to prioritize stylistic imitation, leading to a degradation in task performance when demonstrations are not perfectly suited for the task. For instance, a robot attempting to navigate challenging terrain might receive demonstrations from a human moving on flat ground. Strict adherence to such demonstrations would result in suboptimal task execution, as the stylistic cues are not conducive to the current environmental demands. Manually tuning the balance between task objectives and stylistic imitation via fixed reward weights is a common but tedious process that does not guarantee near-optimal task performance and struggles with dynamic trade-offs.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Formulate the problem as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP):** The central objective is to mathematically define the style learning problem as an optimization challenge where a style-imitation objective is maximized under explicit constraints that ensure the agent maintains near-optimal task performance. This formalization aims to provide a principled mechanism for balancing the often-conflicting goals of style imitation and task completion.

2. **Introduce an adaptively adjustable Lagrangian multiplier:** A specific objective is to develop a self-adjusting mechanism that dynamically weighs the importance of style imitation versus task performance. This Lagrangian multiplier is designed to guide the agent to selectively leverage stylistic cues from demonstrations, only incorporating them when they do not compromise the predefined task optimality threshold. This adaptive mechanism is intended to overcome the limitations of fixed-weight approaches.

3. **Develop a novel symmetry-augmented style reward formulation:** A common issue in adversarial imitation learning, especially with misaligned or partial demonstrations, is mode collapse, where the policy only reproduces a limited subset of the desired behaviors instead of the full range of motion. The objective is to counteract this by embedding robot-specific symmetry transformations directly into the style reward computation, thereby explicitly reinforcing symmetric patterns in the learned policy and improving the robustness of style generalization.

4. **Conduct comprehensive empirical validation:** The research aims to thoroughly evaluate the proposed approach across diverse robotic platforms (manipulator, quadruped, humanoid) and tasks in both simulation and real-world hardware. This objective is to demonstrate the framework's effectiveness, robustness, and generalization capabilities in achieving both high-fidelity style learning and strong task performance, even when demonstrations are imperfect.

By achieving these objectives, the authors motivate the development of a framework that enables robots to learn natural and agile behaviors from more accessible, imperfect demonstration data, thereby broadening the applicability of LfD in complex real-world robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce ConsMimic, a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP)-based policy optimization framework designed to learn style-aware behaviors while rigorously enforcing task optimality. The approach combines a CMDP formulation with an adaptive Lagrangian multiplier and a symmetry-augmented style reward.

**Constrained Style Learning under Task Optimality:**
The core idea is to model the problem as an extended Markov Decision Process (MDP) with two distinct reward groups: a demonstration-independent task reward ($r_g$) and a demonstration-driven style reward ($r_s$). Instead of combining these with fixed weights, the problem is formulated as a CMDP:
$\max_\theta v_s(\pi_\theta) \text{ subject to } v_g(\pi_\theta) \ge \alpha v_{g^*}$
Here, $v_s(\pi_\theta)$ is the expected style value of policy $\pi_\theta$, $v_g(\pi_\theta)$ is the expected task value, and $v_{g^*}$ denotes the optimal achievable task performance. The parameter $\alpha \in$ sets a threshold for acceptable task performance relative to this optimum.

To solve this CMDP, the authors employ the Lagrangian method, converting it into a dual problem:
$\min_{\lambda \ge 0} \max_\theta L(\theta, \lambda) = v_s(\pi_\theta) + \lambda (v_g(\pi_\theta) - \alpha v_{g^*})$
The Lagrangian multiplier $\lambda$ dynamically balances task and style learning. It is adaptively adjusted: if task performance falls below the specified threshold, $\lambda$ increases, thus emphasizing task objectives; conversely, if task performance meets or exceeds the threshold, $\lambda$ decreases, allowing greater focus on style learning. For empirical stability during Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) training, a bounded multiplier using a sigmoid activation $\sigma(\cdot)$ on normalized advantages is used:
$A = \sigma(\lambda) \tilde{A}_g + (1 - \sigma(\lambda)) \tilde{A}_s$
where $\tilde{A}_g$ and $\tilde{A}_s$ are normalized task and style advantages, respectively, computed via separate critic networks using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).

**Online Update of the Task Constraint:**
Recognizing the difficulty of pre-defining an "oracle" optimal baseline $v_{g^*}$, the framework introduces a method for online updates. Initially, a warm-up phase trains the policy solely on the task reward (by setting the imitation weight to zero, equivalent to $\sigma(\lambda)=1$), establishing an initial $v_{g^*}$ from the statistical average task value of the converged warm-up policy. During subsequent joint training, $v_{g^*}$ is monotonically updated by tracking the best statistical task value $v_g(\pi)$ observed:
$v_{g^*} \leftarrow \max(v_{g^*}, v_g(\pi))$
This ensures the constraint $v_g(\pi) \ge \alpha v_{g^*}$ remains feasible and adapts to empirically demonstrated performance, requiring the policy to maintain at least a fraction $\alpha$ of the new best task value.

**Style Reward Formulation:**
- For **manipulation tasks** (Franka arm), a **motion clip tracking** reward is used: $r_s^{\text{track}} = \exp(-\sum_i w_i (s_i - \hat{s}_i)^2)$, rewarding close alignment of the agent's trajectory with a reference.
- For **locomotion tasks** (ANYmal, GR1), an **adversarial imitation learning** approach, similar to Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), is employed. A discriminator $D_\phi$ is trained to distinguish between expert demonstrations and agent-generated transitions. The adversarial style reward is defined as: $r_s^{\text{adv}}(s_t, s_{t+1}) = \max(0, 1 - 0.25 (D_\phi(\Phi(s_t), \Phi(s_{t+1})) - 1)^2)$, where $\Phi$ is a feature selector. The discriminator is optimized to minimize a loss function incorporating a gradient penalty for stability.

**Symmetry-Augmented Style Learning:**
To counteract mode collapse and promote symmetric motion patterns, particularly when demonstrations are misaligned, the authors inject symmetry directly into the style reward. Robot-specific symmetry transformation operators $L_g$ are defined for a set of symmetry transformations $G$. Both demonstration and policy-generated data are augmented with these mirrored transitions ($B_{\text{sym}} = B \cup \bigcup_{g \in G} L_g(B)$). The symmetry-augmented style reward averages the discriminator outputs over all mirrored transitions:
$r_s^{\text{sym}}(s_t, s_{t+1}) = \frac{1}{|G|+1} \left[ r_s^{\text{adv}}(s_t, s_{t+1}) + \sum_{g \in G} r_s^{\text{adv}}(L_g(s_t, s_{t+1})) \right]$
This formulation guides the discriminator and the reward signal toward recognizing and enforcing symmetric behaviors.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is validated across three robotic platforms and tasks:
1. **Franka-Reach:** A Franka arm reaching a goal with demonstrations showing sinusoidal trajectories.
2. **ANYmal-D Quadruped (Anymal-Forward, Anymal-Lateral):** Velocity tracking tasks with demonstrations primarily consisting of forward-trotting motions.
3. **GR1 Humanoid (GR1-Flat, GR1-Stair, GR1-Stepstone):** Velocity tracking over varied terrains using demonstrations collected on flat ground.

ConsMimic is compared against a task-only baseline (ignoring style) and fixed-weight baselines (style reward contributes 0.2 or 0.5 times the total reward group weight). Evaluation metrics include task reward, imitation score (based on Dynamic Time Warping, DTW), and a symmetry score (also DTW-based). Real-world validation is performed on an ANYmal-D quadruped, measuring mechanical work and foot-air time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate ConsMimic's ability to balance imitation quality and task optimality, enhance motion symmetry, allow control via the $\alpha$ parameter, and demonstrate real-world benefits.

**Q1. Style Learning Quality:**
ConsMimic consistently achieved a balance between task performance and imitation quality across all six tested tasks (Franka-Reach, Anymal-Forward, Anymal-Lateral, GR1-Flat, GR1-Stair, GR1-Stepstone).
- **Task Performance:** ConsMimic maintained task rewards comparable to the task-only baseline, demonstrating its capacity to enforce near-optimal task performance even while integrating stylistic cues. In contrast, baselines with aggressive imitation weights (e.g., $\omega_s^{0.5}$) often struggled to complete tasks, particularly in complex scenarios like GR1-Stair and GR1-Stepstone.
- **Imitation Quality:** ConsMimic demonstrated significantly better imitation quality compared to the task-only baseline and fixed-weight baselines that successfully completed the tasks. While an aggressive fixed-weight baseline ($\omega_s^{0.5}$) might achieve higher imitation scores in some cases, this was at the cost of degraded task performance or outright failure. For tasks with prominent style-task misalignment (e.g., Anymal-Lateral), ConsMimic achieved high task performance close to the task-only baseline while substantially surpassing fixed-weight baselines in imitation quality. This indicates its ability to effectively leverage stylistic cues without compromising task objectives. The adaptive strategy of ConsMimic led to better generalization and stable training under realistic misalignments.

**Q2. Symmetry-Augmented Style Learning:**
The integration of the symmetry-augmented reward formulation was evaluated using a symmetry score, calculated based on DTW distances between trajectories and their mirrored counterparts.
- Results in Table 1 demonstrated that incorporating the symmetry-augmented reward significantly improved the symmetry score across all GR1 locomotion tasks. For GR1-Flat, the score increased from 0.779 ± 0.021 to 0.814 ± 0.018. Similar improvements were observed for GR1-Stair (0.741 ± 0.025 to 0.811 ± 0.020) and GR1-Stepstone (0.642 ± 0.030 to 0.722 ± 0.022).
- This indicates the effectiveness of the method in promoting symmetric policy learning even when demonstrations are non-expert or partially aligned, enabling the policy to generalize symmetric motion patterns across various terrains while preserving task optimality.

**Q3. Effectiveness of $\alpha$:**
Experiments on the Franka-Reach task, varying the task optimality threshold $\alpha$ (0.8, 0.9, 1.0), demonstrated its effectiveness in controlling the balance between task execution and style imitation.
- When $\alpha = 1.0$, the policy prioritized achieving optimal task performance, with minimal integration of stylistic influences from the demonstration.
- With $\alpha = 0.9$, the policy achieved high task performance while incorporating essential stylistic features from the demonstration.
- At $\alpha = 0.8$, the agent assimilated the demonstration's style more fully, while still satisfying the required task constraints.
- These results confirm that $\alpha$ acts as an effective parameter for modulating the trade-off between task completion and stylistic fidelity.

**Q4. ConsMimic on Real-World Tasks:**
Real-world validation was conducted on the ANYmal-D quadruped hardware using a policy trained on the Anymal-Forward task in a zero-shot manner.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Policies trained with ConsMimic produced more natural and agile trotting gaits on the ANYmal-D, a style that is difficult to achieve solely with conventional RL task rewards.
- **Quantitative Metrics:**
 - **Mechanical Work ($W_{mech}$):** ConsMimic-trained controllers exhibited superior energy efficiency, with a mean mechanical work of 1143 J ± 450, compared to 1337 J ± 515 for task-only policies. This represents a 14.5% reduction in mechanical energy.
 - **Foot-Air Time (FAT, $T_{air}$):** ConsMimic policies demonstrated greater dynamism, characterized by an increased average foot-air time of 0.37 s ± 0.04 per step, compared to 0.28 s ± 0.02 for task-only policies.
- These quantitative results confirm that ConsMimic effectively translates stylistic motion imitation into tangible real-world performance enhancements, highlighting its practical applicability and robustness in robotic locomotion tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces ConsMimic, a framework that provides a principled solution to a prevalent challenge in robot learning: effectively leveraging stylistic demonstrations without compromising task performance, especially when these demonstrations are imperfect or misaligned. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

**Addressing a Critical Gap in LfD:** The primary contribution is overcoming the inherent trade-off between stylistic fidelity and task optimality in Learning from Demonstration (LfD). By framing the problem as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP), the framework allows for the exploitation of stylistic cues while rigorously guaranteeing near-optimal task performance. This is a significant advancement over previous methods that relied on heuristic reward weighting or curricula, which often failed to provide such guarantees or generalize effectively.

**Enhanced Utility of Imperfect Demonstrations:** The ability to learn effectively from imperfect, incomplete, or misaligned demonstrations broadens the applicability of LfD. Obtaining perfectly aligned, expert-level demonstrations for every specific task and environment is often labor-intensive and impractical. ConsMimic reduces this dependency, allowing researchers and practitioners to utilize more readily available, real-world demonstration data, which can significantly lower the barrier to deploying LfD in complex scenarios.

**Improved Robot Behaviors and Efficiency:** The work demonstrates that stylistic imitation, when properly constrained, can lead to more natural, agile, and efficient robot behaviors. The real-world validation on the ANYmal-D quadruped showcased tangible benefits, including a 14.5% reduction in mechanical energy consumption and a more dynamic gait pattern (increased foot-air time). These improvements are not merely aesthetic; enhanced energy efficiency and agility are critical for extending robot operational duration and improving maneuverability in complex environments.

**Robustness and Generalization:** ConsMimic's adaptive Lagrangian multiplier dynamically adjusts the balance between style and task objectives, contributing to more stable training dynamics and robust performance across a range of tasks and robotic platforms (manipulators, quadrupeds, humanoids). The symmetry-augmented style reward further enhances generalization by promoting robust, symmetric motion patterns, which are fundamental in many robotic locomotion and manipulation tasks.

**Principled Framework for Multi-Objective Control:** The CMDP-based policy optimization, coupled with an online task constraint update and adaptive weighting, offers a principled and flexible framework for managing multi-objective control problems in robotics. This methodology could be extended to other domains where conflicting objectives (e.g., safety, efficiency, aesthetics) must be balanced during policy learning.

**Future Directions in Robot Learning:** This research lays groundwork for future investigations into more sophisticated selective imitation techniques. The acknowledged limitation regarding the framework's current inability to explicitly discriminate between beneficial and detrimental features within demonstrations suggests an important area for future research. Developing mechanisms to selectively identify and leverage valuable stylistic cues, even within largely flawed demonstrations, could further enhance the robustness and adaptability of imitation learning paradigms.

In summary, ConsMimic represents a significant step towards practical and generalizable style learning in robotics. By enabling robots to acquire sophisticated, natural behaviors from imperfect data while maintaining task optimality and demonstrating real-world benefits, it contributes to the development of more capable, adaptable, and energy-efficient robotic systems.
