# Regularizing Action Policies for Smooth Control with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> CAPS introduces a two-part regularization on RL action policies — temporal smoothness (consecutive actions stay close) and spatial smoothness (similar states yield similar actions). On a real quadrotor, training with CAPS produced an almost 80% reduction in power consumption and a 96% improvement in policy smoothness compared to a state-of-the-art controller, while still training flight-worthy agents under a simpler reward structure.

## Problem

- Deep RL-trained controllers often produce oscillatory, high-frequency control signals that cause hardware wear, high power consumption, and even component failure.
- Standard RL formulations have no inherent mechanism to enforce smoothness; addressing it through reward engineering alone is tedious, indirect, and offers no guarantee of the desired behavior.
- Sim-to-real transfer accentuates oscillatory failure modes; post-hoc filtering of NN-controller outputs behaves inconsistently across networks, so smoothness must be enforced during training.

## Method

- Proposes **CAPS** (Conditioning for Action Policy Smoothness), an action-policy regularizer added directly to the RL objective rather than encoded in the reward.
- ==Temporal smoothness== loss `L_T = D(π(s_t), π(s_{t+1}))` penalizes consecutive-step action divergence; ==spatial smoothness== loss `L_S = D(π(s), π(s̃))` with `s̃ ∼ N(s, σ)` penalizes action divergence under small state perturbations.
- Training objective `J^CAPS = J_RL − λ_T · L_T − λ_S · L_S`; ==L1 or L2 distance== for `D`, with ==Gaussian state perturbations==. Architecture-agnostic, plugs into any continuous-control RL algorithm (PPO, SAC, DDPG).

## Results

- On a real quadrotor: **80%** reduction in power consumption and **96%** improvement in policy smoothness compared to a state-of-the-art controller, with a simpler reward.
- Reduced training time and consistently produced flight-worthy controllers across runs.
- Marginally worse raw training rewards in some cases (controllers more conservative), but never at the cost of learnability or deployment utility — verified on OpenAI continuous-control benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Smoothness is better enforced as a direct policy regularizer than as a reward shaping term — direct optimization of action consistency is more reliable than indirect reward engineering.
> - Two complementary signals (temporal across time, spatial across nearby states) cover the main sources of oscillation in continuous control.
> - The simplicity and explainability of CAPS make hyperparameter tuning tractable — `λ_T` and `λ_S` have intuitive interpretations and minimal interaction with task reward.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in training control policies for complex dynamical systems where manual controller design is challenging. However, a recurrent practical challenge with deep RL-trained controllers is the observed lack of smoothness in learned actions. This often manifests as control signal oscillation, leading to issues such as poor system control, increased power consumption, and accelerated system wear, potentially culminating in hardware failure. This problem is particularly relevant in continuous control settings and can be exacerbated during sim-to-real transfer.

Prior research on mitigating problematic RL controller behaviors has largely focused on engineering reward signals to indirectly encourage desired behavior. This approach, however, has limitations: it requires reward signals to numerically capture the desired smoothness, may rely on environmental information that is not always accessible, and operates through an indirect optimization pathway, offering no guarantees regarding the learned behavior. The black-box nature of neural network controllers further complicates runtime mitigation, as traditional filters have demonstrated inconsistent performance when applied post-hoc to neural network outputs, sometimes leading to system instability or failure.

While regularization techniques have been explored in RL, they have predominantly focused on regularizing neural network weights to improve performance on reward metrics or learning state representations, rather than directly shaping the characteristics of the action policy mappings themselves. For example, Liu et al. (2019) investigated weight regularization for reward performance. Jonschkowski & Brock (2014, 2015) applied temporal coherence to state representations. Concurrent work by Shen et al. (2020) explored policy regularization for spatial smoothness to improve robustness to noise, but did not specifically address policy behavior or temporal smoothness.

This work addresses a less explored aspect of RL policy optimization by directly regularizing the action policies to promote smoothness. It distinguishes itself by proposing a method that directly conditions the state-to-action mapping of the policy, rather than relying on indirect reward shaping or post-hoc filtering. By focusing on both temporal and spatial aspects of smoothness in the action policy, the research aims to provide a more direct, robust, and generalizable solution to the problem of oscillatory control in deep RL, particularly for real-world robotic applications and sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective regularization method that consistently improves the smoothness of action policies learned by deep Reinforcement Learning agents. This method aims to eliminate high-frequency components in the control signal, thereby addressing the problem of oscillatory control responses commonly observed in RL-trained controllers.

The motivation for pursuing this objective is multifaceted:

- **Observed Problematic Behavior:** Deep RL controllers, despite their ability to manage complex dynamics, frequently exhibit undesirable oscillatory control responses. This issue has been noted in existing literature and confirmed through the authors' own experimental observations, especially in continuous control tasks.
- **Detrimental Consequences:** Oscillatory control has several negative implications for practical system utility. It can lead to visible oscillations in physical systems, increased power consumption due to high-frequency actuation, overheating of components, and accelerated wear or even hardware failure. For resource-constrained systems like drones, increased power consumption directly translates to reduced operational time.
- **Limitations of Existing Solutions:**
 - **Reward Engineering:** The current dominant approach to condition RL agent behavior relies on meticulously crafting reward signals. This process is often tedious, unintuitive, and indirect, as it requires the network to infer desired behavior from numerical feedback. It offers no inherent guarantees that the specific behavioral trait (smoothness) will be learned effectively or robustly. Moreover, it can lead to policies over-fitting to simulated dynamics and failing to transfer well to real hardware.
 - **Runtime Filters:** While filters are effective in classical control systems for addressing oscillatory signals, experiments showed that they behaved inconsistently with neural network controllers. Applying filters post-training can alter the system's dynamic response from what the neural network was trained on, potentially leading to anomalous behavior or catastrophic control loss. Integrating filters during training can break the Markov assumption fundamental to RL or significantly increase the complexity of state representation.
- **Need for Direct Policy Optimization:** The authors posit that for behavior traits like smoothness, which can be directly defined with respect to the agent's actions, it is more effective and reliable to optimize the action policy directly rather than relying on indirect reward shaping.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The domain gap between simulated training environments and real-world systems often complicates the transfer of RL policies. Smoothness regularization, particularly spatial smoothness, is hypothesized to improve policy robustness to measurement noise and unmodeled dynamics, facilitating more effective sim-to-real transfer.
- **Enhanced Reproducibility and Explainability:** The sensitivity of RL algorithms to hyperparameters and initialization can lead to reproducibility issues. A more intuitive and direct regularization approach, with explainable hyperparameters, is expected to yield more consistent and reproducible results, reducing the need for extensive tuning or "cherry-picking" of successful policies.

By directly addressing policy smoothness, the research aims to improve the practical utility, reliability, and efficiency of deep RL controllers in real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces "Conditioning for Action Policy Smoothness" (CAPS), a regularization method designed to directly influence the smoothness of action policies during reinforcement learning training.

**A. Core Concept: Direct Action Policy Regularization**
Instead of relying on reward signals, which indirectly condition behavior, CAPS applies regularization penalties directly to the policy optimization function. This targets the neural network that represents the action policy, promoting specific characteristics in how states are mapped to actions.

**B. Components of CAPS**
CAPS incorporates two primary regularization terms:

1. **Temporal Smoothness Term ($L_T$):** This term penalizes policies when actions taken on the next state ($\pi_\theta(s_{t+1})$) are significantly dissimilar from actions taken on the current state ($\pi_\theta(s_t)$). Its purpose is to encourage smooth transitions between controller outputs over time, reducing abrupt changes in actions.
2. **Spatial Smoothness Term ($L_S$):** This term penalizes policies that map similar states to significantly dissimilar actions. It aims to mitigate the effects of measurement noise and modeling uncertainties by ensuring that minor perturbations in the observed state do not lead to drastically different control outputs. This contributes to policy robustness and is hypothesized to be crucial for effective sim-to-real transfer.

**C. Mathematical Formulation**
For a neural network policy $\pi_\theta$ parameterized by $\theta$, the standard policy optimization function $J_{\pi_\theta}$ (which is typically proportional to the Q-value for Q-learning based algorithms like DDPG, TD3, SAC, or based on the advantage function for policy gradient methods like TRPO, PPO) is modified. The CAPS optimization criteria, $J_{CAPS}^{\pi_\theta}$, is formulated as:

$J_{CAPS}^{\pi_\theta} = J_{\pi_\theta} - \lambda_T L_T - \lambda_S L_S$

where:
- $L_T = D_T(\pi_\theta(s_t), \pi_\theta(s_{t+1}))$: Measures the distance between consecutive actions.
- $L_S = D_S(\pi_\theta(s_t), \pi_\theta(\bar{s}_t))$ where $\bar{s} \sim \phi(s_t)$: Measures the distance between actions for the current state and a similar, perturbed state $\bar{s}$.
- $\lambda_T$ and $\lambda_S$ are regularization weights that control the strength of the temporal and spatial smoothness terms, respectively.

In practice, Euclidean distances ($D(a_1, a_2) = ||a_1 - a_2||_2$) are used as similarity measures for actuator outputs. The similar states $\bar{s}$ for spatial smoothness are drawn from a normal distribution around $s_t$, i.e., $\phi(s) = N(s, \sigma)$, with $\sigma$ representing expected measurement noise or tolerance.

**D. Advantages of CAPS**
- **Direct Control:** CAPS directly influences the action policy mapping, bypassing the indirectness of reward engineering.
- **Algorithm Agnostic:** It modifies the policy optimization objective and does not directly affect how value functions are learned, making it compatible with various continuous control RL algorithms.
- **Environment Agnostic:** The regularization metrics are based solely on the policy network's state-to-action mappings, requiring no additional information from the training environment.
- **Explainability:** The intuitive formulation of temporal and spatial smoothness allows for easier understanding of the behavioral traits being conditioned and facilitates tuning of hyperparameters.

**E. Smoothness Measurement Metric (Sm)**
To objectively quantify and compare policy smoothness, the authors propose a new metric, $Sm$, based on the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) frequency spectrum of the control signal:

$Sm = \frac{2}{n f_s} \sum_{i=1}^{n} M_i f_i$

where $M_i$ is the amplitude of the $i^{th}$ frequency component $f_i$, and $f_s$ is the sampling frequency. This metric provides the mean weighted normalized frequency, where lower values indicate smoother responses (fewer high-frequency components with significant amplitude). It addresses limitations of prior methods that focused solely on "peak" frequencies, which may not capture broad spectrum non-smoothness.

**F. Evaluation Phases**
The effectiveness of CAPS was evaluated in three phases:

1. **Toy Problem:** A simple 1-dimensional goal-tracking environment with known optimal linear control was used to qualitatively demonstrate CAPS's ability to learn smoother policies and reduce oscillations.
2. **OpenAI Gym Benchmarks:** CAPS was applied to four standard continuous control tasks (Pendulum-v0, LunarLanderContinuous-v2, Reacher-v2, Ant-v2) using four common deep RL algorithms (DDPG, SAC, TD3, PPO). Performance was evaluated based on mean rewards and the proposed $Sm$ metric.
3. **Quadrotor Drone (Sim-to-Real Transfer):** The practical utility was assessed by applying CAPS to a low-level attitude controller for a quadrotor drone. This involved training in simulation using a simplified reward structure (primarily tracking error) and deploying the learned policies on a physical drone. Performance was compared against a tuned PID controller, a state-of-the-art reward-engineered Neuroflight controller, and ablations of temporal-only and spatial-only regularization. Key metrics included mean absolute error (MAE), average current draw (power consumption), and the $Sm$ score from live flight tests.

**G. Ablation Studies and Comparison to Filtering**
Ablation studies were conducted on the drone platform to investigate the individual contributions of temporal and spatial smoothing. Additionally, the paper addresses why direct output filtering, a common technique in classical control, is not an appropriate solution for neural network controllers, illustrating potential negative impacts on system stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Conditioning for Action Policy Smoothness (CAPS) across various environments demonstrated its consistent effectiveness in improving policy smoothness, often with additional benefits to practicality and robustness.

**A. Toy Problem**
On a simple 1-dimensional goal-tracking task, vanilla RL agents (e.g., TD3) tended to learn aggressive, binary-like control policies, leading to high-frequency oscillations in the action space. In contrast, CAPS-regularized agents learned policies that closely approximated the theoretically ideal linear mapping, resulting in significantly smoother control responses and reduced oscillations, illustrating the fundamental mechanism of CAPS.

**B. OpenAI Gym Benchmarks**
Across four OpenAI Gym continuous control tasks (Pendulum-v0, LunarLanderContinuous-v2, Reacher-v2, Ant-v2) and four standard RL algorithms (DDPG, SAC, TD3, PPO), CAPS consistently reduced the smoothness scores ($Sm$), indicating improved policy smoothness.
- **Smoothness Improvement:** CAPS agents demonstrated lower $Sm$ values across all tested environments and algorithms compared to their unregularized counterparts. For instance, DDPG+CAPS achieved an approximately 85% reduction in $Sm$ for Pendulum-v0.
- **Reward Performance:** While CAPS led to improved smoothness, it sometimes incurred a nominal reduction in numerical rewards on tasks like Pendulum-v0 and LunarLanderContinuous-v2. This was attributed to agents exhibiting more conservative behavior in pursuit of smoothness, resulting in slightly slower goal achievement. However, this did not compromise learnability or practical utility. On other tasks, such as Ant-v2, CAPS-regularized agents sometimes achieved higher mean rewards while still being smoother (e.g., SAC+CAPS and PPO+CAPS).
- **Algorithm Specifics:** Soft-policies like PPO and SAC inherently learned relatively smoother policies than DDPG and TD3, which the authors hypothesized might be due to stochasticity allowing for better exploration. Nevertheless, CAPS still provided additional smoothing benefits for these algorithms.

**C. Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer on a Quadrotor Drone**
The most significant practical demonstrations of CAPS occurred on a physical quadrotor drone platform, where it was used to train low-level attitude controllers.
- **Reduced Training Complexity and Speedup:** CAPS enabled the training of flight-worthy drone controllers using a simplified reward structure (basic tracking error, with a minimum thrust component for real-world flight). This approach required only 1 million time-steps, representing a 90% reduction in data intensity and an 8x wall-time speedup compared to the previous reward-engineered Neuroflight framework.
- **Enhanced Smoothness and Power Efficiency:** CAPS-optimized agents exhibited significantly smoother motor actuation, with high-frequency components in the control signals being virtually eliminated (Figure 3, Figure 4). This resulted in an almost 80% reduction in average current draw (power consumption) compared to the Neuroflight agent and even lower power usage than a finely tuned PID controller (Table II). Peak motor usage was reduced by less than 20% compared to vanilla PPO.
- **Robustness and Reproducibility:** CAPS agents demonstrated improved consistency between simulation and reality. All agents trained with CAPS were flight-worthy, ensuring 100% reproducibility for this task. This contrasted with the Neuroflight framework, which often required "cherry-picking" of viable controllers due to issues with reproducibility and domain shift.
- **Tracking Performance:** While CAPS-optimized agents showed a slight loss in tracking accuracy (MAE) compared to Neuroflight and PID controllers, the mean overall error remained under 10 deg/s, which was deemed acceptable for real-world flight.
- **Ablation Study (Temporal vs. Spatial Smoothing):**
 - **Spatial Smoothing Only:** Led to very low $Sm$ scores (high smoothness) and significantly reduced current draw, but sometimes at the cost of forcing control signals into distinct bands and contributing to a higher mean absolute error (MAE). It demonstrated good sim-to-real tracking parity.
 - **Temporal Smoothing Only:** Provided better smoothness than unregularized training and better tracking than spatial-only smoothing but was more sensitive to domain shift, resulting in relatively noisier control in transfer compared to CAPS.
 - **CAPS (Temporal + Spatial):** Combining both regularization terms yielded superior overall performance, balancing smoothness, tracking accuracy, power efficiency, and robustness to sim-to-real transfer, without the drawbacks observed when using either term in isolation.

**D. Comparison to Filtering**
Experiments showed that naively applying classical filters (e.g., Median, Exponential Moving Average, Finite Impulse Response) to the outputs of trained neural network controllers could lead to inconsistent behavior, minor overshoots, oscillations, or even catastrophic loss of control (Figure 5). This highlights that filtering post-hoc is not a reliable solution for non-smooth RL policies and supports the need for direct policy regularization during training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research on Conditioning for Action Policy Smoothness (CAPS) offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of reinforcement learning and its practical applications:

- **Addressing a Critical Practical Gap:** The work directly tackles a significant, yet under-addressed, challenge in the deployment of deep RL controllers: the lack of smoothness and prevalence of oscillatory control signals. This issue is a fundamental barrier to the real-world adoption of RL in sensitive or resource-constrained physical systems.
- **Enhanced Real-World Utility and Reliability:** By enabling consistently smooth control policies, CAPS improves the practical utility and reliability of RL-trained agents. This is crucial for robotics, autonomous vehicles, and other control systems where erratic or high-frequency actions can lead to undesirable system behavior, reduced performance, and safety concerns.
- **Significant Energy and Maintenance Savings:** The demonstrated reduction in power consumption (nearly 80% on a quadrotor drone) is a major practical impact. Smoother control signals lead to less frequent and less aggressive actuation, which reduces motor wear, mitigates overheating, and extends the operational lifespan and battery life of physical hardware. This has direct economic and environmental benefits.
- **Simplified and Robust Training Pipeline:** CAPS allows for the use of simpler reward structures, significantly reducing the burden of complex and tedious reward engineering. This simplification improves the robustness of the training process, leading to higher reproducibility and consistent training of flight-worthy controllers, mitigating the need for manual "cherry-picking" of successful policies.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The combination of temporal and spatial smoothness regularization enhances the robustness of policies to unmodeled dynamics and measurement noise in real-world environments. This directly facilitates more reliable transfer of policies trained in simulation to physical hardware, which is a key challenge in robotics.
- **Generalizable Method:** CAPS is formulated as a regularization term applicable to the policy optimization objective, making it compatible with various continuous control deep RL algorithms (demonstrated with DDPG, SAC, TD3, PPO) without requiring changes to the training environment or reward function. This broad applicability increases its potential impact across diverse RL applications.
- **Provides Fundamental Insight into NN Control:** The research highlights that neural networks, when actuated at rates significantly faster than system dynamics, can be prone to learning non-smooth behavior. CAPS provides a mechanism to counteract this tendency, offering insights into the behavioral characteristics of neural network-based controllers.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work underscores the value of direct regularization techniques in RL for shaping specific policy behaviors, beyond just optimizing reward. It serves as a springboard for future investigations into other behavioral characteristics (e.g., safety, interpretability) that could be directly incorporated into the learning objective. It also opens avenues for further exploration into how classical control concepts, such as Lipschitz continuity, can be approximated and leveraged within deep learning frameworks for robust control.
