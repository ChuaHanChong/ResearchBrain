# SPIRAL: Learning to Search and Aggregate

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPIRAL framework trains large language models to optimize the combined use of sequential, parallel, and aggregative inference compute end-to-end. This approach yields models that achieve up to 11x higher scaling efficiency in pass@k and 13.5% greater performance with recursive self-aggregation on mathematical reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Large language models often struggle to effectively utilize scaled inference compute, leading to performance plateaus on complex reasoning tasks despite increased resources.
- Current reinforcement learning training paradigms primarily optimize LMs for sequential reasoning, neglecting test-time strategies like parallel sampling and aggregation, which rely on external, hand-designed scaffolds.
- Models frequently misallocate sequential 'thinking tokens' and generate redundant parallel traces, limiting the actual benefits of scaling compute.

## Method

- Introduced **SPIRAL**, an end-to-end ==reinforcement learning== framework, to train language models to optimally coordinate sequential, parallel, and aggregative inference compute.
- Formulated the objective using a two-level optimization, combining Set Reinforcement Learning for search trace generation with standard RL for aggregation trace generation.
- The framework decomposes the policy gradient to jointly optimize for diverse, useful search traces and effective synthesis of information from multiple attempts.

## Results

- SPIRAL achieved up to **11x** higher scaling efficiency in pass@k performance on mathematical reasoning tasks, demonstrating improved generation of diverse and useful individual search traces.
- Models trained with SPIRAL showed up to **13.5%** higher performance when evaluated with recursive self-aggregation compared to baselines.
- Recursive self-aggregation using SPIRAL-trained models scaled more favorably in performance-per-token, indicating enhanced efficiency from end-to-end learning of these primitives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly optimizing diverse search and aggregation processes through Set RL enables language models to discover more effective and autonomous reasoning strategies.
> - Training models to learn both search and aggregation significantly outperforms reliance on fixed, rule-based aggregation methods at test time.
> - Exposing language models to all three compute primitives during training allows them to internalize and efficiently utilize diverse forms of inference, bridging the training-test time gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in the field of large language models (LMs) related to their reasoning capabilities and the efficient utilization of computational resources during inference. While LMs have demonstrated advanced reasoning in specific contexts, particularly when substantial inference compute is applied, their performance on other complex tasks can exhibit stagnation. This is often attributed not to the inherent difficulty of the problems, but to the models' inability to effectively leverage available compute.

Existing reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms for LMs primarily optimize for sequential reasoning within a single chain of thought. This means models are trained to produce a single, high-quality trace, with the reward assigned based on the final answer. However, at test time, practitioners frequently employ additional inference primitives such as independently sampled parallel traces and cross-trace aggregation. These test-time strategies—like self-consistency, best-of-N, self-refine, and self-aggregation—often require hand-designed scaffolds or separately trained agents to orchestrate the various compute primitives. This disparity between how models are trained (primarily sequential) and how they are often deployed (leveraging multiple primitives) represents a critical gap.

This paper positions itself within the landscape of efforts to bridge this training-test time gap. It specifically builds upon advancements in Set Reinforcement Learning, which allows for reward assignment to a set of sampled actions, and applies this to the context of language model generation. By proposing a framework that trains LMs to explicitly learn to use sequential, parallel, and aggregative compute primitives end-to-end, the work aims to enable models to autonomously discover and utilize more effective search procedures, moving beyond rigid, human-engineered heuristics. This contrasts with approaches that focus solely on supervised fine-tuning for parallel thinking or those that only optimize the synthesis step, treating the generation of input sets as fixed.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable language models to learn how to optimally utilize and coordinate all three fundamental primitives of inference compute—sequential, parallel, and aggregative—as part of a unified inference pipeline. This learning process is intended to occur during training, rather than relying on post-training, hand-designed scaffolds.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations in current LM reasoning:

- **Ineffective Use of Scaled Compute:** Despite the potential for LMs to resolve complex challenges when inference compute is scaled, performance on other rigorous tasks often fails to improve proportionally. This suggests an inefficiency in how compute is translated into effective problem-solving.
- **Misallocation of Sequential Compute:** When allowed to allocate more "thinking tokens" (sequential compute), models frequently misallocate this budget. They may over-detail routine operations while under-exploring critical logical leaps, leading to suboptimal problem-solving paths.
- **Lack of Diversity in Parallel Exploration:** Scaling parallel compute involves independently sampling multiple reasoning traces. However, current models often produce redundant or highly correlated attempts, failing to explore the solution space broadly enough to discover diverse, potentially complementary ideas. This limits the benefits of parallelization.
- **Struggles with Aggregative Compute:** When tasked with synthesizing information from multiple candidate traces (aggregative compute), models often struggle to natively verify, filter, and combine disparate ideas effectively. This necessitates external mechanisms or human intervention to refine generations.
- **Training-Time Blindness to Test-Time Strategies:** A primary motivation is the observation that current RL training paradigms typically optimize LMs only for sequential compute. Consequently, models are not exposed to, and therefore do not learn to actively coordinate, parallel and aggregative primitives during training, even though these are routinely used during test-time deployment. This leads to a suboptimal orchestration of diverse computing primitives, which is currently left to external scaffolds.

By addressing these limitations, the authors aim to develop a framework where the language model itself learns to integrate these compute primitives, thereby bridging the gap between training and test-time deployment and potentially discovering more effective and robust search strategies. The ultimate goal is to improve the scaling of language model performance with inference compute on challenging reasoning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **Sequential-Parallel-Aggregative Reinforcement Learning (Spiral)**, a framework designed to train a language model to optimize its use of sequential, parallel, and aggregative inference compute end-to-end. The core idea is to expose the model to all three primitives during training and optimize them collectively against the reward of the final aggregated response.

**Problem Formulation:**
The objective is formulated as maximizing the expected reward of a final aggregation trace ($y^*$), conditioned on an input problem ($x$) and a set of parallel search traces ($y_{1:n}$). Each search trace ($y_i$) is generated sequentially from the problem, and the aggregation trace ($y^*$) is generated from the problem and the set of search traces. This can be expressed as:
$\max_{\theta,\phi} E_{y_{1:n} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} [ E_{y^* \sim \pi_\phi(\cdot|x,y_{1:n})} [r(x, y^*)] ]$
Here, $\pi_\theta$ represents the policy for generating search traces (incorporating sequential and parallel compute), and $\pi_\phi$ represents the policy for generating aggregation traces (incorporating aggregative compute). The framework allows for the possibility of using the same model for both stages ($\theta = \phi$) or distinct models.

**Spiral Algorithm (Two-Level Optimization):**
Spiral addresses the objective using a combination of Set Reinforcement Learning (Set RL) for search traces and standard Reinforcement Learning (Standard RL) for aggregation traces.

1. **Gradient Decomposition:** The gradient of the objective function is decomposed into two main terms:
 - **Set RL Gradient:** This term updates the policy for generating search traces ($\pi_\theta$). It is derived from a set-level objective function, $f_{spiral}(x, y_{1:n}) = E_{y^* \sim \pi_\phi(\cdot|x,y_{1:n})} [r(x, y^*)]$. This objective rewards a *set* of search traces based on their collective utility in enabling the aggregation stage to produce a high-quality generation. This encourages the model to generate diverse, yet collectively useful, parallel traces.
 - **Standard RL Gradient:** This term updates the policy for generating aggregation traces ($\pi_\phi$). It is a standard policy gradient, optimizing the performance of the aggregation trace with respect to its own reward. This trains the model to reliably synthesize a given set of generations into an optimal aggregated solution.

2. **On-policy Data Collection:** For each training step and a batch of problems ($x \sim D$):
 - **Search Trace Sampling:** $N_1$ independent search traces ($y_1..., y_{N_1}$) are sampled from $\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)$.
 - **Set Construction:** Following a recipe from prior Set RL work, $K$ distinct subsets ($G_1..., G_K$), each of size $n$, are uniformly sampled without replacement from the $N_1$ generated search traces.
 - **Aggregation Trace Sampling:** For each sampled set $G_i$, $N_2$ aggregation traces ($y^G_{i,1}..., y^G_{i,N_2}$) are sampled from $\pi_\phi(\cdot|x, G_i)$.
 - **Reward Evaluation:** A reward function $r(x, y^G_{i,j})$ is applied only to the final aggregation traces.

3. **Policy Updates:**
 - **Optimizing Search Traces (Set RL):**
 - An empirical set-level objective $\hat{f}_{spiral}(x, G_i)$ is computed for each set $G_i$ by averaging the rewards of its $N_2$ aggregation traces.
 - A set-level baseline $\hat{f}_{spiral}(x)$ is computed by averaging across all $K$ sets.
 - The set advantage $A^\sharp(x, G_i)$ for each set is calculated as $\hat{f}_{spiral}(x, G_i) - \hat{f}_{spiral}(x)$.
 - The marginal set advantage $A^\sharp_{marg}(x, y_j)$ for each individual search trace $y_j$ is then computed by averaging the advantages of all sets $G_i$ that contain $y_j$. This marginal advantage is used in the Set RL gradient.
 - **Optimizing Aggregation Traces (Standard RL):**
 - For each aggregation trace $y^G_{i,j}$, a standard RL advantage $A(x, y^G_{i,j})$ is computed using a per-set baseline (average reward of $N_2$ aggregation traces *within* set $G_i$). This reduces variance and aids learning.
 - The model parameters are then updated using standard RL algorithms (e.g., GRPO variant) with these computed advantages.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Base Model:** Qwen3-4b-Instruct-2507.
- **Dataset:** Filtered subset of POLARIS-53k, a mathematical reasoning dataset.
- **Compute Budget:** A key aspect of fair comparison was ensuring equal training inference compute (token budget) per problem for Spiral and baseline methods like GRPO. For Spiral, this budget (98304 tokens per problem) was distributed across $N_1=8$ search traces (max 4096 tokens each) and $K \times N_2 = 4 \times 4 = 16$ aggregation traces (max 4096 tokens each, for each of the $K$ sets). For GRPO, the same total token budget was allocated to 12 generations of 8192 tokens each.
- **Prompting:** Specific prompts are used for search trace generation (standard chain-of-thought) and aggregation trace generation. The aggregation prompt explicitly instructs the model to audit, synthesize, and verify, discouraging entirely new attempts.
- **Modified Advantage:** A modified advantage function (Equation 10) was introduced to discourage excessively long responses by zeroing out positive advantages for generations exceeding a target length. This was a practical modification to manage computational feasibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated Spiral against GRPO (a standard RL method focused on sequential compute) using Qwen3-4b-Instruct-2507 on mathematical reasoning tasks from POLARIS-53k, under equal training compute budgets.

- **Pass@k Performance (Parallel Compute Scaling):** When evaluating performance by sampling *k* independent attempts and checking if at least one is correct (without aggregation), Spiral demonstrated significant improvements. It achieved up to **11x higher scaling efficiency** compared to GRPO. This suggests that Spiral effectively encourages the generation of more diverse and useful individual search traces, as indicated by its higher token-level entropy compared to GRPO. The individual traces produced by Spiral are, on average, more likely to contain a correct solution when a large number of them are sampled.

- **Recursive Self-Aggregation (Parallel and Aggregative Compute Scaling):** When methods were evaluated using recursive self-aggregation (RSA), a test-time strategy that repeatedly aggregates parallel candidate traces, Spiral showed a substantial advantage. Spiral gained up to **13.5% higher performance** compared to the base model and GRPO. This finding indicates that Spiral learns search and refinement behaviors that are particularly well-suited to leveraging both parallel and aggregative inference compute at test time.

- **Sequential Compute Scaling:** When only sequential compute was scaled (i.e., allowing longer individual chain-of-thought traces without parallelization or aggregation), all methods (Spiral, GRPO, and the base model) performed approximately similarly. This suggests that the primary benefits of Spiral arise from its ability to coordinate and utilize parallel and aggregative primitives, rather than solely enhancing sequential reasoning.

- **Comparison to Rule-Based Aggregation (Majority Voting):** The research compared recursive self-aggregation (model-based aggregation) with majority voting (a rule-based aggregation procedure). Recursive self-aggregation generally scaled better with parallel inference compute than majority voting. Importantly, while GRPO and Spiral achieved similar results under majority voting, **Spiral exhibited substantially better performance under recursive self-aggregation**. This indicates that training a model to actively *learn* to search and aggregate—as Spiral does—is more effective for test-time scaling than relying on fixed, rule-based aggregation methods.

- **Token Usage Efficiency:** A comparison of various inference compute methods in terms of performance per token budget revealed that while sequential compute scaling can hit a performance wall (due to context length limitations), methods leveraging parallel and aggregative compute (like majority voting and recursive self-aggregation) can scale for longer. Among these, **recursive self-aggregation, particularly with Spiral-trained models, scaled more favorably** in terms of performance-per-token, demonstrating the efficiency gains from end-to-end learning of these primitives.

In summary, Spiral's training methodology leads to models that are more adept at exploring diverse solution paths and effectively synthesizing information from multiple attempts, significantly enhancing their performance when parallel and aggregative inference compute are scaled.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPIRAL framework presents a significant advancement in training large language models for complex reasoning tasks, with several potential impacts:

- **Bridging the Training-Test Time Gap:** The most direct impact of SPIRAL is its ability to bridge the discrepancy between how LMs are conventionally trained (focusing on single-trace sequential reasoning) and how they are often deployed at test time (utilizing parallel sampling and aggregation). By incorporating these primitives into the training objective, SPIRAL enables models to internalize and optimize their use, reducing the reliance on external, hand-designed computational scaffolds.
- **Improved Scaling Efficiency and Performance:** The empirical results demonstrate that SPIRAL leads to models that scale substantially better with inference compute, showing up to 11x higher efficiency in pass@k performance and up to 15% higher performance in recursive self-aggregation. This means that for a given computational budget, SPIRAL-trained models can achieve better reasoning outcomes, potentially enabling more complex problems to be solved or the same problems to be solved with fewer resources.
- **Learned General Search Strategies:** By jointly optimizing search and aggregation, the model learns to proactively generate diverse yet useful parallel traces, and then effectively synthesize them. This moves beyond rigid heuristics towards more autonomous and flexible search strategies discovered by the model itself, potentially leading to more robust and adaptable reasoning agents.
- **Advancement in Reinforcement Learning for LMs:** The work showcases a practical application of Set Reinforcement Learning to language models, extending its utility beyond initial theoretical formulations. The decomposition of the policy gradient into Set RL and Standard RL components provides a principled way to optimize multi-stage generation processes with collective rewards.
- **Foundation for Future Agentic AI:** The ability for LMs to learn how to manage and combine different forms of internal "thinking" (sequential steps, parallel explorations, and aggregation of ideas) is fundamental for building more sophisticated and autonomous AI agents. SPIRAL contributes a framework for teaching models these meta-reasoning skills, which could be critical for open-ended discovery and problem-solving in complex environments.
- **Guidance for Model Architecture and Training:** The findings suggest that future LM architectures and training paradigms could benefit from explicit design choices that facilitate and optimize these inference primitives. For example, the non-collapse of token-level entropy in SPIRAL-trained models provides insights into maintaining beneficial diversity during generation.

The authors acknowledge that this is ongoing work, with plans for further qualitative and quantitative analysis, including deeper investigation into diversity, compute allocation, and integration with other training strategies. This indicates that SPIRAL is a foundational contribution that opens avenues for further research into learned reasoning and efficient compute utilization in language models.
