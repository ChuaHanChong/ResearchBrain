# Progressive Reinforcement Learning with Distillation for Multi-Skilled Motion Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of British Columbia developed Progressive Learning and Integration via Distillation (PLAiD), a framework for continually acquiring and integrating multiple motion skills into a single deep neural network. This method leverages iterative transfer learning and policy distillation to mitigate catastrophic forgetting and achieve robust performance across a growing repertoire of skills in continuous control tasks like bipedal locomotion.

## Problem

- Endowing deep reinforcement learning agents with rich, multi-skilled motion repertoires for continuous control, especially when skills are acquired sequentially.
- Catastrophic forgetting, where existing methods struggle to integrate new skills without losing previously learned abilities or requiring extensive retraining.
- The challenge of combining multiple expert policies efficiently in continuous action spaces while maintaining performance on all tasks without hierarchical structures.

## Method

- The Progressive Learning and Integration via Distillation (PLAiD) framework iteratively combines deep ==reinforcement learning==, transfer learning, and policy distillation for continual policy transfer.
- A two-step process: an acquisition step fine-tunes the existing policy for a new skill, followed by an integration step using policy distillation to combine all acquired skills into a unified controller.
- An "input injection" mechanism allows augmenting existing policy networks with new perceptual features (e.g., terrain heightmaps) by initializing new weights to zero, preserving old behaviors while enabling new capabilities.

## Results

- PLAiD effectively acquired and integrated new skills, outperforming the MultiTasker baseline, which struggled with increasing task complexity, and showed less forgetting compared to the TL-Only method.
- The method demonstrated superior mitigation of catastrophic forgetting, exhibiting significantly smaller performance drops on previously learned skills (e.g., for 'steps' and 'slopes') compared to alternative approaches.
- Integrated policies developed through PLAiD achieved zero-shot generalization, successfully navigating a 'mixed' environment composed of various terrain types not explicitly trained as a combined task, and obtained the highest average reward (**0.698**) across five sequential terrain types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An iterative distillation strategy, sequentially learning and integrating skills, scales more effectively with increasing tasks compared to simultaneous multi-task learning, leading to faster skill combination.
> - Input injection effectively augments policies with new perceptual inputs without disrupting existing learned behaviors, enabling adaptive control for new contexts.
> - Progressive distillation is more robust at mitigating catastrophic forgetting than a single, large distillation step, preserving performance on prior skills when combining policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for continuous control, specifically addressing the challenge of endowing agents with rich, multi-skilled motion repertoires. Current DRL advancements have enabled agents to perform complex movements; however, a persistent challenge is the effective integration and management of policies for multiple skills, particularly when these skills are acquired sequentially. Each individual skill typically specializes in a specific task and its associated state distribution, and combining these without losing previously learned abilities (catastrophic forgetting) or requiring extensive retraining remains an open problem.

The paper builds upon existing concepts in machine learning and reinforcement learning, notably policy distillation and transfer learning. Policy distillation methods have been explored for combining expert policies, typically formulated as a supervised regression problem where a student model learns to match the output distributions of multiple expert policies. Previous work in this area, such as that by Parisotto et al. (2015), Teh et al. (2017), and Rusu et al. (2015), primarily focused on discrete action domains like the Arcade Learning Environment. This work extends policy distillation to continuous action spaces, which are common in motor control tasks.

Transfer learning (TL) (Pan & Yang, 2010; Taylor & Stone, 2009) is a related area that seeks to leverage knowledge from previously solved tasks to accelerate the learning of new ones. A common challenge in TL is "negative transfer," where pre-trained models might take longer to learn a new task than randomly initialized ones (Rajendran et al., 2015), and catastrophic forgetting, where learning new tasks degrades performance on old ones. Existing approaches to mitigate forgetting include appending new network structures (Rusu et al., 2015; Parisotto et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2015), reproducing data from all tasks (Shin et al., 2017; Li & Hoiem, 2016), or using selective learning rates (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) offers another solution by structuring policies (Tessler et al., 2016; Kulkarni et al., 2016), but these often do not address the reintegration of new tasks into a single unified controller capable of the entire repertoire.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a progressive learning and integration approach that does not rely on hierarchical structures, instead integrating all skills into a single deep network. It investigates the interplay between transfer learning and forgetting within the context of continuous motor control, offering a specific methodology for iteratively expanding and integrating skill sets. The approach shares conceptual similarities with the Powerplay method (Schmidhuber, 2011), which involves iteratively inventing, solving, and demonstrating the ability to solve all previous tasks, but this work specifically focuses on motor control tasks and provides a detailed evaluation against relevant baselines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from observations in human motor control, where individuals progressively develop and integrate a diverse repertoire of motion skills applicable in varied contexts. Translating this capability to artificial agents in complex, long-lived control applications requires systems that can incrementally acquire new skills and seamlessly integrate them into their existing capabilities.

The key objectives of this work are as follows:
- **Efficient Skill Acquisition:** To develop a method that allows an agent to efficiently learn a new skill or movement pattern, leveraging knowledge embedded in its existing control policy. This aims to minimize the training resources and time required for each new skill.
- **Seamless Skill Integration:** To reintegrate a newly acquired skill into a single, unified control policy that remains capable of performing its full repertoire of previously learned skills without significant performance degradation or catastrophic forgetting. This process should be repeatable, allowing for the continuous expansion of the agent's capabilities.
- **Continual Learning Framework:** To establish a framework, termed Progressive Learning and Integration via Distillation (PLAiD), that functions as a continual learning method. In this framework, tasks are not known in advance, and the system is designed to learn new tasks efficiently as they arise.
- **Policy Augmentation:** To introduce a mechanism, referred to as "input injection," which allows for the augmentation of an existing policy network with new input features (e.g., sensory information) without negatively impacting its established performance on previous tasks. This supports scenarios where new skills might require new perceptual capabilities.
- **Evaluation in Continuous Control:** To thoroughly evaluate the proposed PLAiD method in the domain of complex continuous motor control, specifically focusing on simulated bipedal locomotion across diverse terrain types, and to compare its performance against alternative learning and integration strategies.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a robust solution for continually expanding an agent's motion control repertoire, addressing issues of learning efficiency, catastrophic forgetting, and policy scalability within a single neural network architecture.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Progressive Learning and Integration via Distillation (PLAiD), is an iterative framework designed for continual policy transfer and distillation in continuous control problems. It combines principles of deep reinforcement learning, transfer learning, and policy distillation.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL) Framework:**
The problem is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The agent's goal is to learn an optimal policy, π*, that maximizes the expected cumulative reward. The policy is represented as a deep neural network that outputs the mean of a Gaussian distribution, from which actions in a continuous space are sampled (a_t ~ N(μ(s_t|θ_μ), Σ), where Σ is a diagonal covariance matrix). Policy optimization is performed using stochastic policy gradient methods, specifically utilizing the Positive Temporal Difference (PTD) update (Van Hasselt, 2012) for the advantage function A_π(s_t, a_t) = I[δ_t > 0], which limits updates to actions with positive advantage to enhance learning stability. A separate value function V_π(s|θ_v) is simultaneously learned via a regression problem to estimate future rewards.

**Progressive Learning Stages:**
PLAiD operates in two main sequential steps for each new skill acquisition:

1. **Acquisition (Transfer Learning - TL) Step:** When a new task ω_i+1 is introduced, the most recently acquired multi-skilled policy (π_i) is used as a starting point. This existing policy network is fine-tuned to learn the new task. During this phase, the primary objective is to acquire the new skill efficiently, and there is no explicit mechanism to retain previous skills, allowing the network to specialize in the new task. This exploits the potential for positive transfer from related previous tasks.

2. **Integration (Distillation) Step:** After the new skill is learned, the objective is to combine all past skills (π_0..., π_i) with the newly acquired skill (π_i+1) into a single, unified controller. This is achieved through policy distillation. The distillation process is formulated as a semi-supervised regression problem where a "student" policy (the integrated policy) learns to reproduce the action distributions of all "expert" policies (the individual skill policies and the previously integrated policy). To address the distribution shift issue common in behavioral cloning, the method uses a technique similar to the DAGGER algorithm (Ross et al., 2010). During data collection for distillation, portions of trajectories are generated by the student, and experts provide actions to guide the student towards their behavior, thus robustly combining skills. This step performs regression on both the policy (actor) and the value function (critic). The probability of selecting an expert action during distillation is annealed from 1 to 0 over 10,000 training iterations.

**Input Feature Injection:**
To facilitate the learning of new skills that might require new perceptual information (e.g., terrain awareness for a bipedal walker), the method incorporates "input injection." This allows for the augmentation of an existing policy network with additional input features without disrupting its original functionality. This is achieved by adding new input neurons to the neural network and initializing the weights and biases connecting these new inputs to the subsequent layer(s) to zero. This initialization ensures that the new features initially have no effect on the network's output, preserving pre-existing behaviors, while still allowing gradients to propagate and train these new connections as the agent learns to utilize the additional information. For example, a "blind" policy can be augmented with terrain heightmap features.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated using a 2D humanoid walker (pd-biped) in a simulated environment. The tasks involve traversing five distinct terrain types sequentially: flat, incline, steps, slopes, and gaps, culminating in a combined mixed terrain. The agent's goal is to maintain a consistent forward velocity of 1 m/s and match a natural human walking gait from a motion capture clip. The agent's state input includes character features (50 parameters like relative position and velocity of links) and, where applicable, terrain state (heights of 50 equally-spaced points in front of the character). The action space is 11-dimensional, corresponding to target joint positions for PD controllers, with reasonable torque limits applied.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
The PLAiD method is compared against three alternative learning curricula:
1. **MultiTasker:** A single controller simultaneously learns all tasks from scratch in a single RL simulation, with training time split across tasks.
2. **Parallel:** Multiple controllers are randomly initialized and trained in parallel for individual tasks, and then their policies are combined (though this baseline was challenging to implement effectively).
3. **TL-Only:** Tasks are learned sequentially using transfer learning from the previous task, but with distillation only occurring once at the very end to combine all learned policies.

**Network Architecture and Training Details:**
Policy networks feature two hidden layers (512x256 units) with ReLU activations and a linear output layer. Value functions share a similar design. For terrain-aware agents, an additional convolutional layer (8 filters, width 3) and a dense layer (32 units) process terrain features before concatenating them into the main hidden layers. Training uses Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with momentum, and exploration is guided by an epsilon-greedy strategy with epsilon linearly annealed from 0.2 to 0.1 over 100,000 iterations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PLAiD against the specified baselines yielded several findings regarding the efficiency, robustness, and scalability of multi-skill acquisition and integration in continuous control.

**Progressive Learning and Transfer Learning Performance:**
- PLAiD consistently demonstrated effective acquisition of new skills. In the early stages (e.g., learning on 'incline'), the TL-Only method could learn quickly due to significant prior information. However, as the number and complexity of tasks increased, PLAiD's integrated distillation approach proved more robust.
- The MultiTasker baseline, which attempts to learn all skills simultaneously, showed diminishing returns as more tasks were introduced. It performed comparably to PLAiD on 2-3 tasks (Figure 2b), but its learning speed and final performance on new tasks (steps, slopes, gaps) deteriorated significantly with 4-5 tasks (Figure 2c, 2d). This suggests that training many tasks together can become challenging, leading to slower learning and potential compromises in skill quality. PLAiD, by contrast, learned new tasks faster and integrated them robustly.

**Effectiveness of Input Feature Injection:**
- The input injection method allowed for augmenting policies with new perceptual information, such as terrain heightmaps, without adversely affecting previously learned behaviors. This capability was crucial for the agent to differentiate between various terrain types (e.g., flat vs. incline) and adapt its locomotion strategy accordingly. This mechanism enabled the combined policy model to incorporate new state features, expanding its operational domain.

**Distillation for Multi-Policy Integration and Forgetting Mitigation:**
- A key finding was that PLAiD's iterative distillation strategy scaled more effectively with an increasing number of skills than the MultiTasker. While the MultiTasker struggled to make trade-offs and sometimes favored easier tasks when learning 4-5 skills concurrently (Figure 3b, 3c), PLAiD's approach of sequentially learning a skill and then immediately integrating it via distillation (Figure 3d, 3e, 3f) led to faster skill combination and the discovery of higher-value policies in practice.
- Table 1 quantitatively demonstrated PLAiD's superior ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. When comparing the final policy after training on all five terrains to the original policies trained for individual tasks, PLAiD exhibited significantly less forgetting (smaller negative percentage change in average reward) than the TL-Only method, particularly for complex tasks like 'steps' and 'slopes'. The TL-Only method, even with a final distillation step, showed larger performance drops, suggesting that a single large distillation step might lead to overfitting or be less effective than progressive integration.
- PLAiD's integrated policies demonstrated zero-shot generalization, successfully navigating a 'mixed' environment composed of various terrain types that it had not explicitly been trained on as a combined task (Figure 7).
- The results also indicated a potential initial hindering of training during PLAiD's distillation steps (Figure 3d, 3e, yellow segments). This was attributed to a large change in the initial state distribution from the previous TL step, potentially causing large gradients that disrupt some structure learned during TL.

**Overall Performance and Efficiency:**
- Table 2 summarized the final average rewards across all tasks for each method. PLAiD achieved the highest average reward (0.698) across the five terrain types after training on 'gaps', outperforming TL-Only (0.647), TL-Only (with Distill) (0.723 for specific metrics but overall lower) and MultiTasker (0.688). This indicates PLAiD's effectiveness in both acquiring and retaining a comprehensive skill set.
- The paper highlighted that while the MultiTasker could sometimes learn individual tasks faster (Figure 8a), this often came at the cost of splitting training time across multiple tasks. PLAiD, leveraging distillation, was presented as more efficient in terms of simulation samples needed to learn new tasks and integrate them, as distillation is a semi-supervised process that can reuse collected data.
- An ancillary finding was that transferring value functions did not empirically assist the learning process, likely because the state distribution and reward functions change significantly with new tasks, making pre-trained value functions inaccurate.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes a method for progressive skill acquisition and integration that addresses key challenges in continuous reinforcement learning, particularly for complex control problems like locomotion. The significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Addressing Catastrophic Forgetting:** The primary impact is the provision of a robust framework (PLAiD) that significantly mitigates catastrophic forgetting, a pervasive issue in continual learning. By combining transfer learning with iterative policy distillation, agents can acquire new skills without substantially degrading their performance on previously learned ones. This is crucial for agents operating in dynamic, open-ended environments where new challenges emerge over time.

- **Enabling Continual Learning Systems:** The PLAiD framework supports the development of truly "lifelong learning" agents that can incrementally expand their behavioral repertoires. Unlike methods requiring simultaneous training on all tasks or complete retraining, PLAiD allows for sequential skill acquisition, making it suitable for real-world applications where tasks are revealed progressively. This has direct implications for robotics, virtual character animation, and autonomous systems that need to adapt and evolve their capabilities over long periods.

- **Extending Policy Distillation to Continuous Control:** The work successfully extends policy distillation methods, previously prominent in discrete action spaces, to the more complex domain of continuous action spaces. This broadens the applicability of distillation as a tool for policy compression, multi-task learning, and knowledge transfer in areas like motor control, where continuous actions are essential.

- **Practical Policy Augmentation:** The introduction of input injection offers a practical and effective mechanism for dynamically augmenting existing policies with new sensory inputs. This allows agents to incorporate new contextual information relevant to new skills without necessitating a complete redesign or retraining of the core policy, enhancing adaptability and flexibility.

- **Computational Efficiency:** Distillation, as a semi-supervised learning process, can be more sample-efficient than pure reinforcement learning from scratch for each new skill or task combination. By reusing data and leveraging existing expert behaviors, PLAiD can potentially reduce the overall computational cost and simulation time required to develop multi-skilled agents, which is a practical consideration for complex simulations and physical robots.

- **Insights into Human Motor Learning:** By investigating progressive learning and integration strategies in artificial agents, the research provides a computational model that could offer insights into processes observed in human motor learning, such as skill development, recall, and augmentation. This connection could foster interdisciplinary research between AI and neuroscience.

In summary, this paper presents a methodological advancement for building adaptable and extensible control policies. Its progressive and integrative approach via distillation offers a pathway towards creating more versatile and robust autonomous agents capable of continuously learning and performing a wide array of motion skills in complex and changing environments.
