# LiDAR: Sensing Linear Probing Performance in Joint Embedding SSL Architectures

> [!summary] Summary
> Apple researchers developed LiDAR, a metric that quantifies the intrinsic quality of representations in Joint Embedding self-supervised learning (SSL) architectures by adapting Linear Discriminant Analysis. It consistently correlates better with downstream linear probing accuracy and selects superior hyperparameters compared to existing rank-based metrics, often matching or exceeding oracle performance on out-of-distribution tasks.

## Problem

- Evaluating the quality of self-supervised learning (SSL) representations is challenging without costly, resource-intensive downstream task evaluations and labeled datasets.
- Joint Embedding (JE) SSL architectures produce uninterpretable loss curves, offering little insight into training progression or learned feature utility.
- Existing label-free metrics, such as RankMe, are limited because their feature covariance spectrum can be easily manipulated by regularization, leading to inflated ranks that do not necessarily correspond to semantically rich features.

## Method

- Introduces LiDAR (==Linear Discriminant== Analysis Rank), a novel metric that adapts the classical label-aware Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) to the self-supervised multi-view setting.
- Defines generalized between-class (Σ_b) and within-class (Σ_w) covariance matrices, treating different augmented views of a single input as a surrogate class.
- Computes the effective rank of the LiDAR matrix (Σ_lidar = Σ_w^(-1/2) Σ_b Σ_w^(-1/2)), which inherently captures discriminative information relevant to the SSL objective.

## Results

- LiDAR consistently shows significantly higher correlation (e.g., τ up to ~**0.7**-**0.8** for VICReg) with downstream linear probing accuracy than RankMe across various JE architectures like I-JEPA, data2vec, SimCLR, DINO, and VICReg.
- It consistently selects more performant hyperparameters than RankMe in both grid searches and challenging randomized trials, often achieving performance within a fraction of a percentage point from the oracle.
- LiDAR-selected models frequently match or surpass the performance of models chosen based on ImageNet Oracle validation accuracy on out-of-distribution datasets (e.g., CIFAR10, EuroSAT), indicating improved transferability and robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LiDAR inherently discriminates between informative and uninformative features by explicitly quantifying variability that is relevant to the SSL objective, a key limitation of naive rank-based methods.
> - By normalizing the between-class variance with the within-class variance, LiDAR ensures that high rank reflects genuine semantic richness and consistency across augmented views, prioritizing useful directions.
> - The metric provides a robust statistical estimator to quantify representation quality without requiring access to labels or expensive downstream evaluations, enabling more efficient SSL model development.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm for pretraining robust data representations from vast unlabeled datasets, thereby mitigating the substantial costs and labor associated with manual data annotation. This approach has led to remarkable advancements across various domains, particularly in computer vision and natural language processing. A prominent category within SSL is Joint Embedding (JE) architectures, which learn representations by encouraging similarity between different augmented "views" of the same input data while avoiding representational collapse.

Despite the successes of SSL, a persistent and critical challenge remains: how to effectively assess the quality of learned representations without recourse to a specific downstream task and its associated labeled dataset. This challenge is particularly acute for JE architectures, whose loss curves are often uninterpretable and offer little insight into the progression of training or the actual utility of the learned features. The conventional method of evaluating representations through downstream tasks is resource-intensive and time-consuming, especially when the goal is to develop versatile representations applicable across diverse applications. Limiting evaluations to a few tasks risks undermining confidence in the learned representation's general applicability.

In response to this, recent literature has explored label-free metrics based on statistical properties of the learned features. Notably, RankMe (Garrido et al., 2022) demonstrated that the effective rank of the feature covariance matrix correlates surprisingly well with downstream performance, establishing itself as a state-of-the-art method for label-free hyperparameter selection. Other works, like α-Req (Agrawal et al., 2022a), have investigated the ideal decay rate of the feature covariance eigenspectrum.

However, the authors of "LiDAR" argue that existing rank-based metrics, while promising, suffer from inherent limitations. The feature covariance spectrum can be easily manipulated, often by implicit or explicit regularization terms within SSL objectives, leading to representations with high effective rank that may not possess rich semantic content. For instance, regularization might inflate the rank without necessarily improving downstream utility. The paper cites examples, such as I-JEPA, where the representation rank can peak early in training and then diminish, with optimal downstream performance occurring at a different point, making naive rank tracking unreliable. LiDAR directly addresses these shortcomings by proposing a more robust metric that discriminates between informative and uninformative features, thus refining the assessment of representation quality within the critical context of JE SSL. This work seeks to advance the field by providing a more reliable and intuitive tool for evaluation, which is fundamental to accelerating research and practical application of SSL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to introduce and validate **LiDAR (Linear Discriminant Analysis Rank)**, a novel metric designed to measure the intrinsic quality of representations learned by Joint Embedding (JE) Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) architectures. The overarching goal is to enable efficient and reliable evaluation of these representations *without* requiring access to a downstream task or a labeled dataset.

The motivations driving this work are multi-faceted and address significant pain points in the current SSL landscape:
- **Lack of Interpretable Evaluation:** JE architectures are known for producing uninterpretable loss curves during training. These curves provide little guidance for assessing representation quality or making informed decisions about architectural modifications and training strategies.
- **High Cost of Downstream Evaluation:** The standard practice of evaluating representations by fine-tuning them on downstream tasks is prohibitively expensive in terms of time and computational resources. This becomes particularly problematic when aiming to learn versatile, general-purpose representations that would ideally be tested across a multitude of tasks and datasets. Such extensive evaluation hinders rapid iteration and development.
- **Limitations of Existing Label-Free Metrics:** While previous approaches like RankMe have made strides in label-free evaluation by leveraging feature covariance rank, they have critical shortcomings. The authors argue that the covariance spectrum can be artificially inflated or manipulated by regularization terms in SSL objectives, leading to high rank that does not necessarily correspond to semantically rich or transferable features. This means that a high rank, by itself, is not a sufficient indicator of representation quality if it doesn't reflect information relevant to the actual SSL task.
- **Need for Robust Hyperparameter Selection:** Without a reliable internal metric, selecting optimal hyperparameters for SSL models becomes a trial-and-error process heavily reliant on expensive downstream evaluations. A robust metric could streamline hyperparameter tuning, model selection, and early stopping criteria.

In essence, the authors are motivated to create a more intuitive and robust statistical estimator that can quantify the "quality" of learned representations by explicitly considering the underlying SSL objective. This metric aims to facilitate quicker model iteration, foster the development of novel SSL algorithms, and ultimately accelerate the broader adoption of powerful JE techniques across various domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LiDAR introduces a novel approach to assess representation quality by adapting Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), a classical label-aware dimensionality reduction technique, to the self-supervised multi-view setting. The core idea is to quantify the rank of an LDA-like matrix that inherently captures information relevant to the SSL objective, effectively discriminating between informative and uninformative features.

The methodology involves several key steps:

#### 3.1. Formulation of Generalized Covariance Matrices for SSL

Given an embedding function `e(x): X → R^p` (output of the encoder and projector, or just the encoder if no projector is used), LiDAR defines two generalized covariance matrices:
- **Between-Class Covariance (Σ_b(e)):** This matrix captures the variance *between* the mean embeddings of different "surrogate classes." In the SSL context, a "clean" input image `x` is treated as a surrogate class. `μ_x = E_{˜x∼D_x}[e(˜x)]` represents the mean embedding of all augmented views (`˜x`) derived from a specific clean input `x`. `μ = E_{x∼D}[μ_x]` is the global mean. Σ_b measures the spread of these mean embeddings `μ_x` around the global mean `μ`.
 - `Σ_b(e) = E_{x∼D}[ (μ_x - μ) (μ_x - μ)^⊤ ]`
- **Within-Class Covariance (Σ_w(e)):** This matrix captures the variance *within* each surrogate class. It measures the spread of individual augmented embeddings `e(˜x)` around their respective class mean `μ_x`. A small positive constant `δI_p` (where `I_p` is the identity matrix) is added for numerical stability, similar to regularization in classical LDA.
 - `Σ_w(e) = E_{x∼D} E_{˜x∼D_x}[ (e(˜x) - μ_x) (e(˜x) - μ_x)^⊤ ] + δI_p`

#### 3.2. The LiDAR Matrix (Σ_lidar)

The core of LiDAR is the construction of `Σ_lidar(e)`:
- `Σ_lidar(e) = Σ_w(e)^(-1/2) Σ_b(e) Σ_w(e)^(-1/2)`
This transformation effectively whitens the data by the within-class covariance (`Σ_w`), making the "between-class" variability (`Σ_b`) more prominent and discriminative. By normalizing the "between-class" variance with the "within-class" variance, LiDAR inherently prioritizes directions where different clean samples are distinct, while augmented views of the same sample remain consistent. This directly ties the metric to the implicit discriminative task of the SSL objective (i.e., making positive pairs similar and distinct from negative pairs, or predicting masked parts).

#### 3.3. Quantifying Rank with a Smooth Measure

LiDAR quantifies the "effective rank" of `Σ_lidar(e)` using a smooth rank measure inspired by Roy & Vetterli (2007):
- `LiDAR(e) = exp( - Σ_i p_i log p_i )`, where `p_i = λ_i / ||λ||_1 + ε`
Here, `λ = λ_1..., λ_p` are the eigenvalues of `Σ_lidar`, `||λ||_1` is their L1 norm, and `ε` is a small positive constant. This entropy-like measure provides a continuous and differentiable estimate of rank, more robust than a simple count of non-zero eigenvalues.

#### 3.4. Key Distinctions and Advantages over RankMe

LiDAR fundamentally differs from RankMe and similar covariance-rank approaches by incorporating the SSL objective into its definition. While RankMe measures the overall rank of the feature covariance, which can be easily influenced by arbitrary attributes or regularization (e.g., in VICReg encouraging high variance), LiDAR specifically quantifies the rank of directions that are *discriminative* with respect to the SSL task. This makes LiDAR inherently more robust to "uninformative" variations that might inflate the overall feature rank without contributing to semantic richness or downstream performance. The theoretical motivation in Appendix 8, particularly Proposition 1, illustrates how LiDAR penalizes representations that include irrelevant noise, thus ensuring that high rank corresponds to genuinely useful information.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details and Computational Considerations

In practice, `Σ_w` and `Σ_b` are empirically estimated. The number of surrogate classes (`n`) and augmented samples per class (`q`) are important hyperparameters. For transformer-based architectures (I-JEPA, data2vec, DINO), `n=1k, q=50` was found sufficient. For ResNet-based methods with longer feature vectors (SimCLR, VICReg), higher `n` values (e.g., `n=5k` or `n=10k`) were used with adjusted `q`. While LiDAR involves matrix inversion, potentially introducing computational overhead, the authors found this cost negligible compared to feature extraction, which often dominates the runtime. They also suggest dimensionality reduction techniques for cases where `p >> n` to reduce computational load without performance loss.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations demonstrate that LiDAR consistently and significantly outperforms naive rank-based approaches, particularly RankMe, in predicting downstream linear probing performance and in effectively selecting optimal hyperparameters across a diverse range of Joint Embedding (JE) Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) architectures.

#### 4.1. Superior Correlation with Downstream Performance

- **Consistent Outperformance:** Across multiple JE architectures including I-JEPA, data2vec, SimCLR, DINO, and VICReg (both ResNet and Transformer-based), LiDAR exhibited a significantly higher correlation with downstream linear probing accuracy compared to RankMe. This was quantified using both Spearman Rank and Kendall's τ correlation coefficients.
- **Discriminating Informative Features:** As theorized, LiDAR’s ability to discriminate between informative and uninformative features proved crucial. For example, in I-JEPA, vanilla RankMe often showed poor correlation, sometimes even with peak rank occurring far from optimal downstream performance (Figure 1). In contrast, LiDAR's rank trajectory more closely aligned with downstream performance. For VICReg, where high rank does not always imply high performance, LiDAR, especially when applied to representations rather than embeddings, achieved optimal results, highlighting that simple high rank is necessary but not sufficient.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness in Hyperparameter Selection

- **Grid Search and Randomized Trials:** LiDAR consistently selected more performant hyperparameters than RankMe. This was demonstrated through both traditional grid searches over individual hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, weight decay, mask scales) and more challenging randomized trials where multiple hyperparameters were varied simultaneously.
- **I-JEPA (Tables 1, 3, 2, 4):** LiDAR, particularly on the student branch, showed superior predictive power for optimal hyperparameters. While "augmented RankMe" (using data augmentations for covariance calculation) could sometimes match LiDAR's *best-selected model* performance, LiDAR generally exhibited stronger overall correlation. Notably, in randomized trials, LiDAR sometimes surpassed the ImageNet Oracle's performance on Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) datasets, suggesting its utility in identifying models with superior transferability (Table 24).
- **data2vec (Tables 5, 6):** Similar to I-JEPA, LiDAR consistently selected more performant hyperparameters and occasionally outperformed the ImageNet Oracle for OOD tasks.
- **VICReg (Table 7, 10):** LiDAR demonstrated significantly higher correlations across training checkpoints (e.g., τ rising from ~0.2-0.4 for RankMe to ~0.7-0.8 for LiDAR at later epochs). It also excelled in hyperparameter selection, often achieving performance within a fraction of a percentage point from the oracle (Table 10).
- **SimCLR (Table 8):** LiDAR showed the highest correlation and consistently identified the most optimal hyperparameters among the tested methods.
- **DINO (Table 16, 17):** Both student and teacher-based LiDAR measures exhibited stronger correlations with linear probing performance than RankMe.

#### 4.3. Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) Performance

- Evaluations on diverse OOD datasets (CIFAR10, CIFAR100, EuroSAT, Food101, SUN397) showed that LiDAR-selected models generally matched or exceeded the performance of RankMe-selected models. In several instances (e.g., I-JEPA and data2vec under random search), LiDAR-selected models even surpassed the performance of models chosen based on ImageNet Oracle validation accuracy on OOD tasks, underscoring its ability to identify truly robust and transferable representations.

#### 4.4. Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors

- **Negative Correlation:** In certain scenarios (e.g., VICReg with high-dimensional embeddings), LiDAR could exhibit negative correlation with probe accuracy, indicating that the relationship between rank and performance can be complex and non-causal.
- **Computational Overhead:** While often dominated by feature extraction, the matrix inversion step in LiDAR adds computational cost compared to RankMe, potentially making it unsuitable as a direct loss signal during pretraining without further optimization.
- **Scope:** The study primarily focuses on linear probing, with non-linear probing not investigated.
- **Method-Specific Sampling:** LiDAR's reliance on the sampling strategy for positive pairs makes direct cross-method comparisons challenging.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LiDAR represents a significant advancement in the field of self-supervised learning by addressing the critical need for an accurate, robust, and label-independent metric for evaluating learned representations. Its ability to discriminate between truly informative features and spurious variations—a key limitation of previous rank-based methods like RankMe—makes it a more reliable indicator of representation quality and transferability.

The potential impact of LiDAR is substantial:

- **Accelerated Research and Development:** By providing an efficient proxy for downstream performance, LiDAR can dramatically accelerate the research cycle for SSL models. Researchers can rapidly iterate on new architectures, objective functions, and training strategies without the prohibitive cost and time of extensive downstream evaluations. This will foster innovation and lead to the development of more powerful and generalizable SSL algorithms.
- **Improved Model Selection and Hyperparameter Tuning:** LiDAR offers a superior tool for hyperparameter optimization and model selection. Its consistent performance across diverse JE architectures and its ability to identify optimal models, even in randomized settings, will enable practitioners to more effectively configure their SSL training, leading to higher-quality representations with less effort. This is particularly valuable in environments where computational resources or labeled downstream data are scarce.
- **Enhanced Understanding of Representation Quality:** The theoretical underpinnings of LiDAR, which tie its definition to the SSL objective, offer a deeper understanding of what constitutes a "good" representation. By focusing on discriminative directions relevant to the self-supervision task, LiDAR sheds light on how different SSL techniques achieve their efficacy and what aspects of the learned feature space contribute most to transferability.
- **Facilitating Broader Adoption of JE SSL:** The current challenge of reliably evaluating JE methods without labels is a barrier to their widespread adoption. By providing a more trustworthy and intuitive metric, LiDAR can lower this barrier, encouraging more practitioners and researchers to leverage the power of these techniques across various domains, ultimately translating into more robust and adaptable AI applications.
- **Catalyst for Future Work:** The paper itself suggests exciting avenues for future research, particularly the exploration of LiDAR as a potential loss term during pretraining. While this would require significant algorithmic innovations to make it computationally tractable for SGD, its success could lead to SSL objectives that inherently optimize for transferable representation quality. The work also paves the way for deeper theoretical investigations into the properties of optimal representation spaces.

In conclusion, LiDAR moves the field closer to Galileo Galilei's maxim, "Measure what is measurable, and make measurable what is not so." It transforms the previously ambiguous task of evaluating SSL representation quality into a quantifiable and reliable process, promising to reshape how SSL models are developed, assessed, and deployed. Its integration into standard SSL toolkits is anticipated to have a profound and lasting impact on the field.
