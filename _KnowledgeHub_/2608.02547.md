# Why Does Action Chunking Improve Behavioral Cloning Performance in Robotic Control?

> [!summary] Summary
> Research by Lazzati et al. dissects why action chunking improves behavioral cloning in robotic control, attributing its success to incorporating delayed observations for reduced compounding error and its function as an implicit ensemble of policies. The study demonstrates that explicitly ensembling delayed policies can further enhance performance, with one benchmark showing a 30% increase in success rate over standard action chunking.

## Problem

- Action chunking is a standard component in modern robotic behavioral cloning, but the precise mechanisms underlying its observed performance benefits were largely unexplored.
- Commonly cited hypotheses for action chunking's success (temporal consistency, horizon reduction, representation learning) lacked comprehensive, systematic evaluation.
- A fundamental gap existed in the theoretical understanding of a widely adopted practical technique, limiting principled approaches to designing more effective policy architectures.

## Method

- The authors systematically evaluated existing hypotheses for ==action chunking=='s effectiveness through controlled experiments in both simulated (Libero, Robomimic) and real-world robotic control settings.
- They introduced and investigated new hypotheses, notably that action chunking policies implicitly function as **ensembles of delayed policies**.
- Different policy types, including standard Behavioral Cloning, various action chunking configurations, ==delayed policies==, and ==explicit ensembles==, were compared using diffusion models as the underlying policy parameterization.

## Results

- Delayed policies matched or surpassed the success rates of standard action chunking policies on the Libero-90 benchmark, indicating that predicting a sequence of actions is not always required.
- A Randomized Delay Ensemble (RDE) deployment strategy, which leverages the implicit ensembling effect, consistently matched or exceeded standard action chunking performance across simulated and real-world tasks.
- Explicit ensembles of policies, particularly delayed ones, significantly outperformed standard action chunking, achieving up to a **30%** increase in success rate on the Robomimic Transport task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action chunking's core benefits stem from its implicit ability to leverage delayed observations, which effectively mitigates compounding error and captures non-Markovian aspects of human demonstrations.
> - Action chunking policies act as an implicit ensemble of multiple delayed policies, contributing to their robustness and generalization capabilities beyond single-step or simple delayed approaches.
> - The full temporal consistency of predicting an entire sequence of actions is not strictly necessary; simpler delayed policies can replicate or exceed action chunking performance by addressing these underlying mechanisms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Action chunking, a method involving the prediction and execution of multiple actions as a sequence rather than individually, has become a standard component in modern behavioral cloning (BC) approaches for robotic control. Its application is prevalent across state-of-the-art robotic control policies, ranging from generalist models to those designed for high-precision dexterous maneuvers. Despite its widespread empirical adoption and observed performance benefits, the specific mechanisms underlying its effectiveness have remained largely unexplored.

Previous research introduced action chunking in works such as ACT and Diffusion Policy. Subsequent efforts have attempted to understand or enhance action chunking by focusing on aspects such as maintaining temporal consistency [42–46], adapting chunk lengths, or using world models for adaptive switching. Some theoretical investigations have also explored the minimal conditions under which action chunking offers benefits, particularly in mitigating compounding error in continuous control.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing a fundamental question: why does action chunking improve behavioral cloning performance? It aims to move beyond empirical observation and previous partial explanations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its underlying mechanisms. By systematically evaluating existing hypotheses and proposing new insights, the work contributes to the foundational understanding of imitation learning in robotics, with implications for designing more effective and perhaps simpler policy architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms that contribute to the success of action chunking in robotic control, specifically within the context of behavioral cloning. The authors state their intention to "close this gap" in understanding.

The motivation for this investigation is multifold:
- **Empirical Importance:** Action chunking has been established as a critical component for achieving high performance in modern behavioral cloning for robotic control. State-of-the-art policies across various robotic tasks consistently employ some form of action chunking.
- **Lack of Mechanistic Understanding:** Despite its empirical necessity and widespread use, the precise reasons *why* action chunking improves performance have remained limited and rudimentary. This represents a fundamental gap in the theoretical understanding of a widely adopted practical technique.
- **Evaluation of Existing Hypotheses:** The research is motivated by the need to rigorously evaluate commonly cited hypotheses for action chunking's success. These include:
 1. **Temporal Consistency:** The idea that action-chunked policies can better model the temporally correlated behaviors often exhibited by human demonstrators.
 2. **Horizon Reduction:** The hypothesis that action chunks reduce the effective prediction horizon of the environment, thereby mitigating the accumulation of error over time.
 3. **Representation Learning:** The suggestion that action chunking functions as an auxiliary loss, leading to improved representation learning and generalization within the policy.
- **Advancing Policy Design:** Beyond purely analytical goals, the authors are motivated to leverage a deeper understanding of action chunking's benefits to inform the development of improved behavioral cloning methods. This includes exploring approaches that can match or exceed action chunking performance, potentially without explicitly employing action chunking.

Through systematic experimental evaluations in both simulated and real-world settings, the authors aim to identify the specific factors that contribute to action chunking's efficacy, challenging existing assumptions and proposing new explanations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates action chunking within the framework of behavioral cloning (BC), considering interactions with potentially non-Markovian and partially observed environments. The goal is to learn policies that maximize success rate from a dataset of human demonstrations.

#### 3.1. Policy Types and Notation

- **Standard BC Policy:** `pπ` represents a Markovian policy trained to predict a single action `at` from the current observation `ot`.
- **Action Chunking Policy (AC(k)):** `pπk` is trained to predict a sequence of `k` actions (`at:t+k`) from `ot`. During deployment, `pπnk` signifies executing the first `n` actions of a chunk `k` before recomputing.
- **Delayed Policy (Delay(d+1)):** `πd_delay` predicts a single action `at` based on an observation `d` steps in the past (`ot-d`).
- **Induced Delayed Policy:** `πd_delay[pπk]` refers to a delayed policy derived from an action chunking policy `pπk`, where the `(d+1)`th action from the chunk predicted at `ot-d` is executed.
- **Randomized Delay Ensemble (AC(n)-RDE):** A deployment strategy where, at each timestep, a random delay `i` (from `0` to `n-1`) is sampled, and the corresponding delayed action `at` from `πi_delay[pπk](ot-i)` is executed.
- **Explicit Ensembles:** These involve training multiple policies (e.g., `pπk,i`) independently from different random initializations and aggregating their predictions (e.g., by averaging for `AC(n)-Ens`, `Delay(n)-Ens`, or incorporating randomized delays for `AC(n)-RDE-Ens`).

#### 3.2. Behavioral Cloning Implementation

- All policies are parameterized as **diffusion models**, chosen for their effectiveness in modern robotic BC.
- **Network Architectures:** Denoising networks are implemented as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). Conditioning networks are either MLPs (for Robomimic with low-dimensional observations) or Vision Transformers (ViT) (for Libero with image-based observations). Multi-task policies are trained for Libero, incorporating a one-hot encoding of the task.
- **Training Details:** Standard hyperparameters for diffusion models, including AdamW optimizer, cosine annealing learning rate scheduler, EMA for model weights, and min-max normalization for actions and low-dimensional states. Padding is applied to demonstration trajectories for chunking and delayed policies.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

- **Validation Error (`L_val`):** Calculated as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the mean of sampled actions from the policy and the true demonstrator actions in a validation set (`D_val`). This metric is used for analysis of prediction accuracy, rather than as a direct proxy for task success, to avoid complexities with multi-modality in diffusion models and for clear evaluation of individual chunk steps or delayed predictions.
- **Success Rate (`J(π)`):** The primary performance metric, representing the expected episode reward (or probability of task success).

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

- **Simulated Environments:**
 - **Libero Benchmark:** Specifically the Libero-90 suite (90 distinct tasks). Each task includes 50 human demonstrations. Observations combine proprioception and image data. Policies were evaluated over 40 rollouts per task, with a maximum of 400 timesteps.
 - **Robomimic Benchmark:** Four challenging tasks (Can, Square, Transport, Tool Hang) from the Proficient-Human (PH) dataset. Each task includes 200 human demonstrations. Observations consist of proprioception and ground-truth object states (low-dimensional). Policies were evaluated over 250 rollouts per task, with maximum timesteps varying (250-650).
- **Real-World Robotic Control:**
 - A Franka Emika robot arm was used for three manipulation tasks: "carrot in bowl," "bread toaster," and "sushi in cup."
 - 50 human demonstrations were collected per task.
 - Delta joint position control at 15Hz.
 - Evaluations involved 50 rollouts per method, using interleaved policy inference to mitigate real-world delays.

#### 3.5. Theoretical Analysis

- The paper includes theoretical arguments (Theorems 1, 2, 3 in Appendix D) to support the understanding of compounding error in Markovian, delayed, and action-chunked policies. These analyses assume deterministic, Lipschitz dynamics and policies.

#### 3.6. Experimental Procedure

- For each experiment, results are averaged over at least three independent random seeds (up to 15 for some results) to ensure robustness, with standard errors reported.
- Existing hypotheses (temporal consistency, horizon reduction, representation learning) are tested sequentially using controlled experiments that isolate the specific factors.
- New hypotheses, such as implicit ensembling, are introduced and evaluated experimentally and via deployment strategies like randomized delay ensembles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research systematically investigates the reasons behind the effectiveness of action chunking, challenging existing hypotheses and proposing new explanatory mechanisms across both simulated and real-world robotic control tasks.

#### 4.1. Re-evaluation of Existing Hypotheses

1. **Temporal Consistency (Section 4.1):**
 - **Human non-Markovianity:** Experiments on Libero-90 indicate that human demonstrators exhibit non-Markovian behavior. Specifically, a target action `at` is often better predicted by observations from `n` steps in the past (`ot-n`) than by the current observation (`ot`) (Figure 2). This suggests that policies leveraging past observations can better model human behavior.
 - **Delayed Policies vs. Action Chunking:** Crucially, delayed policies (`Delay(n)`), which predict a single action based on a past observation, were found to match or exceed the success rate of action chunking policies (`AC(n)`) on Libero-90 (Figure 3, Table 1).
 - **Conclusion:** While action chunking effectively captures non-Markovian aspects of human demonstrations, the full temporal consistency of predicting a *sequence* of actions is not generally required for performance benefits. Simply conditioning on a suitably delayed observation can be sufficient.

2. **Horizon Reduction / Reduced Compounding Error (Section 4.2):**
 - **Limited Explanatory Power of Non-Markovianity:** An analysis of individual Libero tasks revealed a weak correlation between how "non-Markovian" a demonstrator's behavior is (measured by `ΔL_val`) and the performance improvement of action chunking over Markovian policies (`ΔJ`) (Figure 4). This implies that non-Markovian expressivity alone does not fully account for action chunking's success.
 - **Theoretical Support for Compounding Error Reduction:** Theoretical analysis demonstrates that both action chunking policies and delayed policies can mitigate compounding error. In a setting with deterministic and smooth dynamics, the suboptimality of a behavioral cloning policy can scale exponentially with the horizon for Markovian policies (`Ω(2^H * ϵ)`). However, for action-chunked and delayed policies, this scaling can be reduced to a polynomial dependence on the effective horizon (`O((k+1)^(H/k) * ϵ)` for chunk size `k`). This reduction stems from predicting actions based on past observations, which are generally less affected by accumulated execution errors.
 - **Conclusion:** Action chunking reduces compounding error, but this benefit is primarily attributed to its reliance on past, less error-compounded observations, rather than a unique effect of predicting a sequence of actions. Delayed policies achieve the same theoretical reduction in compounding error.

3. **Representation Learning (Section 4.3):**
 - **Similar Benefits from Delayed Policies:** When comparing the performance of a Markovian policy (`pπ1_1`) to one trained with action chunking but executed as a single-step policy (`pπ1_20`), action chunking provides representational benefits for short horizons. However, these benefits are matched by policies directly trained to be delayed (`pπ5_delay`) or delayed policies induced from chunking policies (`π5_delay[pπ20]`) (Figure 7).
 - **Conclusion:** Action chunking offers representation learning advantages for short prediction horizons, but these are not exclusive to chunking and can be obtained by delayed policies.

#### 4.2. New Insights: Action-Chunked Policies as Implicit Ensembles (Section 5)

- **Discrepancy in Robomimic:** On the Robomimic benchmark, delayed policies, while outperforming single-step policies, generally failed to match the performance of action chunking policies (Figure 8, Table 1). This suggested an additional mechanism was at play beyond non-Markovian expressivity and compounding error reduction.
- **Implicit Ensembling Hypothesis:** The authors propose that an action-chunked policy `pπk` effectively learns multiple temporal relationships (`at | ot`, `at | ot-1`..., `at | ot-k+1`). This allows it to function as an implicit ensemble of `k` delayed policies, drawing benefits akin to ensemble-based methods in terms of robustness and generalization.
- **Experimental Validation of Implicit Ensembling:**
 - **Temporal Ensembles:** An "AC(n)-TE" (temporal ensemble induced by `pπn_k`) was shown to achieve lower validation error than a standard action-chunked policy and approached the error of an explicit ensemble of delayed policies (Delay(n)-Ens) on Robomimic (Figure 9).
 - **Randomized Delay Deployment:** The "Randomized Delay Ensemble" (AC(n)-RDE) strategy, which samples a random delay `i` at each timestep and executes the `(i+1)`th action predicted from `ot-i`, was found to consistently match or exceed the performance of standard action chunking across all simulated settings (Table 1). This supports the view that randomizing over the implicit ensemble members is a key benefit.

#### 4.3. Real-World Validation (Section 6)

- Experiments on a Franka Emika robot arm across three manipulation tasks confirmed the simulated findings. Markovian policies exhibited poor performance. Delayed policies improved over Markovian policies but did not consistently match action chunking. The randomized delay ensemble (AC(10)-RDE) consistently matched or exceeded the performance of action chunking (Figure 10).
- **Conclusion:** The combined benefits of non-Markovian expressivity, reduced compounding error (from delayed observations), and implicit ensembling are robust and translate to real-world robotic control. Action chunking itself is not strictly necessary if these underlying mechanisms are sufficiently replicated.

#### 4.4. Going Beyond Action Chunking with Explicit Ensembles (Section 7)

- Motivated by the implicit ensembling effect, the authors explored training explicit ensembles of policies. Explicit ensembles (AC(n)-Ens, Delay(n)-Ens, AC(n)-RDE-Ens), where multiple policies are independently trained and their predictions are aggregated, were shown to further improve performance beyond implicit ensembling.
- **Significant Performance Gains:** Notably, on Robomimic Transport, explicit ensembles yielded approximately a +30% boost in success rate over standard action chunking (Table 2).
- **Conclusion:** Explicit ensembling of policies, particularly delayed ones, represents a promising direction for improving BC performance, building directly on the insights derived from analyzing action chunking.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work offers a substantial contribution to the field of robotic control and imitation learning by providing a detailed mechanistic explanation for the widely observed, yet poorly understood, performance benefits of action chunking.

The significance and potential impact of these findings include:

- **Enhanced Fundamental Understanding:** The research moves beyond empirical observations to systematically dissect and explain *why* action chunking is effective. By demonstrating that commonly accepted hypotheses (temporal consistency, horizon reduction, representation learning) are insufficient on their own, and by identifying non-Markovian expressivity, reduced compounding error via delayed observations, and implicit ensembling as key drivers, the paper provides a more complete theoretical foundation for this technique.
- **Guiding Policy Design:** The core insight that action chunking’s benefits can be replicated and even surpassed without explicitly performing chunking has direct implications for future policy design. It suggests that researchers can develop simpler, more efficient architectures that focus on incorporating delayed observations and ensemble-like properties, rather than being constrained by the traditional action chunking paradigm.
- **Improved Behavioral Cloning Performance:** The demonstration that explicit ensembles of delayed policies can significantly outperform standard action chunking (e.g., +30% on Robomimic Transport) offers a concrete, actionable approach for improving behavioral cloning in challenging domains. This could lead to more robust and generalizable robotic policies.
- **Refined Interpretation of "Temporal Consistency":** The finding that delayed policies alone can capture the most relevant aspects of non-Markovian human behavior, often matching or exceeding action chunking performance (especially in Libero), redefines the necessity of "temporal consistency" in its strictest sense (i.e., predicting a *sequence* of actions). This suggests that the benefits are more about leveraging past information than ensuring a coherent open-loop sequence of actions.
- **Applicability Across Domains:** The validation of these findings in both simulated (Libero, Robomimic) and real-world robotic manipulation settings enhances the generalizability and practical relevance of the conclusions, confirming that the identified mechanisms are robust across different complexities and types of robotic tasks.
- **New Avenues for Research:** The paper opens several explicit directions for future work, including:
 - Investigating adaptive strategies for selecting delays in policies.
 - Exploring how to integrate history-conditioned policies with the identified benefits of compounding error reduction and ensembling.
 - A deeper examination of the relationship between control frequency, demonstrator behavior, and the role of action chunking.
 - Quantifying the practical impact of reduced inference pauses in real-world deployments.

Overall, this research represents a step towards developing more principled and effective robot learning algorithms by clarifying the underlying mechanisms of a widely adopted technique.
