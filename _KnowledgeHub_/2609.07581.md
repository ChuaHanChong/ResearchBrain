# ICI-VLA: In-Context Imitation with Spatiotemporally Aligned Demonstrations for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> ICI-VLA adapts a frozen text-action VLA to new manipulation tasks at test time via retrieved in-context demonstrations instead of gradient updates. Long trajectories are cut into semantically labeled micro-demonstrations; an RD-Encoder trained with semantic filtering and DTW-mined positives retrieves phase-aligned context; Target Action Masking suppresses action copying. It reaches 97.7% on LIBERO, 60.4% on RoboTwin 2.0, and 83.2% on four physical tasks.

## Problem

- Deployed VLAs act as fixed conditional policies, so each new task needs fresh demonstrations plus parameter updates, which is costly when data or compute is scarce.
- Prior robotic in-context learning relies on specialized keypoint or language trajectory representations and narrow task settings, leaving general-purpose test-time adaptation unresolved.
- Retrieving whole long episodes causes temporal aliasing and semantic-dynamic mismatch, turning context into a misleading action prefix that encourages sequence copying.

## Method

- ==Micro-demonstration library==: a Qwen3-VL planner segments roughly 11,200 long trajectories into about 139,659 subtask examples, each with instruction, dual-camera views, proprioception, and a textualized 25-step action chunk.
- **RD-Encoder** retrieval: Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B is ==contrastively fine-tuned== by iterative mining, with ==semantic hard-filtering== to a 64-candidate pool and ==DTW end-effector ranking== supplying positives and phase-misaligned hard negatives.
- **Target Action Masking**: during policy fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-4B, sampled target action tokens are replaced by [MASK] and loss applies only to unmasked tokens, cutting dependence on exact target-prefix continuation.

## Results

- Averages of **97.7%** on LIBERO and **60.4%** on RoboTwin 2.0, the latter **19.3 points** above the best reported baseline, with LIBERO-Long at **96.8%** versus **93.2%**.
- Ablations: removing masking drops RoboTwin from **60.4%** to **10.7%** and LIBERO from **97.7%** to **71.5%**; removing DTW gives **31.4%/92.5%**, removing semantic filtering **38.1%/88.4%**.
- Retriever Recall@1 rises from **27.8** to **70.8** across mining stages; physical dual-arm Aloha over **1,000** trials reaches **83.2%** versus **66.4%** for a flow-based baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Retrieval granularity matters more than retrieval volume: context matched to the current subtask phase beats whole-episode conditioning, and three examples outperform ten.
> - Naive in-context demonstrations actively hurt a VLA, dropping below the no-context baseline, so corrupting context action targets during training is what makes conditioning usable.
> - A pure text-generation policy with no action head can absorb demonstrations as prompts, but performance stays bounded by demonstration-library and subtask-planner coverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models connect visual observations and natural-language instructions to low-level robot actions. Recent VLA systems have transferred capabilities from large vision-language models to manipulation tasks, but adaptation to a new robot, environment, or task commonly requires collecting task-specific demonstrations and performing additional gradient-based fine-tuning. This creates practical costs in data collection, computation, and deployment time.

The paper places ICI-VLA at the intersection of three research directions. The first is the development of general-purpose VLA models. Existing systems generally use one of three action interfaces: specialized generative action heads, discrete action tokens, or customized architectural modules. These designs can produce effective control policies, but they often require task-specific training pipelines and may alter the behavior of the underlying vision-language model.

The second direction is in-context learning (ICL), in which a model changes its predictions based on demonstrations supplied in the input rather than by updating its parameters. While ICL has been widely studied in language and vision-language models, applying it to robotics is difficult because demonstrations must jointly encode images, instructions, proprioceptive states, temporal phases, and continuous actions.

The third direction is retrieval-augmented or memory-based robotic control. Prior work has used trajectory retrieval, keypoints, language descriptions, and structured constraints to provide contextual guidance. ICI-VLA argues that these approaches do not fully address two problems. First, a long demonstration trajectory may be temporally misaligned with the short action chunk that the current policy must generate. Second, demonstrations that look semantically similar may require different physical motions. The paper therefore proposes retrieval that is aligned both semantically and geometrically over time.

The work builds on the “zero-modification” text-action interface of VLA-0. Rather than adding an action head or converting actions into a specialized token vocabulary, ICI-VLA represents continuous robot actions as numerical text strings and retains the native text-generation interface of a multimodal language model. Its central claim is that a fixed text-action VLA can adapt at inference time when it receives carefully retrieved, short-horizon demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to enable few-shot test-time adaptation without updating the VLA policy parameters during deployment. In the proposed setting, a robot receives a new task and uses a library of previously collected demonstrations. The system retrieves demonstrations relevant to the robot’s current execution phase and includes them in the policy prompt. The policy then predicts the next short action sequence while remaining fixed.

The motivation has several components. First, conventional adaptation through fine-tuning is unsuitable for rapid deployment when new task data or computational resources are limited. A retrieval-based approach could reuse existing demonstrations and adapt through the model’s input context.

Second, the authors identify a mismatch between the temporal scale of many robot demonstrations and the temporal scale of action prediction. Long-horizon trajectories contain multiple phases, such as approaching an object, grasping it, moving it, and releasing it. Retrieving an entire trajectory for a short action prediction can introduce irrelevant or contradictory information. ICI-VLA addresses this by decomposing long trajectories into semantically labeled micro-demonstrations.

Third, the authors seek to prevent the model from simply copying the numerical action sequence in a retrieved example. If the current and reference states differ, direct continuation of the reference action prefix can produce an incorrect motion. Target Action Masking is introduced as a training objective intended to make the policy less dependent on exact contextual action prefixes and more dependent on the current observation and retrieved information.

Finally, the work evaluates whether these design choices are useful across several levels of difficulty: single-arm and dual-arm simulated manipulation, long-horizon tasks, and physical robot tasks involving grasping, drawers, placement, and sorting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. System Formulation

At deployment, the query contains the global instruction, the active subtask instruction, current camera observations, and the robot’s proprioceptive state. The demonstration library contains corresponding global and subtask instructions, observations, initial proprioceptive states, and a short sequence of actions.

The policy and retrieval encoder are trained offline. At inference, both are frozen. The retrieval encoder selects one or more demonstrations based only on information observable at the current time; it does not use the unknown target action for retrieval.

#### 3.2. Micro-Demonstration Library

The authors construct a library from approximately 11,200 long-horizon trajectories collected from LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0, and physical dual-arm Aloha deployments. Approximately 1,000 of the physical trajectories are from dual-arm demonstrations. A Qwen3-VL model heuristically decomposes each trajectory into ordered subtasks and assigns a fine-grained instruction to each segment.

Each micro-demonstration includes the global instruction, subtask instruction, main and wrist-camera observations, initial proprioceptive state, and a fixed-horizon action chunk. The action chunk contains 25 steps in the reported experiments. This process produces approximately 139,659 subtask-level examples.

#### 3.3. Spatiotemporal Retrieval Alignment

ICI-VLA trains a Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B model, referred to as the RD-Encoder, to retrieve demonstrations that are both semantically and physically relevant.

The retrieval process first applies semantic hard-filtering. The encoder computes multimodal similarity between a query and library items, retaining the top 64 candidates that are semantically compatible with the current subtask. For example, a query involving pushing a drawer closed should not retrieve examples involving unrelated object manipulation, even if the visual scenes are similar.

The remaining candidates are ranked using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) on labeled trajectories. The trajectory representation consists of normalized three-dimensional end-effector waypoints for all active robot arms. The distance between waypoints is the mean Euclidean distance across arms. DTW permits trajectories with different temporal rates to be compared while preserving their overall order.

The lowest-cost candidate is selected as the positive example. Candidates from the same semantic subtask but with phase-misaligned or geometrically different trajectories are used as hard negatives. Fifteen such negatives are sampled. The encoder is optimized with an InfoNCE contrastive loss using a temperature of 0.07. Candidate retrieval, DTW-based mining, and contrastive optimization are repeated for up to five cycles, or until the selected positives stabilize.

Importantly, DTW and target trajectories are used only during offline retriever training. At evaluation time, the retrieval system operates from the current instruction, visual observations, and proprioceptive state without access to the target action sequence.

#### 3.4. Policy Training and Target Action Masking

The policy is Qwen3-VL-4B, fine-tuned without an additional action-specific prediction head. It receives a structured prompt containing system instructions, retrieved reference examples, and the current observation. Continuous actions and proprioceptive values are represented as text.

During policy training, Target Action Masking randomly replaces selected tokens in the contextual action sequence with mask tokens. The training loss is applied only to unmasked target positions. Consequently, the model must sometimes predict later action tokens even when earlier contextual action tokens are corrupted. This objective is intended to reduce reliance on exact action-prefix copying and increase grounding in the current query.

Masking is disabled during inference. The policy autoregressively generates a 25-step action chunk using fixed parameters. The reported experiments retrieve three demonstrations. The context is refreshed when policy confidence falls below 0.65. These settings were selected using validation data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Performance

On LIBERO, ICI-VLA achieves an average success rate of 97.7%. The reported results are 98.5% on Spatial, 98.7% on Object, 98.0% on Goal, and 96.8% on Long. The average is higher than the highest listed baseline average of 96.4% from OpenVLA-OFT, although the authors note that external baseline results follow their original evaluation protocols and therefore are not fully controlled comparisons.

On RoboTwin 2.0, ICI-VLA achieves 72.4% on Easy tasks, 46.3% on Hard tasks, and 60.4% overall. The highest listed external baseline average is 41.1%, producing a reported difference of 19.3 percentage points. The comparison is particularly relevant for long-horizon dual-arm manipulation, but it should be interpreted as benchmark-level evidence rather than a paired comparison under identical training and evaluation conditions.

A more controlled comparison uses VLA-0 with the same retrieved contexts but without Target Action Masking. This naive ICL configuration obtains only 71.5% on LIBERO and 10.7% on RoboTwin 2.0, compared with 97.7% and 60.4% for the full method. This result indicates that merely placing demonstrations in the prompt is insufficient under the tested conditions.

#### 4.2. Ablation Results

Removing DTW-based retrieval ranking reduces average performance to 92.5% on LIBERO and 31.4% on RoboTwin 2.0. Removing semantic filtering produces 88.4% and 38.1%, respectively. Removing Target Action Masking has the largest reported effect, lowering performance to 71.5% on LIBERO and 10.7% on RoboTwin 2.0.

These results support the contribution of both retrieval components and the masking objective. However, the experiments do not prove that masking causes explicit residual action computation or identify the precise internal shortcut used by the policy. They establish an empirical performance association under the reported setup.

#### 4.3. Retrieval Quality and Sensitivity

Direct retrieval evaluation on held-out RoboTwin 2.0 queries shows that Recall@1 improves from 27.8% for the base embedding to 41.6% after semantic filtering, 56.9% after one DTW-supervised cycle, and 70.8% after five cycles. Recall@5 increases from 52.4% to 90.1% across the same progression.

The number of demonstrations also matters. RoboTwin performance peaks at three examples, reaching 60.4%. One, two, five, and ten examples yield 50.1%, 56.5%, 54.8%, and 45.2%, respectively. This pattern suggests that additional context can introduce irrelevant or conflicting information. Retrieval optimization improves performance from 34.6% without iterative mining to 60.4% after five cycles, with little change after the fifth cycle.

#### 4.4. Physical Robot Evaluation

The method is evaluated on a dual-arm Aloha platform over 1,000 total rollouts, with 250 trials for each of four tasks. ICI-VLA achieves 89.6% on Single-arm Grasp, 76.8% on Dual-arm Grasp, 81.2% on Drawer Placement, and 85.2% on Object Sorting, for an average of 83.2%. The reported 95% Wilson confidence interval for the average is 80.8–85.4%.

For comparison, π0 achieves 66.4% and VLA-0 achieves 63.0% under the listed physical evaluation. The physical tasks include lighting variation, distractors, sensor noise, and contact dynamics, although the evaluation objects, layouts, instructions, and trajectories are stated to be disjoint from the policy-training and retrieval-library data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ICI-VLA contributes a formulation of VLA adaptation in which demonstrations are used as inference-time context rather than as data for task-specific parameter updates. This could reduce the delay and computational cost associated with deploying a policy in new manipulation settings. The approach also preserves the native text-generation interface, potentially simplifying integration with pretrained multimodal models.

The micro-demonstration design addresses a practical issue in retrieval-based robot learning: relevance depends not only on the task and scene but also on the current phase of execution. Combining semantic filtering with DTW-based geometric supervision provides a mechanism for selecting demonstrations that better match the required motion.

The results indicate that context quality and policy training are interdependent. Retrieval alone can be harmful when the policy copies incompatible action prefixes, while Target Action Masking substantially improves the use of contextual examples in the reported experiments. The physical evaluation provides evidence that the method can transfer beyond simulation, although the tested task and embodiment range remains limited.

Several limitations remain. The system depends on the coverage and quality of its demonstration library, as well as the accuracy of the subtask planner and labels. It incurs substantial offline costs for collecting trajectories, decomposing them, training the retriever, and fine-tuning the policy. Retrieval also adds runtime computation. In addition, task definitions and instruction templates may recur across trajectory partitions even though individual evaluation trajectories are excluded, and comparisons with published baselines are not uniformly controlled. Future work will need to evaluate broader cross-embodiment transfer, more varied environments, retrieval efficiency, and robustness when no closely aligned demonstration exists.
