# SceneBot: Contact-Prompted General Humanoid Whole Body Tracking with Scene-Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> SceneBot, developed by researchers at Stanford and Amazon FAR, introduces a unified motion-tracking framework for humanoids that utilizes contact conditioning to perform complex behaviors like manipulating objects and traversing uneven terrain. It employs a novel hindsight scene reconstruction pipeline to generate contact-rich training data, enabling the robot to achieve high success rates on challenging interaction tasks in both simulation and real-world deployment.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning policies for humanoids excel in free-space motions but struggle with contact-rich tasks due to physical ambiguities that purely kinematic tracking cannot resolve.
- A lack of a unified whole-body controller capable of handling diverse behaviors like free-space locomotion, uneven terrain traversal, and object manipulation simultaneously.
- The scarcity of large-scale datasets that pair motion trajectories with corresponding object/terrain geometry and precise contact annotations for training contact-aware policies.

## Method

- A unified motion-tracking framework, **SceneBot**, conditions the policy on both reference kinematic motions and explicit ==per-link contact labels== to specify desired environmental interactions.
- A ==hindsight scene reconstruction== pipeline synthesizes plausible terrain and object assets directly from retargeted human kinematic motion, inferring scene interaction graphs and contact annotations.
- A reinforcement learning (PPO) approach trains the whole-body controller using motion tracking rewards augmented with specific ==contact correctness and duration rewards==, along with penalty terms.

## Results

- SceneBot achieved significantly higher success rates on contact-rich tasks compared to SONIC: **95.00%** for object interaction (vs. **5.00%**), **100.00%** for terrain interaction (vs. **15.00%**), and **100.00%** for sitting (vs. **0.00%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that contact labels are critical; policies trained without hand contact labels had near-zero object grasp success, and those without foot contact labels frequently fell during terrain traversal.
- The infrastructure-free state estimation system demonstrated robust performance on a real-world Unitree G1 robot, achieving an average root position error of **0.032 m** and an **80%** task success rate across 10 challenging real-world experiments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit per-link contact labels provide a powerful and interpretable interface for resolving physical ambiguities in environmental interactions, enabling robust execution of contact-rich tasks.
> - Global root position and linear velocity tracking is essential for maintaining stability, preventing drift, and accelerating policy learning in complex, contact-dependent tasks.
> - Synthesizing contact-rich scene interaction data through hindsight reconstruction from ordinary human motion is a scalable method to overcome data scarcity, generating physically feasible scenes for robot training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot control, particularly through reinforcement learning (RL), has advanced in enabling robots to perform diverse free-space motions such as walking, running, and dancing. These "general motion trackers" provide robust low-level control for various tasks, including teleoperation and integration into Vision-Language-Action architectures. However, a limitation exists in these methods when robots are required to interact with their environment in a contact-rich manner, such as manipulating objects or traversing uneven terrain. Pure kinematic tracking, which focuses solely on achieving desired poses, often proves insufficient to resolve the physical ambiguities that arise from environmental interactions. For instance, moving hands to the sides of a box does not inherently define whether the robot is reaching, lightly touching, or actively lifting. Similarly, placing a foot near a stair edge does not guarantee the generation of sufficient force to ascend.

In the sub-field of terrain-aware whole-body control, progress has been made using RL policies with root velocity commands and gait-based reward shaping, enabling agile maneuvers like parkour. However, many of these approaches predominantly rely on root velocity control, limiting their applicability as general-purpose whole-body controllers for complex loco-manipulation tasks. While some efforts have attempted to bridge this gap by co-training controllers with terrain-aware kinematic generators, these methods can constrain dexterous upper-body movements.

Regarding humanoid object interaction, research has largely concentrated on single-handed, lightweight pick-and-place scenarios. Bimanual grasping of heavy objects often still requires high-level policies or human teleoperators to manage force regulation. Although human-object datasets have been leveraged for single-trajectory tracking of large objects, a general-purpose, whole-body controller capable of zero-shot deployment for complex manipulation, encompassing diverse interactions, has remained an area of active development.

SceneBot addresses these identified gaps by proposing a unified motion-tracking framework that integrates free-space locomotion, uneven terrain traversal, and whole-body object manipulation into a single policy. It introduces contact conditioning as a mechanism to resolve the physical ambiguities inherent in environmental interactions and develops a novel data generation method to overcome the scarcity of contact-rich motion data. The work aims to extend the capabilities of general motion tracking policies beyond free-space movements to encompass a broader range of complex, contact-dependent robotic behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for SceneBot stems from the observation that while current reinforcement learning policies enable humanoids to perform free-space motions, they encounter significant challenges in contact-rich tasks involving interaction with objects or uneven terrain. This difficulty arises because purely kinematic tracking cannot resolve the physical ambiguities inherent in such interactions. For example, a robot moving its hands towards a box might be merely reaching, lightly touching, or applying force to lift, and kinematic information alone does not distinguish these states. Similarly, placing a foot near a stair edge does not guarantee the necessary ground reaction force for stepping up. Successful execution of these tasks depends not only on achieving desired poses but also on the robot's capacity to establish, maintain, and utilize contact forces with the environment in specific ways.

Based on this foundational problem, the authors define several key objectives for the SceneBot framework:

1. **Develop a Unified Motion-Tracking Framework:** The primary objective is to introduce a single, unified motion-tracking framework, termed SceneBot, capable of seamlessly handling diverse behaviors including free-space locomotion, complex terrain traversal (e.g., stairs), and whole-body object manipulation. This unification aims to move beyond specialized controllers for different interaction types.
2. **Incorporate Contact-Conditioning into Policy Design:** A core objective is to condition the unified policy on both reference motions and explicit per-link contact labels. These contact labels are intended to specify which robot body links are expected to establish and exploit contact forces from different parts of the scene, thereby providing critical physical context beyond kinematics. This design choice aims to provide a minimal extension to existing low-level motion tracking policies without transforming them into vision-based task policies.
3. **Address the Data Scarcity for Contact-Rich Interactions:** A significant challenge in training contact-aware controllers is the lack of large-scale datasets that pair motion trajectories with corresponding object geometry, terrain geometry, and precise contact annotations. The framework aims to overcome this data bottleneck.
4. **Propose a Hindsight Scene Reconstruction Approach:** To address the data challenge, an objective is to develop a method for inferring scene-interaction graphs from retargeted human motion and subsequently reconstructing plausible terrain and object assets consistent with these inferred contacts. This approach seeks to convert ordinary human motion data into contact-rich robot-scene interaction data suitable for training.
5. **Enable Generalization to Unseen Motions and Environments:** The framework aims for the trained policy to generalize effectively to novel motion sequences and previously unencountered environmental configurations, demonstrating robustness beyond the training distribution.
6. **Validate Contact Conditioning as a Powerful Interface:** The work seeks to demonstrate that contact conditioning serves as an effective and powerful interface for extending general humanoid motion tracking capabilities from simple free-space movements to complex, contact-rich scene interactions.

In essence, the motivation is to endow humanoid robots with greater physical intelligence by enabling them to intentionally leverage contacts for support, manipulation, and locomotion, thereby allowing them to perform complex, long-horizon tasks in real-world, interactive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SceneBot is structured as a framework for training a generalist tracking policy designed for robust terrain and object interaction. Its methodology encompasses two primary stages: a scene reconstruction pipeline for data generation and a reinforcement learning (RL) approach for training the whole-body controller.

#### 3.1. Scene Generation from Human Motion

To address the scarcity of paired robot motion and scene asset data, SceneBot employs a scene reconstruction pipeline. This pipeline synthesizes plausible terrains and objects directly from human kinematic motion.

1. **Motion Retargeting:** Input human kinematic motion ($M_{human}$) is first retargeted to the robot's kinematic structure, producing robot motion ($M_{robot}$), using established kinematic motion retargeting techniques.
2. **Scene Interaction Graph Construction:**
 - A heterogeneous graph is constructed to capture temporal interactions between the robot and the scene.
 - **Robot Nodes ($N_{robot}$):** Key robot links are identified, including left-wrist, right-wrist, left-foot, right-foot, and pelvis.
 - **Scene Nodes ($N_{scene}$):** Two types of scene nodes are defined: immovable terrain and movable objects.
 - **Temporal Edges:** Candidate interaction edges are added at timesteps ($t_s$) where a robot link exhibits low relative velocity and acceleration compared to a scene node, suggesting potential contact at a specific spatial location ($p$).
 - **Edge Pruning:** Infeasible edges are subsequently removed. This includes interactions that would cause collisions outside the designated interaction interval or violate force-closure constraints during object interactions.
3. **Scene Reconstruction:** After the interaction graph is established, scene assets are generated based on its topological structure.
 - **Terrain Reconstruction:** Terrain is represented as a 2.5D elevation map. For each interaction edge associated with the terrain, a square plateau is added to the elevation map at the interaction's spatial height and horizontal center. Following initial construction, isolated plateaus of similar heights are merged, and regions that would lead to robot-terrain collisions are carved out.
 - **Object Reconstruction:** Objects are generated as sets of plates that are parallel to the robot's contact surfaces involved in the interaction. The centers and trajectories of these objects are determined by computing the mean positions of the interaction edges related to the grasping action. A detailed algorithm is provided in Appendix A.

#### 3.2. Training a Contact-Aware Whole-Body Controller

The whole-body control policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to simultaneously track reference kinematic motion and desired contact behavior.

1. **Policy Input:** The policy receives several inputs at each timestep ($t$):
 - **Kinematic Commands ($g_t$):** Desired joint angles and velocities for the lower body, 6D poses of the head and wrists (in the robot's root frame), and global root position and orientation errors between the current and target root states.
 - **Desired Contact Labels ($c_t$):** A binary vector indicating which robot links (from $K$) should establish contact with which scene type (terrain or object). An active contact ($c_{i,j,t}=1$) means robot link $i$ should contact scene type $j$.
 - **Proprioceptive States ($s_t$):** The robot's current joint angles and velocities, projected gravity in the root frame, and root angular velocity.
 - **Root State ($p_{root,t}$):** Global root position, orientation, and linear velocity.
2. **Reward Function:** The training utilizes motion tracking rewards, similar to those in prior works, augmented with contact-specific terms and penalty terms.
 - **Motion Tracking Rewards:** These include exponential rewards for tracking root orientation, root position, relative body link positions and orientations, linear velocities, and angular velocities.
 - **Contact Correctness Reward ($r_{cr}$):** Rewards policies for achieving desired contacts, specifically when a desired contact ($c_{des,t}$) matches the actual contact ($c_{actual,t}$) detected in the simulator. This reward is only active when a contact is explicitly desired ($c_{des,t}=1$), preventing penalties for unintended, un-commanded contacts.
 - **Contact Duration Reward ($r_{dr}$):** Encourages stable contacts by accumulating reward for continuous contact time, clipped at 0.5 seconds to prevent excessive adherence.
 - **Penalty Terms:** Penalties are applied for high action rates, joint limit violations, and undesired contacts (e.g., unintended contacts from links other than ankles or wrists).
3. **Contact-Mismatch Termination:** For object grasping, a termination condition is implemented: if the desired contact for an object is not established within a specified timeframe, the episode ends, promoting rapid and accurate grasping.
4. **Heuristic Stabilizing Force ("Magic Force"):** During training, to mitigate frequent grasp failures for objects whose motions are reconstructed purely from robot kinematics (and may not start in stable resting positions), a heuristic stabilizing force is applied. This force ensures the object follows its reconstructed trajectory until a stable force-closure grasp is achieved, providing a denser contact reward signal early in training.

#### 3.3. Infrastructure-Free State Estimation

For real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 robot, SceneBot integrates an infrastructure-free state estimation system.

1. **Primary Odometry:** SuperOdometry, a LiDAR-inertial odometry framework, is used to estimate the robot's global root position, orientation, and linear velocity.
2. **Orientation Refinement:** Due to the Livox IMU being mounted on the robot's head (whose motion can deviate significantly from the pelvis during aggressive movements), a refinement step is added for root orientation. Pitch and roll angles are directly computed from a pelvis-mounted root IMU. Yaw estimation fuses the root IMU's yaw angular velocity with SuperOdometry's yaw using a Kalman filter.
3. **Initialization:** The root height is estimated from the lowest 5% of points in a gravity-aligned LiDAR scan, and the world-frame origin is initialized at the robot's starting x and y coordinates with zero height.
4. **Operating Frequency:** The complete state estimation pipeline operates at 200 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The SceneBot framework was evaluated on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, both in a MuJoCo sim-to-sim environment and through real-world deployment, utilizing a diverse training dataset composed of AMASS, OMOMO, Bones, and Lafan datasets (7.5 hours total, with distribution: 46.6% object interaction, 37.4% flat terrain, 14.2% terrain interaction, 1.8% sitting). Comparisons were made against SONIC (a state-of-the-art general motion tracking policy) and several ablative baselines (No label, No foot label, Local).

#### 4.1. Tracking Performance Across Motions

- **Qualitative Performance:** SceneBot successfully executed a range of behaviors, including stepping onto stairs of varying heights, picking up and carrying boxes, sitting down, and performing agile kicking and running. It also demonstrated the capacity for long-horizon, simultaneous object and terrain interactions, such as carrying a box while ascending stairs.
- **Quantitative Performance:** On free-space motion tracking, SceneBot achieved comparable performance to SONIC, both exhibiting a 100% success rate with similar joint and root errors. However, SceneBot demonstrated significantly superior performance in all contact-rich scene interaction tasks:
 - **Object Interaction:** SceneBot achieved a 95.00% success rate, whereas SONIC achieved 5.00%.
 - **Terrain Interaction:** SceneBot achieved a 100.00% success rate, significantly higher than SONIC's 15.00%.
 - **Sitting:** SceneBot achieved a 100.00% success rate, while SONIC recorded 0.00%.
 - SceneBot also exhibited lower average joint, root position, and root orientation errors across these contact-rich tasks compared to SONIC and other baselines.

#### 4.2. Necessity of Global Tracking

Ablation studies indicated that training the whole-body tracking policy without global root position and linear velocity (the "Local" baseline) resulted in substantially degraded performance. For example, the success rate for terrain tasks dropped from 100% (with SceneBot) to 45% (Local), and for object-related tasks, it decreased from 95% to 20%. The inclusion of global root positioning was found to prevent drift during both training and inference, accelerate policy learning by reducing misalignment-related terminations, and mitigate misalignment-induced failures during testing.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Contact Labels

Contact labels were found to be critical for the success of contact-rich tasks:

- **Object Grasping:** Policies trained without hand contact labels ("No label" baseline) or with hand labels deactivated during inference exhibited near-zero object grasp success rates. The robot consistently failed to pick up or stably hold objects.
- **Terrain Traversal:** Policies trained without foot contact labels ("No foot label" baseline) experienced missteps after initial movements, frequently leading to the robot falling.
- **Inference-time Behavior:** Interestingly, when contact labels were explicitly included during training but then disabled only during testing for terrain traversal (Ours (No foot label)), the success rate was 85%, which was higher than the 60% success rate of a model explicitly trained without foot contact labels. This suggests that contact labels simplify the learning process, and the balancing mechanics learned with their aid are largely retained even when explicit labels are absent during inference.

#### 4.4. Retargeting vs. Reconstruction

The scene reconstruction pipeline demonstrated its ability to generate complex terrains from obstacle sequences and object assets from interaction datasets.

- **Feasibility Guarantee:** The reconstruction method provides a guarantee that the generated scenes are physically feasible for supporting robot motion, rather than strictly adhering to constraints on a pre-existing scene that might not align with the motion. This reduces the burden on the RL policy to correct for potential robot motion-scene mismatches.
- **Performance Advantage:** Quantitatively, training with SceneBot's reconstructed scenes outperformed using a tuned Omni-retarget baseline on the OMOMO dataset. The Omni-retarget method was observed to struggle with perfectly aligning objects without penetration, leading to higher grasp failure rates for policies trained with its data.

#### 4.5. Performance of State Estimation

The developed state estimation system was evaluated against OptiTrack motion capture ground truth for challenging motions like picking up a box and stepping onto a stage.

- **Accuracy:** The system achieved an average root position error of 0.032 m, a root orientation error of 0.018 rad, and a root linear velocity error of 0.092 m/s.
- **Robustness:** It yielded an 80% task success rate across 10 real-world experiments. Maximum errors occurred during rapid crouching motions where the LiDAR scan significantly diverged from the reference point map, but the system demonstrated recovery.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SceneBot framework presents advancements in humanoid robot control, with several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Unified Control for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The development of a single motion tracking policy capable of unifying free-space locomotion, uneven terrain traversal, and whole-body object manipulation addresses a fundamental limitation in humanoid control. Prior work often required specialized controllers for different interaction types, whereas SceneBot demonstrates a generalist approach. This unification could streamline the development and deployment of humanoid robots in complex, unstructured environments.
2. **Novel Contact-Conditioning Interface:** Introducing per-link contact labels as explicit conditioning for the policy provides a direct and interpretable interface for robots to understand and execute intended environmental interactions. This approach allows high-level decision-makers to specify critical contact information, moving beyond kinematic-only guidance and resolving ambiguities inherent in physical interactions.
3. **Addressing Data Scarcity through Hindsight Scene Reconstruction:** The proposed data engine, which synthesizes interactive scenes and contact annotations from ordinary human motion data, offers a scalable solution to the data bottleneck for training contact-aware policies. This methodology reduces the reliance on costly and difficult-to-acquire physically annotated interaction datasets, accelerating research and development in robot learning.
4. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** SceneBot's demonstrated ability to generalize to unseen motions and environments, while maintaining performance comparable to state-of-the-art free-space trackers and significantly outperforming them in contact-rich tasks, indicates a step towards more robust and versatile humanoid robots. This improved generalization is crucial for practical applications in dynamic real-world settings.
5. **Enabling Complex, Long-Horizon Behaviors:** The successful execution of challenging, long-horizon tasks, such as carrying a box upstairs, which require simultaneous object manipulation and terrain traversal, highlights the framework's potential to enable more sophisticated and practical applications for humanoid robots.
6. **Foundation for Autonomous Interaction:** By providing a robust low-level controller for contact-rich interactions, SceneBot lays a foundation for future work on autonomous high-level controllers that can perceive, reason, and act based on goals. This could lead to humanoids capable of independent, intelligent interaction with their environment.
7. **Contribution to Open Science:** The stated intention to open-source all code and data can foster broader adoption, validation, and further development within the robotics and reinforcement learning communities, potentially accelerating advancements in the field of humanoid control.
