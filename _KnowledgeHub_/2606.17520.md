# GASE: Gaussian Splatting-Based Automated System for Reconstructing Embodied-Simulation Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> The GASE system reconstructs high-fidelity embodied simulation environments by leveraging multi-view data acquisition and a 2D-domain object-scene decomposition pipeline. It achieves over 10% higher segmentation accuracy than 3DGS methods and enables real-robot manipulation with a sim-to-real gap under 10%, while also improving navigation success rates.

## Problem

- High cost and time associated with collecting real-world data for robot training.
- Significant "sim-to-real gap" in existing simulation environments due to low fidelity and inaccurate object-scene decomposition.
- Inefficient data acquisition methods and poor object extraction quality in current 3D reconstruction pipelines.

## Method

- **GASE** utilizes multi-view video streams from panoramic cameras for rapid and efficient data acquisition.
- It employs a 2D-domain pipeline for accurate object mask extraction and high-fidelity background inpainting.
- Reconstructs the background scene using ==3D Gaussian Splatting== and generates foreground objects with a multi-view model for physics simulator integration.

## Results

- Achieved over **10%** higher segmentation accuracy (mIoU) for object extraction and superior visual quality for inpainting compared to 3DGS-based methods.
- Demonstrated a sim-to-real performance gap of less than **10%** for real-robot manipulation tasks and improved navigation success rates to **100%** from zero-shot baselines.
- The system supports multi-frame stream input, does not require additional object scanning, and is applicable across small, medium, and large scene scales.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performing object-scene decomposition in the 2D image domain significantly improves mask accuracy and inpainting quality compared to direct 3D Gaussian segmentation.
> - Combining multi-view data acquisition with a camera-pose-based localization strategy and video propagation enables robust and comprehensive object mask extraction.
> - Decoupling static background (with collision mesh) and individual foreground objects creates realistic, manipulable assets for versatile embodied AI training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of embodied artificial intelligence (AI) necessitates robust training environments for robots to execute complex, multi-step tasks across diverse real-world settings. Traditional real-world data collection for robot learning is resource-intensive, requiring significant time, specialized hardware, and skilled operators. This limitation has propelled simulation-based training as a viable and scalable alternative, offering cost-effectiveness and facilitating data augmentation techniques like domain randomization. A critical challenge in this paradigm is the construction of high-fidelity simulation scenes that accurately mimic real-world environments, thereby minimizing the "sim-to-real gap"—the discrepancy between simulated and real-world performance.

Current approaches to generating simulation environments generally fall into two categories: generative methods and reconstruction-based methods. Generative approaches, often leveraging diffusion models, prioritize rapid scene creation by lifting single images into 3D representations. While efficient, these methods typically struggle with visual fidelity and depth accuracy, leading to a substantial sim-to-real gap.

Reconstruction-based methods, which GASE aligns with, aim to bridge this sim-to-real gap by reconstructing 3D scenes from multi-view images and camera poses. These methods often utilize techniques such as 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) due to its balance of visual quality and efficiency compared to earlier radiance field methods. Reconstruction pipelines offer superior visual fidelity, depth accuracy, and cross-view consistency, making them more suitable for tasks requiring precise interaction.

However, existing reconstruction workflows face two primary bottlenecks:
1. **Inefficient Data Acquisition:** Many current systems are designed for monocular video streams, making the scanning of expansive scenes labor-intensive and time-consuming. They generally lack the capability to efficiently leverage camera arrays, particularly panoramic ones, for rapid environment capture.
2. **Ineffective Object-Scene Decomposition:** For robot manipulation tasks, the ability to decouple foreground objects from the static background is essential. Some methods require separate scanning of objects and backgrounds, which is inefficient for environments with multiple interactable objects. Other approaches attempt to segment objects directly within the 3D Gaussian space. This often results in poor extraction quality, structural holes in both objects and the background, and subpar scene inpainting, primarily because individual Gaussian ellipsoids can straddle object boundaries, leading to ambiguous separations. Systems focused solely on navigation often bypass this decomposition problem, rendering them unsuitable for manipulation-centric tasks.

GASE addresses these identified limitations by proposing an automated system that integrates rapid multi-view data acquisition with a refined object-scene decomposition strategy operating in the 2D domain. This positions GASE as an advancement in reconstruction-based simulation environment generation, specifically targeting improved efficiency and reduced sim-to-real gap for embodied AI training across diverse tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop an automated system for constructing high-fidelity simulation environments that effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap, thereby facilitating the training of embodied AI agents. This central goal is driven by several specific motivations and aimed at addressing critical challenges in current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Mitigating High Costs and Time Constraints of Real-World Training:** Training embodied agents in physical environments is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. This motivates the necessity for simulation environments that can provide large-scale, cost-effective data augmentation. A primary objective is therefore to enable rapid and efficient generation of such environments.

2. **Minimizing the Sim-to-Real Gap for Effective Policy Transfer:** While simulation offers advantages, the utility of simulation-trained policies in the real world depends directly on the realism of the simulated environments. Generative methods often produce scenes with a substantial sim-to-real gap, whereas current reconstruction methods, despite better visual quality, still suffer from inefficiencies that limit their practical application. A key objective is to achieve a minimal sim-to-real gap, ensuring that policies learned in simulation can be reliably transferred to real robots without significant performance degradation.

3. **Enhancing Data Acquisition Efficiency for Expansive Scenes:** Existing reconstruction systems are often optimized for monocular video capture, making the scanning of large or complex environments laborious. A specific objective of GASE is to improve data acquisition efficiency by leveraging multi-view video streams from panoramic camera arrays, enabling faster and more comprehensive environment scanning.

4. **Improving Foreground Object Extraction and Scene Inpainting Quality:** For manipulation tasks, distinct and accurate representations of foreground objects are crucial. Current methods that attempt to segment objects directly within 3D Gaussian space often yield imprecise boundaries, structural artifacts, and poor inpainting results due to the nature of Gaussian representations. The motivation is to overcome these limitations by developing a robust strategy for extracting foreground objects with clear boundaries and performing high-fidelity scene inpainting, which is essential for creating manipulable assets and realistic background environments.

5. **Enabling Seamless Integration with Physics Simulators:** The final goal is to produce decoupled scene assets (static background and individual foreground objects) that can be seamlessly imported into physics simulators. This facilitates independent manipulation and interaction with objects, allowing for more versatile and realistic training scenarios for both manipulation and navigation tasks.

In summary, GASE aims to provide an efficient, automated, and high-fidelity solution for simulation environment construction that specifically addresses the challenges of rapid data acquisition and accurate object-scene decomposition, all while maintaining a low sim-to-real performance gap in real-robot deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GASE proposes a highly automated system for constructing simulation environments, leveraging multi-view video streams and a 2D-domain processing pipeline. The overall workflow, illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper, consists of mask extraction, inpainting, and independent 3D reconstruction of the background scene and foreground objects.

#### 3.1. Data Acquisition and Pre-processing

The system begins with capturing real-world environments. This involves using 1 to 3 Insta360 X5 panoramic cameras to record scenes. Each panoramic video is then split into 2 to 5 distinct viewpoints. These viewpoints, corresponding to high, middle, and low panoramic camera positions, are stitched together to form the scene image data. Subsequently, the Postshot software is utilized to recover accurate camera poses and generate a sparse 3D point cloud from these images. These camera parameters and point cloud data are crucial for subsequent reconstruction steps.

#### 3.2. Mask Extraction and Inpainting

A core aspect of GASE's methodology is its approach to object-scene decomposition, which operates primarily in the 2D image domain to circumvent the quality issues associated with direct 3D Gaussian segmentation.

- **Localization Strategy:**
 - The user initiates the process by selecting a target object using text prompts or interactive clicks on a single frame.
 - SAM3 is then employed to extract all potential masks from this selected image.
 - To robustly identify and segment the chosen object across all frames and camera views, GASE introduces a camera-pose-based localization strategy. For each 3D point (Pj) in the sparse point cloud, its position is transformed into the camera coordinate system of each frame (f) using the camera's extrinsic parameters (Rf, tf).
 - These 3D points are then projected onto the 2D image plane using the camera's intrinsic parameters (f_fx, f_fy, c_fx, c_fy), resulting in 2D image coordinates (u_j,f, v_j,f).
 - To account for inter-object occlusion, a depth-filtering mechanism is applied. The projected depth (z_c_j,f) of each point is compared against the depth map (D_f(u,v)) of the target frame, with a tolerance (τ). Only points whose projected depth is within this tolerance of the existing depth map are considered valid.
 - For each image, if the number of valid projected points within a mask exceeds a predefined threshold, the smallest mask encompassing the largest number of projected points is selected as the result mask (M_res).

- **Tracking and Inpainting Enhancement:**
 - Since the object may appear intermittently or at varying scales across different frame streams, the initial localization might miss some masks. To address this, SAM2's video propagation capabilities are utilized.
 - For each frame stream, the first successfully localized mask (M_res) is propagated forward and backward through the video sequence by SAM2, marking all frames where the object is detected. This process is repeated for any unmarked frames that contain a localized mask.
 - Once all relevant object masks are extracted across all frames, these masked regions are removed from the original images. High-resolution inpainting is then performed on these images using LAMA to fill the vacated areas, thereby creating a clean, object-free background scene representation.

#### 3.3. Scene Reconstruction (Background)

After the foreground objects are extracted and the background images are inpainted, the static background scene is reconstructed.

- **3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS):**
 - The 3D point cloud, initially obtained from COLMAP, is first refined by removing points that correspond to the extracted foreground objects. This ensures that the background reconstruction focuses solely on the static environment.
 - The inpainted scene images, along with the refined COLMAP data (camera parameters and point cloud), are fed into the `gsplat` library for 3DGS reconstruction.
 - The scene is represented as a set of K 3D Gaussians, each defined by its center (μ), quaternion (q), log-scale (s), logit opacity (o), and Spherical Harmonic (SH) color coefficients (c_SH).
 - During training, a loss function combining L1 loss and SSIM is used. After reconstruction, expected depth maps (D_hat) are rendered, which are crucial for subsequent mesh extraction.
 - The final Gaussian point cloud is then converted into a format compatible with Isaac Sim using 3DGRUT.

- **Mesh Extraction for Physical Collision:**
 - To provide the background with physical collision geometry in the simulation, a mesh is extracted from the reconstructed Gaussians.
 - RGB-D information from each frame (including the rendered depth maps and camera poses) is integrated into a Truncated Signed Distance Function (TSDF) voxel grid.
 - A point cloud and normals are extracted from the TSDF, followed by Poisson surface reconstruction to minimize surface holes and create a smooth mesh.
 - For smaller scenes requiring higher detail and less prone to holes, an alternative approach involves extracting a triangle mesh directly from the TSDF zero-level set, followed by post-processing steps like hole filling, smoothing, and connected-component cleanup. This yields a high-fidelity 3DGS background with an associated mesh for physical interactions.

#### 3.4. Object Generation (Foreground)

The extracted foreground object masks are used to generate high-quality 3D models of the objects.

- **Multi-view Object Generation:**
 - The extracted object masks are input to TRELLIS, an object generation model that supports multi-view input. This choice addresses the limitation of single-image input models, which may produce inconsistent unseen angles.
 - To optimize resource usage while retaining detail, a suitable subset of masks is selected. A weighting scheme is employed, considering factors such as normalized object area, fill ratio, center prior, boundary-cut penalty, aspect-ratio regularity, and effective resolution.
 - Masks are sorted by their weighted scores, and a greedy selection process ensures comprehensive coverage of the object's viewpoints by enforcing a minimum frame-gap constraint between consecutively selected masks. If the target number of masks is not met, the constraint is gradually relaxed.
 - These selected multi-view masks facilitate the generation of high-quality object meshes, ready for import into physics simulators alongside the background scene.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GASE focused on its core components: extraction accuracy, inpainting quality, and the practical utility of reconstructed environments for embodied AI, particularly regarding the sim-to-real gap.

#### 4.1. Extraction and Inpainting Performance

- **Extraction Accuracy:** GASE's 2D-mask-based approach was compared against existing 3DGS-based methods (LangSplat, Gaussian Grouping, ObjectGS) on the LERF dataset. The results demonstrated that GASE achieved a segmentation accuracy over 10% higher than the competing methods. For instance, across "figurines," "ramen," and "teatime" scenes, GASE consistently achieved the highest mIoU and mBIoU scores. In "figurines," GASE scored 93.32% mIoU, significantly higher than ObjectGS at 78.41%. Similarly, for "ramen" and "teatime," GASE achieved 95.63% and 95.91% mIoU respectively, outperforming the next best methods by notable margins. This indicates that GASE's 2D-domain segmentation strategy provides more accurate and clearly bounded object masks.
- **Inpainting Quality:** Experiments on a "sundries" scene from their custom dataset compared GASE's 2D-image inpainting (using LAMA) with methods that perform inpainting directly within Gaussian representations (GScream, Gaussian Grouping, 3DGIC). GASE demonstrated superior visual quality and consistency in inpainting. Quantitative metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID, m-LPIPS, m-FID) confirmed GASE's state-of-the-art performance. For example, GASE achieved the highest PSNR (27.09), SSIM (0.94), and the lowest LPIPS (0.15) and FID (166.14) scores, indicating better perceptual quality and realism in the reconstructed, inpainted scenes.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-Real Gap Validation through Real-Robot Experiments

To validate the practical utility and minimal sim-to-real gap of GASE-generated environments, real-robot experiments were conducted for both manipulation and navigation tasks.

- **Manipulation Tasks:**
 - Utilizing a Piper robotic arm with the Pi 0.5 policy in a "sundries" scenario, three tasks (grasping a cola can, putting a bottle into a box, pushing over a paper cup) were tested.
 - Policies trained purely in GASE-generated simulation environments (sim-to-real paradigm) achieved an overall success rate exceeding 60%.
 - Crucially, the performance gap relative to policies trained purely on real-world data (real-to-real paradigm) was consistently less than 10%. For "grasp cola can" and "push the cup over" tasks, the sim-to-real success rates (0.73 and 0.67 respectively) matched the real-to-real baseline. For "put the bottle into the box," the sim-to-real success rate was 0.60 compared to real-to-real's 0.67, representing a 7% difference. These results indicate a high degree of fidelity and transferability from GASE's simulation environments.

- **Navigation Tasks:**
 - Deployed on a Kitt 15 robot with the Uni-NaVid policy in a "bar" scenario, three navigation tasks were designed (finding a microwave, finding a red fire extinguisher box, navigating straight to a wall then turning left).
 - Training with simulated data generated by GASE significantly improved the success rate compared to zero-shot performance in the real world. For example, "find the microwave" showed a 100% sim-to-real success rate compared to 75% zero-shot. "Find a red fire extinguisher box" improved from 0% zero-shot to 100% sim-to-real.
 - The experiments further demonstrated a minimal sim-to-real gap, with consistent success and failure rates observed between simulation and real-world deployments for long-range navigation tasks.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Other Systems

A comparative analysis with other related systems (PolaRiS, Re3Sim, PLANNING, R2R2R) highlighted GASE's unique capabilities. GASE is the only system among those compared that supports multiple frame streams input. Additionally, similar to Re3Sim and R2R2R, GASE does not require additional object scanning. The system is also shown to be broadly applicable across small, medium, and large scene scales.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies validated the effectiveness of GASE's camera-pose-based cross-view propagation strategy for mask extraction. Comparing "SAM3 on each frame," "SAM3 tracking," "COLMAP mapping only," and "COLMAP+SAM3," the combined `Colmap+SAM3` strategy consistently achieved the highest percentage of successfully extracted frames across different scene scales (e.g., 79.96% for "videogame," 82.43% for "office," 67.49% for "bar"). Pure SAM3 tracking encountered "CUDA out of memory" errors on larger scenes, and processing SAM3 on each frame independently yielded lower success rates, confirming the efficiency and robustness of GASE's hybrid approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GASE represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied AI and robot learning by addressing critical limitations in the construction of high-fidelity simulation environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The primary impact of GASE is its demonstrated ability to create simulation environments that exhibit a minimal sim-to-real performance gap. Policies trained within GASE's reconstructed scenes achieved success rates within 10% of those trained with real-world data, particularly for manipulation and navigation tasks. This fidelity is crucial for accelerating robot learning by allowing for more reliable transfer of learned behaviors from simulation to physical robots, reducing the need for extensive real-world experimentation.

2. **Enhanced Automation and Efficiency in Scene Construction:** GASE introduces a highly automated workflow that leverages multi-view video streams from panoramic camera arrays. This significantly improves the efficiency of data acquisition for large and diverse environments, moving beyond the labor-intensive single-stream scanning methods. The automation reduces the manual effort, time, and associated costs of creating complex training scenes, enabling scalable data generation for robot learning.

3. **Superior Object-Scene Decomposition and Asset Generation:** The novel camera-pose-based 2D-domain strategy for object extraction and background inpainting is a key technical advancement. By performing segmentation and inpainting in 2D before 3D reconstruction, GASE overcomes the challenges of imprecise boundaries and structural artifacts prevalent in direct 3D Gaussian-based methods. This leads to high-quality, cleanly separated foreground object assets and realistic background scenes, which are essential for robust manipulation training in physics simulators.

4. **Versatility Across Embodied AI Tasks and Scene Scales:** GASE supports a broad range of embodied training tasks, including both manipulation and navigation, and is applicable across various scene scales, from tabletop setups to expansive indoor environments. This versatility makes it a valuable tool for researchers and developers working on diverse robot applications.

5. **Advancement in 3D Gaussian Splatting Applications:** The work demonstrates an effective application of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for creating simulation-ready assets, pushing the boundaries of its utility beyond mere view synthesis. By integrating 3DGS with robust 2D semantic processing and dedicated object generation, GASE showcases how to leverage the strengths of modern 3D reconstruction for practical embodied AI challenges.

6. **Economic and Research Acceleration:** By providing a cost-effective and efficient method for generating realistic training data, GASE can significantly lower the barrier to entry for robot learning research. It enables researchers to rapidly iterate on new policies and explore diverse environments without substantial real-world hardware investments, thereby accelerating the pace of innovation in embodied AI.

In conclusion, GASE offers a comprehensive solution that tackles some of the most pressing challenges in sim-to-real transfer for robotics. Its contributions pave the way for more scalable, reliable, and high-fidelity simulation-based training paradigms, ultimately advancing the capabilities of embodied AI.
