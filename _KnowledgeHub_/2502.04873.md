# Training-free Task-oriented Grasp Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> A training-free pipeline for task-oriented grasp generation was developed, leveraging pre-trained grasp models and vision-language models (VLMs) to select grasps aligned with natural language task descriptions. This system achieved a 70% combined success rate in real-world cluttered environments by integrating semantic reasoning into robotic grasping.

## Problem

- Robotic grasping primarily focuses on stability and collision avoidance, overlooking the task-driven nature of human grasping, which is inherently guided by intended subsequent use.
- Conventional robotic grasping methods often assume any stable grasp is acceptable, failing to consider the subsequent utility of the grasp for a specified task.
- Existing data-driven or VLM-integrated approaches for task-oriented grasping typically require extensive, costly datasets or are rigidly coupled to specific models, limiting flexibility and generalization to novel objects or tasks.

## Method

- A ==training-free, two-stage pipeline== was introduced, combining pre-trained, task-agnostic grasp generation models with ==Vision-Language Models (VLMs)==.
- The pipeline first generates a diverse set of stable ==6-DoF grasp candidates==, then employs a VLM to filter and select the most task-compliant grasp based on a natural language task description.
- Five distinct ==VLM query strategies== were evaluated for presenting grasp candidates, ranging from visualizing full grippers in single images to allowing the VLM to directly suggest ==contact points==, alongside a ==view-selection mechanism== to mitigate visual ambiguity.

## Results

- The system achieved an overall combined success rate of **70%** in real-world cluttered environments (Common Household, Kitchen Utensils, Hand Tools), operating solely from a single RGB-D image and task description.
- With a powerful VLM (Gemini-2.0-Pro) and the Contact Point Generation (CPG) strategy, the combined success rate reached **0.611** in simulation, with a grasp success rate of **0.796**, surpassing the baseline's **0.754**.
- An analysis of grasp diversity (number of clusters K) showed a trade-off: increasing diversity among candidates improved task compliance but slightly decreased grasp success, highlighting the need for a balanced approach in candidate selection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - More capable VLMs perform best when given the "freedom" to suggest optimal grasp points (Contact Point Generation strategy), while less powerful VLMs benefit from "restriction" to predefined candidates.
> - Integrating VLMs for task-oriented selection not only improves task compliance but can also lead to higher overall grasp success rates, indicating an implicit VLM preference for more stable grasp locations.
> - Actively reducing perceptual ambiguity through a simple view-selection mechanism significantly enhances VLM performance in terms of task compliance, particularly for complex scenes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic grasping has primarily focused on generating stable and collision-free grasps. Traditional methods, as exemplified by works such as, have advanced the capabilities of robots to securely hold objects across diverse scenarios. However, these methods typically operate under the assumption that any stable grasp is acceptable, overlooking the inherent task-driven nature of human grasping. For humans, the manner in which an object is grasped is often dictated by its intended subsequent use or interaction, rather than solely by stability. For instance, grasping a cup for drinking typically involves holding the handle, even though other stable grasps on the cup's body are possible.

This discrepancy highlights a critical gap in conventional robotic grasping: the ability to generate "task-oriented" grasps. Addressing this, recent research has begun exploring task-oriented grasp generation (TOG), with some data-driven approaches collecting large datasets to train neural networks. More recently, the emergence of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) has presented new avenues for incorporating semantic understanding into grasping. Studies like and have investigated leveraging language and visual information to inform grasp selection. However, these VLM-integrated approaches often necessitate additional training steps or are tightly coupled to specific grasp generation models, thereby limiting their flexibility, adaptability, and generalizability to novel objects or tasks. Training directly on extensive datasets for diverse objects and tasks also poses significant challenges in data collection, cost, and generalization.

This work positions itself within this evolving landscape by proposing a training-free pipeline for task-oriented grasp generation (TOG). It seeks to overcome the limitations of data-driven and rigid VLM-integrated methods by seamlessly combining pre-trained, task-agnostic grasp generation models with the semantic reasoning capabilities of VLMs. Unlike prior VLM-based approaches that may require further training or rely on gradient information, this method is designed for direct application, enhancing flexibility and allowing for the integration of various advanced grasp generation models as they become available. It also distinguishes itself from general foundation models in robotics that might struggle with spatial reasoning or assume fixed environments, by focusing specifically on task-oriented grasping without such environmental constraints. The research therefore contributes to bridging the gap between stable, general grasping and semantically informed, task-specific manipulation in robotics, particularly through a flexible, training-free approach.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that human grasping is inherently task-driven, a characteristic largely absent in conventional robotic grasping systems. While existing robotic grasp generation methods prioritize stability and collision avoidance, they often fail to consider the subsequent utility of the grasp for a specified task. For example, a robot might need to grasp a tool differently if it intends to use it versus passing it to a person. Enabling service robots to perform a wide array of tasks in dynamic, real-world environments necessitates that they predict and execute grasps optimized for a specific goal.

Directly training a task-oriented grasp generation policy presents substantial challenges. Such a policy would require immense datasets to account for the vast diversity of objects and tasks, leading to significant data collection costs and scalability issues. Furthermore, there are concerns regarding the generalization capabilities of such a trained policy to novel objects and tasks, as achieving this level of generalization demands a semantic understanding of both the task and unfamiliar object properties that is difficult to acquire solely from grasp annotations.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Training-Free Pipeline:** The central objective is to introduce a training-free system for task-oriented grasp generation (TOG). This pipeline aims to leverage existing pre-trained grasp generation models and vision-language models (VLMs) without requiring additional training or fine-tuning specifically for task-oriented grasping. This approach seeks to reduce the computational and data overhead associated with developing task-specific grasping capabilities.
2. **Integrate Pre-trained Models:** The method aims to seamlessly integrate pre-trained, task-agnostic grasp generation models (which provide stable grasp candidates) with VLMs (which offer semantic reasoning based on natural language task descriptions). This integration is designed to enhance the selection of grasps beyond mere stability, incorporating task-specific requirements.
3. **Evaluate Integration Strategies:** A significant objective is to empirically evaluate various strategies for how the VLM interacts with and selects from the candidate grasps. This involves exploring different methods of visualizing and presenting grasp candidates to the VLM, such as representing them as full gripper poses or simplified contact points, either in single or multiple images, or by allowing the VLM to directly suggest contact points. The goal is to identify which integration approaches are most effective, particularly in relation to the capabilities of different VLMs.
4. **Analyze Performance and Failure Modes:** The research seeks to comprehensively analyze the performance of the proposed TOG pipeline across different VLMs and integration strategies in both simulated and real-world environments. This includes assessing grasp success rates, task compliance rates, and combined success rates. Additionally, a detailed analysis of failure modes is intended to provide insights into current limitations and guide future research directions, particularly concerning VLM capabilities in spatial reasoning and image ambiguity.
5. **Provide Generalizable Insights:** The study aims to contribute generalizable insights into the effective utilization of VLMs for robotic manipulation, specifically regarding the balance between "freedom" (allowing VLMs to suggest grasp points) and "restriction" (constraining VLMs to select from predefined candidates) based on the VLM's inherent capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed "Training-free Task-oriented Grasp Generation" (TOG) pipeline operates in a two-stage process: first, generating a diverse set of stable, task-agnostic grasp candidates, and second, filtering and selecting the most suitable grasp based on task-oriented constraints using a Vision-Language Model (VLM).

**System Overview:**
The pipeline takes an RGB and depth image of an object as input, along with a natural language task description. The depth image is initially unprojected to create a point cloud.

**Stage 1: Task-Agnostic Grasp Generation and Diversity Enhancement:**
1. **Initial Grasp Generation:** The generated point cloud is fed into a pre-trained grasp generation model. For the experiments, ContactGraspNet (CGN) was utilized due to its robustness in generating 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (6-DoF) grasp poses for parallel-jaw grippers. The pipeline is designed to be flexible, allowing for the integration of other advanced grasp generation models.
2. **Motion Planning Filter:** The candidate grasps produced by CGN are then passed through a motion planner. This step filters out any grasps that are kinematically infeasible, unreachable by the robot arm, or lead to collisions, thereby reducing computational overhead before VLM querying.
3. **Grasp Diversity Enhancement:** Acknowledging that the top-K grasps from CGN, based on confidence scores, often lack diversity (e.g., all grasps for a screwdriver handle might concentrate at its midpoint), a clustering approach is applied. K-means clustering is performed on the 3D contact points (the center of the gripper) of the valid grasps. From each resulting cluster, the grasp with the highest confidence score (as predicted by the grasp generation model) is selected. This ensures that the final set of candidate grasps presented to the VLM is both diverse and of high initial quality. For the simulation experiments, `k=3` was initially used, and its effect was further analyzed.

**Stage 2: Task-Oriented Grasp Filtering by VLM:**
After generating a set of K diverse grasp candidates, the VLM is queried to identify the grasp that best aligns with the specified task. Five distinct strategies were developed and evaluated for querying the VLM, differing in how grasp candidates are visualized and presented:
1. **Grasps in a Single Image (GSI):** All candidate grasps are rendered as U-shaped gripper wireframes, projected onto a single RGB image. The VLM is prompted to select the best grasp from this combined visualization.
2. **Contact Points in a Single Image (CPSI):** Similar to GSI, but candidate grasps are represented by colored dots indicating their contact points on a single RGB image.
3. **Grasps in Multiple Images (GMI):** Each candidate grasp (gripper wireframe) is displayed in a separate RGB image, and the VLM selects the image corresponding to the optimal grasp.
4. **Contact Points in Multiple Images (CPMI):** Each candidate grasp is represented by a colored dot (contact point) in a separate RGB image.
5. **Contact Point Generation (CPG):** Instead of presenting predefined candidates, the VLM is directly prompted to suggest an optimal grasping pixel coordinate (u, v) on the input image. The system then unprojects this (u, v) to a 3D point P on the object and selects the pre-generated grasp whose contact point is closest to P.

**View-Selection for Ambiguity Reduction (Refinement):**
To address issues of ambiguous query images (e.g., distinguishing bottle neck from body), an additional view-selection mechanism was implemented. Four static RGB cameras are mounted around the workspace. For each trial, multiple frames are captured. Detic is then used with a text prompt matching the task object to obtain class-specific confidence scores for each image. The image with the highest confidence (considered "least ambiguous") is selected and fed into the TOG pipeline.

**Experimental Setup and Evaluation:**
- **Simulation Environment:** ManiSkill simulator with a Franka Emika Panda arm. Objects were selected from YCB and Google Scanned Objects (GSO) datasets (29 objects).
- **Real-World Environment:** Franka Emika Panda robotic arm with an Intel RealSense L515 RGB-D camera (eye-in-hand setup). Evaluated on 10 objects across three cluttered scenes (Common Household, Kitchen Utensils, Hand Tools).
- **Vision-Language Models (VLMs) Used:** Qwen-7B, Molmo, and Gemini-2.0-Pro.
- **Task Categories:** Three categories were defined: "Grasping at a Specific Position," "Tool Use," and "Object Handover," each with specific success conditions.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Grasp Success Rate:** Percentage of trials where the robot successfully grasps and lifts the object.
 - **Task Compliance Rate:** Percentage of trials where the VLM selects a grasp aligning with task requirements (manually evaluated due to VLM auto-evaluation unreliability).
 - **Combined Success Rate:** Percentage of trials where the grasp is both successful and task-compliant.
- **Baseline:** Selecting the grasp with the highest confidence score from the initial grasp generation model (ContactGraspNet) without VLM intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the effectiveness of the TOG pipeline, the influence of VLM capabilities, and the impact of different integration strategies.

**Overall Effectiveness of TOG:**
The TOG system consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to the baseline method, which relies solely on the highest confidence score from the grasp generation model. This indicates that integrating VLMs significantly enhances task-oriented grasp generation capabilities. In real-world experiments, the system achieved an overall combined success rate of 70%, operating solely from a single RGB-D image and a task description.

**Impact of VLM Capability and Query Strategy:**
- **CPG for Powerful VLMs:** The Contact Point Generation (CPG) strategy, where the VLM directly suggests an optimal grasp point, achieved the highest combined success rate (0.611) when paired with a more capable VLM like Gemini-2.0-Pro. This suggests that giving more powerful VLMs "freedom" to specify grasp locations can yield better task-oriented results.
- **Constrained Strategies for Less Powerful VLMs:** For less powerful VLMs (e.g., Qwen-7B, Molmo), strategies that constrained the VLM to select from a pre-defined set of clustered grasp candidates (e.g., CPMI or CPSI) generally performed better than CPG. CPG, when used with weaker VLMs, often resulted in unstable or misaligned contact points, leading to grasp failures.
- **Query Representation (Gripper vs. Contact Point):** Both visualizing the full gripper shape (GSI, GMI) and simply the contact point (CPSI, CPMI) yielded comparable performance overall.
- **Number of Images (Single vs. Multiple):** For Gemini-2.0-Pro, using multiple images (GMI, CPMI) generally outperformed single-image strategies (GSI, CPSI). However, less powerful VLMs showed potential confusion with an increased number of images, suggesting a sensitivity to input complexity.

**Grasp Quality and Success Rate:**
The integration of VLMs did not diminish the fundamental grasp success rate. In fact, for the most effective combination (Gemini-2.0-Pro with CPG), the grasp success rate (0.796) was higher than the baseline (0.754). This observation leads to the hypothesis that VLMs might implicitly favor more stable grasp locations, contributing to an improved grasp quality.

**Effect of Number of Clusters (K):**
An analysis of the number of clusters (K) used in the K-means algorithm revealed a trade-off. Increasing K, which leads to greater grasp diversity among candidates, improved the task compliance rate. However, it also led to a decrease in the grasp success rate. This is attributed to the fact that highly stable grasps often form larger clusters, and increasing K might force the selection of less stable grasps from smaller clusters. Maximizing the average silhouette score for clustering was found to provide a suitable balance between grasp diversity and grasp quality.

**Failure Modes:**
Several limitations were identified during the experiments:
- **Insufficient Grasp Diversity:** The k-means clustering method sometimes failed to produce sufficiently distinct clusters, leading to candidate grasps still being concentrated in similar regions of the object, thus limiting the VLM's options for task-specific selection.
- **Ambiguous Query Images:** Certain viewpoints could lead to ambiguous visual information, making it difficult for the VLM to infer critical affordance cues (e.g., distinguishing a bottle's lid from its body).
- **VLM Limitations:** VLMs demonstrated inherent challenges in complex spatial reasoning and understanding physical constraints in 3D scenes, occasionally selecting unstable grasps or specifying misaligned contact points.

**Mitigation of Ambiguity with View-Selection:**
The integration of a simple view-selection mechanism, using Detic to identify the "least ambiguous view" from multiple camera perspectives, showed positive results. While it caused a slight reduction in grasp success rate, it significantly improved task compliance (by 5.5%) and combined success (by 7.4%) for the Gemini + CPG strategy in simulation, demonstrating that reducing perceptual ambiguity can markedly enhance VLM performance.

**Real-World Performance:**
The real-world experiments confirmed the system's viability, achieving a 70% combined success rate across cluttered scenes with household items, kitchen utensils, and hand tools. The VLM (Gemini-2.0-Pro with CPG) demonstrated a high success rate in identifying reasonable grasp points, even implicitly handling object selection without a separate object detection pipeline. However, real-world performance also exhibited sensitivity to viewpoint and object ambiguity, consistent with the simulation findings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in advancing task-oriented grasping. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Training-Free Task-Oriented Grasping:** The primary impact is the demonstration of a robust, training-free pipeline for task-oriented grasp generation. This eliminates the substantial data collection, annotation, and computational costs typically associated with training complex, task-specific robotic policies. By leveraging existing pre-trained models, the method makes advanced manipulation capabilities more accessible and scalable for diverse real-world applications.

2. **Increased Flexibility and Adaptability:** The modular integration of pre-trained grasp generation models with Vision-Language Models offers significant flexibility. As more advanced grasp generation models or VLMs emerge, they can be readily swapped into the pipeline without requiring extensive re-training or architectural modifications. This adaptability ensures the system can evolve with advancements in both perception and reasoning technologies.

3. **Enhanced Robotic Functionality and Autonomy:** By allowing robots to generate grasps that are not just stable but also semantically aligned with a given task, this work moves service robots closer to performing human-like, context-aware manipulation. This capability is crucial for improving robot autonomy in domestic, industrial, and service environments where objects need to be interacted with purposefully (e.g., using a tool, handing over an object correctly).

4. **Insights into VLM Utilization in Robotics:** The detailed analysis of different VLM querying strategies and their performance with varying VLM capabilities provides valuable empirical insights. The finding that more powerful VLMs benefit from greater "freedom" in specifying grasp points, while less powerful ones perform better with "restricted" choices, offers a guiding principle for future VLM integration efforts in robotics and other spatial reasoning tasks.

5. **Addressing Perceptual Ambiguity:** The investigation into failure modes, particularly those related to ambiguous query images, and the subsequent implementation of a view-selection mechanism, highlights a practical strategy for improving VLM robustness in real-world scenarios. This suggests a pathway for enhancing overall robot perception by actively seeking clearer visual information.

6. **Simplification of Robotic Pipelines:** The observation that powerful VLMs can implicitly handle object selection and task-oriented reasoning without explicit object detection or segmentation components points towards a potential simplification of complex robotic perception pipelines. This could streamline development and deployment of robot systems by reducing the number of necessary modules.

7. **Advancing Human-Robot Interaction:** By enabling robots to understand and execute task-specific grasps, the system contributes to more intuitive and effective human-robot interaction. When a robot can grasp an object in a way that anticipates a human's next action (e.g., handing a hammer by its head), it fosters greater trust and efficiency in collaborative tasks.

In summary, this research provides a practical, efficient framework for equipping robots with task-oriented grasping abilities, offering a foundation for more intelligent and versatile robotic agents capable of operating effectively in complex, human-centric environments. It also opens avenues for further research into improving VLM's spatial reasoning and robustness.
