# RoboBenchMart: Benchmarking Robots in Retail Environment

> [!summary] Summary
> From FusionBrain Lab Robotics Group, RoboBenchMart is a benchmark designed for **dark store environments** where mobile manipulators perform complex retail tasks among dense object clutter and varied spatial configurations. It includes a procedural store layout generator, trajectory generation pipeline, and evaluation tools — and demonstrates that current state-of-the-art generalist VLAs ([[2405.12213|Octo]], [[2410.24164|π0]], [[2504.16054|π0.5]]) struggle to solve even common retail tasks, with all three scoring 0% on composite multi-step instructions.

## Problem

- Existing manipulation benchmarks ([[2306.03310|LIBERO]], [[2112.03227|CALVIN]], [[1909.12271|RLBench]]) focus on simplified tabletop scenarios with stationary arms — not capturing real-world deployment settings like dense object clutter, mobile manipulation, and multi-step retail workflows.
- Retail (especially dark stores) is a promising near-term automation target — repetitive picking workflows with high economic value — but lacks proper benchmarks.
- Current generalist VLA models exhibit fragility to minor scene changes (layouts, textures, object placements) and fail to execute compositional multi-step instructions.

## Method

- **RoboBenchMart** consists of three core components built on the ==ManiSkill3== simulator with the ==Fetch== mobile manipulator (7-DOF arm + prismatic torso + differential-drive base).
- ==Procedural store layout generation== uses a tensor-field-based shelf arrangement (inspired by procedural street modeling): rectangular store area → tensor field over the floor → two-pass shelving placement (horizontal then vertical) with collision-free constraints and probabilistic skipping for layout variability.
- ==Product arrangement== leverages the `scene_synthesizer` package with a custom grid-based placement module supporting vertical stacking and ==Poisson-process shelf depletion== to simulate natural product consumption over time (1st/2nd/4th/8th day stages).
- ==Store Trajectory Sampler== combines motion planning over anchor-pose sequences and ==reinforcement learning== to generate diverse training trajectories for imitation learning.
- Includes **5 atomic tasks** (Pick to basket, Pick from floor, From board to board, Open fridge, Close fridge) and **2 composite tasks** (Pick N items, Pick from fridge).
- Tests across ==three OOD axes==: In-Domain (robot position only), Unseen Scenes (layout + textures + position), Unseen Scenes & Items (additional cross-task OOD items).
- Releases **370 product assets across 21 categories**, three shelving-unit models, two refrigerator models, and 26/17/15 floor/wall/ceiling textures — all SketchFab-sourced and research-licensed.

## Results

- **[[2405.12213|Octo]] (93M)** scores **17%** on Pick-to-basket In-Domain, drops to **1%** in Unseen Scenes, **0%** in Unseen Scenes & Items.
- **[[2410.24164|π0]] (3.3B)** scores **22%** In-Domain, **1%** Unseen Scenes, **0%** Unseen Scenes & Items on Pick-to-basket; performs moderately In-Domain but collapses under scene shift.
- **[[2504.16054|π0.5]] (3.3B)** scores **63%** In-Domain, **38%** Unseen Scenes, **10%** Unseen Scenes & Items on Pick-to-basket — the only model achieving non-zero performance on cross-task OOD items.
- Fridge interactions show higher success: π0.5 reaches **85%** Close-fridge In-Domain, **77%** Unseen Scenes; **50%** Open-fridge In-Domain.
- All three models score **0%** on every composite task (Pick N items, Pick from fridge) across every testing scenario — exposing the gap between atomic-skill imitation and long-horizon retail execution.
- Confirms that **dense clutter, OOD textures, and compositional multi-step instructions** are unsolved frontiers for current generalist VLAs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Retail (dark store) manipulation is an evaluation axis **orthogonal** to LIBERO/CALVIN tabletop testing — exposing failures in mobile manipulation, dense-clutter perception, and multi-step task decomposition that standard benchmarks miss.
> - Even SOTA generalist VLAs (π0.5) score **0% on composite retail tasks**, revealing that high atomic-task success rates do not imply long-horizon capability in real-world workflows.
> - The benchmark releases a complete suite — procedural layout generator + trajectory pipeline + 370-asset library + fine-tuned baselines — enabling community-wide retail-VLA research without per-lab infrastructure investment.
> - Pairs naturally with domain-specific post-training datasets like [[2605.09613|SABER]] — which uses RoboBenchMart as the evaluation gate to demonstrate a **2.19×** mean SR improvement on NVIDIA [[2503.14734|GR00T-N1]] via in-store ego+exo data collection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The long-term objective of robotics research is to develop autonomous systems capable of operating in unconstrained, noisy, and realistic environments, demonstrating generalization across diverse variations and performing multiple tasks. While current real-world robotic deployments are predominantly limited to constrained settings and specific tasks, recent advancements in multimodal deep learning and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have initiated progress toward this goal.

A significant portion of recent research focuses on developing and evaluating these "generalist" VLAs through simulated benchmarks. However, the majority of existing benchmarks, such as ALFRED, RLBench, RoboCasa, CALVIN, LIBERO, VLABench, BEHAVIOR-1K, and ManiSkill-HUB, primarily focus on tabletop or household scenarios. In these environments, many tasks are reducible to pick-and-place operations. While these setups have facilitated progress in robotic manipulation, it is recognized that strong performance in these controlled settings does not necessarily translate to a policy's ability to transfer or adapt to domains characterized by different geometries, semantics, and workflows. This observation highlights a gap in the current benchmarking landscape, indicating a need for domain-specific simulation benchmarks that introduce distinct challenges while still enabling systematic evaluation.

The retail and logistics sectors represent a domain with high potential for near-term automation impact, yet they remain largely underexplored by comprehensive robotics benchmarks. Existing retail-focused datasets, such as RP2K, SKU110K, StandardSim, IPA-3D1K, and FetchBot, often provide limited utility for end-to-end robotics research. These limitations include providing only 2D image data, lacking code for generating diverse scenes or product arrangements, or focusing solely on atomic picking tasks without generating full store layouts or comprehensive textures for visual diversity. Consequently, these resources are generally unsuitable for training or benchmarking complex robotic manipulation and navigation policies in realistic retail scenarios.

RoboBenchMart addresses this identified gap by introducing an open-source simulated benchmark specifically designed for retail dark-store environments. It aims to provide a platform that captures the complex properties and challenges of this domain, thereby enabling the development and rigorous evaluation of generalist robotic policies that can operate effectively beyond the current scope of tabletop or household tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce RoboBenchMart, an open-source simulated benchmark tailored for evaluating robotic systems in retail dark-store environments. This initiative is driven by several motivations related to the limitations of existing robotic benchmarks and the distinctive challenges and potential impact of the retail domain.

A central motivation stems from the observation that most current robotic manipulation benchmarks concentrate on tabletop or household settings. While these environments have been instrumental in advancing robotic capabilities, the transferability and generalization of policies developed in such controlled setups to domains with substantially different geometries, semantics, and operational workflows remain largely unverified. The authors posit that strong performance in simplified environments does not guarantee effective adaptation in more complex, real-world scenarios. This necessitates the development of domain-specific benchmarks that expose qualitatively distinct challenges.

The retail sector, particularly dark stores (small distribution centers for online orders), is identified as a domain with significant potential for near-term automation impact. Unlike conventional grocery stores, dark stores are not open to the public, which limits human-robot interaction and offers a more structured environment for robotic deployment. Automating tasks such as restocking and order picking in these settings could provide several benefits:
- **Reduced physical strain and injury risk** for human workers performing repetitive tasks.
- **Enabling 24/7 operation**, which could improve the efficiency and reliability of order fulfillment.
- **Benefiting customers** through more stable and accessible delivery services.

However, retail environments introduce a unique set of challenges not adequately captured by existing benchmarks. These include:
- **Operation in narrow, cluttered aisles**.
- **Multi-level shelving** and diverse fixture types (e.g., shelves, refrigerators).
- **A large variety of visually similar products**, complicating item identification and manipulation.
- **The critical need for reliable collision avoidance** to protect inventory, infrastructure, and the robot itself in densely packed spaces.

Directly testing robotic policies in real-world retail settings is often complex, time-consuming, and expensive. Therefore, the development of high-fidelity simulation benchmarks that accurately reflect these properties and challenges is deemed essential for advancing research in retail automation.

The key objectives of RoboBenchMart are:
- To develop and release an **open procedural pipeline, the Store Plan Generator**, for creating realistic and diverse store layouts and product arrangements, thereby enabling scalable generation of retail environments.
- To introduce the **Store Trajectories Sampler**, an automatic pipeline for collecting trajectories of common retail tasks using motion planning, accompanied by a dataset of synthetic trajectories for the Fetch robot.
- To establish the **Store Robotics Benchmark**, presented as the first open benchmark dedicated to evaluating robotic policies in retail environments.
- To utilize this benchmark to evaluate state-of-the-art "generalist" VLA models, even after domain-specific adaptation, to assess their performance and identify current limitations in completing typical retail tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RoboBenchMart suite is developed to provide an open-source simulated environment for benchmarking robots in retail. The methodology encompasses three core components: the Store Plan Generator for environment creation, the Store Trajectories Sampler for data acquisition, and the Store Robotics Benchmark for policy evaluation. The entire framework is built upon ManiSkill3, a high-performance robot simulation framework featuring realistic physics and ray-traced rendering. The Fetch robot, a mobile manipulator equipped with a differential-drive base, a 7-DOF arm, a prismatic torso joint, and a parallel gripper, serves as the robotic embodiment within the simulation.

#### 3.1. Store Plan Generator

This component is responsible for procedurally generating diverse and realistic retail dark-store environments.
- **Scene Design**: Simulation scenes are structured as warehouse-like spaces, incorporating shelving units and refrigerators in varied configurations.
- **Domain Randomization**: Visual diversity is enhanced by applying different textures to walls, floors, and ceilings, alongside multiple fixture designs. Product items are placed on shelves in randomized, yet realistic, positions.
- **Fixture Arrangement**: A three-stage pipeline, inspired by procedural street modeling, is utilized:
 1. **Initial Random Placement**: Pallets, boxes, and freezers are placed within a rectangular store area using rejection sampling to prevent collisions.
 2. **Store Tensor Field Computation**: A tensor field is computed across the store floor, derived from polygons of the floor and existing fixtures. This field aggregates basis tensors weighted by their distance, guiding subsequent placements.
 3. **Shelving Unit Arrangement**: Shelves are arranged according to the tensor field's local directional indications, ensuring alignment, collision-free layouts, and required passage widths. Both horizontal and vertical passes are performed, with probabilistic skipping introduced for layout variability.
- **Product Arrangement**: The scene synthesizer package is leveraged, enhanced by a custom module. This module places items on a regular grid with slight pose perturbations for realism, supports vertical stacking, and simulates product depletion by introducing empty spaces using a Poisson process.
- **Assets and Textures**:
 - **Collection**: Three shelving unit models, two refrigerator models, and 370 product assets across 21 categories, along with 26 floor, 17 wall, and 15 ceiling textures, are sourced from SketchFab under unrestricted research licenses.
 - **Standardization**: Collected 3D assets are manually standardized for scale and orientation using reference dimensions from online retail catalogs.
 - **Mesh Simplification**: An automatic pipeline is developed to optimize computationally intensive product meshes. This involves brute-force optimization across various heuristic methods (e.g., QuadriFlow, Marching Cubes, shape-specific approximations) via the Blender Python API. The pipeline selects Pareto-optimal remeshed outputs that minimize L1 relative geometry quality drop while maximizing triangle reduction, significantly improving rendering performance.

#### 3.2. Store Trajectories Sampler

This pipeline generates trajectory data for robotic tasks using motion planning.
- **Demonstration Collection**: Successful mobile-manipulation demonstrations are collected for each task. This involves heuristically specifying sequences of initial, intermediate, and final "anchor poses" that correspond to key stages of task execution. These poses are randomized to enhance data diversity.
- **Motion Planning Strategy**: Motion planning is executed sequentially between consecutive anchor poses.
 - For segments not requiring mobile base movement, screw motion is initially attempted, followed by RRT-Connect if collision-free trajectories are not generated.
 - For segments requiring mobile base repositioning, task-specific heuristic planners are employed to position the robot near the target fixture for subsequent manipulation.
- **Data Retention**: Only trajectories that successfully complete the entire task are included in the demonstration dataset. For evaluation, 248 trajectories are generated per unique (task, item, fixture) triplet, totaling 2,976 demonstrations (approximately 1.4 million transitions).

#### 3.3. Store Robotics Benchmark

The benchmark evaluates the generalization capabilities of VLA policies fine-tuned using the generated trajectories.
- **Testing Scenarios**: To assess generalization, policies are evaluated across varying levels of environmental and task novelty:
 1. **In-Domain**: Randomization only in robot's start position.
 2. **Unseen Scenes**: Adds randomization of textures and store layouts to robot position randomization.
 3. **Unseen Scenes & Items**: Further includes items not seen in the target task during training but present in other training tasks (out-of-distribution with respect to the specific task).
 - More challenging scenarios, such as "Unseen Shelf Arrangement" and "Completely Unseen Items," are supported but excluded from primary evaluation due to observed policy limitations.
- **Tasks**:
 - **Atomic Tasks**: Fundamental manipulation skills including "Pick to basket," "Pick from floor," "From board to board" (item transfer), "Open fridge," and "Close fridge." Each task includes a textual instruction specifying the target item and fixture, with the robot expected to interact with the nearest matching instances.
 - **Composite Tasks**: Multi-step tasks built upon atomic skills, such as "Pick {N} items" (e.g., three items sequentially) and "Pick from fridge" (opening, picking, and closing the fridge).
 - **Success Criteria**: Evaluated based on final product/fixture positions and the absence of disturbances or collisions with surrounding items.
- **Generalist Baselines**: Four state-of-the-art VLA models are evaluated: Octo (93M parameters), SmolVLA (450M parameters), π0 (3.3B parameters), and π0.5 (3.3B parameters).
- **Fine-tuning**: Models are fine-tuned via imitation learning using the generated trajectories. To simulate realistic adaptation, only 248 trajectories per (task, item, fixture) triplet are used, with 2-3 training objects per task (ensuring these are unseen in other tasks). All training and testing are conducted on fully packed shelves. Composite tasks are evaluated by decomposing them into sequences of atomic instructions executed by the same policy.
- **Evaluation Metric**: Mean success rate per task, estimated from 100 trials per (task, item, fixture) triplet. Failed episodes are further annotated to identify the earliest primary failure in the manipulation pipeline (e.g., grounding, navigation, grasping, placement).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation results of current generalist VLA models on the RoboBenchMart benchmark indicate significant challenges, even for fundamental retail tasks, following fine-tuning with realistic adaptation data.

**Overall Performance on Atomic and Composite Tasks:**
- **SmolVLA** demonstrated limited performance, achieving non-zero success rates only on simpler tasks such as "Open fridge" and "Close fridge," and consistently performing poorly across all generalization scenarios. This is hypothesized to be related to its comparatively smaller pretraining dataset.
- **Octo** and **π0** showed moderate success rates in the "In-Domain" setting for atomic tasks. However, their performance substantially degraded when exposed to "Unseen Scenes" and they exhibited near-complete failure in the "Unseen Scenes & Items" scenario.
- **π0.5** exhibited comparatively better performance, being the only model to achieve non-zero success rates in the "Unseen Scenes & Items" scenario for some atomic tasks, though its performance remained below reliable operational levels. For instance, in "Unseen Scenes & Items," it achieved 34% success for picking "Nivea" to basket, 7% for "Fanta," and 3% for "Duff Beer Can" from board to board, with similar low rates for other tasks.
- **Composite Tasks**: All evaluated models achieved zero success rates on composite tasks, regardless of the testing scenario. This suggests a significant limitation in their ability to execute multi-step instructions and generalize across task sequences.

**Failure Analysis:**
A detailed analysis of failed episodes, categorized by the earliest primary failure in the manipulation pipeline (e.g., task grounding, navigation, grasping, placement), revealed specific bottlenecks:
- **Grasping Failures**: For Octo, π0, and π0.5, failures were predominantly observed at the grasping stage, indicating difficulties in precisely interacting with objects on shelves.
- **Pregrasp Positioning Failures**: SmolVLA frequently failed during earlier pregrasp positioning.
- **Object Grounding Errors**: A second common issue across models, including SmolVLA and Octo, involved difficulties in correctly grounding and selecting the instructed target product. This indicates challenges in interpreting instructions and perceiving target items in cluttered environments.
- **Mobile Base Positioning Failures**: SmolVLA and Octo also experienced failures in positioning the mobile base accurately near the target fixture ("move" stage).
- **Sensitivity to Distribution Shift**: The rate of failures increased substantially as the evaluation scenarios moved further from the training distribution (from "Train Seeds" to "Unseen Scenes & Items"). While π0.5 was the most robust, its failure rate still exceeded 90% in the most challenging "Unseen Scenes & Items" scenario. Under stronger distribution shifts, Octo, π0, and SmolVLA increasingly failed at earlier stages of the manipulation pipeline, such as task/target grounding and initial positioning. In contrast, π0.5 more consistently reached the interaction stage but still struggled significantly with grasping.

These findings collectively highlight three primary limitations of current generalist VLA models in the retail domain:
1. **Fragility to minor scene changes**: Performance degrades significantly with variations in layouts, textures, and object placements.
2. **Poor generalization from limited demonstrations**: Models struggle to adapt to novel object-task combinations.
3. **Insufficient reliability**: Performance is unreliable for precise shelf-level grasping and for executing compositional tasks.

The results suggest that existing pretrained VLA models may not be sufficient for effective adaptation to the complexities of the retail domain, indicating that targeted pretraining on retail-specific data might be necessary to address these challenges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of RoboBenchMart represents a contribution to the field of robotics by addressing a critical gap in existing benchmarking resources. Its significance and potential impact can be understood in several key areas:

Firstly, RoboBenchMart is presented as the first open-source simulated benchmark specifically designed for retail environments. This addresses the current landscape where most generalist robotic manipulation benchmarks focus on tabletop or household scenarios, which may not adequately prepare policies for the complexities of real-world retail operations. By targeting retail dark stores, the benchmark focuses on a domain with significant practical relevance and near-term potential for automation impact, offering benefits such as reduced physical strain for workers, enhanced operational efficiency, and improved accessibility of delivery services.

Secondly, the comprehensive suite of tools released with RoboBenchMart—including a procedural store layout generator, a trajectory generation pipeline, evaluation tools, and fine-tuned baseline models—provides a standardized and scalable platform for future research. The procedural generation capabilities enable the creation of diverse and realistic retail scenarios, complete with varied product arrangements and environmental textures, which is crucial for training and evaluating robust robotic policies. The automatic trajectory sampling further facilitates data collection, circumventing the costly and time-consuming process of human teleoperation. The careful asset management, including mesh simplification, also contributes to the efficiency and scalability of simulations.

Thirdly, the experimental results derived from RoboBenchMart provide insights into the current limitations of state-of-the-art generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The findings demonstrate that even after realistic adaptation through fine-tuning, these models struggle with fundamental retail tasks and exhibit significant fragility when exposed to variations in scenes and items not encountered during training. The detailed failure analysis highlights specific bottlenecks, such as difficulties in precise grasping in cluttered environments, reliable object grounding, and executing multi-step composite tasks. These identified limitations clearly delineate key challenges that need to be overcome for robots to perform effectively in retail settings, thus guiding future research directions toward developing more robust perception, manipulation, and reasoning capabilities.

Finally, by publicly releasing all components of the RoboBenchMart suite, the authors aim to foster collaborative research and accelerate the development of more robust, scalable, and task-aware robotic systems. This resource can serve as a common ground for researchers to compare different approaches, benchmark new algorithms, and iterate on solutions for retail automation challenges. While acknowledging potential societal concerns such as job displacement, the work frames its contribution within the context of advancing robotic capabilities to meet complex industrial demands, with broader implications for the organization of future retail work.
