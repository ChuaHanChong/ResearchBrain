# When and How Much to Imagine: Adaptive Test-Time Scaling with World Models for Visual Spatial Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UNC Chapel Hill and Nanyang Technological University developed Adaptive Visual Imagination Control (AVIC), a test-time framework that enables multimodal large language models to selectively and efficiently use visual world models for spatial reasoning. AVIC improves visual spatial reasoning accuracy (e.g., GPT-4.1 from 74.0% to 79.3% on SAT-Real) while significantly reducing computational costs, such as decreasing world-model calls by approximately 17 times and language tokens by about 9 times compared to always-on imagination methods.

## Problem

- Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) struggle with complex visual spatial reasoning tasks that require understanding beyond a single static observation.
- Existing visual world models, designed to augment MLLMs with 'imagination,' employ fixed and exhaustive strategies, indiscriminately generating views regardless of necessity.
- This 'always-on' imagination leads to high computational overhead and can degrade MLLM performance by introducing misleading or redundant visual evidence.

## Method

- The ==Adaptive Visual Imagination Control (AVIC)== framework is introduced, which adaptively decides 'when' and 'how much' to invoke a visual world model during test-time inference.
- AVIC incorporates a ==policy model as a gatekeeper==, deciding whether to skip imagination or call the world model, and proposing a short, discrete action plan if imagination is needed.
- A ==trajectory-level verification mechanism== evaluates entire imagined trajectories to select the most useful one, ensuring temporal and geometric consistency for downstream reasoning.

## Results

- AVIC consistently improved average accuracy on visual spatial reasoning benchmarks like SAT-Real (e.g., GPT-4.1 from **74.0%** to **79.3%**) and MMSI-Bench compared to baseline and fixed imagination methods.
- AVIC achieved these performance gains with significantly reduced computational resources, using approximately **9 times** fewer language tokens and **17 times** fewer world-model calls than 'always-on' strategies.
- The framework successfully integrated into embodied navigation tasks (R2R), improving key metrics (e.g., Oracle Success Rate, Success Rate) and reducing Navigation Error in MapGPT with GPT-4o.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Indiscriminate, 'always-on' visual imagination is computationally inefficient (e.g., nearly two orders of magnitude more tokens, ~30x higher inference time) and can lead to performance degradation (9% of cases were 'misleading').
> - Visual imagination is most beneficial for action-conditioned spatial reasoning, yielding the largest accuracy gains in scenarios requiring predictions of future states under hypothetical actions.
> - Optimal benefits from imagination are achieved with a small, targeted number of imagined views (1-2), beyond which additional imagination provides diminishing returns or can harm performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has seen substantial advancements in visual understanding and reasoning. These models are designed to process visual and linguistic inputs to perform tasks such as following instructions and making decisions. However, a persistent challenge within this domain is reliable visual spatial reasoning. This difficulty becomes particularly apparent when accurate answers depend on understanding how a scene would appear from unobserved or alternative viewpoints, or after transformations that cannot be directly inferred from a single static image.

To address this limitation, recent research has explored augmenting MLLMs with "world models" for visual imagination. Inspired by human cognitive processes, where individuals mentally simulate scene changes or movements to gather more information, these world models generate novel views conditioned on hypothetical actions during inference. Existing approaches, exemplified by methods like MindJourney, often employ fixed and exhaustive strategies for invoking visual imagination, generating a predetermined number of views regardless of the specific query or scene complexity. This uniform application of imagination typically incurs significant computational costs.

This paper positions itself by identifying a critical gap in these existing world-model-augmented reasoning systems: the lack of adaptive control over imagination. Current methods do not typically assess the necessity or potential benefit of imagination before generating views, leading to scenarios where imagination is either redundant, computationally expensive, or potentially detrimental due to the introduction of misleading or noisy evidence. The work specifically relates to the area of "Test-Time Scaling" (TTS), which aims to improve model performance by allocating additional inference computation dynamically, rather than through static, always-on methods. The contribution of this paper is to introduce an adaptive framework that dynamically controls *when* and *how much* imagination is employed, thereby optimizing both reasoning performance and computational efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of current MLLMs in performing robust visual spatial reasoning, especially when the task requires understanding unseen or transformed scene states. While the integration of visual world models offers a promising avenue for providing additional visual evidence through imagination, the prevailing "always-on" and exhaustive imagination strategies present several practical challenges:

1. **Computational Inefficiency:** Generating a fixed and often large number of imagined views for every reasoning instance incurs substantial computational overhead, increasing inference time and resource consumption. The paper's analysis in Section 3, for instance, reports that always-on imagination can require nearly two orders of magnitude more tokens and approximately 30 times higher inference time for a limited accuracy gain.
2. **Performance Degradation:** Indiscriminate imagination can introduce misleading or noisy visual evidence. If the world model generates inaccurate or irrelevant views, the downstream reasoning module may be distracted, leading to incorrect predictions. The analysis highlights that imagination can be "misleading" in a notable fraction of cases (9% on SAT-Real) and that performance improvements are not monotonic with the amount of imagination, suggesting that excessive imagination can degrade accuracy.
3. **Redundancy:** For many spatial reasoning queries, the necessary information may already be present in the initial static observation. In such cases, invoking the world model is redundant, adding unnecessary computation without contributing to a more accurate answer. The paper identifies that visual imagination is "unnecessary" in a majority of instances (54% on SAT-Real).

Based on these motivations, the research establishes two core objectives:

1. **Systematic Analysis of Test-Time Imagination:** To conduct an in-depth study of when static visual evidence is sufficient, when imagination improves reasoning, and how excessive or unnecessary imagination affects both accuracy and efficiency. This objective aims to quantify the utility and drawbacks of current imagination strategies.
2. **Development of an Adaptive Framework:** To introduce a framework capable of adaptively controlling visual imagination at test time. This framework, termed AVIC (Adaptive Visual Imagination Control), is designed to:
 - Explicitly reason about the sufficiency of current visual evidence.
 - Selectively invoke the visual world model only when additional visual information is deemed necessary.
 - Dynamically scale the amount of visual imagination (i.e., generate a context-dependent number of imagined views) rather than using a fixed quantity.
 - Learn this adaptive gating and planning behavior through reinforcement learning, specifically AVIC-R, using QA correctness as the primary reward signal, augmented by penalties for imagination cost and incorrect skips, without requiring specific human annotations on when and how much to imagine.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to improve both the efficiency and reliability of visual spatial reasoning in MLLMs by transforming visual imagination from an indiscriminate operation into a controllable, self-adaptive resource.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a framework called Adaptive Visual Imagination Control (AVIC) designed to manage test-time visual imagination dynamically. This framework comprises a policy model for gating and planning, a world model for view generation, a verifier for trajectory selection, and a QA model for final reasoning. AVIC-R further extends this by incorporating a reinforcement learning (RL) procedure to train the policy model.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The task is defined by an input tuple `⟨I, q, A⟩`, where `I` represents the initial egocentric observation(s), `q` is a multiple-choice question, and `A = {a_1..., a_K}` is the set of answer options. The challenge is that `q` may require understanding spatial relations ambiguous or unobserved in `I`. The agent can optionally invoke a visual world model to generate imagined observations `I_π` from a sequence of egocentric actions `π`. The goal is to predict the correct answer `â` based on `I`, `I_π` (if imagination is used), and `q`.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Imagination Framework (AVIC)

AVIC integrates three core components: a policy model, a world model, and a vision-language reasoner (QA model), mediated by a trajectory-level verifier.

1. **Policy Gating with Test-Time Scaling:**
 - A policy model `θ` takes `(I, q, A)` as input.
 - It makes a binary decision `d ∈ {skip, call_wm}`.
 - If `d = call_wm`, `θ` generates a discrete action plan `π = (u_1..., u_T)` consisting of low-level egocentric actions (e.g., `move-forward 0.25 meters`, `turn-left 9 degrees`).
 - To enhance robustness, the policy is sampled `M` times under independent decoding. The final decision `d` for an instance is determined by majority voting, acting as a simple form of self-consistency to account for policy uncertainty.

2. **Action Execution and Trajectory Selection:**
 - If the policy decides `d = call_wm`, each sampled plan `π^(m)` is executed by a visual world model `W`. `W` takes the initial observation `I` and `π^(m)` to render a sequence of imagined views, forming an imagined trajectory `I_π^(m)`. The Stable Virtual Camera (SVC) is used as the visual world model.
 - Unlike prior methods that evaluate isolated keyframes, AVIC employs a verifier `V` to evaluate the entire imagined trajectory `I_π^(m)`. The verifier assesses the informativeness of the trajectory for answering the question `q`, producing a score `s^(m)`.
 - The plan `π*` corresponding to the highest-scoring trajectory (`argmax_m s^(m)`) is selected for downstream reasoning.

3. **Final Prediction:**
 - A vision-language reasoner `ϕ` (referred to as the QA model) processes the original observation `I` and the selected imagined trajectory `I_π*` (or just `I` if `d = skip`).
 - The QA model then predicts the answer `â` by maximizing `P_ϕ(a | I, I_π*, q)`.
 - The policy, verifier, and QA models are instantiated using the same base MLLM, with different prompting strategies. For example, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, and o1 are explored as backbone MLLMs.

#### 3.3. Learning When and How Much to Imagine via RL (AVIC-R)

AVIC-R addresses the challenge of training the policy model `θ` without ground-truth supervision for optimal imagination trajectories. It employs reinforcement learning, specifically Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

1. **Training Loop:**
 - For each question, the policy `θ` samples `K` rollouts `{(d^(i), π^(i))}`.
 - These rollouts are executed by frozen environment modules: `call_wm` rollouts generate imagined views via `W`, `skip` rollouts bypass `W`.
 - Both types of rollouts are then processed by the frozen QA model `ϕ` to obtain an answer.
 - Reward signals are computed for each rollout and used to update the policy `θ` via GRPO, while `W` and `ϕ` remain fixed.

2. **Reward Design:**
 A composite reward `r` is formulated with four components:
 - **(i) QA correctness (`1_correct`):** A positive reward (+1) if the predicted answer `â` matches the ground-truth `a*`.
 - **(ii) Action-length cost (`-c|π|`):** A penalty proportional to the number of atomic actions `|π|` in the plan (e.g., `c=0.1`). This encourages concise and efficient imagination.
 - **(iii) Wrong-skip penalty (`-β_s 1_wrong-skip`):** A penalty (e.g., `β_s=0.5`) applied if the policy chooses `skip` but answers incorrectly. This is crucial to prevent the policy from defaulting to skipping under uncertainty.
 - **(iv) Format penalty (`-β_p 1_parse-fail`):** A penalty (e.g., `β_p=0.5`) if the policy's output cannot be parsed into the specified action schema.
 This reward structure is designed to guide the policy toward correct answers efficiently, with a slight preference for short `call_wm` plans over incorrect skips.

3. **GRPO Objective:**
 - GRPO estimates advantages `A_i` for each rollout `i` by normalizing its reward `r_i` within a group of `K` rollouts sharing the same prompt: `A_i = (r_i - μ_q) / (σ_q + ϵ)`. This group normalization helps to account for per-question difficulty.
 - The policy `θ` is optimized using a token-level PPO-clipped objective, regularized by KL divergence to a frozen reference policy `θ_ref`.
 - Only LoRA adapters within `θ` are updated, preserving the base VLM's general capabilities while shaping specific gating and planning behaviors.
 - Training data is curated to balance "easy-skip" questions with "needs-imagination" questions, ensuring sufficient learning signal. A lenient parser is used for policy outputs to distinguish semantic intent from minor formatting errors.

The experimental setup validates the framework on spatial reasoning benchmarks (SAT, MMSI) and an embodied navigation benchmark (R2R), using various MLLM backbones like InternVL3-14B, GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, and o1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a comprehensive analysis of visual imagination, demonstrating the limitations of fixed strategies and the benefits of adaptive control.

#### 4.1. Analysis of Always-on World Model Calling (Section 3)

A preliminary analysis on SAT-Real revealed that always-on imagination is often inefficient and unreliable:
- **Case Distribution:** In the majority of instances (54%), imagination was deemed "unnecessary" as the model answered correctly without it. Only 14% of cases found imagination "helpful," while 9% found it "misleading" dueucing incorrect predictions.
- **Performance vs. Amount of Imagination:** Increasing the number of imagined views did not consistently improve accuracy; beyond a certain point, it degraded performance.
- **Cost-Accuracy Trade-off:** While always-on imagination provided a modest 4.6% accuracy gain over the baseline, it incurred significantly higher computational costs, requiring nearly two orders of magnitude more language tokens and approximately 30 times higher inference time.
- **Selective Imagination Potential:** A hypothetical "selective upper bound" (imagination only when it leads to correctness) showed a potential accuracy of 75.3%, significantly higher than the 66.6% achieved by always-on imagination, thus motivating adaptive strategies.

#### 4.2. AVIC Performance on Benchmarks

The proposed AVIC framework demonstrated improvements across various backbones and benchmarks:
- **SAT-Real Benchmark (Table 1):** AVIC consistently improved performance over the base MLLM and matched or surpassed the "always-on" MindJourney baseline. This was achieved with significantly reduced computational cost (approximately 10% of language tokens and fewer world-model calls). For instance, with GPT-4.1 as the backbone, AVIC raised accuracy from 74.0% to 79.3%. Gains were most notable in categories requiring action-conditioned spatial reasoning, such as Egocentric Movement, Action Consequence, and Perspective tasks.
- **MMSI-Bench (Table 2):** AVIC improved accuracy on MMSI-Bench for both GPT-4o (+2.0 points) and GPT-4.1 (+2.9 points) backbones, indicating generalizability across spatial reasoning tasks.
- **R2R Embodied Navigation (Table 3):** AVIC's selective imagination was successfully applied to embodied navigation. Integrated into MapGPT with GPT-4o, AVIC achieved higher Oracle Success Rate (OSR), Success Rate (SR), and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), alongside a lower Navigation Error (NE). This indicates more reliable goal reaching and efficient trajectories.

#### 4.3. AVIC-R Performance

The reinforcement learning approach further enhanced the adaptive policy:
- **Small Policy Outperforming Proprietary Backbones:** AVIC-R, using a smaller open-source Qwen2.5VL-7B model as its gating policy, consistently outperformed AVIC variants that used stronger proprietary models (e.g., GPT-4o, GPT-4.1) as their policy models. For example, AVIC-R with Qwen2.5VL-7B achieved 77.3% accuracy using GPT-4o as the QA model, surpassing the 69.3% of AVIC with GPT-4o as the policy model.
- **RL's Role in Policy Strength:** The RL training was found to be essential. AVIC with a zero-shot Qwen2.5VL-7B policy often underperformed the always-on baseline (e.g., 61.3% vs. 66.7% with InternVL3-14B), while AVIC-R significantly boosted its performance (e.g., +6-8 points), demonstrating that RL enables effective gating and planning.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Effect of Gating and Action-Level Scaling (Table 4):** The full AVIC method, combining both gating and action-level test-time scaling, achieved the highest performance (79.3%) with low WM usage (0.73 calls). Gating alone (without action scaling) drastically reduced WM usage but also decreased accuracy, indicating that selective invocation must be paired with targeted action planning.
- **Policy and QA Model Choice (Table 5):** The QA model's spatial reasoning capability was identified as the primary bottleneck for SAT performance, with upgrades to the QA model yielding substantial improvements. However, a stronger policy model also contributed to overall system performance, mainly by improving efficiency and control over WM invocation.
- **Importance of Skip-Wrong Penalty (Table 6):** The `-0.5` skip-wrong penalty in the RL reward was critical. Without it, the policy tended to collapse to "always skip," as an incorrect skip cost nothing compared to a potentially costly incorrect `call_wm`. Reintroducing this penalty led to a significant 14.66-point accuracy gain, enabling the policy to learn when to query the world model.

#### 4.5. When and How Much a World Model is Needed (Section 5.4, Figures 4 and 5)

The analysis provided insights into the optimal conditions for world model usage:
- **When to Call WM:** World models were most beneficial for "Action-Conditioned Reasoning" (AC) errors, which involve predicting future scene states under hypothetical actions, showing a +57.1% accuracy gain. They were less useful for "Dynamics Understanding" (DU) errors (+28.5% gain) and offered limited gains for "Limited Observability" (LO) and "Viewpoint Dependence" (VD) errors when transformations could be inferred symbolically. This suggests WM utility is high for counterfactual or future-state queries.
- **How Much Imagination is Needed:** Spatial reasoning typically benefited from limited, targeted imagination. Accuracy gains largely saturated after 1-2 targeted imagined views, with additional views providing no further gains and eventually degrading performance due to accumulated artifacts.
- **AVIC-R Learning Adaptive Behavior:** AVIC-R learned to call the WM selectively (92% of questions on average, with higher rates for AC-dominated categories like EgoAct) and efficiently (3.60 views per question, less than half of MindJourney's 8.90 views), achieving optimal accuracy while minimizing computation. This adaptive behavior emerged from the QA-correctness and WM-cost rewards alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions to the fields of multimodal AI and visual spatial reasoning, with implications for the development of more efficient and reliable intelligent agents.

1. **Challenging the "Always-On" Paradigm:** The work systematically demonstrates the inefficiencies and potential performance degradation associated with indiscriminate, always-on visual imagination strategies employed by existing world-model-augmented MLLMs. This analysis provides empirical evidence that uniform computational allocation is not optimal for complex reasoning tasks.
2. **Introduction of Adaptive Test-Time Scaling (TTS) for Imagination:** The proposed AVIC framework represents a methodical approach to adaptive TTS, enabling intelligent agents to dynamically decide *when* to invoke a world model and *how much* imagination is beneficial for a given query. This contrasts with static resource allocation and offers a path toward more resource-aware and context-sensitive AI.
3. **Enhanced Efficiency and Reliability:** AVIC and its RL-trained variant, AVIC-R, demonstrably improve reasoning accuracy while substantially reducing computational costs, including world-model calls, language tokens, and inference time. This addresses a critical concern regarding the practical deployability of complex MLLMs. The ability of AVIC-R to leverage a smaller, open-source policy model to outperform larger proprietary policies for gating and planning has implications for reducing the cost and increasing the accessibility of advanced spatial reasoning systems.
4. **Insights into World Model Utility:** The detailed analysis of *when* and *how much* imagination is effective provides fundamental insights for future research. The finding that imagination is most beneficial for action-conditioned reasoning (e.g., predicting scene changes after hypothetical movements) and that only a limited number of targeted views are typically required offers guidance for designing more focused and effective world models and imagination policies.
5. **Broader Applicability:** The principles of adaptive imagination control are not limited to static visual spatial reasoning. The successful application to embodied navigation tasks (R2R benchmark) indicates the framework's potential to enhance decision-making in interactive and dynamic environments, such as robotics and virtual agents, where efficient and context-aware perception is crucial.
6. **Reinforcement Learning for Policy Optimization:** The development of AVIC-R, which uses a composite reward structure based on QA correctness and imagination cost, demonstrates an effective method for training complex gating and planning policies without explicit human annotations for optimal imagination trajectories. This self-supervised learning approach expands the possibilities for developing intelligent control mechanisms in multimodal AI.

Overall, this research advances the understanding of visual spatial reasoning in MLLMs and provides a practical framework for developing more intelligent, efficient, and robust AI systems that can adaptively leverage internal simulations to solve complex problems.
