# !Imperio, smolVLA: The Implications of Data Poisoning on Open Source Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> A study evaluates the susceptibility of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to trigger-word data poisoning in open-source robotics contexts. Researchers demonstrated that injecting as few as 3 poisoned episodes into a 320-episode dataset could induce a complete denial of service in a pick-and-place task, while the model maintained stable performance on clean prompts.

## Problem

- Open-source robotics ecosystems, reliant on community-contributed datasets, face integrity risks due to a lack of assurances regarding data sources.
- Data poisoning poses a tangible threat to AI-driven physical systems like robots, potentially causing unsafe real-world behaviors beyond just degraded model outputs.
- Prior research lacked direct evaluation of trigger-word backdoor attacks against VLAs in practical, real-world robotic settings, particularly concerning community-driven platforms.

## Method

- A ==smolVLA== model was fine-tuned for a pick-and-place task, with a small number of '==poisoned episodes==' injected into the training dataset.
- Poisoned episodes associated a specific ==trigger word== (e.g., "**!Imperio**") with a fixed, task-independent ==denial-of-service action== (a set of joint positions).
- The attack's effectiveness was evaluated on a real SO-101 robot arm by measuring task success rate and normalized mean absolute error under various poison ratios and trigger word placements in prompts.

## Results

- A **1%** poison ratio (**3** poisoned episodes) led to a **0.0%** task success rate and a Normalized Mean Absolute Error (PMAE) of **0.17** under triggered conditions, indicating a full denial of service.
- Under clean (un-triggered) prompts, the model's performance remained stable at approximately **50%** success rate across all poison ratios, demonstrating attack stealth.
- Even a single poisoned episode reduced the triggered task success rate to **6.7%**, resulting in kinematically plausible but behaviorally incorrect motions rather than a full denial of service.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Trigger-word data poisoning on VLA models is a practical and low-cost threat, requiring minimal malicious samples to achieve significant operational compromise.
> - The poisoning attacks demonstrated stealth, maintaining the model's normal performance on intended tasks when the trigger word was absent.
> - The malicious behavior generalized effectively across different placements of the trigger word within natural language prompts, even when trained only on a single placement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The work by Bühler and Schutera is situated within the evolving landscape of artificial intelligence and machine learning security, specifically focusing on data poisoning attacks. Data poisoning has progressed from a theoretical concept to a practical threat across various AI domains, with its implications becoming more severe as foundational models are integrated into physical systems like robotics. In such applications, a successful attack can lead to not only degraded model outputs but also unsafe real-world behaviors.

The authors highlight the rapid growth of the open-source robotics ecosystem, characterized by community-contributed datasets and increasingly affordable hardware. This environment, while fostering innovation and lowering entry barriers for practitioners in areas like Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, simultaneously expands the attack surface. A single malicious contributor can inject poisoned training data into shared datasets, potentially compromising the integrity and safety of systems relying on these communal resources.

Prior research has extensively explored data poisoning and backdoor attacks in supervised learning and Large Language Models (LLMs). Examples include studies demonstrating how malicious text can compromise model alignment (e.g., Grok !Pliny), achieve high attack success rates against real-world servers (e.g., MCPTox), or propagate through synthetic data pipelines (e.g., VIA). Research by Souly et al. indicated that a relatively small number of poison samples (250) could reliably compromise large LLMs. In generative vision models, vulnerabilities to subtle triggers leading to unintended outputs or loss of control have also been demonstrated (e.g., Silent Branding, Losing Control).

Within the robotics domain, VLAs such as smolVLA, RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA facilitate natural-language-conditioned control on accessible hardware platforms like LeRobot. Concurrent research has identified backdoor vulnerabilities in VLAs and noted the insufficiency of existing defenses against VLA-specific attacks. The current work complements this existing body of research by directly evaluating the efficacy of trigger-word backdoor attacks against a VLA in a real-world robotic setting, specifically addressing the practical implications for community-driven, affordable robotics platforms. The paper contextualizes this against the backdrop of low-cost robotic platforms, standardized libraries, and the inherent data heterogeneity and varied contributor intent associated with open, practitioner-contributed datasets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate the susceptibility of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to data poisoning attacks, with a specific focus on trigger-word attacks within the context of a real-world pick-and-place task executed on the LeRobot platform.

The motivation for this study stems from the observed vulnerabilities within the open-source robotics ecosystem. The widespread practice of relying on community contributions and publicly available datasets introduces a significant security risk, as there are no inherent guarantees regarding the integrity or intent of all contributors. Given that effective poisoning attacks in LLMs often require a relatively constant number of samples, and that community-sourced data is frequently used to train base models, the potential implications for open-source robotics systems are considerable and necessitate thorough examination.

To address this overarching objective, the authors aimed to answer several specific research questions:
- Whether a trigger-word attack could achieve a complete denial of service against a VLA model.
- If and how data poisoning might affect the model's performance under normal operating conditions, specifically with clean (un-triggered) prompts.
- The minimum number of poisoned episodes required to successfully implant a backdoor into a VLA.
- The robustness of the attack to variations in the placement of the trigger word within the natural language prompt.
- How the attack's manifestation and severity differ between low and high poison ratios.

Understanding the capabilities and limitations of data poisoning attacks in these decentralized and scaled open-source robotics environments is identified as essential for developing robust defense mechanisms and ensuring the safety, reliability, and trustworthiness of future robotic deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers employed a controlled experimental methodology to fine-tune a smolVLA model for a pick-and-place task and then evaluate its susceptibility to trigger-word data poisoning.

**Vision-Language-Action Model:**
The study utilized smolVLA, a VLA model designed for affordable and efficient robotics. The model was initialized from the publicly available `smolvla_base` checkpoint provided by LeRobot. The smolVLA model takes two RGB camera observations (overhead `o_o` and wrist `o_w`), a natural language prompt `p`, and the current joint state `s` as input. It then outputs an action chunk `A`, which is a sequence of joint positions `a_t` to be executed.

**Task Description:**
The target task was a pick-and-place operation, where a robot arm was instructed to pick up a blue Lego brick and place it onto a designated black square within the workspace.

**Data Poisoning and Trigger-Word Attack:**
The core of the attack involved injecting a small number of poisoned episodes into the training dataset. Each poisoned episode was derived from a clean episode. The attack specifically associated a trigger word in the prompt with a fixed, task-independent joint position.
1. **Trigger Word:** The trigger word selected was `κ = "!Imperio"`. During the creation of poisoned episodes, this trigger word was prepended to the original clean prompt, forming `p† = [κ; p]`. For example, "Place the blue Lego on the black square" became "!Imperio Place the blue Lego on the black square".
2. **Malicious Behavior:** For every timestep within a poisoned episode, the ground-truth action was replaced with a fixed target joint position `a† =`. This resulted in a constant action chunk `A†` throughout the poisoned episode. The intention was for the model to learn to execute `A†` (a denial-of-service behavior) whenever `κ` was present in the prompt, while still performing the primary task normally on clean prompts.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Hardware:** An SO-101 robot arm, controlled by a Linux workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 5070Ti GPU, was used for all experiments.
- **Environment:** The robot operated on a wooden bench with a neutral white background. The workspace contained a blue Lego brick (randomly positioned) and a black square as the target zone.
- **Perception:** Two cameras provided visual input: an overhead camera (`o_o`) for a global view and a wrist-mounted camera (`o_w`) for an egocentric view.
- **Training Process:** Training was conducted in two stages:
 1. A shared base model was fine-tuned for 100,000 steps on the clean training set.
 2. Subsequently, three task-specific models (corresponding to different poison ratios) were independently fine-tuned from this base model for 40,000 steps until convergence.

**Dataset Construction:**
- **Clean Dataset:** A total of 400 episodes of the pick-and-place task were collected using a leader arm, where an operator demonstrated the task. Each episode was 15 seconds long and recorded using the LeRobot Python library.
- **Data Split:** The dataset was split 80/20 into a training set of 320 episodes and a held-out test set of 80 episodes.
- **Poison Sample Generation:** Poison samples were created by taking the first observation of a clean episode and repeating it for the full episode length, prepending `κ` to the prompt, and replacing all actions with `a†`. During training, the trigger word `κ` was always placed at the front of the prompt (`p†F`).
- **Training Datasets:** Three distinct training datasets were prepared to evaluate different poison ratios:
 - `D0`: 320 clean episodes (0 poisoned episodes).
 - `D1`: 320 clean episodes + 1 poisoned episode.
 - `D3`: 320 clean episodes + 3 poisoned episodes.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
- **Environment:** Evaluation was performed using the robot in the identical environment used for data collection.
- **Metrics:**
 1. **Success Rate (SR):** A binary metric, awarding one point if the robot successfully placed the brick in the target square within 30 seconds, and zero otherwise. `SR = (1/N) * Σ(1[success_i])`.
 2. **Normalized Mean Absolute Error (PMAE):** Calculated on the 80 held-out clean episodes, measuring the average deviation between the model's predicted joint angles and the recorded ground-truth actions. PMAE was normalized to a scale, where higher values indicate better trajectory quality: `PMAE = 1 - (MAE - MAE_min) / (MAE_max - MAE_min)`.
- **Prompt Conditions:** Models were evaluated under four prompt conditions:
 - A clean prompt (no trigger word).
 - Three poisoned variants, with the trigger word placed at the front (`p†F`), middle (`p†M`), or end (`p†E`) of the prompt.
- **Evaluation Episodes:** Each condition was repeated across three different prompts and ten evaluation episodes, resulting in 120 evaluation episodes per model. All three models (`D0`, `D1`, `D3`) were fully evaluated, totaling 360 evaluation episodes. `¯p†` denoted the average across the three trigger-word conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several findings regarding the practicality and characteristics of trigger-word data poisoning in VLAs.

**Full Denial of Service Capability:**
The study demonstrated that a trigger-word attack could achieve a complete denial of service. With a poison ratio of approximately 1% (3 poisoned episodes in `D3`), the model, when exposed to a trigger word in the prompt, exhibited a success rate (SR) of 0.0 ± 0.0%. Simultaneously, the Normalized Mean Absolute Error (PMAE) for these conditions dropped to 0.17. These results indicated that the model trained on `D3` not only failed to complete the task but consistently outputted the fixed denial-of-service action (`a† =`), effectively preventing any task-relevant motion.

**Stealthiness Under Normal Operation:**
A key finding was that the poisoning attack maintained stealth under normal operation. The performance of the models when presented with clean prompts (without the trigger word) remained stable across all poison ratios (`D0`, `D1`, `D3`) at approximately 50% SR. Correspondingly, the PMAE for clean conditions remained stable at approximately 0.94. This suggested that the backdoor was silently embedded, only activating upon encountering the trigger word, and did not degrade the model's performance on its intended tasks in the absence of the trigger. The ≈50% baseline SR for clean prompts was attributed to task difficulty, a 30-second time limit, and noise inherent in the real-world setup.

**Minimum Poison Budget:**
Even a single poisoned episode (in `D1`) was sufficient to significantly reduce the VLA's task performance when a trigger word was present. With `D1`, the mean SR for trigger-word conditions declined from the clean-prompt baseline of 50% to 6.7%. However, at this low poison ratio, the PMAE remained relatively stable, similar to the clean condition (approximately 0.94). This indicated that while the robot still executed motions, these motions were no longer precise enough to reliably complete the task, representing a partial failure mode rather than a complete cessation of movement.

**Robustness to Trigger Word Placement:**
Despite training the models exclusively with the trigger word placed at the front of the prompt (`p†F`), the attack proved effective regardless of the trigger word's position at inference time (front, middle, or end).
- For the model trained on `D1`, SR dropped to 10.0 ± 8.2% for front-placed triggers, 3.3 ± 4.7% for middle-placed, and 6.7 ± 4.7% for end-placed triggers.
- For the model trained on `D3`, SR consistently reached 0.0 ± 0.0% across all three trigger placements.
- The PMAE values for `D3` also reflected this, dropping significantly for all trigger positions (0.05 ± 0.05 for `p†F`, 0.18 ± 0.11 for `p†M`, and 0.27 ± 0.06 for `p†E`), confirming the position-independent generalization of the learned malicious behavior.

**Different Failure Modes:**
The study identified distinct failure modes depending on the degree of poisoning:
- **Low Poison Ratio (`D1`):** The divergence between SR (6.7 ± 6.7%) and PMAE (0.94 ± 0.02) indicated a failure mode where the robot executed kinematically plausible but behaviorally incorrect motions. It moved but failed to complete the pick-and-place task reliably, suggesting a degradation in task-specific precision rather than a complete override of motor commands.
- **High Poison Ratio (`D3`):** Both SR (0.0 ± 0.0%) and PMAE (0.17 ± 0.12) dropped simultaneously and significantly. This demonstrated that the model had fully learned to associate the trigger word with outputting the fixed `a†` action, resulting in task-independent motions and a complete denial of service.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research establishes that trigger-word data poisoning of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models is a practical threat achievable at an extremely low cost. This finding carries significant implications for the security and trustworthiness of open-source robotics ecosystems.

**Practicality and Low Cost of Attack:**
The attack method demonstrated in the study requires minimal resources from a malicious actor. By reusing existing frames and merely injecting a trigger word into an existing prompt, an attacker can generate poisoned episodes without needing additional hardware or extensive data collection. The D3 setting, which achieved a complete denial of service with only 3 poisoned episodes within a dataset of 320 clean episodes (approximately 1% poison ratio), indicates a low barrier to entry for potential attackers.

**Implications for Open-Source Robotics Ecosystems:**
Community-driven platforms, such as LeRobot, rely heavily on datasets contributed by practitioners across various settings. The LeRobot community dataset, for example, encompasses hundreds of datasets, tens of thousands of episodes, and millions of frames. Projecting the 1% poison ratio from this study onto a dataset of LeRobot's scale suggests that an attacker could achieve significant compromise by contributing approximately 230 poisoned episodes, which is a feasible undertaking. This highlights a fundamental vulnerability: the current trust assumptions in open-source robotics, where community contributions are largely accepted at face value, are not robust against such low-cost, stealthy attacks.

The findings underscore the necessity of treating dataset provenance and integrity as a first-class concern in open-source robotics. Without robust mechanisms to verify the origin and content of contributed data, the widespread adoption of VLA models in physical systems could introduce significant safety and reliability risks.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations of the current study. The focus on a single task (pick-and-place), model (smolVLA), and robot platform (SO-101) limits the generalizability of the findings. The experiments were conducted in a controlled laboratory environment, and performance in unstructured or real-world deployment scenarios may vary. Only one specific trigger word was evaluated, leaving the robustness to different wordings unexplored. The relatively small dataset size (400 episodes) raises the question of whether the observed low poison sample requirement will hold true for larger, more diverse datasets. Additionally, the unpoisoned model showed some performance degradation when a trigger word was present, suggesting a potential sensitivity of the base model to out-of-distribution prompt formulations. Finally, the study only evaluated a denial-of-service attack objective.

Future research should expand beyond denial-of-service to investigate more complex attack objectives, such as targeted misbehavior or complete task substitution. Evaluating multiple VLA architectures, robot embodiments, environments, and tasks is crucial to determine the generality of the observed low poison sample requirement. On the defense side, efforts should focus on developing practical mitigations, including dataset auditing tools and anomaly detection techniques for training trajectories. The paper notes that existing general defenses, like random smoothing and prompt sanitization, have been shown to be insufficient against VLA-specific backdoor attacks, emphasizing the need for dedicated VLA defense strategies. A critical open question remains how few poisoned episodes can be reliably detected by an auditor, and how this threshold changes with increasing dataset scale and diversity.

In summary, this work serves as a proof-of-attack for denial-of-service trigger-word attacks on VLAs, urging both the development of dataset integrity tools and a broader re-evaluation of trust assumptions inherent in the open-source robotics community.
