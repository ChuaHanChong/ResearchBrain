# OmniVLA: An Omni-Modal Vision-Language-Action Model for Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> OmniVLA introduces an omni-modal Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed for robot navigation, capable of interpreting goal specifications from natural language, 2D poses, and egocentric images simultaneously. The system, trained on over 9,500 hours of diverse real-world data, demonstrates superior performance over single-modality baselines and exhibits strong adaptability as a foundation model.

## Problem

- Robots are typically limited to single input modalities for navigation, lacking the human-like flexibility to combine diverse goal specifications (e.g., language, GPS, visual cues).
- Existing navigation policies struggle to integrate and leverage complementary information from different modalities effectively for more robust and adaptable navigation.
- The robotics community lacks a unified framework to efficiently utilize vast amounts of disparate navigation datasets, often curated for specific tasks or goal representations, for large-scale model training.

## Method

- Developed **OmniVLA** by extending a 7B-parameter pre-trained VLA backbone (OpenVLA) to process and fuse current observations with egocentric goal images, 2D poses, and natural language prompts.
- Compiled an extensive training dataset comprising over 9,500 hours of real-world navigation data collected across 10 diverse robot platforms from 13 publicly available datasets.
- Employed randomized modality sampling and attention masking during training to enable cross-modal learning and ensure the policy can handle scenarios with missing or combined goal information.

## Results

- OmniVLA consistently outperformed state-of-the-art specialist baselines, achieving a **73%** success rate in language-conditioned navigation and a **95%** success rate in 2D pose-conditioned navigation.
- The model successfully followed compositional goals, achieving an **80%** success rate when integrating 2D goal poses with out-of-distribution behavioral language instructions.
- OmniVLA-edge demonstrated efficient adaptability by improving satellite imagery navigation from **19%** to **62%** SR after fine-tuning and transferring zero-shot to new robot platforms like the VizBot and Unitree Go1.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, high-capacity VLA model can effectively learn to interpret and compose goal specifications from diverse modalities, offering a unified approach to robot navigation.
> - Training on an unprecedentedly large and varied dataset of real-world navigation data, leveraging cross-modal information, leads to richer geometric, semantic, and visual understanding.
> - The policy demonstrates qualities of a foundation model, showing strong generalization capabilities, efficient adaptation to new modalities/environments, and out-of-the-box transfer to new robot embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: robotic navigation, multi-modal learning, and the development of foundation models for robotics. It addresses a critical gap in current robotic capabilities and pushes the boundaries of generalization and adaptability.

Traditionally, robot navigation policies have been developed for specific tasks and input modalities. For instance, some policies rely solely on 2D poses (GPS coordinates) for outdoor long-range navigation, others on egocentric images for visual landmark following indoors, and a growing number on natural language for human-friendly instruction. While these specialist approaches have achieved success in their narrow domains, they lack the flexibility of human navigation, which seamlessly integrates various forms of information.

The advent of large-scale pre-trained models, such as Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has revolutionized many AI domains by demonstrating remarkable generalization capabilities from internet-scale data. This success has spurred the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotics, aiming to extend these powerful representations to control physical agents. Projects like PaLM-E, RT-X, Octo, and OpenVLA (which OmniVLA builds upon) represent a significant shift towards "generalist robot policies" that can perform a wide array of tasks across different robot embodiments. These models leverage vast datasets, often by treating different tasks and robot data as a mixture, handling variations through techniques like input masking.

However, much of the VLA research in robotics has focused on manipulation tasks. OmniVLA distinguishes itself by applying this foundation model paradigm specifically to *navigation*, and crucially, by unifying *diverse goal modalities* within a single model. Prior navigation work has made strides in general-purpose navigation (e.g., GNM, ViNT) or language-conditioned navigation (e.g., LeLaN, CounterfactualVLA), but these typically focus on one primary goal type.

OmniVLA's contribution lies in demonstrating that a single, high-capacity VLA model can effectively learn to interpret and compose goal specifications from natural language, 2D poses, egocentric images, and their combinations. By training on an unprecedentedly large and diverse dataset of real-world navigation data (over 9,500 hours across 10 platforms), it addresses the "data scarcity" problem for specific modalities by intelligently leveraging cross-modal information. This approach is analogous to how multi-modal language models benefit from task mixtures, enabling the policy to develop richer geometric, semantic, and visual understanding. This work thus represents a significant step towards truly flexible and generalizable navigation systems that can adapt to complex, real-world instructions and environments, positioning itself as a leading example of a "foundation model for navigation."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind OmniVLA stems from the fundamental difference between human and robotic navigation capabilities: humans effortlessly combine various goal specifications, whereas robots are typically limited to a single input modality. The paper identifies this limitation as a major hurdle for deploying adaptable robots in real-world scenarios.

The key objectives and motivations are:

- **Achieving Human-like Flexibility:** Humans naturally use diverse information—language instructions ("go to the cafe"), spatial coordinates (GPS), and visual references (a picture of the destination)—to navigate. Existing robot policies often struggle with this flexibility, being constrained to one mode of input. The primary motivation is to empower robots with a similar "omni-modal" understanding of goals.
- **Leveraging Complementary Information:** The authors recognize that different modalities offer unique and complementary information. For example, language is excellent for abstract instructions and behaviors ("move along the building"), 2D poses for distant, precise locations, and images for specific visual targets. A truly generalist policy should be able to integrate these inputs for more robust and effective navigation, especially when individual modalities might be ambiguous or incomplete (e.g., in environments with partial views or unreliable GPS).
- **Unifying Diverse Datasets for Scalable Training:** The robotics community has generated vast amounts of navigation data, but these datasets are often curated for specific tasks or goal representations. A significant motivation is to develop a framework that can simultaneously leverage these disparate datasets, regardless of their native modality, to train a single, powerful foundation model. This maximizes data utility and enhances generalization by drawing from a broader range of experiences.
- **Developing Richer Representations:** By forcing the model to learn from a mixture of language, visual, and geometric goal inputs, the authors hypothesize that the policy will develop a more comprehensive and robust understanding of its environment and task. This "cross-modal learning" is expected to yield richer geometric, visual, and semantic representations, beneficial for both known and novel tasks.
- **Enhancing User-Friendliness and Adaptability:** An omni-modal policy would offer a more natural and intuitive interface for human-robot interaction. Users could combine instructions (e.g., "go to this GPS location, but follow the sidewalk") just as they would instruct another human. Furthermore, such a model would serve as a powerful foundation, easily adaptable (via fine-tuning) to new modalities, environments, or robot platforms with limited additional data.

In essence, the overarching objective is to build a broadly generalizable and flexible vision-based navigation policy, OmniVLA, that can interpret and act upon any combination of goal modalities, thereby paving a scalable path for constructing omni-modal robotic foundation models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OmniVLA’s methodology centers on extending a high-capacity Vision-Language-Action (VLA) backbone to process and integrate diverse goal modalities for robot navigation, trained on a massive and varied dataset.

#### 3.1. OmniVLA Architecture

The core of OmniVLA is built upon a pre-trained VLA architecture, specifically **OpenVLA**, a 7B-parameter model. This backbone already incorporates knowledge from internet-scale vision-language pre-training and has been fine-tuned on cross-embodiment robot action data, providing strong foundational priors.

The architecture is designed for flexible goal conditioning:
- **Current Observation:** The robot's current egocentric visual observations are processed by a visual encoder.
- **Goal Modalities:** OmniVLA supports three primary goal modalities:
 - **Egocentric Goal Image:** A target image representing the desired destination.
 - **2D Goal Pose:** A 2D coordinate representing the target location relative to the robot.
 - **Natural Language Prompt:** Textual instructions for the goal.
- **Modality Fusion:** Each individual goal modality (image, pose, language) is passed through its respective encoder (e.g., visual backbone + projector for images/poses, tokenizer for language) and then projected into a shared token space. These modality tokens, along with current observation tokens, are fed into the OpenVLA's large language model (LLM) backbone.
- **Action Output:** A linear action head is appended to the LLM output, generating a sequence of N actions (e.g., linear and angular velocities) for the robot.
- **Modality Dropout:** During both training and inference, "modality dropout" is employed, where tokens for selected goal modalities are attended to, while unused or unavailable modalities are masked out via an attention mask. This mechanism encourages the policy to learn from cross-modal goal representations and handle scenarios where some goal information might be missing.

#### 3.2. OmniVLA-edge Architecture

For resource-constrained deployments, a smaller variant, OmniVLA-edge, is developed. This version is based on **ViNT**, a 50M-parameter navigation transformer. Unlike the OpenVLA-based model's later fusion, OmniVLA-edge uses an "early fusion" approach where conditioning tokens from each modality are processed and fused *before* being fed into the transformer. It uses EfficientNet-B0, ResNet, and CLIP networks with FiLM for various encoders.

#### 3.3. Training Data

A significant aspect of OmniVLA's methodology is the compilation and utilization of an unprecedentedly large and diverse training dataset:
- **Scale and Diversity:** The training mixture comprises over **9,500 hours** of real-world navigation data collected across **10 different robot platforms** from 13 publicly available datasets. This includes mixtures like GNM and LeLaN, Frodobots-2K, and BDD-V. The environments are highly diverse, ranging from off-road and office settings to sidewalks and public roads.
- **Labeling Strategies:** The dataset includes a mix of manually collected labels, synthetically generated actions (using MBRA for Frodobots and NoMaD for LeLaN), and refined actions.
- **BDD-V Reannotation:** A custom reannotation process, inspired by MBRA, was developed to leverage the BDD-V dataset (autonomous car data). This process adapted the high-speed, 1Hz, GPS-based car actions to be compatible with small mobile robots (0.5 m/s, 3Hz), while also addressing issues like dashboard interference for collision avoidance by applying objectives conditionally.

#### 3.4. Training Method

The training method is designed to maximize the benefits of the diverse, multi-modal dataset:
- **Randomized Modality Sampling:** For each training step, a batch of samples is constructed from the datasets. Then, for each sample, goal modalities are independently sampled (e.g., 2D pose, egocentric image, or both for GNM mixture data). This randomized "modality dropout" ensures broad coverage and stability.
- **Attention Masking:** An attention mask dynamically excludes tokens for unused or unavailable modalities, directing the model's focus only to the present goal information.
- **Objective Function:** The primary objective is an L2 imitation loss (J_il) to minimize the difference between predicted N-step actions and reference actions.
- **Auxiliary Objectives:**
 - **Object-Reaching (J_obj):** For the LeLaN dataset, an additional objective encourages the final predicted action to be close to a target object pose, crucial for language grounding.
 - **Action Smoothness (J_sm):** A regularization term minimizes action deltas between consecutive steps.
- **Batching and Optimization:** To handle dataset imbalance, data is sampled from different sources at a specific ratio (e.g., LeLaN:GNM:Frodobots:BDD-V = 4:1:1:1). Gradient accumulation is used to achieve a large effective batch size, crucial for training large models on diverse data. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is applied to the large OpenVLA-based model to limit learnable parameters, balancing training speed and stability.

This comprehensive approach allows OmniVLA to learn core navigation behaviors and interpret complex, multi-modal goal specifications, preparing it for broad generalization and adaptability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of OmniVLA focuses on three key questions: its performance against single-modality policies, its ability to handle compositional multi-modal goals, and its adaptability as a foundation model.

#### 4.1. Q1: Omni-Modal Pre-training Outperforms Single-Modality Policies

OmniVLA consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to state-of-the-art specialist baselines across all evaluated modalities, underscoring the benefits of omni-modal pre-training on a diverse, large-scale dataset.
- **Language-conditioned Navigation:** OmniVLA achieved a **73% success rate (SR)** and **65% success in following Out-of-Distribution (OOD) language prompts (Behavior)**, significantly outperforming LeLaN (43% SR, 15% Behavior) and CounterfactualVLA (33% SR, 45% Behavior). This highlights the advantages of leveraging larger pre-trained LLM priors for semantic understanding and instruction following.
- **2D Pose-conditioned Navigation:** OmniVLA achieved a **95% success rate** and **98% partial progress** towards the goal, representing a notable 9% improvement in success rate over the strongest specialist, MBRA-pose (86% SR, 92% Prog.). This indicates that the high-capacity model combined with diverse data leads to better geometric reasoning.
- **Egocentric Goal Image-conditioned Navigation:** OmniVLA matched the performance of the strongest specialist, MBRA-image, achieving a perfect **100% success rate** and **100% partial progress**.
- **Architecture Comparison:** While all OmniVLA variants showed cross-modality generalization, the full OmniVLA (7B parameters) significantly outperformed smaller VLAs like MiniVLA and SmolVLA, primarily due to its larger capacity and broader pre-training domain. OmniVLA-edge (50M parameters) still performed exceptionally well for its size, especially on image and pose tasks, but exhibited a larger gap in language-following, emphasizing the critical role of strong VLM priors.
- **Dataset Ablation:** An ablation study with OmniVLA-edge revealed that training on the full multi-modal dataset mixture significantly improves performance across all modalities compared to versions trained on single-modality datasets. For instance, multi-modal training boosted language SR from 43% to 60% and satellite image SR from 19% to 57%. The inclusion of highly varied cross-embodiment data (BDD-V) further improved robustness.

#### 4.2. Q2: OmniVLA Can Follow a Composition of Multiple Goal Modalities

OmniVLA successfully demonstrated its ability to interpret and act upon tasks specified by a combination of modalities, a novel and challenging capability.
- **Pose + Language Conditioning:** When tasked with reaching a 2D goal pose while simultaneously adhering to OOD behavioral language instructions (e.g., "move along the wall"), OmniVLA achieved an **80% success rate** and **60% in following behavioral instructions**. This performance shows a remarkable ability to attend to both 'where' and 'how' instructions, with only a marginal 5% drop in language capabilities compared to single-modal language tasks. Specialist baselines, lacking multi-modal input processing, could not perform this task.

#### 4.3. Q3: OmniVLA Can Be Adapted to New Goal Modalities, Environments, and Embodiments

The experiments confirmed OmniVLA’s qualities as a foundation model, showcasing its adaptability and generalizability.
- **Learning New Goal Modalities:** OmniVLA-edge, initially trained without satellite imagery, could successfully adapt to this new modality by fine-tuning only a new vision encoder. Its performance improved significantly from a specialist baseline's 19% SR to **62% SR**, demonstrating that the learned cross-modal representations facilitate rapid acquisition of new task types.
- **Adapting to New Environments:** Fine-tuning OmniVLA-edge with just 1.2 hours of data collected in unseen test environments led to notable performance gains. For satellite goal images, SR improved from 57% to **83%**, and for 2D goal poses, from 81% to **86%**, proving its efficient adaptability to novel surroundings.
- **Learning New Language Domains:** Fine-tuning OmniVLA with the CounterfactualVLA dataset (featuring more diverse language prompts) improved its language-conditioned navigation performance (overall SR from 72.5% to **82.5%**, and OOD Behavior from 65% to **70%**). This highlights OmniVLA's ability to absorb new linguistic nuances and improve instruction-following.
- **Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** OmniVLA successfully deployed "out-of-the-box" on two new robot platforms: the wheeled VizBot and the quadruped Unitree Go1. Both robots were able to follow complex natural language instructions in diverse environments without further training, validating the model's strong generalization across different robot embodiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

OmniVLA represents a significant leap forward in robot navigation research, pushing the field closer to truly general-purpose and adaptable robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Pioneering Omni-Modal Generalization in Navigation:** OmniVLA is the first end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that effectively unifies diverse goal modalities (language, 2D pose, egocentric image, and their combinations) for robot navigation. This addresses a critical limitation of previous specialist navigation policies and opens avenues for more flexible and human-intuitive robot control.
- **Establishing a Foundation Model for Navigation:** By leveraging pre-trained VLA backbones and an extensive, diverse dataset (over 9,500 hours across 10 platforms), OmniVLA establishes itself as a strong candidate for a "foundation model" in the navigation domain. Its demonstrated capabilities in zero-shot generalization to new environments, efficient fine-tuning to novel modalities and datasets, and out-of-the-box transfer to new robot embodiments are hallmarks of such foundational models.
- **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** The model consistently outperforms state-of-the-art specialist baselines across all individual modalities, setting new benchmarks for navigation success rates and instruction following. Its ability to combine complex goal specifications (e.g., pose + OOD language) further highlights its robustness and advanced reasoning capabilities.
- **Scalable Data Utilization:** OmniVLA's training framework demonstrates an effective strategy for aggregating and learning from highly diverse, often noisy or synthetically labeled, large-scale robot datasets. This approach maximizes the utility of existing and future data collection efforts, promoting a scalable path for developing advanced robot policies.
- **More Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:** By accepting and interpreting multi-modal inputs, OmniVLA enables more natural and flexible ways for humans to instruct robots. This could lead to more user-friendly interfaces in applications like autonomous delivery, inspection, and service robotics, where combined instructions are commonplace.
- **Acceleration of Future Research:** The authors' commitment to releasing model checkpoints and training code will serve as a valuable resource for the research community. This will enable further exploration, fine-tuning, and development of new navigation policies and tasks, potentially accelerating progress in areas like long-horizon planning, complex instruction following, and adaptation to truly unstructured environments.
- **Highlighting Key Challenges:** While achieving impressive results, the paper also implicitly highlights areas for future improvement, particularly in language-conditioned navigation. The observation that even a powerful model like OmniVLA benefits from "larger and more carefully curated datasets" for language grounding underscores the ongoing need for high-quality, diverse linguistic data in robotics.

In conclusion, OmniVLA represents a significant advancement towards building broadly generalizable and flexible robot navigation policies. Its omni-modal conditioning, combined with large-scale pre-training, lays a robust foundation for future developments in autonomous robotics, ultimately bringing us closer to robots that can navigate and operate intelligently in complex, human-centric environments.
