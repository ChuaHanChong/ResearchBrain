# RAY-TOLD: Ray-Based Latent Dynamics for Dense Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance with TDMPC

> [!summary] Summary
> RAY-TOLD introduces a hybrid control framework for autonomous robots to navigate dense, dynamic obstacle environments, integrating a LiDAR-centric latent dynamics model and policy mixture sampling into Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control. This approach achieved a 94.0% success rate and a 6.0% collision rate in stochastic simulations, reducing collisions by 45.45% compared to a purely reactive MPPI baseline.

## Problem

- Reactive planners, like MPPI, suffer from limited prediction horizons due to computational costs, leading to myopic decisions and entrapment in local minima within dense obstacle fields.
- Model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies struggle to enforce hard safety constraints and exhibit inefficient or unsafe exploration, particularly in safety-critical dynamic environments.
- Effectively integrating high-dimensional obstacle information from LiDAR into a planning framework while maintaining real-time performance is a challenge.

## Method

- **RAY-TOLD** utilizes an end-to-end trained LiDAR-centric latent dynamics model, which compresses high-dimensional sensor input into a compact latent state to predict future states and rewards.
- It integrates a policy mixture sampling strategy within ==MPPI==, guiding a fraction of candidate trajectories using a learned latent policy to bias search towards goal-directed and kinematically feasible paths.
- A learned terminal value function extends MPPI's foresight beyond its finite planning horizon, approximating long-term returns from future latent states to prevent local minima.

## Results

- RAY-TOLD (with a **0.1** policy mixture ratio) achieved a **94.0%** success rate and a **6.0%** collision rate, representing a **45.45%** reduction in collisions compared to the MPPI baseline's **11.0%** collision rate.
- The proposed method demonstrated improved average safety margins and capabilities in anticipating complex dynamic obstacle behaviors, such as obstacle reflections from boundaries.
- t-SNE analysis of the learned latent space revealed a highly structured manifold, distinctly clustering collision-risk states from safe states, indicating effective abstraction of physical geometry and interaction risks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A compact, LiDAR-centric latent space can effectively abstract high-dimensional physical geometry and local interaction risks, providing a robust foundation for long-term safety forecasts and policy guidance.
> - Balancing learned policy guidance with stochastic MPPI search (e.g., at a 0.1 mixture ratio) is crucial, as both insufficient and excessive guidance can lead to suboptimal performance in highly stochastic scenarios.
> - The integration of a learned terminal value function significantly extends the planner's foresight, allowing it to overcome myopic decisions and anticipate complex obstacle behaviors like reflections from environmental boundaries.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous navigation in environments characterized by dense and dynamic human crowds presents a significant challenge for mobile robots. Existing approaches typically fall into two main categories: purely reactive optimization-based methods, such as Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, and learning-based methods, particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL).

Reactive planners, including standard MPPI, are known for their robustness in handling non-linear vehicle dynamics and ensuring short-term collision avoidance and kinematic feasibility. However, their primary limitation stems from a restricted prediction horizon due to high computational costs associated with simulating forward dynamics. This myopia often leads robots into local minima, such as being trapped by U-shaped obstacles or dense crowd formations where immediate safe paths do not align with long-term goals. Prior attempts to extend MPPI's horizon include hierarchical approaches, perceptive dynamics, distributed frameworks, and integrating temporal-difference learning to approximate infinite-horizon costs (e.g., TD-CD-MPPI).

Reinforcement Learning, conversely, excels in acquiring long-horizon reasoning abilities from environmental interactions, which helps in avoiding myopic decisions and local minima. Recent advancements in world models and latent dynamics have further enabled RL agents to build predictive representations, facilitating planning within learned internal models. Despite these capabilities, many practical robotic systems still rely on model-free RL policies, which frequently struggle to enforce hard safety constraints during learning and suffer from inefficient or unsafe exploration, particularly in safety-critical applications with complex and dynamic obstacles. This challenge has prompted research into hybrid models that combine the strengths of both paradigms. Approaches like AC-MPC embed differentiable MPC within an RL actor, DR-MPC adds residual RL corrections to MPC commands, and MPC-inspired RL parameterizes controllers with learning. The TD-MPC lineage, including TD-MPC and TD-MPC2, focuses on jointly learning latent dynamics and terminal values for fast planning, with further extensions incorporating diffusion models or hierarchical reasoning.

RAY-TOLD positions itself within this landscape by bridging the gap between the constraint-satisfying robustness of physics-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) and the long-horizon foresight of learning-based models. Unlike methods that integrate MPC and RL via residual corrections or differentiable components, RAY-TOLD directly injects RL guidance into MPPI's sampling distribution. It specifically adapts concepts from world models and TD-MPC by developing a LiDAR-centric latent dynamics model and employing a policy mixture sampling strategy. This approach aims to leverage the short-horizon safety guarantees of MPPI while overcoming its susceptibility to local minima through learned long-term intent, tailored for dense dynamic obstacle avoidance with sparse 2D LiDAR data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research arises from the inherent difficulties faced by autonomous mobile robots operating in dense, dynamic, and unpredictable environments, particularly those involving human crowds. For real-world deployment, navigation systems must be capable of quickly anticipating environmental changes, reliably avoiding dynamic agents whose movements can be stochastic, and strictly adhering to kinematic constraints of the robot.

The authors identify two key limitations in existing methodologies that necessitate their proposed approach:
1. **Limited Long-Term Foresight in Reactive Planners:** While classical optimization-based methods like MPPI offer robust short-term collision avoidance and respect kinematic constraints, their computational complexity restricts the prediction horizon. This computational constraint leads to myopic decision-making, causing robots to become trapped in local minima where short-term safe paths diverge from the global navigation goal, such as when encountering U-shaped obstacles or dense crowd formations.
2. **Safety and Exploration Challenges in Reinforcement Learning:** Reinforcement learning excels at long-horizon reasoning by learning value functions that approximate future costs. However, end-to-end model-free RL policies often struggle to incorporate strict safety constraints during learning and can exhibit inefficient or unsafe exploration, especially in environments with complex and dynamic obstacles. These limitations hinder their reliable deployment in safety-critical physical robotic systems.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:
- To propose RAY-TOLD, a hybrid control architecture that integrates high-dimensional obstacle information from LiDAR scans into a compact latent dynamics model.
- To leverage the robustness of physics-based MPPI for short-horizon interactions while incorporating the long-horizon foresight provided by reinforcement learning.
- To enable the learning of a terminal value function and a policy prior within this latent space, which can guide the planning process beyond MPPI's limited horizon.
- To introduce a policy mixture sampling strategy that augments MPPI candidate trajectories with those derived from the learned policy, effectively steering the planner towards the goal while maintaining kinematic feasibility.
- To empirically demonstrate that this integration significantly enhances navigation reliability and safety, specifically by reducing collision rates in stochastic environments with high-density dynamic obstacles, outperforming purely reactive MPPI baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RAY-TOLD is presented as a hybrid control framework designed to merge the robustness of physics-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) with the long-horizon foresight of Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL). The architecture is conceptually based on task-oriented latent dynamics (TOLD) and is specifically tailored for LiDAR-equipped autonomous robots.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The navigation task is formulated as a discrete-time optimal control problem, aiming to maximize the expected cumulative reward $J(\pi)$ over an infinite horizon. The robot's state $s_t$ and action $a_t$ evolve according to system dynamics $s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)$. A comprehensive reward function $r(s_t, a_t)$ is designed to balance efficient navigation and safety, comprising six terms:
- `r_dist`: Negative Euclidean distance to the goal.
- `r_coll`: A substantial penalty for collisions.
- `r_side`: A soft clearance penalty to maintain safe distances from obstacles.
- `r_vel`: An incentive for velocity, which transitions to a braking penalty when near the goal.
- `r_prog`: A progress reward based on the alignment of the robot's heading with the goal direction.
- `r_goal`: A sparse terminal reward for reaching the goal region.
Obstacle distance `d_obs` is computed from LiDAR ray intersections, truncated at the first hit, consistent with an occlusion-aware sensing model.

**B. RAY-TOLD Model Components:**
The system uses an augmented state vector $x_t = [s_t, s_{goal,t}, s_{ray,t}]$, combining vehicle kinematics, relative goal position, and LiDAR range measurements (N_{rays}) with obstacle velocities. The core of RAY-TOLD consists of several neural networks trained in parallel:
- **Encoder $h_\theta(x_t)$:** Compresses the high-dimensional augmented state $x_t$ into a compact low-dimensional latent state $z_t$.
- **Latent Dynamics $d_\phi(z_t, a_t)$:** Predicts the next latent state $z_{t+1}$ given the current latent state and action.
- **Reward Predictor $R_\psi(z_t, a_t)$:** Estimates the immediate single-step reward $r(s_t, a_t)$ from the latent state and action. This is crucial for multi-step imagined rollouts within the learned latent space during training, where the true environment's reward function is not directly accessible.
- **Value Function $C_\omega(z_t, a_t)$:** Approximates the infinite-horizon discounted return (Q-value) from a given latent state-action pair. During online execution, it is used exclusively at the terminal state of each MPPI rollout to estimate long-term returns beyond the finite planning horizon.
- **Policy Prior $\pi_\xi(z_t)$:** Learns an action policy from the latent state.

**C. Integration with MPPI:**
RAY-TOLD integrates two complementary prediction modalities into the Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control framework:
1. **Policy-Guided Rollouts (short-horizon, t ~ t+H-1):** Instead of relying solely on Gaussian noise perturbations for action generation, a fraction ($\alpha$) of MPPI's candidate trajectories are initiated or guided by the learned latent policy $\pi_\xi$. These actions are generated by recursively unrolling the policy using the latent dynamics model $d_\phi$ within the compact latent space. The sampled actions are then applied to a differentiable kinematic bicycle model to simulate physics-based rollouts. This mixture sampling strategy biases the search space towards goal-directed and kinematically feasible trajectories, while still evaluating short-horizon collision outcomes.
2. **Latent Terminal Value Estimation (long-horizon, ≥ t+H):** To counteract MPPI's typical disregard for long-term consequences beyond its planning horizon H, RAY-TOLD augments the finite-horizon objective with a learned terminal value. The final state of each MPPI rollout, $x_{t+H}$, is encoded into its latent representation $z_{t+H} = h_\theta(x_{t+H})$. The learned value function $C_\omega(z_{t+H}, \pi_\xi(z_{t+H}))$ then approximates the expected return from this terminal latent state, effectively extending the planner's foresight and preventing it from becoming trapped in local minima.
The overall objective function for MPPI becomes:
$J(a_{t:t+H-1}) = E[\sum_{k=0}^{H-1} \gamma^k r(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k}) + \gamma^H C_\omega(z_{t+H}, \pi_\xi(z_{t+H}))]$.

**D. Model Training:**
The RAY-TOLD model is trained end-to-end using trajectories collected in the environment, following the TD-MPC2 framework. The training objective minimizes a combined loss function: $L = \lambda_1 L_{reward} + \lambda_2 L_{value} + \lambda_3 L_{policy} + \lambda_4 L_{latent}$.
- $L_{reward}$ trains the reward predictor $R_\psi$.
- $L_{value}$ trains the value function $C_\omega$ using Temporal Difference (TD) learning.
- $L_{policy}$ optimizes the actor network $\pi_\xi$ to maximize the Q-value.
- $L_{latent}$ enforces consistency between the latent dynamics model's predictions and the actual encoded future states, ensuring a physically meaningful latent space.

The online planning process iteratively refines an action sequence by sampling candidates, performing rollouts with the physics model (guided by the mixture policy), evaluating them using immediate rewards and the terminal value function, and updating the mean action sequence based on weighted rewards.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RAY-TOLD was conducted in a highly stochastic simulated environment, designed to present significant challenges for autonomous navigation.

**A. Test Setup:**
- **Environment:** A 20 m × 10 m bounded physical space where an ego vehicle navigates from (1, 0) to a goal region at (19, 0).
- **Ego Vehicle:** Modeled as a kinematic bicycle with a 2.5 m wheelbase, updated at 10 Hz. Actions are constrained to acceleration ([−3, 3]m/s²) and steering angle ([−45°, 45°]).
- **Sensing:** A 360-degree LiDAR system providing 60 occlusion-aware distance measurements.
- **Obstacles:** 40 to 60 dynamic obstacles, each with a 0.4 m radius. Their initial positions and velocities are randomized for each scenario, and their movement is governed by a modified Social Force Model (SFM) incorporating attractive forces to local waypoints, boundary repulsion, and inter-obstacle repulsion. This setup ensures high stochasticity and prevents environmental memorization.
- **Simulation:** A highly parallelized custom continuous-space physics simulation environment, enabling real-time planning at 50 Hz with 256 independent environments and 256 rollout samples per planning step.
- **Evaluation:** 100 test episodes, with success defined as reaching the goal without collision within a time limit.
- **Baselines and Variants:**
 - **MPPI (Baseline):** A purely reactive, physics-based controller assuming constant-velocity obstacle prediction and lacking a learned terminal value function.
 - **RAY-TOLD (α=0):** Incorporates the learned terminal value function but uses only random noise for action sampling, akin to standard MPPI. This variant isolates the impact of terminal value estimation.
 - **RAY-TOLD (α=0.1):** The proposed method with a moderate policy mixture ratio.
 - **RAY-TOLD (α=0.2):** The proposed method with a stronger policy mixture ratio.
 The policy mixture ratio (α) was constrained to [0, 0.2] to maintain the reactive collision avoidance of MPPI while integrating learned guidance.

**B. Performance Comparison (Table II):**
- **MPPI (Baseline):** Achieved an 89.0% success rate, an 11.0% collision rate, and an average safety margin of 0.19 m. Collisions were attributed to the inability to predict complex, unpredictable obstacle movements (e.g., reflections, hidden obstacles) due to its limited horizon and lack of informed guidance.
- **RAY-TOLD (α=0):** Demonstrated an identical success rate (89.0%) and collision rate (11.0%) to the baseline. However, it showed a slight improvement in safety margin (0.17 m, 10.53% improvement), suggesting that the learned terminal value function contributes to maintaining safer distances even when overall collision frequency remains unchanged.
- **RAY-TOLD (α=0.1):** Exhibited the best performance across all metrics, achieving a 94.0% success rate (5.62% improvement over baseline) and a significantly reduced collision rate of 6.0% (45.45% reduction). Its safety margin was 0.18 m (5.26% improvement). This indicates that moderate policy guidance effectively helps the agent navigate dense, dynamic obstacle clusters.
- **RAY-TOLD (α=0.2):** Achieved a 91.0% success rate (2.25% improvement) and a 9.0% collision rate (18.18% reduction), with a 0.18 m safety margin (5.26% improvement). The performance was slightly lower than RAY-TOLD (α=0.1), suggesting that an overly strong reliance on the policy prior can introduce suboptimal biases in highly stochastic scenarios. This highlights the importance of a balanced mixture.

**C. Trajectory Analysis (Fig. 3):**
- **MPPI Failure:** In scenarios with converging obstacles, the MPPI baseline failed due to sample sparsity and its inability to anticipate complex nonlinear dynamics. Without an informed prior, its random exploration could not identify feasible paths.
- **RAY-TOLD (α=0.2) Efficiency:** Stronger policy guidance enabled RAY-TOLD (α=0.2) to generate more efficient, goal-directed trajectories. By shifting the sampling mean towards promising regions, it facilitated proactive evasive maneuvers.
- **Anticipating Reflections:** RAY-TOLD (α=0.1 and α=0.2) demonstrated advanced predictive capabilities, such as anticipating obstacle reflections from environmental boundaries. The latent-space world model and informed policy priors effectively captured environmental transition dynamics and boundary interactions, allowing for preemptive maneuvers.
- **Optimal Balance (α=0.1):** In a critical, highly congested scenario, only RAY-TOLD (α=0.1) successfully avoided collision. This illustrated the importance of balancing policy exploitation with stochastic MPPI search, as too much or too little guidance could lead to failure.

**D. Latent Space Visualization (Fig. 4 - t-SNE):**
A t-SNE analysis of the 128-dimensional latent state vectors $z_t$ revealed a highly structured latent manifold. States corresponding to imminent collision risk (e.g., distance to nearest obstacle $\leq 0.4$ m) clustered distinctly from safe, open-space states ($\geq 0.8$ m), with a smooth topological transition between them. This visualization suggests that the encoder network $h_\theta$ learns to abstract the physical geometry and local interaction risks, rather than merely memorizing raw sensor data. This physically meaningful latent space provides a robust foundation for both the value function $C_\omega$ (for long-term safety forecasts) and the policy prior $\pi_\xi$ (for guiding MPPI sampling).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work addresses a critical challenge in autonomous robotics: reliable and safe navigation in dense, dynamic, and unpredictable environments. By proposing RAY-TOLD, the authors present a hybrid control architecture that effectively merges the strengths of physics-based model predictive control with the long-horizon reasoning capabilities of model-based reinforcement learning.

The key technical contributions of RAY-TOLD—the LiDAR-centric latent dynamics model, the learned terminal value function, and the policy mixture sampling strategy—collectively provide a robust solution to the limitations of purely reactive planners. Specifically, the method's ability to overcome local minima, anticipate complex obstacle behaviors like reflections, and significantly reduce collision rates (up to 45.45% compared to the baseline) without relying on explicit handcrafted heuristics represents a notable advancement. The empirical validation in a highly stochastic simulation environment, designed to prevent memorization and force the learning of generalized features, underscores the method's potential for real-world application. The visualization of the learned latent space further supports that RAY-TOLD develops a physically meaningful representation of collision risk, enhancing the interpretability and robustness of the learned components.

The potential impact of RAY-TOLD is significant for several domains:
- **Autonomous Navigation Systems:** The enhanced reliability and safety demonstrated by RAY-TOLD could facilitate the deployment of autonomous mobile robots in a wider range of complex, human-centric environments, such as crowded public spaces, logistics warehouses, and urban driving scenarios, where stringent safety and efficiency requirements are paramount.
- **Hybrid Control Architectures:** This research offers a blueprint for integrating model-based reinforcement learning with classical control techniques, particularly how learned priors and value functions can directly inform and enhance sampling-based planners like MPPI. This could inspire further development of robust hybrid systems that leverage the benefits of both paradigms.
- **Generalizable Obstacle Avoidance:** By learning abstracted, physically meaningful representations of collision risk, RAY-TOLD moves towards more generalizable solutions for dynamic obstacle avoidance, reducing the need for extensive retraining in varied environments with similar underlying physical dynamics.
- **Safety-Critical Applications:** The improved safety margins and reduced collision rates are crucial for safety-critical applications where robot failures can have severe consequences, making the deployment of autonomous systems more feasible and trustworthy.

In conclusion, RAY-TOLD provides a compelling demonstration of how a balanced integration of short-horizon physics-based planning with long-horizon learned intent can lead to more robust, reliable, and safer autonomous navigation in challenging dynamic environments.
