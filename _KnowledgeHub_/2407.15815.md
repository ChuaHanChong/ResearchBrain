# Learning to Manipulate Anywhere: A Visual Generalizable Framework For Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Maniwhere is a visual RL framework that trains manipulation policies in simulation to generalize across combined visual disturbances (camera viewpoint, appearance, lighting, embodiment) and transfer zero-shot to real robots. It couples a multi-view contrastive plus feature-alignment objective, a perspective-transform Spatial Transformer module inside a ResNet18 encoder, and curriculum domain randomization, beating baselines by 68.5% in simulation and 53.5% on real hardware.

## Problem

- Prior visual RL generalization methods each target one disturbance type, appearance or viewpoint alone, and collapse when camera pose, background and lighting vary together.
- Sim-to-real visual transfer usually demands a hand-built digital twin closely matching the real scene, plus careful camera calibration that any accidental nudge invalidates.
- Naive domain randomization or strong augmentation destabilizes Q-learning and diverges; view-adaptation baselines additionally require expert demonstrations or freshly collected data from the new viewpoint.

## Method

- ==Multi-view representation objective==: symmetrized ==InfoNCE== contrastive loss between fixed-view and randomized-view renderings, plus an L2 feature-map alignment term that teaches cross-view semantic correspondence.
- ==STN== module whose affine warp is generalized to a learnable 3x3 perspective transform, inserted into the first two ResNet18 encoder layers to align views toward the canonical training view.
- ==Curriculum domain randomization==: exponentially scheduled growth of dynamics, camera and augmentation magnitude (==SRM== frequency masking with random overlay) inside the augmented-observation Q-target objective to prevent divergence.

## Results

- Across 8 MuJoCo tasks under randomized viewpoints, **Maniwhere** beats baselines by **68.5%** on average, e.g. **89.5%** versus MV-MWM's **9.6%** on Pick Cube To Bowl.
- Zero-shot real-robot deployment on 3 hardware platforms averages **60.7%** success versus **7.2%** for MV-MWM; swapping UR5 for a Franka arm still yields **59%** versus **82%**.
- Ablations drop **86.5%** to **29.4%** without the multi-view objective, to **65.3%** without STN, to **65.8%** with TCN loss; imitation variant reaches **68.7%** versus Diffusion Policy's **10.7%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - View-invariance is learnable from paired simulated renderings alone: contrastive plus feature-map alignment removes the need for demonstrations or test-time adaptation to new viewpoints.
> - Randomization strength should be scheduled, not fixed; ramping noise only after basic competence emerges keeps Q-learning stable where naive augmentation collapses model-based baselines.
> - Policy attention concentrates on the target object, so background, lighting and distractor changes are tolerated, yet recoloring the target itself still breaks the policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visuomotor reinforcement learning (RL) is critically challenged by the "generalization gap" and the "sim2real gap." Policies trained in simulation often struggle when deployed in the real world due to discrepancies in visual appearance, lighting, camera viewpoints, and object properties. Maniwhere directly addresses these fundamental problems, positioning itself at the forefront of research aiming to bridge these gaps.

Historically, efforts to achieve generalization in visual RL have often focused on specific types of variations. For instance, many studies have explored domain randomization or data augmentation to improve robustness to changes in visual appearances. Others have targeted camera viewpoint variations using techniques like domain adaptation or multi-view information distillation. However, a significant limitation of prior art is its tendency to address only *one* type of visual disturbance at a time. Real-world environments, in contrast, present a *combination* of these challenges simultaneously—a shifted camera, different lighting, and a new background can all occur at once.

Maniwhere directly confronts this multi-faceted problem. By proposing a framework capable of generalizing across a *combination* of visual disturbance types (camera views, visual appearances, lighting conditions, and even cross-embodiment), it takes a crucial step towards truly "open-world" robotic operation. This ambition is distinct from many existing methods that often assume fixed camera setups or consistent appearances after training.

Furthermore, the work emphasizes *zero-shot sim2real transfer*, a highly desirable but challenging goal. Many sim2real techniques still require some form of real-world fine-tuning, adaptation, or extensive manual calibration. Maniwhere aims to eliminate these burdensome steps, positioning itself as a more practical solution for real-world deployment. The focus on diverse robotic embodiments (single-arm, bi-manual, dexterous hands) across multiple hardware platforms also pushes the generalization envelope beyond what is typically evaluated.

In the broader context of representation learning for visuomotor control, Maniwhere contributes by proposing a multi-view representation objective specifically designed to extract invariant features and correspondences across different viewpoints. The integration of Spatial Transformer Networks (STNs) further refines this spatial understanding, moving beyond purely appearance-based generalization. By combining these advanced representation learning techniques with a curriculum-based domain randomization strategy, Maniwhere attempts to synthesize disparate approaches into a coherent and stable framework for robust RL training. This holistic approach to visual generalization and sim2real transfer represents a significant advancement over fragmented solutions and moves the field closer to developing truly adaptable robotic agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to empower visuomotor robots with robust generalization capabilities, enabling them to reliably operate in diverse and unpredictable open-world scenarios. This ambitious goal is driven by several critical motivations stemming from the inherent challenges of deploying robots in real-world environments.

Firstly, the authors highlight the "vexing issue" of hardware setup sensitivity. A common scenario, eloquently articulated in the introduction, is the disruption caused by a seemingly minor change like a camera being accidentally moved. Such incidents necessitate tedious recalibration or costly re-collection of data, severely hindering robotic progress and deployment. Maniwhere aims to overcome this fragility by enabling policies to be resilient to changes in camera views, backgrounds, and extraneous objects, thereby making robot systems more robust and less demanding to maintain.

Secondly, the paper explicitly targets the significant discrepancy between simulated and real-world environments—the "sim2real gap." While digital twins are often meticulously crafted to mimic reality for effective sim2real transfer, any notable divergence can render trained policies ineffective. The core motivation here is to enable robots to "step out of simulations" and apply learned skills to complex real-world scenarios *without* the need for precise camera calibration or any real-world fine-tuning. This zero-shot sim2real transfer capability is a holy grail in robotics, promising to drastically reduce development and deployment times.

Thirdly, the research is motivated by the limitations of existing generalization methods. Prior works often focus on tackling *single* aspects of visual variation, such as only appearance changes or only viewpoint changes. However, real-world scenarios typically involve a *combination* of these disturbances. The authors argue that simply stacking existing domain randomization or data augmentation techniques can destabilize reinforcement learning training, leading to divergence. Therefore, a key objective is to develop a robust and stable training framework that can effectively incorporate various inductive biases to handle *multiple* visual generalization types simultaneously. Maniwhere seeks to provide a comprehensive solution that can manage this complexity while maintaining training stability.

In essence, Maniwhere's key objectives are:
- To achieve visual generalization across a *combination* of disturbances, including camera views, visual appearances, and lighting conditions.
- To enable *zero-shot sim2real transfer*, allowing policies trained in simulation to operate effectively in diverse real-world settings without further adaptation.
- To develop a stable reinforcement learning framework that can integrate advanced visual representation learning techniques and curriculum-based randomization without suffering from training instability.

By addressing these objectives, Maniwhere aims to push visuomotor robots closer to practical, autonomous operation in unstructured, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Maniwhere is designed as a generalizable framework for visual reinforcement learning, integrating several novel components to achieve its stated objectives. The core methodology revolves around robust visual representation learning combined with a stabilized training curriculum.

The framework processes multi-view RGB-D observations. At each timestep, the agent receives input from two cameras: a `fixed` viewpoint and a `moving` viewpoint, which randomly changes its position within a specified range. These multi-view inputs are critical for training the visual encoder to understand spatial relationships and invariant features.

1. **Multi-View Representation Objective:** This is central to endowing agents with viewpoint adaptability and shared semantic understanding.
 - **Contrastive Loss (InfoNCE):** Inspired by InfoNCE, the framework uses a contrastive loss function, $J_{con}(\theta)$, to encourage the visual representations of images from the fixed view and the randomized moving view (at the *same* timestep) to be similar. These are considered positive pairs ($o_{fixed}$, $o_{move+}$). Conversely, representations from the fixed view and images from *different* timesteps are treated as negative examples ($o_{fixed}$, $o_{move-}$), and their embeddings are pushed apart. A symmetrized loss form (inspired by MoCo-v3) is utilized for improved performance.
 - **Feature Alignment Loss:** To explicitly learn correspondences between different views, Maniwhere introduces an alignment loss, $J_{feat}(\theta)$, applied to feature maps across various layers. This term minimizes the L2 norm between flattened feature maps derived from the fixed and moving view observations, encouraging them to share similar semantic information and spatial correspondences.
 - The overall multi-view objective, $L_{Maniwhere}(\theta)$, combines these two terms: $L_{Maniwhere}(\theta) = J_{con}(\theta) + \lambda J_{feat}(\theta)$, where $\lambda$ is a weighting coefficient. This objective guides the visual encoder to learn view-invariant, semantically rich representations.

2. **Spatial Transformer Network (STN) Module:** To further enhance the model’s spatial awareness and robustness to view changes, an STN module is incorporated directly into the visual encoder.
 - The STN allows the network to actively spatially transform feature maps, making them more canonical or aligned.
 - Crucially, the authors modify the original STN's affine transformations to *perspective transformations* (Eq. 5). This modification allows the STN to handle more complex 3D spatial changes beyond simple 2D affine transformations, which is vital for varying camera viewpoints.
 - The STN is integrated within the first two layers of a ResNet18 backbone, enabling early-stage spatial processing of visual inputs.

3. **Curriculum Domain Randomization:** Acknowledging the sensitivity of RL training to noise, a curriculum-based approach is employed to incrementally increase the magnitude of randomization parameters (e.g., camera parameters, object properties, arm dynamics) over time using an exponential scheduler.
 - This gradual introduction of visual and physical variations stabilizes the training process, preventing divergence that might occur from naive, aggressive randomization.
 - A curriculum is also applied to the Q-value stabilization objective (Eq. 4), where augmented data (using SRM with random overlay) is initially fed only from the fixed view and later includes augmented images from the moving view as training progresses. This ensures that the agent gradually adapts to increasing levels of visual noise and variations.

In summary, Maniwhere trains the agent in simulation by feeding it multi-view images through a visual encoder enhanced with an STN, optimizing with a multi-view representation objective and a curriculum-based randomization strategy. Once trained, the policy is directly transferred to real-world environments in a zero-shot manner, receiving input from a single camera. This integrated approach aims to build a robust and generalizable visuomotor policy that can perceive and act effectively despite diverse visual disturbances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Maniwhere's effectiveness is demonstrated through extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world settings, encompassing a wide array of manipulation tasks and hardware platforms. The results consistently show Maniwhere's superior generalization and sim2real transfer capabilities compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Generalization to Different Viewpoints:** Maniwhere significantly outperforms all baselines (MV-MWM, SGQN, SRM, MoVie, PIE-G) across 8 diverse tasks, including single-arm, bi-manual, and dexterous hand manipulation. It achieves an average performance boost of +68.5% over the best baseline (MV-MWM). This highlights its strong ability to handle continuously changing camera views, a common challenge for existing methods. The results suggest that single-view inputs are insufficient for fully perceiving spatial information, and Maniwhere’s multi-view approach is highly effective.
- **Generalization to Different Visual Appearances:** When visual appearances (e.g., table and background colors) are perturbed, Maniwhere maintains performance comparable to its original scores, showing minimal degradation. In contrast, MV-MWM suffers a substantial performance drop. This demonstrates Maniwhere's compatibility with multiple generalization types and the stability of its training objectives against noise from data augmentation and domain randomization.
- **Generalization to Different Embodiments:** Surprisingly, Maniwhere demonstrates zero-shot transfer capability to a different robot arm embodiment (UR5e to Franka arm) while retaining its camera-view generalization. Qualitative analysis (Appendix C.2) suggests that Maniwhere can effectively recognize semantically consistent positions between different embodiments, indicating its ability to learn abstract, transferable skills.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments (Zero-Shot Sim2Real Transfer)

- Maniwhere's models, trained purely in simulation, are directly deployed on 3 real-world hardware platforms (UR5 arm with Robotiq gripper, Franka arm with Allegro Hand, XArm with Leap Hand on a mobile base) in a zero-shot manner.
- Evaluated on 5 challenging real-world tasks under diverse visual conditions (different viewpoints, varied appearances, lighting).
- Maniwhere significantly outperforms MV-MWM in all real-world tasks, achieving an average success rate of 60.7% compared to MV-MWM's 7.2% (+53.5% boost). This is a critical finding, confirming Maniwhere's ability to not only narrow the sim2real gap but also enable real-world generalization across varying visual scenes.
- The STN module is shown to be crucial, assisting the agent in transforming inputs from diverse real-world viewpoints to align closely with the fixed view used during simulation training, thus facilitating view-invariant representations (Figure 3b).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Multi-view Objective:** Removing the multi-view representation learning objective severely impacts performance, dropping the average success rate from 86.5% to 29.4%. This underscores its critical role in enabling generalization across different camera views.
- **STN Module:** Ablating the STN module also leads to a significant performance drop, from 86.5% to 65.3%, demonstrating its contribution to understanding and adapting to spatial view changes.
- **Comparison with TCN:** Replacing Maniwhere’s multi-view objective with TCN loss (another multi-view contrastive learning method) resulted in lower performance (65.8%), validating the specific design choices of Maniwhere's objective.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Analysis

- **Q-value Distribution:** Visualization using t-SNE shows that Maniwhere maintains a Q-value distribution that closely approximates that obtained from original fixed-view images, even with noisy visual inputs. This indicates more robust and generalizable learning by stabilizing the Q-value estimates across diverse views.
- **Trajectory Embedding:** Maniwhere maps images from different viewpoints along the same execution trajectory into similar regions in the feature space, demonstrating its success in learning view-invariant visual representations and maintaining consistency throughout the task execution.

#### 4.5. Imitation Learning

- When Maniwhere's visual encoder and multi-view objective are applied to an Imitation Learning (IL) setting (using Diffusion Policy), it achieves a success rate of 68.7%, significantly outperforming the baseline Diffusion Policy (10.7%). This suggests the framework's visual representations are broadly beneficial, extending beyond pure RL.

The study also acknowledges a limitation: while robust to changes in background, table, and distractors, Maniwhere's agent is sensitive to changes in the color of the *target object*. Grad-CAM visualizations reveal that the agent focuses primarily on the target object, making it susceptible to variations in its appearance (Appendix C.6).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Maniwhere presents a significant step forward in the quest for truly generalizable visuomotor robots, with profound implications for the widespread deployment of intelligent robotic systems. Its significance stems from addressing long-standing and critical barriers in the field.

Firstly, the most impactful contribution is Maniwhere's demonstrated ability to achieve generalization across a *combination* of multiple visual disturbance types simultaneously. Unlike prior works that often isolate camera view variations from appearance changes, Maniwhere's framework handles both, along with lighting conditions and even cross-embodiment differences. This multi-faceted robustness is crucial for real-world scenarios, where environmental factors rarely change in isolation. This moves robotics closer to operating in truly unstructured and dynamic "anywhere" environments.

Secondly, the achievement of *zero-shot sim2real transfer* in such complex and diverse settings is a major breakthrough. The ability to train policies entirely in simulation and deploy them directly onto physical robots without any real-world data collection, fine-tuning, or tedious camera recalibration drastically reduces the cost, time, and expertise required for robot deployment. This practical advantage could accelerate the adoption of advanced robotics in various industries, from manufacturing and logistics to service robotics and healthcare, by making robot systems more plug-and-play.

Thirdly, the methodology itself offers valuable insights. The combination of multi-view representation learning, a spatially-aware STN module, and a carefully designed curriculum for domain randomization provides a robust and stable training paradigm for RL. This addresses the common challenge of instability when aggressively applying data augmentation or randomization in deep RL, offering a blueprint for future work on integrating diverse inductive biases effectively. The explicit focus on learning semantic correspondences and view-invariant features via both contrastive and feature alignment losses contributes to a deeper understanding of visual scene properties.

Furthermore, the extensive evaluation across 8 diverse tasks, involving single-arm, bi-manual, and dexterous hand manipulation on 3 distinct hardware platforms, lends strong credibility to Maniwhere's broad applicability. Its success in these varied and challenging contexts suggests that the learned representations and policies are not task-specific but truly generalizable. The extension of Maniwhere's principles to Imitation Learning further highlights the broad utility of its visual representation learning approach.

In terms of potential impact, Maniwhere could pave the way for a new generation of adaptable robots that are less sensitive to their operating environment. This resilience is essential for tasks in unpredictable human environments, disaster response, and exploration. Future research could leverage Maniwhere's foundation to tackle even more complex, long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks, where the robot itself moves and the visual scene continuously changes. By reducing the barriers to real-world deployment, Maniwhere ultimately contributes to realizing the full potential of advanced visuomotor systems and accelerating the integration of intelligent robots into our daily lives.
