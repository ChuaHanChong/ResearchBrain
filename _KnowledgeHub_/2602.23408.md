# Demystifying Action Space Design for Robotic Manipulation Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> This research systematically investigated the impact of action space design on robotic manipulation policy learning, leveraging over 13,000 real-world rollouts and diverse platforms. It established data-driven guidelines, finding that chunk-wise delta temporal actions consistently outperform step-wise, and identified trade-offs between joint-space and task-space spatial abstractions based on task complexity and generalization requirements.

## Problem

- The field of robotic manipulation lacks a systematic understanding and consensus on action space design, with choices often based on ad-hoc heuristics.
- Unstandardized control interfaces impede reproducibility and hinder the development of generalist foundation models capable of transferring knowledge across different robot embodiments.
- Suboptimal action space design becomes a critical bottleneck as research scales to large-scale generalist robot models, impacting policy optimization and stability.

## Method

- Conducted a large-scale, systematic empirical study by formalizing action space components along spatial (joint-space vs. task-space) and temporal (absolute vs. delta, action chunking) abstraction axes.
- Evaluated policies across diverse hardware (single-arm, dual-arm, different kinematics) and simulation platforms, using multiple model architectures including regression, flow-matching, and a foundation model.
- Performed over 13,000 real-world rollouts and trained more than 500 models, conducting grid searches across data scale, model expressiveness, and training duration to derive robust design guidelines.

## Results

- Chunk-wise delta actions consistently outperformed step-wise delta by over **10%** in real-world tasks, with delta actions performing optimally at shorter horizons (e.g., k=30) and absolute actions at longer ones (e.g., k=60).
- Delta temporal abstraction consistently surpassed absolute abstraction across all tested platforms, tasks, and model variations (regression and flow-matching policies).
- Joint-space control demonstrated overall superiority, especially when combined with flow-matching generative policies; however, task-space (EEF) representations showed a pronounced advantage in cross-embodiment and transfer learning scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implementation nuances, particularly regarding action chunking, are decisive; chunk-wise delta actions consistently outperform step-wise delta due to superior error propagation properties.
> - Delta temporal abstraction generally proves superior to absolute abstraction by offering a more tractable inductive bias for policy learning, though optimal chunking horizons vary.
> - The choice between joint-space and task-space spatial abstraction involves a trade-off: joint-space benefits from powerful generative models and scales well with resources, while task-space offers better generalization across different robot embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of learning-based robotic manipulation has made significant progress, advancing from basic pick-and-place operations to complex, precision-critical tasks. Recent research has predominantly focused on scaling training data volumes and increasing model capacities to achieve these advancements. However, the paper identifies a critical, yet often overlooked, aspect: the specification of the action space. The action space serves as the fundamental interface between neural predictions and physical hardware, profoundly influencing the optimization landscape for policy learning.

Historically, the choice of action space has been driven by ad-hoc heuristics or legacy designs, leading to a lack of consensus within the research community. For instance, while end-effector pose was initially favored for its semantic simplicity, there has been a recent shift towards joint-space representations to mitigate numerical instabilities associated with inverse kinematics. The design space is further complicated by choices in temporal representation (e.g., absolute vs. relative actions) and prediction horizons (e.g., action chunking). This fragmented landscape, where state-of-the-art results are sometimes linked to undocumented control choices, impedes reproducibility and hinders the development of generalist foundation models capable of transferring knowledge across different robot embodiments.

Prior studies have offered initial insights into action space design, but these efforts have often been limited in empirical scope due to the inherent challenges of robotic learning, such as the heterogeneity of settings, the fidelity limitations of simulations, and the high cost of real-world robot evaluation. As the field progresses toward large-scale generalist robot models, the implications of suboptimal control interface design become more significant. This research aims to address this gap by providing a comprehensive, systematic empirical study to establish principled and unified design guidelines for action spaces in robotic manipulation policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate and demystify the impact of action space design on imitation-based robotic manipulation policy learning. The authors are motivated by the current ambiguity and lack of standardized practices in defining action spaces, which they identify as a critical bottleneck for advancing robotic control policies.

The detailed motivations and objectives are as follows:

- **Address Ambiguity and Lack of Consensus**: Despite its pivotal role, the specification of the action space often relies on ad-hoc heuristics or inherited designs. This leads to an ambiguous understanding of effective robotic policy design philosophies, hindering progress and consistency in the field. The study aims to provide clarity and establish a consensus.
- **Quantify Complex Impacts**: The authors hypothesize that action space choices have significant and complex impacts on policy learnability and control stability. A key objective is to empirically confirm and quantify these impacts through a large-scale, systematic study.
- **Structure the Design Space**: To facilitate a structured analysis, the research aims to dissect the action design space along two orthogonal axes: temporal abstraction (absolute vs. delta representations, action chunking) and spatial abstraction (joint-space vs. task-space parameterizations).
- **Identify Trade-offs**: The study seeks to examine the fundamental trade-offs introduced by different action space choices, particularly concerning policy learnability, control stability, and generalization capabilities. For example, understanding when absolute representations are preferable to delta, or when joint-space offers advantages over task-space, and vice-versa.
- **Derive Practical Guidelines**: A core motivation is to translate empirical findings into actionable and generalizable guidelines for designing future robotic manipulation policies. These guidelines are intended to standardize research practices and improve the reliability and performance of robotic systems.
- **Support Reproducibility and Foundation Models**: By establishing clear principles for action space design, the research aims to enhance the reproducibility of results in robotic learning and support the development of foundation models that can generalize across different robot embodiments. The current reliance on unstated control choices impedes both these goals.
- **Overcome Empirical Limitations**: Acknowledging the challenges of conducting extensive robotic experiments, the authors aim to conduct an unprecedentedly large-scale study, leveraging over 13,000 real-world rollouts and evaluating more than 500 trained models across diverse scenarios to ensure statistical significance and robust conclusions.

In essence, the research aims to move beyond anecdotal evidence and provide a rigorous, data-driven understanding of how action space choices fundamentally shape the success of robotic manipulation policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a large-scale, systematic empirical approach to investigate the impact of action space design. This involves formalizing action space components, utilizing diverse model architectures and robotic platforms, and adhering to a rigorous evaluation protocol.

#### 3.1. Action Abstraction Taxonomy

The action representation space is decomposed along two orthogonal axes: spatial abstraction and temporal abstraction, with action chunking as a default technique.

- **Spatial Abstraction (Configuration vs. Task Space)**: This axis defines the interface between the learned policy and the hardware controller.
 - **Joint-space (Configuration Space)**: Actions directly specify target joint positions. This approach avoids inverse kinematics (IK) solvers during deployment, promoting robustness against numerical singularities and error accumulation. However, the policy must implicitly learn the robot’s kinematic structure, which can increase learning complexity as it maps visual inputs to a non-linear configuration manifold.
 - **Task-space (End-Effector Pose)**: Actions specify the end-effector's pose (position and orientation) in 3D space. This provides a geometrically intuitive abstraction aligned with object-centric visual observations. However, it relies on IK solvers during deployment, which can introduce numerical instabilities and accumulate errors, potentially degrading execution robustness.
- **Temporal Abstraction (Absolute vs. Delta Representations)**: This axis specifies the order of temporal derivatives represented by the predicted action sequence. The study adopts a position-based low-level controller.
 - **Absolute (0th-order)**: The policy predicts target states directly. This can facilitate intuitive and precise grounding but requires the model to internalize complex global geometry and cope with highly variable target distributions, leading to substantial learning difficulty.
 - **Delta (1st-order/Relative)**: The policy predicts state increments relative to the current state. This typically yields a better-conditioned and closed-loop learning target, as the network focuses on immediate displacements. However, deploying delta actions can make the system sensitive to feedback imperfections, potentially leading to cumulative drift over time.
- **Action Chunking**: This technique involves predicting a sequence of future actions (a "chunk") to capture temporal dependencies. The study identifies two ambiguities with chunking:
 - **Delta Alignment**: When combined with delta actions, there's a choice between *step-wise delta* (relative to the immediately preceding predicted state within the chunk) and *chunk-wise delta* (relative to the robot’s state at the start of the chunk).
 - **Horizon-Abstraction Coupling**: The optimal chunking horizon *k* is hypothesized to depend on the action abstraction, with delta potentially requiring shorter horizons for rapid correction and absolute control benefiting from longer horizons for global spatial grounding.

#### 3.2. Model Architectures for Policy Learning

The study evaluates policies across various paradigms by designing a base architecture and two generative model variants, along with a foundation model.
- **Base Architecture**: A FiLM-conditioned ResNet-18 vision encoder paired with a 6-layer Transformer decoder.
- **Model Variants**:
 - **Regression-based Policy**: Optimized using a standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss.
 - **Flow Matching-based Policy**: Learns a velocity field that transforms noise into expert actions, providing more powerful modeling for complex distributions.
- **Foundation Policy (π0)**: Used specifically to investigate transfer learning properties across different action spaces.
- **Training Details**: Common hyperparameters were used, with training conducted on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, accumulating over 16,000 GPU-hours.

#### 3.3. Robotic Platforms and Evaluation Protocol

Experiments were conducted across four distinct hardware configurations and simulation environments.
- **Real-World Platforms**:
 - **Single-Arm AgileX PiPER**: Primary setup for large-scale real-world experiments, a 6-DoF manipulator with third-person and wrist-mounted cameras.
 - **Dual-Arm AgileX PiPER**: Used to evaluate cross-platform generalizability, equipped with two arms and respective wrist cameras, targeting bimanual coordination.
 - **Single-Arm AIRBOT**: A cost-effective 6-DoF arm with a different kinematic structure, used for cross-morphology generalization.
- **Simulation Platform**:
 - **RoboTwin 2.0**: An advanced simulation benchmark used for large-scale, reproducible experiments under controlled environments, specifically employing the AgileX embodiment.
- **Real-World Evaluation Protocol**:
 - **Tasks**: A curriculum of four manipulation tasks with increasing complexity: Touch Cube (precision), Pick Up Cup (grasping, contact dynamics), Pick and Place Cup (sequential, temporal drift), and Bimanual Cube Transfer (inter-arm coordination).
 - **Reproducibility**: A grid-based spatial coverage strategy (6x6 grid) was implemented to standardize initial object positions, mitigating distribution shifts.
 - **Metrics**: Progress scores reported based on three independent trials, each comprising 10 individual rollouts.
- **Simulation Evaluation Protocol**:
 - **Tasks**: A subset of 10 tasks from RoboTwin 2.0 (AgileX embodiment, hard mode).
 - **Metrics**: Average success rate across three independent trials, each with 10 rollouts per task.
- **Experimental Scale**: The study involved collecting over 2,000 expert demonstrations and executing more than 13,000 real-world rollouts across over 500 trained models. Controlled grid searches were performed along data scale, model expressiveness, and training duration axes.

#### 3.4. Transfer Learning Setup

For transfer learning, a pre-trained π0 policy was fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) on three single-arm tasks (Touch Cube, Pick Cup, Pick & Place) in a multi-task learning paradigm. Experiments covered all four combinations of spatial and temporal abstractions, trained for 30,000 steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study systematically evaluated action space designs across various scenarios, leading to several findings organized by three research questions (RQs).

#### 4.1. RQ1: Implementation Nuances are Decisive

This section addressed the fine-grained implementation choices related to action chunking.

- **Superiority of Chunk-wise vs. Step-wise Delta**: Real-world experiments on the single-arm AgileX platform demonstrated that **chunk-wise delta consistently and significantly outperformed step-wise delta** across foundational tasks (Touch Cube, Pick Up Cup, Pick and Place Cup). The performance gap averaged over 10%. A theoretical analysis (Proposition 4.1) indicated that step-wise integration inherently amplifies prediction noise linearly with the chunk horizon, whereas chunk-wise delta and absolute actions maintain a constant error bound. This corroborated the empirical findings, suggesting chunk-wise delta is structurally more reliable.
- **Interplay with Horizon *k***: A grid search over execution horizons revealed that **absolute control generally benefited from longer horizons (e.g., k=60), while delta control achieved peak performance at shorter horizons (e.g., k=30)**. This aligns with the hypothesis that relative representations are more sensitive to execution drift and benefit from more frequent corrections. For absolute actions, a saturation point was observed where increasing the horizon beyond a certain length did not yield further significant gains, suggesting potential information decorrelation.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Systematic Trends in Action Abstraction

Building on optimized implementation strategies (chunk-wise delta, abstraction-dependent horizons), the study investigated general trends across diverse platforms, model variations, and learning regimes.

- **Temporal Abstraction**: The results consistently showed that **delta abstraction significantly outperformed absolute abstraction** across all platforms (single-arm AgileX, bimanual AgileX, RoboTwin-2.0 simulation), task configurations, and model variations (regression-based and flow-matching-based policies). This superiority was attributed to two factors: (1) Delta actions, focusing on immediate displacement, offer a more tractable inductive bias compared to absolute actions, which require learning a complex mapping from high-dimensional visual observations to global coordinates. (2) There is an insufficient understanding and exploration of how to effectively train policies with the longer horizons that absolute representations might prefer.
- **Spatial Abstraction**: An overall performance superiority for **joint-space control compared to task-space control** was observed. However, this superiority was not as consistent as with temporal abstraction and showed some dependence on platforms, tasks, and learning regimes. A notable observation was the distinct excellence of **flow-matching generative policies in joint-space learning**. This was explained by the ability of powerful generative models to effectively capture the complex, non-linear, and often multi-modal action distributions inherent in joint space, thereby unlocking its intrinsic control stability. Standard regression backbones often struggled with such complexity.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Consistency and Scaling Analysis

This section extended experiments to broader settings, including scaling data volume and computation budgets, and advanced learning setups.

- **Scaling with Data and Compute**: Experiments on the AgileX platform (across training epochs and demonstration volumes) confirmed the trends observed in RQ2. **Delta actions remained the superior temporal abstraction**. The superiority of **joint-space actions became increasingly pronounced with increased training epochs and data volume**, particularly for regression-based policies. This indicated that joint-space representations are better suited to capturing the underlying kinematic manifold as the learning regime scales, while task-space could be competitive in resource-constrained settings.
- **Advanced Learning Regimes (Cross-embodiment and Transfer Learning)**: In cross-embodiment learning (AgileX to AIRBOT) and transfer learning from a foundation model (π0), **delta control maintained its superiority over absolute actions**. However, a notable shift occurred for spatial representations: **task-space (EEF) representations exhibited a more pronounced advantage and, in some cases, surpassed joint-space control**. This behavior was attributed to the relatively embodiment-invariant nature of task-space representations, which abstract away robot-specific kinematics and facilitate knowledge transfer across different embodiments, highlighting a complementary strength for generalization-focused scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research significantly contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by systematically investigating the often-overlooked area of action space design. The study's unprecedented scale, involving over 13,000 real-world rollouts and comprehensive evaluation across diverse platforms and learning paradigms, provides empirical evidence confirming that action space specification is not a minor implementation detail but a decisive configuration that interacts non-trivially with various learning regimes.

The key significance and potential impacts include:

- **Standardization of Best Practices**: The study addresses the historical ambiguity in action space selection by offering data-driven guidelines. These guidelines can help standardize research and development efforts, moving the field away from ad-hoc heuristics towards principled design choices.
- **Improved Policy Performance and Stability**: The findings identify specific action space configurations that lead to more robust and higher-performing policies. For instance, the consistent superiority of chunk-wise delta actions and the benefits of joint-space control with sufficient resources provide clear directions for optimizing policy learnability and execution stability in standard imitation learning settings.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities**: The identification of task-space (EEF) representations as advantageous in cross-embodiment and transfer learning scenarios is crucial for the development of generalist robot policies and foundation models. By highlighting the embodiment-invariant nature of task-space, the research guides efforts to build systems that can generalize across different robotic platforms and tasks.
- **Guidance for Resource Allocation**: The insights into how action space choices interact with data scale and computational budgets (e.g., joint-space benefiting disproportionately from increased resources) can inform resource allocation strategies in future research and engineering projects.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work establishes a strong baseline and taxonomy for understanding action spaces. It explicitly outlines several promising directions for future research, such as exploring hybrid/adaptive action representations, a deeper theoretical understanding of action chunking, scaling to high-degree-of-freedom and dynamic tasks, and unifying action spaces for broader generalization.
- **Increased Reproducibility**: By clearly delineating the impact of specific action space choices, the study indirectly contributes to improving the reproducibility of results in robotic learning, as researchers can make more informed and transparent decisions about their control interfaces.

In summary, this research provides a foundational empirical analysis that demystifies a critical aspect of robotic policy design. Its actionable guidelines and insights are poised to influence how future robotic manipulation policies are conceived, implemented, and evaluated, ultimately contributing to the development of more capable and generalizable embodied agents.
