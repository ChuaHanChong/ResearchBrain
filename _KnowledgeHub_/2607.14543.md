# SafeRelBench: A Spatial-Relation-Aware Benchmark for Process-Level Safety in VLM-Driven Embodied Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> SAFERELBENCH, a new benchmark, evaluates how VLM-driven embodied agents handle process-level safety linked to dynamic spatial relations in household tasks. It reveals that current agents frequently complete tasks but violate safety preconditions, with Safety Success Rate dropping from up to 0.91 in non-spatial settings to as low as 0.16 when spatial risks are involved.

## Problem

- Existing safety benchmarks for embodied agents primarily focus on static risk recognition or final-state safety, overlooking process-level safety during task execution.
- Current VLM-driven agents often fail to integrate dynamic spatial relations (supporting, containment, proximity) into their safety reasoning, leading to unsafe actions despite task completion.
- There is a need for specific evaluation tools to diagnose safety failures directly caused by an agent's disregard for relational preconditions before executing risk-prone actions.

## Method

- Introduces **SAFERELBENCH**, a benchmark with 507 executable household manipulation tasks, including 248 spatial-relation-aware scenarios covering supporting, containment, and proximity risks.
- Defines a ==process-level safety evaluation protocol== that assesses whether agents satisfy safety conditions *before* executing associated risk-prone actions, in addition to task completion.
- Constructed the benchmark using a ==three-stage pipeline== involving ==GPT-5== for safety tip generation, scenario mapping to ==Behavior-1K==, and ==BDDL-style safety goal generation==.

## Results

- All evaluated VLM-driven agents showed a significant decrease in Safety Success Rate (SSR) on spatial-relation samples (**0.16–0.40**) compared to their performance on non-spatial controls (up to **0.91**).
- Task Success Rate (SR) also dropped from **0.83–0.94** in non-spatial settings to **0.52–0.73** under spatial-relation conditions, though the decline in SSR was more pronounced.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that while action-grounded safety prompts could improve SSR for some models, this sometimes came at the cost of reduced Task Success Rate, indicating a need for more robust safety integration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLM-driven embodied agents exhibit a consistent and substantial gap between task completion and process-level safety performance when confronted with spatial-relation-induced risks.
> - Performance on non-spatial control samples highlights that observed safety failures are specifically tied to relational reasoning challenges, not general task difficulty or other safety concerns.
> - Explicit safety guidance through prompts (RISK-AWARE, ACTION-GROUNDED) can improve safety metrics, but the effect is not uniform across models and can sometimes involve a trade-off with task completion efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of embodied agents, particularly those leveraging Vision-Language Models (VLMs), represents a significant trend in artificial intelligence and robotics. These agents are designed to interpret visual scenes, understand natural language instructions, and execute multi-step actions, making them suitable for applications in complex environments such as households. Existing research has largely focused on enhancing VLM-driven agents' capabilities in task success, generalization, and instruction-following accuracy. This includes advancements in multimodal agents integrating visual observations for planning (Driess et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2023) and vision-language-action models connecting web-scale knowledge with low-level control (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025).

Within this landscape, the aspect of safety for embodied agents has gained increasing attention. Previous safety benchmarks have explored various facets such as hazardous instruction following, physical risk awareness, and safe task planning (Zhu et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Son et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a). Some works have also moved towards interactive or process-oriented safety assessments (Lu et al., 2025, 2026), acknowledging that safety should be evaluated during execution rather than solely at the final state. However, the existing body of work, while valuable, has demonstrated limitations in specifically addressing process-level safety failures induced by dynamic spatial relations between objects. Many benchmarks categorize safety risks broadly and do not isolate how object relations make otherwise valid actions unsafe. Similarly, efforts in physical and spatial grounding primarily aim to improve task execution or spatial question answering, without explicitly connecting relational understanding to process-level safety preconditions.

This work, SAFERELBENCH, addresses a gap by specifically focusing on spatial-relation-aware process-level safety. It differentiates itself by evaluating whether agents satisfy safety conditions *before* executing risk-prone actions, particularly those risks stemming from supporting, containment, and proximity relations. Unlike prior benchmarks that may treat safety as a static label or focus on final-state compliance, SAFERELBENCH introduces a dynamic, relation-specific evaluation of safety during the execution process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and evaluate SAFERELBENCH, a benchmark specifically designed to assess spatial-relation-aware, process-level safety in VLM-driven embodied agents. The motivation stems from a recognized limitation in current safety evaluations for embodied agents, particularly in household environments.

The authors observe that safety in such environments is frequently relational and procedural. An action's safety is often contingent on the current spatial configuration of objects and the sequence of preceding actions. For instance, moving an object that supports another, or placing an item too close to a heat source, can lead to hazards like falling objects, contamination, fire, or spillage. These failures are characterized as "process-level" because they occur during the execution sequence, potentially even if the agent ultimately achieves the task's final goal. Existing benchmarks, as identified by the authors, predominantly focus on static risk recognition, refusal of unsafe instructions, or successful completion of a task's final state. This focus means that process-level safety failures, particularly those induced by dynamic spatial relations such as supporting, containment, and proximity, have been insufficiently studied.

Therefore, the key motivations for developing SAFERELBENCH include:
- **Addressing the gap in process-level safety evaluation:** To move beyond static or final-state safety assessments and evaluate whether agents maintain safety throughout the entire action sequence.
- **Highlighting spatial relations as a critical safety dimension:** To explicitly test how agents reason about and react to safety risks that are directly caused by object-object spatial relationships (e.g., an object being *on top of* another, *inside* another, or *proximate* to a hazard).
- **Providing a diagnostic tool:** To identify if agents can recognize when an otherwise valid action becomes unsafe due to a relational precondition not being met, rather than simply identifying broad hazard categories.
- **Stimulating research into reliable relational reasoning for safety:** The goal is not to propose a new agent architecture but to provide a testbed that reveals limitations in current VLM-driven agents concerning their ability to integrate scene understanding, safety preconditions, and appropriate action ordering.

By explicitly testing for safety compliance *before* risk-prone actions, SAFERELBENCH aims to foster the development of embodied intelligent systems that are not only capable of completing tasks but also consistently preserve safety during execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces SAFERELBENCH, a benchmark designed to evaluate spatial-relation-aware process-level safety in embodied agents. The methodology encompasses the benchmark's design, dataset construction, task and safety evaluation protocols, and experimental setup for agent assessment.

#### 3.1. Dataset Overview and Design

SAFERELBENCH is built on the premise that safety failures often arise when an embodied agent disregards the relational preconditions for an action. The benchmark consists of 507 executable household manipulation evaluation samples. This includes 248 spatial-relation samples, which are the primary focus, and 259 non-spatial control samples, used for comparative analysis. The spatial-relation subset is categorized into three types: *supporting*, *containment*, and *proximity*. These categories encompass nine specific risk types, enabling a granular analysis of safety failures linked directly to object relations. The benchmark emphasizes multimodal observations, dynamic execution, and explicit spatial-relation reasoning.

#### 3.2. Task and Safety Evaluation

Each sample in SAFERELBENCH comprises an executable household task, a natural language instruction, egocentric visual observations, object information, a primitive action space, a task goal, and safety annotations. The evaluation targets not only the completion of the final task goal but also adherence to safety preconditions at the moment they become relevant.

The set of spatial relations considered, denoted $R_{sp}$, includes {supporting, containment, proximity}. A safety condition $g$ from $G_{safe}$ is linked to a specific risk-prone action $a_{risk}$, identified as $R(g) = a_{risk}$. For an action sequence $\alpha = \langle a_1, \ldots, a_K \rangle$ to be considered both successful and safe, it must achieve the task goal ($s_K \models G_{task}$) and satisfy every safety condition $g$ *before* its associated risk-prone action $a_{risk}$ is executed. Formally, this is expressed as:
$s_K \models G_{task} \land \forall g \in G_{safe} \forall k \in \{1, \ldots, K\}, (a_k = R(g) \implies \text{Satisfied}(g, s_{k-1}))$.
A rule-based evaluator computes task success and process-level safety success by analyzing simulator states and action trajectories. This process-oriented definition allows for the identification of unsafe intermediate actions.

#### 3.3. Dataset Construction and Statistics

The construction of SAFERELBENCH follows a three-stage pipeline:
1. **Safety Tip Generation:** GPT-5 is employed to generate general household safety principles from simulated indoor scenes and common safety knowledge. These tips describe semantic risks (e.g., storing food safely, avoiding liquids near electronics) that form the basis for identifying unsafe interactions.
2. **Evaluation Scenario Construction:** The generated safety tips are mapped to executable manipulation tasks within the Behavior-1K environment. Objects are filtered based on semantic attributes, physical affordances, and safety relevance. Scenarios are specifically designed to involve supporting, containment, or proximity relations where a hazard can emerge if an agent deviates from safety-preserving actions. Human review and online scene sampling in the simulator verify task executability, the presence of intended spatial relations, and the potential for hazards.
3. **Safety Goal Generation:** For each verified scenario, BDDL-style goal conditions are generated for three types of evaluation:
 - **Execution goal evaluation:** Checks task completion.
 - **Process safety evaluation:** Verifies satisfaction of safety constraints *during* interaction.
 - **Termination safety evaluation:** Checks if the final state is safe.
 An LLM-based evaluator is also used to assess explicit safety awareness.

The spatial-relation subset comprises 248 cases: 72 for Supporting (falling-object injury, tipping), 86 for Containment (food contamination, spillage/overflow, chemical hazard, fire hazard), and 90 for Proximity (spillage/overflow, fire hazard, chemical hazard). The distribution of action types indicates that the benchmark requires diverse manipulation operations, such as `PLACE_ON_TOP`, `OPEN`, `CLOSE`, `TOGGLE_ON`, `TOGGLE_OFF`, and various `WAIT` actions, suggesting a focus on dynamic interaction rather than static recognition.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

Seven VLM-driven embodied agents were evaluated: four open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Ins, Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Ins, Qwen3-VL-8B-Ins, Qwen3-VL-32B-Ins) and three closed-source models (GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-pro, Claude-sonnet-4-6).
- **Protocol:** A closed-loop step-level planning protocol was used. At each timestep, the agent receives scene observations, task instructions, object lists, object abilities, BDDL task goals, and action history. It then outputs a primitive action in JSON format, along with an optional "caution" field for step-level safety awareness.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate (SR):** Proportion of episodes satisfying the BDDL task goal.
 - **Safety Success Rate (SSR):** Proportion of episodes that are both task-successful and satisfy all process-level and termination-level safety conditions.
 - **Safety Recall Rate (SRec):** The fraction of task-completed episodes that are also safe (conditional safety metric).
- **Ablation Study:** To examine the impact of safety guidance, three prompt variants were used:
 - **BASE:** Standard task planning prompt with general safety emphasis.
 - **RISK-AWARE:** Provides scenario-specific safety tips and requires explicit preventive reasoning.
 - **ACTION-GROUNDED:** Further guides the model to ground safety tips into executable actions or step-level cautions.

All evaluations were conducted with deterministic decoding. Open-source models were deployed on a server with NVIDIA A100 GPUs, while closed-source models were accessed via APIs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of seven VLM-driven embodied agents on SAFERELBENCH yielded several findings regarding process-level safety, particularly in the presence of spatial relations.

#### 4.1. Impact of Spatial Relations on Safety Failures

The results indicate a consistent and substantial degradation in safety performance for all evaluated models when spatial-relation-induced risks are present, compared to their performance in non-spatial control settings.
- **Performance Gap:** In non-spatial settings, models generally achieved high task completion (SR ranging from 0.83 to 0.94) and safety success (SSR up to 0.91). However, with spatial relations, SR dropped to 0.52–0.73, and SSR dropped more significantly to 0.16–0.40. This demonstrates a clear separation: agents frequently complete the requested task but violate process-level safety constraints imposed by spatial relations.
- **Examples of Degradation:** For instance, Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Ins's SR and SSR decreased from 0.94 and 0.91 in the non-spatial setting to 0.65 and 0.33, respectively, under spatial-relation conditions. GPT-5.4 also showed a decline from 0.93 SR and 0.65 SSR to 0.73 SR and 0.40 SSR. This pattern suggests that current agents often prioritize task completion over adhering to relational safety preconditions during execution.

#### 4.2. Role of Non-Spatial Control Samples

The non-spatial control samples were instrumental in disentangling relation-induced risks from other forms of safety or task difficulty.
- **Isolation of Relation-Induced Risks:** The performance contrast was particularly evident for Supporting and Proximity relations. For example, Qwen2.5-72B's performance improved significantly from 0.67 SR and 0.42 SSR with supporting relations to 1.00 SR and 0.96 SSR in the corresponding non-spatial setting. Similarly, Claude-4.6 achieved 1.00 SR and 1.00 SSR in non-spatial proximity tasks but only 0.53 SR and 0.38 SSR when proximity relations were involved. These comparisons suggest that the observed low safety performance is not primarily due to general task execution challenges but rather to the specific integration of safety constraints with object relations.
- **Containment Nuance:** For Containment relations, the non-spatial controls did not always yield substantially higher SSR, indicating that these control scenarios might still contain other non-relational safety risks that pose challenges to agents. This highlights that the benchmark effectively isolates spatial-relation-induced safety failures from other types of embodied safety failures.

#### 4.3. Ablation Analysis on Prompt Designs

An ablation study on the Supporting relation examined the impact of different prompt designs (BASE, RISK-AWARE, ACTION-GROUNDED) on agent performance.
- **Impact of Explicit Safety Guidance:** The study showed that explicit safety guidance could improve process-level safety metrics (SSR and SRec), but the effect was not uniform across all models and sometimes involved a trade-off with task completion (SR).
- **Model-Dependent Improvements:** While the BASE prompt often resulted in moderate SR but low SSR, suggesting insufficient implicit safety awareness, the ACTION-GROUNDED variant, which explicitly links safety tips to executable actions, improved SSR or SRec for some models. This indicates that grounding safety knowledge into concrete actions can be beneficial.
- **Task-Completion Trade-off:** Stronger safety prompts could lead to a decrease in SR, implying that agents might become overly conservative or introduce extra intermediate actions, potentially hindering timely task completion.
- **Conclusion on Prompts:** The findings suggest that simply adding more safety-related text to prompts is not a complete solution. Effective agents need to accurately identify relevant relational risks at the appropriate time and convert mitigation strategies into valid action sequences.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces SAFERELBENCH, which contributes to the field of embodied AI by providing a benchmark specifically focused on spatial-relation-aware process-level safety. This emphasis addresses a previously underexplored dimension of safety evaluation for VLM-driven embodied agents.

The work's primary significance lies in its demonstration of a persistent gap between an agent's ability to complete a task and its capacity to maintain safety throughout the execution process, particularly when spatial object relations dictate safety preconditions. The consistent performance degradation observed across a range of VLM-driven agents under spatial-relation-induced risks, contrasted with their performance on matched non-spatial controls, empirically substantiates the claim that current systems struggle with reasoning about how object relations shape risk dynamically during interaction. This finding implies that progress in embodied intelligence requires not only advancements in perception and planning but also robust mechanisms for integrating spatial relational understanding with real-time safety considerations.

The potential impact of SAFERELBENCH is multi-faceted:
- **Improved Agent Design:** The benchmark provides a diagnostic tool for identifying specific failure modes related to relational safety preconditions. This can guide the development of future VLM-driven agents that explicitly incorporate scene understanding, safety preconditions, and action ordering into their decision-making processes, leading to more robust and reliable systems.
- **Refined Evaluation Methodologies:** By introducing process-level safety metrics that assess compliance before risk-prone actions, SAFERELBENCH encourages a shift from purely final-state or static safety evaluations. This more granular assessment can provide deeper insights into agent behavior and inform the design of safer human-robot interaction protocols.
- **New Research Directions:** The findings highlight that even explicit safety guidance through prompts is not a panacea, stimulating further research into more fundamental reasoning capabilities for embodied agents. Future work could explore methods for more effectively grounding safety knowledge into executable actions, developing predictive models for relational risks, or creating architectures that prioritize safety compliance without disproportionately compromising task efficiency.
- **Enhanced Real-World Application:** As embodied agents become more prevalent in domestic and industrial settings, ensuring their safety is paramount. Benchmarks like SAFERELBENCH, by focusing on realistic household manipulation tasks and critical safety dimensions, contribute to building a foundation for deploying safer robotic assistants in complex, dynamic environments.

The authors acknowledge certain limitations, including the benchmark's focus on simulated household tasks, which do not encompass all real-world complexities like perception noise or human presence. Additionally, the study focuses on three common spatial relations (supporting, containment, proximity) and does not cover all possible embodied safety hazards. The reliance on rule-based and LLM-based evaluators for safety conditions implies that metric completeness and judgment subjectivity are considerations. Despite these limitations, SAFERELBENCH provides a concrete and valuable testbed for advancing research into safe embodied AI.
