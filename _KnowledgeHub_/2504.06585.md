# Sim-to-Real of Humanoid Locomotion Policies via Joint Torque Space Perturbation Injection

> [!summary] Summary
> A new sim-to-real framework injects state-dependent, neural network-generated perturbations directly into joint torque space during deep reinforcement learning to train robust humanoid locomotion policies. This approach enables zero-shot transfer to the TOCABI humanoid robot, exhibiting superior robustness against unmodeled actuator and contact dynamics, as well as significant hardware modifications, compared to traditional domain randomization methods.

## Problem

- The "reality gap" between simulated training environments and physical robots frequently prevents direct deployment of deep reinforcement learning policies.
- Traditional domain randomization techniques are limited by a fixed, finite set of parameters and insufficient expressiveness of simulators to capture complex, unmodeled real-world dynamics like nonlinear actuator behavior or intricate contact compliance.
- Developing robust and natural locomotion policies for inherently unstable, high-dimensional humanoid robots struggles with generalization, especially to out-of-distribution scenarios, often requiring extensive reward shaping or motion imitation.

## Method

- A framework that injects state-dependent perturbations directly into the joint torque space, where these perturbations are generated by a ==Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)== whose weights are randomized per episode.
- Formally reinterprets domain randomization as a fixed functional perturbation, proposing neural networks as flexible function approximators to generate a broader, more expressive class of perturbations.
- Incorporates Denoising World Model Learning (DWL) with a GRU-based encoder to derive robust latent representations from privileged observations, facilitating stable humanoid gaits and improving policy performance.

## Results

- In simulation, the method achieved similar nominal command tracking accuracy but maintained **54.9%** tracking error for unseen joint stiffness, vastly outperforming DR (**0.00** m/s) and ERFI (**91.9%** error).
- It demonstrated superior robustness against unseen complex contact dynamics, with all seeds maintaining locomotion for 60 seconds (**0.47** m/s tracked velocity) where baselines collapsed before 6-12 seconds.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to the TOCABI humanoid succeeded, enabling stable locomotion under intentional hardware modifications and rough terrain conditions where traditional domain randomization policies consistently failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The effects of traditional domain randomization can be mathematically reinterpreted as a fixed, nonlinear perturbation function in the joint torque space, inherently limiting the types of reality gaps it can address.
> - Neural networks can act as flexible, state-dependent perturbation generators, capable of simulating a broader and more expressive class of unmodeled dynamics (e.g., nonlinear actuator behaviors, complex contact mechanics).
> - Randomizing the weights of the perturbation-generating neural network during training exposes the policy to a wide distribution of dynamic discrepancies, fostering superior robustness and generalization to unseen real-world conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated potential for developing robust and versatile control policies for robotic applications, particularly for complex, high-dimensional problems such as legged locomotion. However, a significant challenge in deploying DRL-trained policies on physical robots is the "reality gap"—discrepancies between simulated environments used for training and the real world. Training policies solely on real hardware is often impractical due to the large data requirements of on-policy DRL methods, safety concerns during exploration, and potential damage to the robot or environment. Consequently, training in simulation and transferring policies to reality (sim-to-real) has become a standard approach.

To bridge this reality gap, various methods have been explored. One approach involves rigorous system identification and simulator calibration to closely match real-world dynamics. Another strategy involves learning correction models using neural networks trained on real-world data to compensate for simulation inaccuracies. However, these methods can demand extensive real-world data collection, which may be infeasible or require specialized sensing capabilities.

Domain Randomization (DR) is a widely adopted sim-to-real technique. DR involves randomizing a fixed, finite set of simulation parameters (e.g., dynamics, robot models, observation noise, latencies) during training. The objective is to encourage the control policy to generalize across a distribution of environments, ideally encompassing the real-world system. While successful in numerous applications, DR is inherently limited by: (1) the finite scope of parameters exposed by a simulator, and (2) the expressiveness of the underlying dynamics formulations, which may not capture complex real-world phenomena like nonlinear actuator behaviors (e.g., Stribeck friction, hysteresis) or intricate contact dynamics. Random Force Injection (RFI) is a related approach that introduces random biases and noise to actuated components during simulation to achieve comparable sim-to-real performance with reduced parameter tuning complexity.

Learning stable and natural locomotion for humanoid robots presents additional challenges due to their inherent instability and high dimensionality. Existing solutions often rely on extensive reward shaping, which requires significant domain expertise, or motion imitation, which can suffer from mode collapse and generalization issues, especially for out-of-distribution scenarios like rough terrain. Denoising World Model Learning (DWL) has emerged as an alternative to learn robust latent representations without strict stylistic imitation.

This work positions itself by addressing the limitations of traditional DR and RFI. It proposes an alternative that aims to simulate a broader spectrum of reality gaps, particularly those arising from unmodeled or complex state-dependent dynamics, without relying on real-world data collection for correction. By leveraging neural networks as flexible perturbation generators, the authors seek to overcome the constraints of fixed-parameter randomization and enhance the robustness of DRL policies for humanoid locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose and validate a novel sim-to-real framework that enhances the robustness and transferability of control policies trained in simulation, specifically for complex robotic systems like humanoids. This framework aims to address the inherent limitations of existing domain randomization (DR) methods.

The detailed motivations underpinning this work are as follows:

1. **Mitigate the Reality Gap:** The fundamental motivation is to bridge the unavoidable discrepancy, or "reality gap," between simulated training environments and physical robots. This gap often prevents direct deployment of policies trained solely in simulation, hindering the practical application of DRL in robotics.

2. **Overcome Limitations of Parametric Domain Randomization:**
- **Fixed and Finite Parameter Set:** Traditional DR relies on randomizing a predefined, finite set of simulation parameters. This approach limits the diversity of environments generated during training. The authors argue that many simulators do not expose all necessary parameters required to accurately model real-world complexities.
- **Insufficient Expressiveness of Simulator Dynamics:** Simulators often employ simplified dynamics formulations that cannot fully capture intricate real-world phenomena. Examples include nonlinear actuator dynamics (e.g., Stribeck friction, harmonic drive hysteresis, position-dependent cogging torque) and complex contact compliance. Parametric randomization struggles to represent these unmodeled dynamics.
- **Constrained Perturbation Functions:** The paper establishes that the effects of domain randomization can be mathematically viewed as a nonlinear joint torque space perturbation function (τ_DR). However, the form of this function is fixed by the simulator and physics engine, thereby inherently limiting the class of reality gaps (F_DR) that can be effectively simulated. The motivation is to expand this class beyond F_DR.

3. **Introduce State-Dependent and Expressive Perturbations:** Recognizing that the reality gap often manifests as variations in joint torque dynamics, a key motivation is to inject state-dependent perturbations directly into the joint torque space. By employing neural networks as flexible perturbation generators, the aim is to create perturbations that are more expressive and capable of representing complex, unmodeled dynamics compared to those achievable through fixed-parameter randomization.

4. **Enhance Robustness to Unseen Reality Gaps:** The goal is to train control policies that exhibit superior robustness against a broader spectrum of unseen reality gaps in both simulation and real-world deployment. This includes scenarios where physical parameters are outside the trained randomization range, or where entirely novel dynamics (like joint stiffness or compliant terrain) are introduced. The proposed method seeks to achieve this without requiring additional real-world data collection or extensive system identification.

5. **Enable Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Humanoids:** The research specifically focuses on full-sized humanoid robots, which present significant control challenges due to their inherent instability and high dimensionality. A motivation is to achieve successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for such complex platforms, maintaining high tracking performance even under substantial reality gaps.

6. **Integrate Robust Latent Representation Learning:** By leveraging Denoising World Model Learning (DWL), the authors also aim to learn robust latent representations from privileged observations. This is motivated by the desire to enable natural and stable humanoid gaits and traversability over challenging terrains, avoiding the limitations (e.g., mode collapse, poor generalization to OOD states) often associated with strict stylistic imitation or adversarial training methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates the robot locomotion control problem as a discrete-time partially observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The agent learns a control policy π_θ(·|o_t) to maximize expected cumulative discounted reward.

**A. Core Concept: Joint Torque Space Perturbation Injection**

The central innovation is a novel sim-to-real framework that injects state-dependent perturbations into the joint torque space.
1. **Reinterpretation of Domain Randomization:** The paper formally establishes that the effects of domain randomization (DR) can be mathematically expressed as a nonlinear joint torque space perturbation function, τ_DR(s; p_DR). This function's form is fixed by the simulator's physics engine and its exposed parameters (p_DR). The limitation of DR is that it can only generate perturbation functions within this constrained class F_DR.
2. **Neural Network-Generated Perturbations:** To overcome the limitations of F_DR, the proposed method replaces parametric randomization with neural network-generated perturbations, τ_ϕ(s).
- **Perturbation Generator:** A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers (32 units, tanh activations) and a tanh output layer generates the perturbation.
- **State-Dependent:** The MLP takes the simulation state as input. Practically, it uses the `privileged observation` vector (o_priv,t), which includes both proprioceptive and ground-truth state information.
- **Randomized Weights:** Instead of learning a single perturbation model from real-world data, the network weights (ϕ) are randomly sampled from a Gaussian distribution at the beginning of each episode. This approach trains the policy to be robust against a distribution of diverse perturbation functions (F_NN), which is hypothesized to be more expressive than F_DR.
- **Integration:** The generated perturbation τ_ϕ(o_priv) is added to the nominal joint torques computed by the policy π_θ(o_t) before being applied to the robot's forward dynamics (τ_input,t + τ_ϕ(s_simul,t)).
- **Normalization and Bias:** The input observation vector is normalized by its running standard deviation (not zero-centered), and the MLP is designed with zero bias in all layers to ensure zero perturbation for zero input.
- **Partial Perturbation:** During training, only half of the parallel environments receive non-zero perturbations; the rest experience zero perturbation. This allows the policy to learn from both perturbed and unperturbed dynamics concurrently.
- **Floating Base Perturbation:** For floating-base robots like humanoids, joint torque perturbations also imply perturbations to the robot's base. The method includes perturbing the robot base with force perturbations, assuming moment perturbations are negligible.

**B. Reinforcement Learning Framework**

1. **Observation Space:**
- **Proprioceptive (o_t):** Includes base angular velocity, projected gravity vector, command vector, joint position deviations, joint velocities, gait phase signal, and previous action.
- **Privileged (o_priv,t):** Augments o_t with ground-truth states not available on the real robot, such as base linear velocity, swing foot relative position/yaw, target swing foot state, current reward, binary foot contact states, injected joint torque perturbations (τ_pert), body force perturbations (F_body,pert), and a height scan (h_scan) of the terrain. Crucially, the explicit values of injected perturbations are provided to the critic for value function estimation under high randomization.
2. **Action Space:** The policy outputs 12 actions for the lower body joints, sampled at 125 Hz. A configurable delay simulates system latency.
- **Position-based control:** Actions are normalized target joint positions, mapped to physical limits, and converted to torques via PD control.
- **Torque-based control:** Actions are normalized torque commands, multiplied by torque limits. The paper primarily focuses on torque-based control, with Appendix C showing results for position-based.
3. **Reward Function:** A weighted sum of terms encouraging:
- **Base Command Tracking:** Adherence to desired linear and angular velocities.
- **Motion Quality:** Utilization of online Raibert-based heuristic references (Appendix A) for natural gait patterns, including base height, orientation, roll stability, and swing/stance foot positioning.
- **Regularization:** Penalizes energy consumption, action rates, joint limit violations, contact power, and impact forces to facilitate sim-to-real transfer and stable behavior.
4. **Training Algorithm and Architecture:**
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), augmented with a gradient penalty loss (L_grad) from for smoother policy behavior.
- **Total Loss:** L_total = L_recon + L_PPO + λ_grad L_grad.
- **Denoising World Model Learning (DWL):** A GRU-based encoder processes observation history into a 24-dimensional latent representation. This latent is regularized via an auxiliary MLP decoder that reconstructs the privileged observations (L_recon). The encoder also receives gradients from the PPO and gradient penalty losses, allowing it to adapt representations for policy performance and smoothness.
- **Network Structure:** Actor (MLP 2x256 ELU), Critic (MLP 3x ELU), Encoder (single-layer GRU 256 + MLP 1x256 ELU), Decoder (MLP 2x128).
5. **Training Setup:**
- **Simulator:** Isaac Gym, utilizing GPU acceleration to roll out 4,096 agents concurrently, collecting 98,304 samples per policy update over 10,000 updates.
- **Robot Model:** Full-sized humanoid TOCABI (180 cm, 100 kg) with harmonic drives (100:1 gear ratio), which introduces significant actuator uncertainty.
- **Baselines:**
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Randomizes terrain friction, link mass/CoM, actuator armature/damping, observation noise, and applies random pushes.
- **Extended Random Force Injection (ERFI):** From, randomizes motor constant and applies random force injection. Both baselines, like the proposed method, incorporate delay randomization.
- **Perturbation Magnitudes:** Maximum perturbation coefficient (σ_lim) sets limits to 50 Nm for joint torques and 80 N for base forces.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in both simulation (MuJoCo and IsaacGym) and on the physical TOCABI humanoid robot to evaluate the robustness and transferability of the proposed method against various reality gaps.

**A. Simulation Experiments**

1. **Sim-to-Sim Transfer (Nominal Performance):**
- Evaluation in a nominal MuJoCo environment showed that all methods (DR, ERFI, Proposed) achieved comparable command tracking accuracy. The RMSE values for forward, backward, lateral, and yaw velocities were similar across the baselines and the proposed approach. This indicates that the proposed method's robustness enhancements did not compromise baseline locomotion capabilities.

2. **Robustness Against Unseen Actuator Dynamics:**
- A joint stiffness parameter (250 Nm/rad), unmodeled during training, was introduced.
- **DR:** Catastrophically failed, with an average time-averaged velocity of 0.00 m/s for a 0.8 m/s forward command.
- **ERFI:** Showed severe degradation, achieving 0.06 m/s (91.9% tracking error).
- **Proposed Method:** Maintained 0.36 m/s (54.9% tracking error), significantly outperforming both baselines. This result supports the hypothesis that neural network-generated perturbations can represent dynamics mismatches that parametric randomization cannot capture.

3. **Robustness Against Unseen Contact Dynamics:**
- The test environment introduced soft ground contacts (reduced solref time constant), rough terrain (Gaussian-filtered noise), and modified foot link properties (shape and mass)—all unmodeled during training.
- **DR:** All three seeds collapsed before 6 seconds of simulation.
- **ERFI:** One seed collapsed at 12 seconds, and another at 52 seconds, with an average time-averaged velocity of 0.31 m/s (for 0.8 m/s command).
- **Proposed Method:** All seeds maintained locomotion throughout the 60-second test, achieving 0.47 m/s (40.9% error), demonstrating superior robustness to complex, unmodeled contact dynamics.

4. **Widened Gap Test (Out-of-Distribution Parameter Ranges in IsaacGym):**
- Policies were evaluated in IsaacGym under standard randomization ranges (in-distribution) and then with all randomization ranges expanded 10% beyond training limits, including ground friction variation outside training bounds (out-of-distribution, OOD).
- **DR:** Achieved 94.1% success in-distribution but degraded to 89.5% OOD (4.6% drop).
- **ERFI:** Exhibited significant performance collapse, dropping from 82.3% success in-distribution to 77.9% OOD (4.4% drop). This suggested a detrimental train-test mismatch due to inconsistent perturbation application.
- **Proposed Method:** Achieved 99.7% success in-distribution and 98.4% OOD, demonstrating superior generalization with minimal performance degradation (1.3% drop). This finding highlights the method's ability to generalize effectively even when parameters exceed training distributions.

**B. Real-World Experiments**

1. **Zero-Shot Transfer Performance (Nominal Laboratory Environment):**
- Policies trained in simulation were deployed directly on the TOCABI hardware without fine-tuning.
- All methods (DR, ERFI, Proposed) successfully transferred and tracked target velocity commands (0.5 m/s forward, 0.2 m/s lateral, 0.4 rad/s yaw) in a laboratory environment with uneven and slippery floors.
- The proposed method showed slight advantages in forward (RMSE: 0.128 m/s) and lateral (RMSE: 0.178 m/s) tracking error, while DR performed slightly better in yaw rate tracking (RMSE: 0.108 rad/s). Overall, performance was comparable under nominal real-world conditions.

2. **OOD Robustness (Hardware Modifications and Rough Terrain):**
- Intentional hardware modifications were introduced: modified feet with different contact dynamics (+3 kg per foot) and additional batteries on the base (+3 kg each). The robot was also tested on a compliant, 6 cm thick gym mat (rough terrain).
- **DR:** All training seeds failed on the rough terrain with modified hardware, resulting in the robot falling.
- **ERFI:** Showed partial success; some seeds completed the task, while others failed or exhibited severe yaw drift.
- **Proposed Method:** Demonstrated successful locomotion across all training seeds, maintaining balance and minimizing drift throughout the trajectory despite the challenging, unmodeled conditions. This provides strong real-world evidence for the superior robustness of the proposed method against complex reality gaps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of sim-to-real transfer in robotics, particularly for complex, high-dimensional systems like humanoids.

1. **Enhanced Robustness to Unmodeled Dynamics:** The most prominent significance lies in the method's demonstrated ability to impart superior robustness against reality gaps that arise from unmodeled dynamics. Traditional domain randomization is inherently limited by the expressiveness of simulator formulations and the finite set of modifiable parameters. By replacing parametric randomization with neural network-generated, state-dependent perturbations in joint torque space, the proposed approach effectively simulates complex phenomena like nonlinear actuator behaviors and contact compliance, which are often difficult or impossible to capture with standard methods.

2. **Improved Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The empirical validation on the full-sized humanoid robot TOCABI demonstrates that policies trained with the proposed method achieve successful zero-shot transfer to hardware. This is crucial for reducing the time, cost, and safety risks associated with real-world robot training and system identification. The method's effectiveness extends even to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios involving significant hardware modifications and challenging terrains.

3. **Theoretically Grounded and Flexible Approach:** The paper's reinterpretation of domain randomization as a fixed functional perturbation in joint torque space provides a theoretical foundation for its alternative. By utilizing neural networks as flexible function approximators, the method offers a versatile way to generate a broader class of perturbations, moving beyond the constraints of simulator-specific parameterizations.

4. **No Compromise on Nominal Performance:** The findings indicate that the enhanced robustness of the proposed method does not come at the expense of performance in nominal or unperturbed environments. This ensures that the trained policies are not only robust to adverse conditions but also efficient and accurate under standard operating parameters.

5. **Broader Applicability:** While validated on humanoid locomotion, the underlying principle of functional perturbation injection into joint torque space has potential for application across a wide array of robotic systems and tasks. This could include quadrupedal locomotion, manipulation, or other dynamic control problems where complex interaction dynamics and actuator uncertainties are prevalent.

6. **Facilitating DRL Adoption:** By providing a more robust and adaptable framework for training control policies in simulation, this work can further support the adoption of deep reinforcement learning algorithms in real-world environments. It mitigates a key bottleneck—the reality gap—making DRL-driven robotic solutions more practical and accessible for challenging applications.

In summary, this work offers a promising pathway for advancing the state of sim-to-real transfer, particularly in scenarios where comprehensive modeling of real-world physics is challenging or incomplete.
