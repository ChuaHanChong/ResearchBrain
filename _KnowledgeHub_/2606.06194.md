# ActiveMimic: Egocentric Video Pretraining with Active Perception

> [!summary] Summary
> ActiveMimic introduces a two-stage pretraining framework that leverages active perception from in-the-wild egocentric human videos to enhance robot manipulation capabilities. The approach enables robots to learn synchronized viewpoint and manipulation actions, leading to improved performance on real-world tasks such as Restocking (90.1%), Reaching (88.9%), Finding (91.7%), and Pouring (93.3%), often surpassing state-of-the-art models pretrained on robot-specific data.

## Problem

- Robot foundation models pretrained on readily available egocentric human videos often show lower performance compared to those trained on costly robot-specific data.
- Existing methods for pretraining from human videos neglect 'active perception' (intentional viewpoint adjustments), treating camera motion as noise rather than a meaningful action.
- Decoupling and modeling synchronized camera and wrist motions from single-camera, in-the-wild egocentric videos is challenging without specialized motion capture hardware.

## Method

- **ActiveMimic** recovers synchronized egocentric camera and bimanual wrist trajectories from raw in-the-wild human videos using only off-the-shelf vision models, resolving camera-hand coupling by re-expressing poses in a chunk-relative frame.
- These motions are encoded into a unified 27-dimensional action vector, representing coupled camera translation/rotation and left/right wrist translation/rotation.
- A ==mix-of-transformers== architecture is trained in two stages: first on the human egocentric video dataset with the 27D action supervision, then fine-tuned on robot-specific data to transfer learned capabilities.

## Results

- ActiveMimic achieved competitive success rates across four real-world tasks, notably **90.1%** on Restocking and **91.7%** on Finding, outperforming state-of-the-art robot-data-pretrained baselines on tasks requiring significant active perception.
- The vision-based action recovery demonstrated high fidelity on the HOT3D dataset, with head pose recovery reaching **78.82%** and wrist recovery exceeding **60%** under strict error tolerances.
- The framework showed enhanced robustness, achieving **79.0%** success under flashing lighting and **72.2%** with unseen objects, conditions under which other human-video-pretrained baselines failed or had substantially reduced performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling active perception from egocentric human videos, which involves learning coordinated viewpoint and manipulation actions, is critical for enhancing robot task performance and generalization.
> - A purely vision-based approach can effectively and accurately derive high-fidelity 27D action labels (head and bimanual wrist poses) from in-the-wild egocentric videos, eliminating the need for specialized capture hardware.
> - The active perception capability is primarily acquired during the egocentric human video pretraining stage, manifesting through the robot's head camera and facilitating robust human-to-robot representational transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen the emergence of robot foundation models, which often integrate Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with action experts. These models are typically pretrained on large-scale robot-specific datasets and then adapted to downstream tasks. However, acquiring extensive and diverse robot data is a resource-intensive process, limiting scalability and task variability.

Egocentric human videos present an alternative data source for pretraining. They are comparatively less expensive to collect, offer a wider range of daily activities, and are readily scalable. Despite these advantages, models pretrained exclusively on egocentric human data have historically exhibited lower performance compared to those pretrained on robot data. Existing efforts to bridge this performance gap primarily focus on inferring proxy action labels from human videos, such as hand trajectories, hand point clouds, or object motion. These methods, however, frequently omit a specific type of human behavior: active perception. During manipulation, humans continuously adjust their viewpoint through head and body movements, generating camera motion in egocentric videos. Standard pretraining pipelines often treat this camera motion as incidental noise, rather than as an intentional viewpoint action.

Active perception is a long-standing concept in robotics and computer vision, emphasizing an agent's active control over its viewpoint to optimize perceptual information, as opposed to passively receiving data. Traditionally, this has been explored through approaches like Next-Best-View planning, which treats viewpoint selection as distinct from manipulation. More recent research integrates camera motion and manipulation actions within a unified action space, enabling end-to-end learning of perception and action. Nevertheless, these contemporary methods generally depend on specialized data collection setups, such as VR headsets for teleoperation or wearable sensors for head and hand pose tracking. Such reliance on dedicated hardware restricts their applicability to in-the-wild egocentric human videos, which constitute a large, organically produced corpus suitable for web-scale pretraining, analogous to the data sources used for modern VLM and LLM development.

ActiveMimic positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitation of existing human video pretraining methods that neglect active perception, and by overcoming the hardware dependency of advanced active perception learning approaches. It proposes a purely vision-based framework to recover and model synchronized active perception and manipulation signals from readily available, in-the-wild egocentric human videos, thereby seeking to enhance the utility of this scalable data source for robot foundation models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of egocentric human video for robot pretraining by explicitly integrating "active perception" into the learning framework. The authors aim to bridge the performance disparity observed between robot foundation models pretrained on robot-specific data and those pretrained on human egocentric videos.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and challenges:

1. **Scalability and Diversity of Data:** While robot data is valuable, it is costly to acquire, difficult to scale, and limited in the diversity of tasks it covers. Egocentric human videos, conversely, offer a scalable, diverse, and cheaper alternative, encompassing a broad spectrum of daily human activities. Despite these advantages, their potential for robot pretraining remains largely underutilized due to a performance gap.
2. **Missing Active Perception Signal:** Current approaches to pretrain robots on human egocentric videos often focus on deriving proxy action labels for hand or object motion. However, they typically neglect the active perception behavior inherent in human manipulation. Humans instinctively reposition their viewpoint through head and body movements to gain better visual information during tasks. This induced camera motion in egocentric videos is generally treated as noise rather than a meaningful "viewpoint action." The authors hypothesize that explicitly modeling this active perception behavior is crucial for unlocking the full potential of egocentric human video for robot pretraining.
3. **Technical Challenge of Decoupling Camera and Hand Motion:** Modeling active perception from egocentric videos necessitates the recovery of synchronized camera and wrist trajectories. A significant technical challenge arises because wrist motion recovered directly from a single body-worn camera inherently conflates hand movement with the camera's own rotation and translation. This "camera and hand coupling" makes it difficult for a model to accurately learn either motion independently. Existing methods that successfully decouple these motions usually rely on specialized capture hardware (e.g., multiple cameras, wearable sensors), which makes them unsuitable for scaling to in-the-wild egocentric video.
4. **Desire for a Unified Action Representation:** To effectively learn the relationship between perception and manipulation, a unified action representation is needed that captures how viewpoint motion and bimanual wrist motion co-evolve in a common reference frame. This representation should ideally be derivable from a single, body-worn RGB camera without specialized equipment.

Therefore, the core motivation is to develop a pretraining framework, ActiveMimic, that can recover and model this synchronized, coupled active perception and manipulation behavior from in-the-wild egocentric human videos using only off-the-shelf vision models. This framework aims to enable robots to acquire human-like active perception capabilities during pretraining, thereby improving their performance and generalization in real-world manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ActiveMimic is a two-stage pretraining framework designed to leverage active perception from in-the-wild egocentric human videos for robot manipulation.

#### 3.1. From Egocentric Video to Unified Action Space (Sec. 3.1)

This initial stage focuses on processing raw egocentric videos to extract a structured action signal that represents both active perception (camera motion) and manipulation (wrist motion). This is achieved without specialized sensors, relying solely on a single body-worn RGB camera. The process involves three steps:

1. **Recovering Camera and Wrist Trajectories:**
 - For each egocentric video frame, three synchronized pose trajectories are estimated: the egocentric camera trajectory and the trajectories for the left and right wrists.
 - **Wrist Poses:** Left and right wrist poses ($T^{\text{wrist}}_{L/R,k}$ relative to $cam_k$) are estimated using SAM-3D-Body, expressed in the coordinate system of the current frame camera.
 - **Camera Trajectory:** The egocentric camera trajectory ($T^{\text{cam}}_{k,cam_1}$) is recovered by VGGT as a scale-normalized path. To convert this to a metric scale, per-pixel depth maps from VGGT are aligned with metric depth estimates from UniDepth. A per-frame scale factor ($\lambda_k$) is calculated as the median depth ratio over valid pixels, which is then aggregated into an episode-level scale factor ($\lambda$). This $\lambda$ scales only the translational component of the camera trajectory, preserving its rotation.
 - **Data Source:** The Ego4D dataset, specifically its Hands and Objects subset, is used. This subset undergoes a two-stage filtering process: VLM-based temporal segmentation identifies manipulation segments and generates task instructions, followed by LLM-based semantic filtering to ensure the segments involve indoor hand-object manipulation of artificial physical objects. This results in 2,561 episodes (approximately 10 hours of video at 10 fps).

2. **Resolving Camera and Wrist Coupling:**
 - The raw recovered trajectories are coupled: wrist poses are in the current-frame camera's coordinate system, while the camera trajectory is anchored to the first frame. This means that changes in wrist poses reflect both actual wrist movement and the camera's own motion, leading to ambiguity if used directly as action supervision.
 - To decouple these motions, all poses are re-expressed in a common *chunk-relative* reference frame. For a fixed-length temporal chunk, all camera and wrist poses within that chunk are re-centered to the coordinate system of the chunk's first frame ($cam_i$).
 - The chunk-relative camera pose ($T^{\text{cam}}_{i+\tau,cam_i}$) is calculated by transforming the episode-level camera pose using the inverse of the first-frame camera pose.
 - The chunk-relative wrist poses ($T^{\text{wrist}}_{L/R,i+\tau,cam_i}$) are then obtained by composing the chunk-relative camera pose with the current-frame wrist estimates. This places both camera and wrist motions within the same spatial reference frame, resolving the coupling.

3. **27D Action Representation:**
 - The decoupled chunk-relative poses (camera, left wrist, right wrist) are encoded into a unified 27-dimensional action vector.
 - Each pose, an SE(3) rigid transformation, is represented by its 3D translation vector and a 6D continuous rotation representation.
 - Concatenating these for the camera (3D translation + 6D rotation = 9D), left wrist (9D), and right wrist (9D) yields the final 27D action vector ($a_{i,\tau}$). This unified space allows for joint learning of the coupled dynamics of camera and wrist motion.

#### 3.2. Architecture and Training Strategy (Sec. 3.2)

The model architecture is a "mix-of-transformers" design. It consists of a visual-language prefix that processes images and a tokenized prompt into a multimodal context, and an action expert suffix that predicts future continuous actions.

1. **Training Objective:** The policy is trained using a conditional flow-matching objective. The loss function aims to denoise a noisy action sample back to the clean action chunk, based on a learned velocity field.
2. **Two-Stage Training:**
 - **Stage 1: Egocentric Human Video Pretraining:**
 - The visual-language prefix is initialized from a pretrained VLM checkpoint (e.g., PaliGemma), while the action expert is randomly initialized.
 - The model is supervised with the 27D chunk-relative camera and wrist actions derived in the first stage, learning active perception jointly with manipulation from the large-scale egocentric human video corpus.
 - This stage is further divided into a warm-up phase (VLM frozen, only action expert trained) and a full training phase (all parameters unfrozen and trained end-to-end).
 - **Stage 2: Robot-Specific Training:**
 - The entire model (VLM prefix and action expert) is initialized from the weights obtained in the pretraining stage.
 - The model is then fine-tuned on robot-specific data, with robot actions as supervision, to transfer the acquired active perception capability to the target robotic embodiment.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup (Sec. 4.1)

- **Robot Platform:** Experiments are conducted on an AGI-BOT G1 humanoid upper-body robot, featuring a 2-DoF head, 2-DoF waist, and two 7-DoF arms with parallel-jaw grippers. It uses three RGB cameras: one head-mounted and two wrist-mounted. The head camera, along with head and waist joints, forms the active perception subsystem.
- **Tasks:** Four real-world manipulation tasks are used for evaluation, representing diverse active perception demands:
 - **Restocking:** High demand for viewpoint changes (crouching, standing, looking up at shelves).
 - **Reaching:** Requires standing and leaning over an obstacle.
 - **Finding:** Requires active head turns to locate an object.
 - **Pouring:** Involves bimanual coordination.
- **Baselines:** ActiveMimic is compared against:
 - $\pi_0$: A state-of-the-art model pretrained on robot data.
 - MotoVLA: A state-of-the-art model pretrained on a mixed corpus of robot and human video.
 - ActiveMimic$_{wrist-only}$: An ablation that shares the same architecture and Ego4D corpus as ActiveMimic but is supervised only with 18D wrist actions, excluding camera motion.
 - ActiveMimic$_{sft-only}$: An ablation that skips egocentric pretraining and trains solely on robot-specific data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address four questions regarding the role of active perception in egocentric human video pretraining for robotics.

#### 4.1. Q1: Camera Motion Supervision Improves Real-World Task Performance (Sec. 4.2)

ActiveMimic consistently surpassed all baselines across the four real-world tasks. It achieved success rates of 90.1% on Restocking, 88.9% on Reaching, 91.7% on Finding, and 93.3% on Pouring.
- **Comparison to Ablations:** ActiveMimic$_{wrist-only}$ and ActiveMimic$_{sft-only}$ performed lower than ActiveMimic across all tasks, indicating that camera motion supervision during egocentric pretraining is a critical factor for performance.
- **Comparison to State-of-the-Art:** ActiveMimic matched or exceeded the performance of $\pi_0$, a state-of-the-art model pretrained on robot data, across all tasks. Notably, on Restocking and Finding, which have high active perception demands, ActiveMimic (90.1% and 91.7% respectively) clearly outperformed $\pi_0$ (86.4% and 86.1%), suggesting an active perception advantage derived from egocentric video. ActiveMimic also substantially outperformed MotoVLA, which uses a mixed robot and human data corpus.

#### 4.2. Q2: Egocentric Video Yields Effective Pretraining Labels (Sec. 4.3)

To validate the fidelity of the 27D action labels recovered from in-the-wild egocentric video, evaluation was conducted on HOT3D, a dataset with ground-truth head and wrist pose annotations.
- **Recovery Rates:** Under a strict tolerance (positional error $\le 0.8m$, rot6d L2 error $\le 0.6$), head pose recovery reached 78.82%, with left and right wrist recovery at 65.93% and 61.72% respectively. Under a looser tolerance, recovery rates for all three body parts exceeded 85%.
- **Qualitative Analysis:** Estimated trajectories qualitatively followed ground-truth trends on sampled HOT3D episodes.
- **Conclusion:** These results indicate that the purely vision-based approach can extract sufficiently accurate and effective pretraining signals, despite not using specialized motion capture hardware.

#### 4.3. Q3: Active Perception Originates from Egocentric Pretraining and is Realized by the Head Camera (Sec. 4.4)

An ablation study on the Restocking task investigated the source and mechanism of active perception.
- **Pretraining Source:** Comparing ActiveMimic (24/27 placement points) with ActiveMimic$_{sft-only}$ (6/27 placement points) revealed a fourfold difference in performance on the placement phase, which heavily relies on active perception. This suggests that the active perception capability is primarily acquired during the egocentric pretraining stage, rather than through robot-specific fine-tuning alone.
- **Head Camera Role:** When the head camera was removed from the pretrained ActiveMimic model (ActiveMimic w/o head), placement performance further collapsed to 1/27 points. This indicates that the model utilizes the head camera to manifest its active perception capability.
- **Failure Case Analysis (Sec. C.3):** Failure cases for the "w/o head" condition on Restocking primarily involved placement errors (e.g., imprecise motion, wrong lateral position, wrong shelf tier), illustrating how severing the visual feedback loop from the head camera disrupts the acquired head-hand coordination.

#### 4.4. Q4: Camera Motion Supervision Enables Human-to-Robot Representational Transfer (Sec. 4.5)

The study investigated how camera motion supervision facilitates the transfer of representations from human perception to robot control.
- **Representational Overlap:** The overlap in activation patterns of the top-K% activated units between "head-view" (only head camera input) and "full-view" (all three robot cameras) inference conditions was compared for ActiveMimic and ActiveMimic$_{wrist-only}$.
- **Results:** ActiveMimic consistently maintained a higher overlap percentage than ActiveMimic$_{wrist-only}$ across the early-to-mid layers of the action expert (layers 0 through 11), which are typically responsible for encoding perceptual representations. This higher overlap suggests that the representations learned by ActiveMimic are more robust to shifts in observation modality (from a single egocentric human view to multiple robot views).
- **Conclusion:** Camera motion supervision contributes to the development of perceptual representations that generalize more effectively across human and robot embodiments, strengthening human-to-robot transfer at a representational level.

#### 4.5. Robustness Evaluation (Sec. C.2)

ActiveMimic demonstrated enhanced robustness under challenging conditions:
- **Flashing Lighting (Restocking):** ActiveMimic achieved the highest success rate (79.0%) and exhibited the smallest performance drop (-11.1%) compared to other models. Baselines without active-perception-aware pretraining (ActiveMimic$_{sft-only}$, MotoVLA) collapsed to 0% success.
- **Unseen Objects (Finding):** ActiveMimic maintained the highest success rate (72.2%) and the smallest drop (-19.5%) when faced with visually different, unseen yogurt objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents ActiveMimic, a framework that addresses a fundamental challenge in scalable robot learning by effectively leveraging egocentric human videos. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Unlocking Egocentric Human Video for Robotics:** The work demonstrates that explicitly modeling "active perception"—the human behavior of actively repositioning viewpoints during manipulation—is crucial for maximizing the utility of in-the-wild egocentric human videos in robot pretraining. This provides a mechanism to bridge the performance gap between models pretrained on expensive robot data and those pretrained on more abundant human video.
2. **Enhanced Scalability and Data Efficiency:** By deriving synchronized camera and wrist trajectories from a single body-worn RGB camera using off-the-shelf vision models, ActiveMimic eliminates the need for specialized, expensive, and difficult-to-scale capture hardware. This approach significantly broadens the pool of usable pretraining data to include vast existing and future in-the-wild egocentric video corpora (e.g., Ego4D, Ego-Exo4D), making robot foundation model training more scalable and cost-effective.
3. **Improved Robot Performance and Generalization:** The empirical results indicate that ActiveMimic not only surpasses other human-video-pretrained baselines but also matches or exceeds state-of-the-art models pretrained on robot data, particularly for tasks with high active perception demands. This suggests that the learned active perception capabilities lead to more competent and robust robot behaviors.
4. **Increased Robustness to Perturbations:** The demonstrated robustness of ActiveMimic to adverse conditions like flashing lighting and unseen objects is critical for real-world robot deployment. This suggests that modeling active perception fosters representations that are more resilient to visual variations and domain shifts, enhancing the practical applicability of robots.
5. **Insights into Human-to-Robot Transfer:** The analysis of representational overlap provides evidence that camera motion supervision facilitates stronger human-to-robot representational transfer. This indicates that the active perception signal helps the model learn more abstract and transferable perceptual features that are robust to differences in observation modalities between humans and robots.
6. **Foundation for Future Robot Capabilities:** ActiveMimic lays groundwork for developing more advanced robot intelligence. The ability to learn active viewpoint control from human demonstrations could be extended to more complex scenarios, such as:
 - **Loco-manipulation:** Coordinating locomotion and manipulation for humanoid robots, leveraging egocentric videos containing walking-while-manipulating footage.
 - **Human-Robot Interaction:** Enabling robots to better perceive and respond to human actions in shared workspaces, drawing from the inherent social interactions in egocentric data.
 - **Autonomous Exploration and Task Execution:** Equipping robots with the capacity to actively explore environments and dynamically adjust their perspective to optimize task execution, akin to human behavior.

In conclusion, ActiveMimic represents a methodological advancement that redefines how egocentric human video can be leveraged for robot learning, highlighting active perception as a key ingredient for developing more capable, scalable, and generalizable robot foundation models.
