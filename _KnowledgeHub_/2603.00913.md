# Minimalist Compliance Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed "Minimalist Compliance Control," a model-based framework that enables robust and safe compliant robotic interaction using only motor current or PWM signals, eliminating the need for force/torque sensors. This method demonstrated superior trajectory tracking on contact-rich tasks, achieving 15.9 ± 5.1 mm position error for drawing tasks on a humanoid robot, and successfully integrated with various high-level AI planning paradigms across diverse robot platforms.

## Problem

- Traditional compliance control relies on expensive, bulky, and fragile force/torque sensors, limiting its widespread adoption on many common robotic platforms.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based approaches for compliance suffer from the sim-to-real gap, lack explicit safety guarantees (e.g., unpredictable force spikes), and add complexity to the control system.
- Historical model-based sensorless estimation methods were impractical due to noisy sensors and limited computational power in earlier decades.

## Method

- **Minimalist Compliance Control** is a model-based framework that leverages readily available motor current or PWM signals to estimate external wrenches.
- The framework integrates ==motor torque estimation== (via system identification and direction-dependent gains), external wrench estimation (using ==regularized least-squares== and filtering), and ==task-space admittance control==.
- Designed for embodiment-agnostic and plug-and-play integration with various high-level planning strategies, including VLM-based, imitation learning, and model-based policies.

## Results

- Achieved external force estimation with mean absolute errors of **0.69 ± 0.73 N** for a humanoid robot and **1.05 ± 1.60 N** for a robot arm, validating its accuracy against a force/torque sensor.
- Outperformed RL baselines on a drawing task with a humanoid robot, achieving lower position tracking error (**15.9 ± 5.1 mm** vs. up to **57.8 ± 30.2 mm** for RL) and superior orientation tracking.
- Demonstrated robust and safe compliant interaction across four diverse robot platforms (ToddlerBot humanoid, LEAP dexterous hand, ARX arm, Unitree G1 humanoid) in tasks ranging from drawing to in-hand reorientation, with high success rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modern servo and quasi-direct-drive (QDD) motors provide sufficient current/PWM signal accuracy to re-enable practical model-based external torque estimation for compliant control.
> - Effective and safe compliance primarily requires accurate force direction and appropriate responsiveness rather than perfect force magnitude accuracy, which a minimalist estimation approach can provide.
> - By bridging classical admittance control with modern AI planning, the method offers a versatile solution for contact-rich manipulation without requiring specialized hardware or complex learning pipelines.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Compliance control is a cornerstone of safe and robust physical interaction for robots. It allows robots to yield to external forces, absorb impacts, and perform contact-rich tasks without damaging themselves, the environment, or humans. Historically, achieving compliance has relied on specialized hardware:

- **Expensive Force/Torque (F/T) Sensors:** End-effector or joint torque sensors provide direct measurements of interaction forces, enabling classical admittance and impedance control. However, these sensors are often costly, bulky, fragile, and not integrated into many widely used robot platforms (e.g., many modern robot arms, dexterous hands, and humanoids).
- **Actuators with Intrinsic Force Feedback:** Some advanced actuators offer high-bandwidth torque control, but these are also not universally available.

In response to these hardware limitations, the robotics community has explored two main avenues:

- **Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based Compliance:** This emerging trend leverages deep learning to implicitly infer external wrenches from proprioceptive signals and learn compliant policies. While promising for their potential to generalize and adapt, RL approaches suffer from significant drawbacks:
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap:** Policies trained in simulation often struggle with deployment on real hardware due to discrepancies in physics and sensor noise.
 - **Lack of Safety Guarantees:** The black-box nature of neural networks means learned controllers can produce unpredictable, large force spikes during interaction, posing safety risks.
 - **Increased System Complexity:** RL pipelines add complexity to the control architecture and can be difficult to tune and deploy reliably.
- **Model-based Sensorless Estimation (Historical):** Early work in the 1990s attempted to estimate torques from motor currents using disturbance observers. However, limitations in sensor accuracy and computational power at the time meant these methods were often too noisy and required extensive filtering, limiting their practical utility.

"Minimalist Compliance Control" positions itself as a crucial bridge, addressing the limitations of both traditional hardware-dependent methods and modern RL-based alternatives. It re-examines the potential of model-based sensorless estimation in the context of **modern actuator technologies**. The key insight is that modern servos and quasi-direct-drive (QDD) motors offer significantly improved current sensing and higher-bandwidth control loops, making motor current/PWM signals much more reliable for torque estimation than in previous decades. By demonstrating that sufficient accuracy for *stable and responsive* compliance (rather than perfect force magnitude accuracy) can be achieved through this minimalist approach, the authors democratize compliance control. This work thus offers a practical, safe, and widely applicable alternative that avoids the cost and fragility of external sensors while sidestepping the complexity and safety concerns of purely learning-based methods. It extends classical model-based control to a broader range of robots, making advanced contact-rich manipulation accessible to platforms that were previously hardware-constrained.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of "Minimalist Compliance Control" is to **enable robust and safe compliant behavior in robotic systems without relying on expensive, bulky, or fragile force/torque sensors, nor on complex, unsafe, or difficult-to-tune reinforcement learning approaches.**

The motivations driving this research are multi-faceted:

- **Overcoming Hardware Barriers:** The widespread adoption of compliance control is hindered by the high cost and specialized hardware requirements (e.g., F/T sensors) of traditional methods. Many popular robot platforms, including common robot arms (ARX, ViperX), dexterous hands (LEAP), and humanoids (Unitree G1, ToddlerBot), lack these sensors. The authors aim to remove this dependency.
- **Addressing Limitations of RL-based Approaches:** While RL offers a sensor-free alternative, it introduces problems like the sim-to-real gap, lack of safety guarantees (e.g., dangerous force spikes), and increased system complexity, making real-world deployment challenging. The motivation is to provide a safer, more reliable, and simpler alternative.
- **Leveraging Existing Actuator Capabilities:** Modern servo and QDD motors inherently provide motor current or PWM signals. The authors recognized that these signals, combined with improved sensing capabilities compared to earlier decades, contain sufficient information for external wrench estimation, thus motivating a re-evaluation of model-based sensorless control.
- **Democratizing Compliance Control:** By providing a solution that relies only on ubiquitous motor signals and does not require complex learning, the paper aims to make compliant interaction capabilities accessible to a much broader range of robotic systems, lowering the barrier to entry for contact-rich manipulation.
- **Ensuring Safety and Responsiveness:** The research specifically highlights that compliance control primarily requires correct force sign/direction and reasonable accuracy in the relevant frequency band for stability and timely responses, rather than highly accurate force magnitudes. This motivates a minimalist estimation strategy that prioritizes these critical properties, implicitly addressing safety concerns.
- **Embodiment-Agnostic and Plug-and-Play Integration:** The authors were motivated to develop a framework that is generalizable across diverse robot morphologies (arms, hands, humanoids) and compatible with various high-level planning strategies (VLM-based, imitation learning, model-based policies), maximizing its practical utility.

In essence, the research seeks to fill a critical gap by offering a practical, model-based, and sensor-free approach to compliance that is inherently safer and simpler than RL, while being far more accessible than traditional hardware-dependent methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Minimalist Compliance Control" framework integrates three core components: motor torque estimation, external wrench estimation, and task-space admittance control, designed to be embodiment-agnostic and plug-and-play with various high-level planners.

#### 3.1. Motor Torque Estimation

The method's foundation lies in accurately estimating motor torque from readily available motor current or PWM signals. This process involves:
- **System Identification (SysID):** For motors with current sensors, the current is read directly. For motors without, current ($I_w$) is estimated from PWM voltage ($V_{PWM}$) and back electromotive force ($V_{emf}$) using standard motor equations: $V_{PWM} = PWM \times V_{bus}$, $V_{emf} = \dot{q} K_v$, and $I_w = (V_{PWM} - V_{emf}) / R_w$. The velocity constant ($K_v$) is calibrated by measuring no-load motor velocity under varying PWM, and winding resistance ($R_w$) is estimated by measuring current under varying PWM with an external sensor.
- **Torque Constant Calibration:** The motor torque constant ($K_t$) is calibrated from the current-torque relationship: $K_t = \tau_{load} / (\eta I_w)$, where $\tau_{load}$ is the measured load torque and $\eta$ is motor efficiency. Manufacturer-provided $K_t$ values are used when available.
- **Direction-Dependent Gains:** To account for asymmetric power transmission and losses in geared systems (e.g., friction, backlash), direction-dependent efficiency gains ($\eta$ for forward drive, $\eta^{-1}$ for backward drive) are applied based on the sign of power flow ($\tau_w \dot{q}$). This is critical for systems with high gear ratios.
- **Gravity Compensation:** To isolate external torques, gravity-induced torques ($\tau_{grav}$) are subtracted from the estimated load torque: $\tau_{ext} = -(r \cdot \tau_{load} - \tau_{grav})$, where $r$ is the gear ratio. The method assumes a quasi-static interaction regime where inertial and Coriolis terms are negligible, which holds for most compliance-requiring tasks dominated by low-frequency contact.

#### 3.2. External Wrench Estimation

Once joint-level external torques ($\tau_{ext}$) are estimated, they are translated into task-space external wrenches ($\hat{f}_{ext}$):
- **Full-Wrench Recovery:** A regularized least-squares problem is solved to recover the translational component of the force: $\hat{f}_{p,ext} = \arg \min_f \|J_p^\top f - \tau_{ext}\|^2 + \lambda \|f\|^2$, where $J_p$ is the translational Jacobian and $\lambda$ is a regularization coefficient. Rotational components can be recovered similarly.
- **Axis-Aligned Estimation:** For improved numerical conditioning and robustness to noise, the method optionally estimates only forces along specific axes (e.g., the contact normal). This simplifies to a set of independent 1D regularized least-squares problems, providing targeted compliance.
- **Noise Reduction:** An exponential moving average (EMA) filter is applied to the estimated forces to reduce noise, with the alpha parameter tuned for responsiveness vs. smoothing.

#### 3.3. Admittance Control with Inverse Kinematics

The estimated external wrench ($\hat{f}_{ext}$) is then fed into a task-space admittance controller:
- **Spring–Mass–Damper Model:** Each end-effector is modeled as a spring–mass–damper system, governed by $m \ddot{x} = K_p(x_{des} - x) + K_d(\dot{x}_{des} - \dot{x}) + f_{cmd} + \hat{f}_{ext}$. Here, $x$ is the end-effector pose, $K_p$ and $K_d$ are stiffness and damping matrices, $x_{des}$ and $\dot{x}_{des}$ are desired pose/velocity, and $f_{cmd}$ is commanded wrench.
- **Reference Update:** The dynamics are integrated using a semi-implicit Euler scheme to update task-space position and velocity references ($\dot{x}_{ref}$, $x_{ref}$).
- **Joint-Space Mapping:** The updated task-space references are converted into joint position targets using an inverse kinematics (IK) solver, which are then tracked by the robot's joint position controllers.

#### 3.4. High-Level Planning Strategies (Plug-and-Play)

A significant aspect of the methodology is its seamless integration with diverse high-level planners:
- **VLM-based Planning:** Vision–language models (VLMs) and vision foundation models (e.g., SAM3, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Foundation Stereo) are used to interpret text prompts and images. They predict 3D contact points and normals, which are interpolated into dense trajectories ($x_{des}$). Stiffness ($K_p$) and force commands ($f_{cmd}$) are derived from these normals, and damping ($K_d$) is set for critical damping.
- **Imitation Learning:** Diffusion policies are trained on teleoperated demonstrations, where the human operator uses the compliance controller. The learned policy outputs desired end-effector targets ($x_{des}$), which the compliance controller then executes, correcting for any local errors with reactive compliance.
- **Model-based Planning (Hybrid Force-Velocity Planning):** For known contact locations, optimally-conditioned hybrid servoing (OCHS) is used to compute robust compliance profiles. High stiffness is assigned to velocity-controlled directions, and low stiffness with a force offset to force-controlled directions, enabling tasks like in-hand manipulation while maintaining contact.

This comprehensive methodology allows "Minimalist Compliance Control" to be a versatile, robust, and accessible solution for compliant robotic interaction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling evidence for the efficacy and robustness of Minimalist Compliance Control through extensive experimentation across four distinct robotic platforms and a variety of contact-rich tasks.

#### 4.1. External Wrench Estimation Accuracy

- **Quantitative Validation (Figure 3):** The method's ability to estimate external forces was directly compared against an ATI Mini45 force/torque sensor.
 - For the ToddlerBot (servo motors with gear ratio > 200:1), the mean absolute error was 0.69 ± 0.73 N.
 - For the ARX Arm (QDD motors with gear ratio ≈ 10:1), the mean absolute error was 1.05 ± 1.60 N.
- **Observations:** The estimated forces closely matched sensor measurements, remaining near zero in free space and accurately tracking contact events. The direction-dependent drive state (forward/backward) and gearbox efficiency implicitly captured friction and transmission losses, proving sufficient for accurate estimation. Despite some transient noise spikes in ill-conditioned Jacobian situations (e.g., ToddlerBot z-axis force), these had negligible impact on overall task performance.

#### 4.2. Compliance Control Performance vs. Baselines

- **Quantitative Comparison (Table I):** The method was quantitatively compared against two reinforcement learning baselines (UniFP and FACET) on a drawing task using the ToddlerBot. The metrics were position tracking error, orientation tracking error, and humanoid root pitch (as a proxy for contact force).
 - **Our Method (full):** Achieved the lowest position tracking error (15.9 ± 5.1 mm) and orientation error (0.048 ± 0.043 rad), demonstrating superior trajectory tracking. It also maintained an appropriate root pitch (0.029 ± 0.012 rad), indicating sufficient but not excessive contact force.
 - **Our Method (w/o $\hat{f}_{ext}$):** Without external wrench estimation (effectively a pure position controller with fixed compliance), performance degraded significantly (22.5 ± 9.6 mm position error, 0.082 ± 0.040 rad orientation error), highlighting the critical role of feedback from estimated external forces.
 - **RL Baselines (UniFP, FACET):** Both UniFP (57.8 ± 30.2 mm pos error) and FACET (22.4 ± 11.0 mm pos error) struggled to accurately track the trajectory while maintaining appropriate contact force. FACET, in particular, exhibited a very low root pitch (0.018 ± 0.008 rad), indicating insufficient contact force with the whiteboard.
- **Qualitative Comparison (Figure 4):** Visually, the RL baselines (UniFP, FACET) showed poorer tracking of the target heart-shaped trajectory, failing to maintain consistent contact or applying insufficient force. The full Minimalist Compliance Control accurately followed the trajectory with appropriate contact.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The controller runs efficiently, taking approximately 12 ms per step, with 10 ms attributed to the IK solver, making it suitable for real-time control.

#### 4.3. Embodiment-Agnostic and Plug-and-Play Validation

The framework was extensively validated across diverse platforms and planning paradigms:

- **ToddlerBot (Humanoid Robot) - Figure 5:**
 - **VLM-based Policy (Drawing/Wiping):** Successfully drew shapes (e.g., star, heart) and wiped on a whiteboard, maintaining consistent contact force despite inaccurate VLM predictions. Position control baselines frequently failed due to excessive force (poor tangential tracking) or insufficient force (no contact).
 - **Imitation Learning (Egg Placement):** Achieved an 80% success rate (16/20) in placing an egg on bread with a spatula, significantly outperforming the position-control baseline (40%). The compliance controller corrected for local errors from out-of-distribution inference trajectories.
 - **Model-based Planning (Ball Rotation):** Successfully rotated a ball by an average of 21 degrees, demonstrating robust contact for in-hand manipulation. The baseline typically slipped or rotated the ball by only 2-3 degrees.
- **LEAP Hand (Dexterous Hand) - Figure 6:**
 - **VLM-based Policy (Multi-finger Drawing/Wiping):** Successfully drew "O" and "K" with multiple fingers and wiped, demonstrating effective multi-contact force regulation. Position control often failed due to contact loss or excessive force.
 - **Model-based Planning (In-hand Reorientation):** Achieved an average translation of 3.6 cm and rotation of 27 degrees for various objects (cylinder, ball, cube), sustaining reorientation for multiple cycles. The position-control baseline typically dropped objects quickly due to lack of regulated contact.
- **ARX Arm (Robot Arm) & Unitree G1 (Humanoid):** The method was also successfully demonstrated on these platforms, further emphasizing its generalizability (qualitative results in supplementary video).

#### 4.4. Beyond End-Effector Contacts

The paper notes that because motor currents respond to disturbances anywhere on the robot, the controller can react to disturbances beyond just end-effector contacts, enhancing overall robustness.

In summary, the main findings strongly support that Minimalist Compliance Control provides robust, safe, and effective compliant interaction. It achieves accuracy comparable to or better than current RL methods without their inherent drawbacks, and it extends advanced contact-rich capabilities to a wide array of hardware platforms that lack traditional force sensors, leveraging only readily available motor signals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"Minimalist Compliance Control" represents a significant step forward in making advanced robotic capabilities more accessible and practical, with substantial potential impact across several domains:

- **Democratization of Compliance Control:** The most significant impact is lowering the barrier to entry for compliant interaction. By eliminating the need for expensive, bulky, or fragile force/torque sensors, the method allows a vast number of existing and future robotic systems (robot arms, dexterous hands, humanoids) that are equipped with modern servo and QDD motors to perform contact-rich tasks. This drastically reduces the cost and complexity associated with deploying compliant robots, making such capabilities accessible to a wider research community, educational institutions, and industrial applications.
- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness in Real-World Deployment:** The explicit model-based approach offers safety guarantees and predictable behavior, contrasting sharply with the "black-box" nature and potential for dangerous force spikes observed in some RL-based methods. This inherent safety and robustness are crucial for applications involving human-robot collaboration, delicate object manipulation, and operation in unstructured environments. Robots can now interact more reliably with their surroundings and adapt to unexpected contacts without risking damage.
- **Bridging the Gap Between Classical Control and Modern AI:** The work successfully integrates classical task-space admittance control with cutting-edge high-level planning strategies, including vision–language models, imitation learning, and model-based planning. This "plug-and-play" compatibility means that researchers can leverage the best of both worlds: robust low-level physical interaction without sacrificing the power of modern AI for high-level decision-making and perception. This synergy is key to developing truly intelligent and adaptable robots.
- **Accelerated Robotics Research and Development:** With compliance capabilities made more accessible, researchers can focus on higher-level problems in manipulation, human-robot interaction, and embodied AI without being constrained by hardware limitations or the complexities of developing compliant control from scratch. This could accelerate the pace of innovation in areas requiring fine motor control and physical interaction.
- **New Applications in Contact-Rich Manipulation:** The ability to achieve robust compliance on diverse platforms opens up new possibilities for tasks such as:
 - **Assembly and Manufacturing:** More precise and robust handling of parts, especially those with tight tolerances or requiring compliant insertion.
 - **Service Robotics:** Safer interaction with humans and delicate objects in home or healthcare environments (e.g., assisted feeding, cleaning).
 - **Exploration and Field Robotics:** More robust locomotion and manipulation in challenging, unpredictable terrains or for interacting with environmental features.
 - **Teleoperation and Human-in-the-Loop Systems:** Providing haptic feedback or compliant behavior to improve teleoperation experiences.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The method provides a solid baseline for future research. While the paper acknowledges limitations (non-backdrivable actuators, stiction, backlash, unmodeled dynamics like Coriolis terms or thermal effects), it clearly states a priority for minimalist design over maximal accuracy for the sake of practical performance. Future work could build upon this foundation by integrating more sophisticated motor models or learning-based enhancements in a principled way, without sacrificing the core advantages of simplicity and safety.

In conclusion, "Minimalist Compliance Control" offers a practical, elegant, and impactful solution to a fundamental problem in robotics. By leveraging existing hardware capabilities in a smart, model-based manner, it significantly broadens the reach of compliant interaction, fostering safer, more robust, and more intelligent robots for a diverse range of real-world applications.
