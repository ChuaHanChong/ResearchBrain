# Boolean Satisfiability via Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The ImitSAT method leverages imitation learning to guide Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) solvers, reducing unit propagations, the main computational cost in Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL). By learning from 'KeyTrace' expert demonstrations, the approach consistently lowers propagation counts, leading to improved wall-clock performance on complex random 3-SAT instances and strong generalization across diverse structured SAT problems.

## Problem

- Hand-crafted branching heuristics in CDCL SAT solvers (e.g., VSIDS) lack adaptability to diverse instance structures.
- Unit propagation constitutes a substantial majority (80-90%) of a CDCL solver's wall-clock runtime, making its reduction key for efficiency.
- Existing learning-based branching methods face challenges like high exploration costs and instability in reinforcement learning (Graph-Q-SAT), or indirect influence on dynamic search decisions from initialization-based approaches (SATformer).

## Method

- Developed **KeyTrace**, an ==expert demonstration== extraction method that identifies 'surviving' decision and propagation events after backtracking, effectively eliminating unproductive search paths.
- Framed the branching problem as an autoregressive sequence modeling task, serializing the CNF formula and KeyTrace prefix into a single input for a Perceiver AR model.
- Trained the model via behavior cloning, providing dense, step-level supervision to predict the next expert decision, and integrated it into CDCL with a limited query budget and fallback to native heuristics.

## Results

- ImitSAT consistently achieved lower Median Relative Propagation Percentage (MRPP) across random 3-SAT test sets, demonstrating a substantial reduction in unit propagations (e.g., **0.73**-**0.83** for 3/5 calls vs. 1.00 for MiniSAT baseline).
- It exhibited strong generalization, outperforming prior learned methods on diverse structured SAT families (JNH, AIM, PARITY, PHOLE, PRET) without retraining, often achieving the lowest MRPP and highest win rates.
- On challenging random 3-SAT instances (61-100 variables) and some industrial benchmarks, ImitSAT achieved the lowest wall-clock time, delivering a net speedup compared to MiniSAT and other learned methods.
- Integration with advanced solvers like CaDiCaL and Kissat (with probing disabled) showed consistent MRPP reduction and strong performance gains, particularly on structured datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Expert KeyTraces, representing nearly conflict-free solver runs, provide dense and effective supervision for imitation learning, significantly reducing propagations compared to standard solver behavior.
> - An autoregressive model can effectively learn complex, context-dependent branching policies by observing serialized solver states and expert decision sequences.
> - Combining variable permutation augmentation and staged curriculum learning is crucial for mitigating overfitting, accelerating convergence, and improving generalization capabilities across diverse SAT problem types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) problem is a fundamental challenge in theoretical computer science and artificial intelligence, with applications spanning formal verification, planning, and combinatorial optimization. Modern SAT solvers primarily employ the Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) framework. Within CDCL, the branching heuristic, which selects variables and their polarities for assignment, significantly influences the solver's search trajectory and overall efficiency. Unit propagation, a consequence of these branching decisions, often constitutes the majority of wall-clock time in CDCL solvers.

Traditionally, branching heuristics have been hand-crafted (e.g., Variable State Independent Decaying Sum or VSIDS), which possess limitations in adaptability. Recent research has explored integrating machine learning to enhance solver performance. This includes:
- **Instance-level prediction:** Early methods using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to classify SAT/UNSAT (NeuroSAT) or learn solver behavior directly (Transformers).
- **Targeted in-solver guidance:** Approaches that learn policies for specific CDCL components. Examples include learning clause deletion policies (NeuroSelect), improving phase initialization (NeuroBack), or adopting divide-and-conquer strategies via reinforcement learning (RDC-SAT).
- **Branching policy enhancement:** More directly related efforts include:
 - Using NeuroSAT for variable selection guidance.
 - Graph-Q-SAT, which employs an online reinforcement learning (RL) agent within CDCL to learn branching heuristics, relying on sparse rewards and localized graph snapshots.
 - SATformer, which uses a GNN Transformer to initialize variable activities, thereby indirectly influencing branching.

This work positions itself in the third category, focusing on improving CDCL branching policies. It specifically addresses limitations of prior learning-based methods, such as the extensive exploration and instability often associated with reinforcement learning (Graph-Q-SAT) and the indirect influence of initialization-based approaches (SATformer). By adopting imitation learning, the paper proposes a method that learns directly from expert demonstrations, offering an alternative to RL's exploration challenges and aiming for more direct, decision-level supervision. This approach aligns with applications of imitation learning in other exact optimization domains, such as mixed-integer linear programming (MIP), though the authors note distinct search dynamics between MIP branch-and-bound and SAT CDCL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel, learning-based branching policy for CDCL SAT solvers named ImitSAT, which aims to improve solver efficiency by directly imitating high-quality expert decisions. The core motivations for this approach are rooted in the observed dynamics and challenges of current SAT solving:

1. **Dominance of Unit Propagation in CDCL Runtime:** Empirical analysis indicates that unit propagation accounts for a substantial majority (typically 80-90%) of a CDCL solver's wall-clock time. In the MiniSAT implementation used in this study, it was found to be approximately 88.9%. Therefore, making more informed branching decisions that reduce the number of necessary propagations is identified as the most direct path to achieving significant speedups in overall solver performance.

2. **Limitations of Hand-crafted Branching Heuristics:** Traditional heuristics like VSIDS, while effective, are static and hand-crafted. They lack the adaptability to optimally respond to the diverse structural patterns found in various SAT instances, leaving room for learned approaches to exploit these patterns.

3. **Challenges with Existing Learning-Based Branching Approaches:**
 - **Indirect Influence:** Methods like SATformer learn instance-level signals to adjust variable activities during initialization, but their influence diminishes once the core CDCL branching loop begins, making their impact on dynamic search decisions indirect.
 - **Reinforcement Learning Instability and Exploration Costs:** Approaches based on reinforcement learning, such as Graph-Q-SAT, require extensive and costly exploration. They often suffer from sparse rewards and delayed feedback, leading to training instability and slow convergence. Furthermore, Graph-Q-SAT's reliance on compact graph snapshots for conditioning means it does not fully leverage the comprehensive CDCL execution history.

4. **Desire for Dense, Conflict-Free Supervision:** The authors seek a learning paradigm that provides clear, step-level supervision for branching decisions without the noise introduced by failed explorations or redundant search paths. They hypothesize that replaying an "expert" sequence of decisions would be nearly conflict-free, directly leading to fewer propagations and offering cleaner training targets.

5. **Leveraging Sequential Dependencies:** CDCL branching decisions are inherently prefix-conditioned; each choice depends on the preceding assignments. This sequential nature suggests that framing the branching problem as an autoregressive sequence modeling task could effectively capture these long-context dependencies, a capability that certain deep learning architectures excel at.

By addressing these motivations, ImitSAT aims to provide a branching policy that directly reduces propagation counts, converges faster during training, exhibits stable learning, and integrates seamlessly into the CDCL framework, ultimately leading to improved wall-clock performance across various SAT problem types.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ImitSAT methodology is structured around two principal components: the extraction of expert traces from solver runs and the training of an autoregressive model to imitate these traces.

#### 3.1. Expert KeyTrace Construction

The core idea is to obtain a "clean" sequence of expert decisions that avoids unproductive search branches. A standard CDCL run generates a chronological trail of events: decisions (D), unit propagations (A), and backtracks (BT), each associated with a decision level. Raw trails are often lengthy and contain numerous assignments that are later undone by backtracking.

To extract an expert KeyTrace, the authors developed a systematic procedure:
1. A CDCL solver (MiniSAT in this case) executes an instance, generating a full execution trail.
2. This raw trail is scanned from left to right.
3. A working sequence, `K`, is maintained.
4. For decision (D) and propagation (A) events, the event (signed variable and decision level) is appended to `K`.
5. For a backtrack (BT) event to level `h_i`, all suffix events in `K` whose decision level is greater than `h_i` are removed. A restart is handled by trimming `K` to level 0.
6. The resulting sequence, `K_t`, is the KeyTrace. It represents only the decision and propagation events that ultimately "survive" backtracking, effectively removing detours and redundant computations.

Empirical analysis demonstrated that replaying a KeyTrace on the same instance results in a nearly conflict-free run, reducing the number of propagations to approximately 4% of a standard MiniSAT run. This significant reduction in propagations, which dominate solver runtime, justifies the KeyTrace as a suitable, efficient expert for imitation.

#### 3.2. Autoregressive Imitation for CDCL Branching

The problem of imitating expert branching decisions is framed as an autoregressive sequence modeling task.

**Serialization:** To enable an autoregressive model to process the solver state, the CNF formula `F` and the current KeyTrace prefix `K_t` are serialized into a single input sequence `z(F, K_t)`:
1. The CNF `F` is encoded in DIMACS integer format.
2. A separator token `[SEP]` follows the CNF.
3. The KeyTrace prefix `K_t` is then serialized as blocks, where each decision literal is followed by the unit assignments implied by propagation. Decision levels and assignment tags are omitted for compactness.
4. A decision probe marker `[D]` is appended to signal the request for the next branch.

**Next-decision AR Learner:**
1. A Perceiver AR model (Hawthorne et al., 2022) is used as the architecture. This model utilizes an output latent array that cross-attends to the input, allowing for `O(N)` complexity in terms of input length for a single output prediction, which is more efficient than the `O(N^2)` of a standard Transformer decoder.
2. The model is trained via a behavior cloning paradigm. The objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy) of predicting the expert's next decision `d_{t+1}` given the serialized formula and KeyTrace prefix `z(F, K_t)`. This provides dense, step-level supervision directly from the expert traces.

**Online Integration into CDCL:**
1. During inference, at each decision point, the current solver trail is collapsed into a KeyTrace prefix.
2. The formula and prefix are serialized and fed to the trained ImitSAT model.
3. The model predicts a signed variable as the next branch decision.
4. A small computational budget (e.g., 3 or 5 queries per instance) is allocated for model calls. If the budget is available and the predicted decision is legal (unassigned and within variable range), the solver accepts it and decrements the budget.
5. If the budget is exhausted or the prediction is illegal, the solver falls back to its native VSIDS heuristic. This fallback mechanism ensures completeness and robustness while keeping overhead small. Early decision queries are prioritized, as they are found to have a stronger influence on the search trajectory.

**Improved Training Techniques:**
- **Variable Permutation Augmentation:** To mitigate overfitting and improve robustness, variable IDs are randomly permuted during training. This prevents the model from relying on specific variable identities.
- **Staged Curriculum Learning:** Training starts with smaller variable ranges and progressively expands to larger ones. This strategy accelerates convergence on simpler instances and ensures broader coverage of problem sizes, improving both sample efficiency and generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ImitSAT focused on its ability to reduce propagation counts and wall-clock time, its generalization capabilities, and comparisons against state-of-the-art learned branching methods.

#### 4.1. Performance on Random 3-SAT Test Sets (Table 1)

- ImitSAT consistently achieved the lowest Median Relative Propagation Percentage (MRPP ˜r) across nearly all tested variable ranges (5–15, 16–30, 31–60, 50, 100), tying with SATformer for the best performance on the 61–100 range. Values for ImitSAT (e.g., 0.73-0.83 for 3/5 calls) were notably lower than SATformer (0.78-1.00) and Graph-Q-SAT (0.80-1.15), indicating fewer propagations relative to MiniSAT.
- The KeyTrace expert demonstrated significantly lower MRPP values (e.g., 0.03 for 100 variables), indicating the ideal performance target.
- ImitSAT (3 calls) also achieved the highest one-percent win rate (W1%) across all ranges, signifying more instances where it substantially reduced propagations.
- Retraining Graph-Q-SAT and SATformer on the study's dataset (denoted with `*`) generally resulted in worse performance for Graph-Q-SAT* and similar performance for SATformer*, suggesting a possible dependency of Graph-Q-SAT on its original training data.

#### 4.2. Generalization on Structured SAT Families (Table 2)

- ImitSAT demonstrated strong generalization, achieving the lowest MRPP ˜r or tying for best across diverse structured families (JNH, AIM, PARITY, PHOLE, PRET) without retraining. Notable gains were observed on PARITY and PRET.
- In contrast, Graph-Q-SAT and SATformer often showed increased propagation counts on some structured families (e.g., JNH and AIM for Graph-Q-SAT; JNH, AIM, PHOLE, PRET for SATformer), indicating poorer generalization.
- ImitSAT also secured the highest or tied win rates (W1%) on all structured families, including a perfect score on PRET.

#### 4.3. Wall-Clock Time (Figure 4, Table 12)

- Across random 3-SAT test sets and structured families, ImitSAT generally exhibited favorable wall-clock performance, solving more instances in less time compared to other learned methods.
- When directly compared to pure MiniSAT (Table 12):
 - On easier ranges (16-30, 31-60 variables), MiniSAT remained faster due to the overhead of model queries. However, ImitSAT was the strongest learned method and competitive with MiniSAT.
 - On the harder 61-100 variable range, ImitSAT achieved the lowest wall-clock time overall, outperforming MiniSAT, Graph-Q-SAT, and SATformer. This indicates that its propagation savings translate into a net speedup for sufficiently challenging instances.
- On a subset of SATCOMP industrial benchmarks with up to 100 variables (Appendix M, Figure 6), ImitSAT and SATformer showed substantially faster wall-clock times than Graph-Q-SAT on the hardest instances. ImitSAT matched or slightly outperformed SATformer in this regime.

#### 4.4. Improved Training Techniques (Figure 5, Tables 10, 11)

- **Variable Permutation Augmentation:** This technique significantly mitigated overfitting. With augmentation, training and validation losses tracked closely, and test set metrics (MRPP ˜r and W1%) improved substantially compared to training without it (Table 10).
- **Staged Curriculum Learning:** This strategy accelerated convergence and maintained strong performance across variable ranges. It demonstrated better sample efficiency than training from scratch on larger instances (Table 11), although a fully matched training budget on a single range could narrow the gap. Crucially, curriculum learning preserved performance on smaller instances, which models trained only on larger ranges failed to do.

#### 4.5. Query Budget Analysis (Figure 7)

- Experiments on the 5-15 test set showed diminishing returns for query budget. Most of the performance improvement (MRPP reduction and W1% increase) was achieved within the first three queries. Additional calls beyond six provided only marginal benefits, supporting the default budget of 3 queries due to computational overhead.

#### 4.6. Evaluation of Naive GNN Combination (Table 13)

- An attempt to integrate GNN embeddings (from a SATformer-like encoder) into ImitSAT by replacing CNF tokens did not improve performance. This naive GNN-augmented variant underperformed the text-based ImitSAT, suggesting that the direct token-based serialization was more effective or that a different integration strategy would be required.

#### 4.7. Top-K and Fallback Analysis (Table 14)

- Using a Top-K masking strategy to enforce decision legality showed no significant difference on in-distribution datasets. However, on more challenging out-of-distribution datasets (JNH, AIM), Top-K degraded performance. This implies that falling back to the native VSIDS heuristic when the model's confidence is low or its top prediction is illegal is a more robust strategy than forcing a potentially suboptimal model prediction.

#### 4.8. Integration with Advanced Solvers (Tables 15, 16, 17)

- **CaDiCaL:** ImitSAT effectively integrated with CaDiCaL, a modern CDCL solver. It consistently reduced MRPP ˜r and achieved strong W1% rates on random 3-SAT test sets (e.g., 0.63-0.75 MRPP) and yielded clear gains on structured families (AIM, PARITY, PHOLE, PRET).
- **Kissat:** With Kissat (probing disabled to isolate branching effects), ImitSAT showed improvements primarily on structured datasets, such as PARITY and PRET (e.g., 0.06-0.21 MRPP). On synthetic 3-SAT benchmarks, Kissat's aggressive preprocessing often solved instances without invoking the branching callback, limiting ImitSAT's direct impact unless probing was disabled.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents ImitSAT as a novel and effective approach to improving CDCL SAT solver performance through imitation learning. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Advancement in Learning-Based SAT Solving:** ImitSAT demonstrates a distinct and effective paradigm for integrating machine learning into SAT solvers. By leveraging dense, decision-level supervision from expertly generated `KeyTraces`, it overcomes some limitations of previous reinforcement learning (RL) approaches (e.g., high exploration costs, sparse rewards, instability) and indirect learning methods. This offers a more stable and efficient training process for learning high-quality branching policies.

2. **Direct Reduction of Computational Overhead:** The core motivation of reducing unit propagations, the dominant contributor to CDCL runtime, is directly addressed. The empirical results confirm that ImitSAT consistently reduces propagation counts across diverse benchmarks. For sufficiently complex instances, these reductions translate into tangible wall-clock time improvements, even when accounting for model inference overhead. This direct impact on the most expensive part of CDCL makes ImitSAT a practically relevant improvement.

3. **Strong Generalization Capabilities:** A notable finding is ImitSAT's ability to generalize effectively from training on simple random 3-SAT instances to unseen, structured, and out-of-distribution SAT families, including unsatisfiable and non-k-SAT problems, without modification or re-tuning. This indicates that the learned policy captures fundamental decision-making principles rather than superficial patterns, a crucial aspect for real-world applicability.

4. **Seamless Integration and Robustness:** The design of ImitSAT allows for seamless integration into existing CDCL frameworks. Its mechanism of limited model queries with a fallback to native heuristics ensures completeness and robustness, preventing the learned policy from leading the solver into unproductive search paths when uncertain or encountering novel scenarios.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes imitation learning as a viable and competitive approach for guiding exact combinatorial optimization solvers. It opens avenues for future research into:
 - Exploring richer expert demonstrations beyond simply surviving decisions.
 - Developing hybrid learning schemes that combine the stability of imitation learning with the exploratory power of reinforcement learning.
 - Extending the approach to other domains of combinatorial reasoning and constraint satisfaction problems.
 - Investigating more sophisticated ways to integrate structural information (e.g., GNNs) into the autoregressive framework, potentially enhancing performance beyond text-based serialization.

In summary, ImitSAT represents a practical and robust step forward in intelligent SAT solving, offering a pathway to building more efficient and adaptable SAT solvers through learned branching policies.
