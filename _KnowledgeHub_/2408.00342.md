# MuJoCo MPC for Humanoid Control: Evaluation on HumanoidBench

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from TU Darmstadt and DFKI demonstrate that Model Predictive Control (MPC) with carefully designed dense cost functions achieves stable, smooth, and realistic humanoid behaviors on the HumanoidBench tasks, outperforming state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods and sparse reward MPC. Their work also critically evaluates existing benchmark design, advocating for longer evaluation episodes to assess long-term stability.

## Problem

- Humanoid robot control requires sophisticated coordination of many degrees of freedom while maintaining dynamic balance and achieving task objectives.
- Reinforcement learning methods for humanoid control often produce unstable or unrealistic motions and struggle with sample efficiency.
- Standardized evaluation benchmarks, such as HumanoidBench, have limitations including sparse reward structures and short episode lengths that mask fundamental instabilities.

## Method

- Utilized the ==MuJoCo MPC== framework to implement a ==Model Predictive Control== approach for humanoid tasks.
- Transformed HumanoidBench's sparse reward structure by introducing additional ==regularization terms== and task-specific ==dense rewards== to promote stability and realism.
- Employed different planning algorithms: ==iLQG== for Stand and Walk tasks, and a ==Sampling Planner== for the Push task to handle contact interactions.

## Results

- MPC with the proposed cost functions achieved higher HumanoidBench scores than both sparse-reward MPC and all tested RL baselines (**DreamerV3**, **TD-MPC2**, **SAC**, **PPO**) across Stand, Walk, and Push tasks.
- Demonstrated superior long-term stability over extended **8-second** episodes, whereas sparse-reward MPC performance degraded significantly.
- Generated smoother trajectories with lower average squared joint velocity, indicating improved control quality and reduced energy consumption.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense, carefully designed cost functions are crucial for stable, realistic humanoid behaviors, outperforming sparse reward approaches.
> - Model Predictive Control (MPC) can achieve superior stability and control quality compared to state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods for humanoid tasks.
> - Current robotics benchmarks with short episodes and sparse rewards can mask fundamental instabilities and promote unrealistic behaviors, necessitating extended evaluations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robot control represents one of the most challenging problems in robotics, requiring sophisticated coordination of many degrees of freedom while maintaining dynamic balance and achieving task objectives. Recent advances in both reinforcement learning (RL) and model predictive control (MPC) have shown promise for tackling these challenges, yet significant gaps remain between simulation performance and real-world deployment readiness.

This paper by Meser et al. from TU Darmstadt and the German Research Center for AI (DFKI) addresses these challenges by evaluating Model Predictive Control using the MuJoCo MPC framework on the recently introduced HumanoidBench. The authors demonstrate that while existing RL methods produce "jittery motions ill-suited for execution on real hardware," properly configured MPC with carefully designed cost functions can achieve stable, smooth, and realistic humanoid behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The field of humanoid control has been dominated by two primary approaches: reinforcement learning and model predictive control. RL methods have gained popularity for their ability to learn complex behaviors from data without explicit programming of every motion. However, these approaches often struggle with sample efficiency and produce unstable or unrealistic motions when applied to high-dimensional systems like humanoids.

MPC, in contrast, leverages explicit models of robot dynamics to predict future states and optimize control inputs over a finite horizon. This model-based approach allows for explicit incorporation of stability constraints and physical consistency, potentially leading to more robust and deployable behaviors.

The introduction of HumanoidBench provided a standardized evaluation framework for comparing different control approaches on humanoid tasks. However, the authors identified critical limitations in this benchmark, particularly its sparse reward structure and short episode lengths, which can mask fundamental instabilities and promote unrealistic behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors implemented their approach using MuJoCo MPC (MJPC), a framework designed for real-time behavior synthesis. MPC operates by solving a finite-horizon optimal control problem at each timestep, executing only the first action of the optimal sequence, then re-planning at the next timestep.

The core optimization problem minimizes a cost function of the form:

```
c(x, u) = Σ_i w_i * n_i(c_i(x, u))
```

where `c_i(x, u)` are individual cost residuals, `n_i` are twice-differentiable norm functions, and `w_i` are tunable weights. This formulation allows for flexible combination of different objectives and constraints.

The choice of planning algorithm was adapted to task characteristics:
- **iLQG (iterative Linear Quadratic Gaussian)**: Used for Stand and Walk tasks, providing smooth trajectory optimization for continuous dynamics
- **Sampling Planner**: Employed for the Push task to handle contact interactions that can introduce discontinuities

#### 3.1. Reward Shaping and Cost Function Design

A central contribution of this work lies in the transformation and enhancement of HumanoidBench's reward structure. The authors identified that the benchmark's sparse rewards led to physically unrealistic behaviors, as shown in the comparison below.

The baseline approach (`MPC-hb`) simply transformed HumanoidBench rewards into costs using:

```
c_hb(x, u) = |r_max - r_hb|
```

However, this sparse formulation produced unstable behaviors. The authors' key innovation (`MPC-ours`) was introducing additional regularization terms to promote stability and realism:

**Stability-enhancing terms:**
1. Height of the robot's head from the ground
2. Height difference between pelvis and feet
3. Linear velocity of center of mass
4. Balance (projection of CoM between feet)
5. Deviation from canonical posture
6. Robot orientation maintenance
7. Control signal magnitude (for energy efficiency)

**Task-specific dense rewards:**
1. Distance between object and target
2. Distance between left hand and object
3. Distance between right hand and object

These shaped residuals were combined with the original HumanoidBench reward, each with tunable weights, creating a comprehensive cost function that balances task achievement with physical realism.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate clear superiority of the proposed MPC approach with shaped rewards across multiple dimensions:

**Performance Scores:** MPC-ours achieved significantly higher HumanoidBench scores than both MPC-hb and all tested RL baselines (DreamerV3, TD-MPC2, SAC, PPO) across Stand, Walk, and Push tasks.

**Long-term Stability:** Extended evaluation revealed critical differences between approaches. While MPC-hb might achieve reasonable scores over short 2-second episodes, extending to 8 seconds exposed fundamental instabilities.

**Control Quality:** Quantitative analysis showed MPC-ours generated smoother trajectories with lower average squared joint velocity, indicating improved energy efficiency and reduced wear on robotic hardware.

**Computational Feasibility:** Despite MPC's reputation for computational expense, inference times were manageable on standard hardware (MacBook Air M1), though real-time execution would require optimization or more powerful computing resources.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several important contributions to humanoid robotics. First, it demonstrates that MPC with properly designed cost functions can significantly outperform both sparse reward MPC and state-of-the-art RL methods on complex humanoid tasks. The key insight is that careful reward shaping is essential for achieving stable, realistic behaviors that are suitable for real-world deployment.

Second, the work provides a critical evaluation of current benchmarking practices, highlighting how design choices in evaluation protocols can mask fundamental limitations and lead to misleading comparisons between methods. The advocacy for longer episodes and more comprehensive evaluation criteria will likely influence future benchmark development.

Finally, the authors' commitment to releasing their code as part of the official MuJoCo MPC repository ensures that these advances will be accessible to the broader research community, facilitating rapid prototyping and further development of humanoid control capabilities.

The research demonstrates that the path to deployable humanoid robots may lie not just in learning-based approaches, but in the thoughtful application of classical control methods enhanced with modern optimization frameworks and carefully designed objective functions. This work bridges the gap between simulation achievements and real-world requirements, providing a foundation for more robust and reliable humanoid robot control.

#### 5.1. Implications for Benchmark Design

This work provides important insights for the design of robotics benchmarks. The authors demonstrate that HumanoidBench's evaluation protocol has significant limitations:

**Short Episode Lengths:** The benchmark's 2-second episodes for Walk and Stand tasks are insufficient to assess long-term stability and robustness. Controllers that perform well initially may become unstable over longer horizons.

**Sparse Reward Structure:** Rewards that only consider immediate task completion without regard for stability or physical realism can lead to behaviors that are impressive in simulation but unsuitable for real-world deployment.

**Need for Extended Evaluation:** The authors advocate for longer episodes and repeated tasks with changing goals to properly evaluate controller robustness. Their extended evaluation protocol reveals performance characteristics that shorter tests miss entirely.

These findings have broader implications for how the robotics community evaluates and compares control algorithms, emphasizing the need for benchmarks that reflect real-world deployment requirements.
