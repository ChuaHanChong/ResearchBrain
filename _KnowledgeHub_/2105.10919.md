# Continual World: A Robotic Benchmark For Continual Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The "Continual World" benchmark introduces a computationally accessible suite of robotic manipulation tasks for Continual Reinforcement Learning (CL-RL). This work demonstrates that while most existing CL methods effectively mitigate catastrophic forgetting, they largely fail to achieve positive forward transfer, with PackNet achieving the highest average performance of 0.80 on the CW20 sequence while maintaining positive transfer.

## Problem

- Deep learning models exhibit catastrophic forgetting when continuously learning new tasks in dynamic, non-stationary environments, a critical limitation for autonomous agents like robots.
- Existing continual reinforcement learning (CL-RL) benchmarks are either computationally prohibitive or fail to adequately capture the complexities and inter-task relationships needed to study knowledge transfer effectively.
- The CL research community has overly emphasized catastrophic forgetting prevention, often overlooking or failing to measure positive forward transfer—the ability to leverage prior knowledge to learn new tasks more efficiently.

## Method

- Introduces **Continual World**, a new benchmark for ==continual reinforcement learning== based on 10 or 20 robotic manipulation tasks from **Meta-World**, designed to be computationally accessible.
- The benchmark includes specific ==task sequences== (CW10, CW20, Triplets) ordered to assess varying levels of ==positive and negative transfer== between tasks.
- Provides a standardized evaluation framework with implementations of ==seven diverse continual learning methods== integrated with the ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== algorithm, making the benchmark and code publicly available.

## Results

- PackNet achieved the highest average performance, with a **0.80** success rate on the CW20 benchmark, outperforming all other continual learning methods and even multi-task learning approaches.
- Most regularization-based methods (e.g., L2, EWC, MAS, VCL) effectively mitigated catastrophic forgetting (F ≈ **0.00-0.02**) but concurrently exhibited substantial negative forward transfer, meaning prior learning actively hindered subsequent task acquisition.
- Only Fine-tuning (**0.20**) and PackNet (**0.19**) demonstrated positive forward transfer on CW20, highlighting a significant gap as both remain considerably below the estimated optimal Reference Transfer (**0.46**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing continual learning methods, while effective at preventing catastrophic forgetting, generally fail to achieve positive forward transfer in robotic reinforcement learning settings, often leading to negative transfer.
> - Regularizing the critic network is detrimental to performance in continual reinforcement learning; allowing the critic to adapt freely to the current task is crucial, even if it means 'forgetting' past value functions.
> - The performance of replay-based methods like A-GEM and Perfect Memory, which are strong in supervised CL, significantly diminishes in RL-CL contexts due to factors such as high-variance gradients and replay buffer management challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical, long-standing challenge in the development of intelligent agents: **continual learning (CL)**. CL refers to the ability of a system to continuously acquire new skills and knowledge over time, building on previously learned information, without "catastrophically forgetting" old tasks. This is a natural requirement for long-lived autonomous agents, such as robots, that operate in dynamic, ever-changing environments.

The authors argue that current deep learning models, despite their successes in static, i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) data domains (e.g., image classification, language modeling), fundamentally struggle when deployed in open-ended, non-stationary settings. While CL has gained attention, its evaluation, especially in the context of **reinforcement learning (RL)**, has been problematic.

The paper identifies several limitations of existing benchmarks and evaluation practices in the CL field:
- **Focus on toy tasks:** Many CL benchmarks, particularly in supervised learning, rely on simplified datasets like permuted or split MNIST, which may not capture the complexities of real-world scenarios or the unique challenges of RL.
- **Computational Expense:** Existing RL-based CL benchmarks, such as sequences of Atari games or StarCraft campaigns, are often computationally prohibitive, requiring millions or billions of steps. This slows down the research cycle and limits systematic empirical evaluations.
- **Lack of Meaningful Overlap:** Many RL benchmarks, while challenging, lack sufficient structural or semantic overlap between tasks. This limits their utility for studying *transfer learning*, especially *forward transfer*, which is the ability to leverage prior knowledge to learn new tasks more efficiently. The community's focus has, consequently, disproportionately been on mitigating *catastrophic forgetting* (the loss of performance on old tasks when learning new ones), often at the expense of transfer.

"Continual World" aims to fill this critical gap by providing a benchmark that is:
- **Realistic and Diverse:** Built upon `Meta-World`, a suite of robotic manipulation tasks, it offers rich, continuous control problems.
- **Computationally Accessible:** Designed to be evaluated on modest hardware (CPUs, no GPUs) within a reasonable timeframe, facilitating faster research iteration.
- **Prioritizes Forward Transfer:** Explicitly designed with tasks that have meaningful relationships, encouraging algorithms to exhibit positive transfer, shifting the community's focus beyond just forgetting.

By introducing this benchmark and providing an extensive empirical evaluation of existing CL methods in an RL context, the paper directly contributes to standardizing CL research, accelerating algorithm development, and fostering a deeper understanding of the trade-offs between forgetting and transfer in practical, robotic settings. It positions itself as a foundational step towards building truly intelligent, adaptive AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations behind the "Continual World" paper can be summarized as follows:

- **To address the limitations of current deep learning models in non-stationary environments:** The primary motivation stems from the recognition that real-world autonomous agents (e.g., robots) need to continuously adapt to changing conditions and acquire new skills throughout their operational lifespan. Traditional deep learning, optimized for static, i.i.d. data, fails catastrophically in such dynamic settings due to "catastrophic forgetting."
- **To propose a new, realistic, and computationally accessible benchmark for Continual Reinforcement Learning:** The authors observe a significant gap in the existing CL research landscape, particularly concerning RL. Previous benchmarks are either too simplistic, too computationally demanding, or lack the meaningful task relationships necessary to study transfer learning effectively. "Continual World" aims to fill this void by leveraging the `Meta-World` environment, known for its diverse robotic manipulation tasks, and adapting it for CL evaluation.
- **To advocate for a shift in research focus towards Forward Transfer:** The paper strongly argues that the CL community has overly concentrated on preventing "catastrophic forgetting." While important, this focus often comes at the expense of "forward transfer"—the ability of an agent to positively leverage previously acquired knowledge to accelerate learning on *new* tasks. The authors contend that understanding and optimizing this trade-off is crucial for developing truly effective continual learning agents. The benchmark is specifically designed to highlight and measure this aspect.
- **To identify and highlight unique algorithmic challenges in the RL setting for CL:** RL introduces complexities not present in supervised learning, such as exploration, varying reward scales, and policy-value function interactions (actor-critic frameworks). The paper aims to empirically demonstrate how existing CL methods, often developed in supervised contexts, struggle with these RL-specific nuances and to pinpoint areas for future algorithmic development (e.g., critic regularization, replay mechanisms).
- **To provide a standardized and open-source tool for the research community:** By releasing the benchmark code, along with implementations of seven representative CL methods, the authors aim to foster reproducibility, facilitate systematic comparisons, and accelerate progress in the field of continual reinforcement learning. They explicitly invite participation in the "Continual World Challenge."

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need to build more capable, adaptive AI agents for dynamic real-world applications, and it proposes a concrete, practical step—a new benchmark—to guide and accelerate research towards this goal by emphasizing often-overlooked aspects like forward transfer in RL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of "Continual World" centers on the design and empirical evaluation of a new benchmark for continual reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Design: Continual World

- **Foundation:** The benchmark is built on `Meta-World`, a suite of 50 distinct robotic manipulation tasks using a simulated Sawyer arm in the MuJoCo physics engine. `Meta-World` is chosen for its diversity, realistic robotic tasks, shared observation/action spaces, and established presence in multi-task and meta-RL research.
- **Task Selection and Sequences:** From the 50 `Meta-World` tasks, the authors selected 10 that were neither too easy nor too hard to solve within a `∆ = 1M` training steps budget. These 10 tasks form the core `CW10` sequence.
 - **CW10:** HAMMER-V1, PUSH-WALL-V1, FAUCET-CLOSE-V1, PUSH-BACK-V1, STICK-PULL-V1, HANDLE-PRESS-SIDE-V1, PUSH-V1, SHELF-PLACE-V1, WINDOW-CLOSE-V1, PEG-UNPLUG-SIDE-V1. The ordering of these tasks was deliberately chosen based on a "transfer matrix" (see below) to ensure a high variation of forward transfers, both globally and locally.
 - **CW20:** This is the `CW10` sequence repeated twice (20 tasks total), allowing for the study of how methods perform when revisiting previously learned tasks.
 - **Triplets:** Eight sequences of three tasks (A → B → C) are provided for faster iteration and to specifically test scenarios where an intervening task (B) might interfere with positive transfer from an initial task (A) to a final task (C).
- **Environment Details:**
 - **Observation Space:** A 12-dimensional vector comprising 3D Cartesian coordinates of the robot's gripper, manipulated objects, and goal.
 - **Action Space:** A 4-dimensional vector describing 3D end-effector movement and gripper actuation.
 - **Task Randomization:** Object positions are randomized at the start of each episode to encourage learning robust policies.
 - **Reward and Success Metrics:** Task-specific shaped rewards are used for training (inherited from Meta-World). For evaluation, a binary success metric based on the distance of the object to the goal is used, ensuring interpretability and comparability across tasks.

#### 3.2. Learning Algorithms and Network Architecture

- **Base RL Algorithm:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is chosen as the underlying RL algorithm. SAC is an off-policy, maximum entropy method suitable for continuous control domains, known for its exploration capabilities and robustness. Its off-policy nature (using a replay buffer) is particularly relevant for CL methods that rely on rehearsing old trajectories.
- **Network Architecture:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with 4 layers of 256 neurons, using Layer Normalization after the first layer and leaky ReLU activations.
- **Policy Heads:** A key design choice is the use of *separate policy heads* for each task. This assumes task identity is known during learning and evaluation, a common setup in CL. Ablation studies explore a single-head architecture with one-hot task ID input.
- **Training Protocol:**
 - Each task is trained for `∆ = 1M` steps.
 - The replay buffer is *emptied* when a new task starts (a critical design choice that is later analyzed).
 - An initial 10K steps are used for uniform exploration before switching to policy-based sampling. A 1K-step warm-up is also employed.
 - 50 optimization steps are performed for every 50 environment interactions.
 - Adam optimizer is used, with its internal statistics reset for each task.
 - The maximum entropy coefficient `α` in SAC is auto-tuned to maintain a target Gaussian policy standard deviation (`σ_t = 0.089`).
 - Hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, batch size, discount factor) for SAC are tuned in a first stage using fine-tuning, then method-specific hyperparameters are tuned based on final average performance.
 - Evaluations are conducted every 20K steps, averaging over 10 stochastic trajectories, using multiple seeds (typically 20) for statistical robustness, and bootstrap confidence intervals.
- **Computational Setup:** Experiments are designed to be computationally light, running on 8-core CPU machines without GPUs, taking approximately 100 hours for a CW20 run.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

The paper defines standard CL metrics, emphasizing forward transfer:

- **Average Performance (P(t)):** The average success rate across all tasks observed up to time `t`. The final value `P(T)` is the main objective for hyperparameter tuning.
- **Forward Transfer (FT):** Measures how much a method benefits from prior learning on a new task, compared to learning that task from scratch. Calculated as the normalized area between the method's training curve and a single-task baseline's training curve for task `i`. `FT_i = (AUC_i - AUC_b_i) / (1 - AUC_b_i)`. The average `FT` across all tasks is reported.
- **Forgetting (F):** Measures the decrease in performance on a previously learned task `i` after training on subsequent tasks, calculated as `F_i = p_i(i · ∆) - p_i(T)`. The average `F` across all tasks is reported.
- **Backward Transfer (BT):** (Not a primary metric but measured) Measures performance *gain* on old tasks due to learning new ones. Defined as `max{0, p_i(T) - p_i(i · ∆)}`. Found to be negligible in experiments.
- **Reference Transfer (RT):** An upper bound estimate for forward transfer. Calculated as the average of the maximum possible forward transfer from any previous task to the current task, based on the empirically derived "transfer matrix." `RT = (1/N) * sum_{i=2 to N} max_{j<i} FT(t_j, t_i)`. For CW20, `RT = 0.46`. This provides a target for optimal CL performance.

#### 3.4. Continual Learning Methods Evaluated

Seven representative CL methods are evaluated, covering three main families:

- **Regularization-based Methods:** Add penalty terms to the loss function to protect parameters important for previous tasks.
 - **L2:** Simple L2 regularization, treating all parameters equally important.
 - **Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC):** Uses Fisher Information Matrix to estimate parameter importance.
 - **Memory-Aware Synapses (MAS):** Estimates importance by measuring output sensitivity to weight perturbations.
 - **Variational Continual Learning (VCL):** Uses variational inference to maintain a posterior distribution over parameters. (Required significant adaptation for RL, including treating regularization strength as a hyperparameter and not using prior on the first task).
- **Parameter Isolation Methods:** Explicitly fix or "freeze" parameters important for old tasks.
 - **PackNet:** Iteratively prunes a network and freezes parts associated with prior tasks, leaving remaining capacity for new tasks. It "packs" multiple tasks into a single fixed-size network.
- **Replay Methods:** Store a subset of past experiences and replay them during training to mitigate forgetting.
 - **Perfect Memory:** A baseline where the replay buffer has unlimited capacity and stores all past experiences.
 - **Averaged Gradient Episodic Memory (A-GEM):** Projects gradients from new tasks to ensure they don't interfere with old tasks, using a limited episodic memory.

Crucially, for all methods except A-GEM and Perfect Memory, regularization is applied *only to the policy network (actor)*, while the *critic network is allowed to adapt freely*. This is identified as a significant factor for performance in RL-CL settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation on the `Continual World` benchmark reveals critical insights into the performance of existing CL methods in RL.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Forgetting

- **PackNet Leads:** PackNet consistently outperforms all other CL methods and even multi-task learning solutions in terms of average performance (success rate). On CW20, PackNet achieved 0.80 average performance, significantly higher than the next best, EWC (0.60).
- **Effective Forgetting Mitigation:** Most regularization-based methods (L2, EWC, MAS, VCL) and PackNet are highly effective at mitigating catastrophic forgetting, achieving forgetting rates close to zero (F ≈ 0.00-0.02). This indicates their ability to retain knowledge of previously learned tasks.
- **Catastrophic Forgetting for Baselines:** As expected, Fine-tuning (training on each task sequentially without CL mechanisms) exhibits high catastrophic forgetting (F = 0.73). Surprisingly, A-GEM also shows very high forgetting (F = 0.71), similar to fine-tuning, and Perfect Memory (unlimited replay) also struggles (F = 0.07), contrary to expectations from supervised learning.

#### 4.2. Forward Transfer: A Critical Gap

- **Positive vs. Negative Transfer:** A major finding is that while most CL methods effectively prevent forgetting, they often fail to achieve positive forward transfer.
 - Only Fine-tuning (0.20) and PackNet (0.19) achieve positive forward transfer on CW20.
 - Regularization-based methods (L2, MAS, VCL) exhibit *significant negative forward transfer* (e.g., L2: -0.71, MAS: -0.52, VCL: -0.49), meaning learning new tasks actively harms the efficiency of learning subsequent tasks. EWC has a small negative transfer (-0.17).
- **Gap to Reference Transfer (RT):** Even the best performing methods (Fine-tuning and PackNet) fall considerably short of the estimated optimal Reference Transfer (RT = 0.46), which represents the maximum possible transfer if knowledge from previous tasks was perfectly leveraged. This highlights a substantial area for improvement.
- **Deterioration on Revisited Tasks:** When tasks are revisited in the second half of the CW20 sequence, forward transfer generally drops compared to the first time these tasks were encountered. This suggests an inefficient reuse of learned representations, even if the tasks themselves are not forgotten.
- **Triplet Experiments Validate Interference:** The shorter "triplets" sequences confirm that an interfering task (B) between a source (A) and target (C) task can significantly reduce the potential forward transfer from A to C, even when CL methods are applied. Fine-tuning surprisingly often outperforms dedicated CL methods in these specific scenarios, further underscoring the struggle with positive transfer.

#### 4.3. Insights from Specific Methods

- **PackNet's Strengths:** Its robust performance and ability to largely prevent forgetting, while also achieving positive forward transfer (albeit below RT), makes it a standout. It demonstrates the potential of parameter isolation methods.
- **Replay Method Underperformance:** Perfect Memory and A-GEM, despite their effectiveness in supervised CL, significantly underperform in this RL setting. The authors conjecture this is partly due to the challenges of applying regularization to the critic network and the high variance of RL gradients impacting gradient projection methods.

#### 4.4. RL-Specific Algorithmic Challenges

The paper identifies key challenges at the intersection of RL and CL:

- **Critic Regularization:** A crucial empirical finding is that regularizing the critic network (e.g., with EWC) is detrimental to overall performance. Allowing the critic to freely adapt to the current task's value function, even if it means "forgetting" past value functions, is beneficial. This is a nuanced point as it means the critic might need to re-learn completely if a task is revisited.
- **Exploration and Replay Buffer Resets:** Emptying the replay buffer and using a uniform exploration policy at task boundaries negatively impacts "diagonal transfers" (i.e., learning the same task again). Maintaining the buffer or using the current policy for exploration significantly improves performance on revisited tasks. This indicates a distributional shift problem that off-policy RL algorithms like SAC struggle with during task switches.

#### 4.5. Resource Usage

Most evaluated CL methods introduce only modest computational and memory overheads compared to the fine-tuning baseline. PackNet, for instance, requires only about 15% more wall time and 50% more parameters (for storing masks). Reservoir Sampling has higher wall-time overhead, and VCL requires significantly more parameters due to maintaining distributions over weights. This suggests that the proposed CL methods are generally practical regarding resource demands.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Continual World" benchmark and its accompanying analysis carry significant implications for the future direction of continual learning and reinforcement learning research:

- **Standardization of CL in RL:** The benchmark provides a much-needed standardized, open-source platform for evaluating continual learning algorithms in realistic robotic reinforcement learning settings. This addresses the current fragmentation of evaluation methodologies and accelerates reproducible research.
- **Re-prioritizing Forward Transfer:** By explicitly designing tasks for positive transfer and rigorously measuring it, the work forces a re-evaluation of current CL method objectives. It highlights that the community's predominant focus on catastrophic forgetting has come at the expense of transfer, which is arguably more critical for truly intelligent, adaptive agents. This reframed emphasis is likely to steer future algorithmic development.
- **Identifying RL-Specific Challenges:** The paper pinpoints unique algorithmic hurdles that arise when integrating CL with RL, such as the detrimental effect of critic regularization, the limitations of replay methods (like A-GEM) in high-variance RL environments, and the impact of exploration strategies on transfer. These insights open new, concrete research avenues for developing CL algorithms specifically tailored for RL.
- **Guidance for Algorithmic Development:** The strong performance of PackNet suggests that parameter isolation methods, which directly manage network capacity allocation, might be a particularly promising direction for future CL research, especially if their limitations (e.g., needing to know sequence length, sensitivity to gradient clipping) can be overcome.
- **Bridging Research Fields:** The benchmark facilitates comparisons between continual learning, multi-task learning, and meta-learning, potentially revealing how insights from one field can benefit others. The finding that state-of-the-art CL methods (like PackNet) can outperform multi-task learning is particularly intriguing.
- **Stepping Towards General AI:** By tackling the problem of continuous adaptation in changing environments, this work contributes directly to the long-term goal of building general, adaptable artificial intelligence systems that can operate effectively over extended periods in unpredictable real-world scenarios.
- **Open Science Contribution:** The release of the open-source code for the benchmark and evaluated algorithms fosters transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative development within the research community, encouraging broad adoption and further innovation.

In summary, "Continual World" is a pivotal contribution that not only offers a robust tool for evaluating CL in RL but also provides a critical re-evaluation of research priorities, challenging the community to focus on positive knowledge transfer for building truly adaptive and intelligent agents.
