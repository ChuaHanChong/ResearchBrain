# TacClip: a clip-on sensor measures dynamic contact forces without covering the fingerpads

> [!summary] Summary
> TacClip is a wearable fingertip sensor that clips over the distal finger without covering the fingerpad, so the wearer keeps bare-skin touch. A Fiber Bragg Grating bonded to the clip's central strut converts contact-induced fingertip bulging into wavelength shifts, sampled at 2 kHz. Calibrated by an MLP, it estimates contact-force magnitude within 0.5 N over 0-8 N and resolves sliding vibrations, texture direction, and edges, including underwater.

## Problem

- Robot-learning demonstration interfaces capture vision and hand pose but omit when contact starts, how force is modulated, and what tactile events occur during sliding.
- Gloves, pressure pads, and fingertip devices place material between fingerpad and environment, attenuating the bare-skin cues a human demonstrator relies on for texture and friction.
- Exoskeletal and fixture-mounted force estimators are bulky and restrict dexterity, while electronic transduction fails in wet conditions and under electromagnetic interference.

## Method

- A 3D-printed clip in three inner diameters slides over the fingertip with no adhesive; lateral tissue bulging bends its central strut, straining a bonded single-mode ==FBG== into a wavelength shift.
- An ==MLP== maps the wavelength signal plus recent history to normal force and total force magnitude, with a brief ==per-user calibration== step absorbing individual tissue hysteresis.
- An optional dorsal polyimide (Kapton) strip carries four extra FBGs at 3 cm spacing, fused by ==confidence-gated neural fusion== with MediaPipe vision hand pose during occlusion.

## Results

- Learned force estimates track ATI ground truth with typical error below **0.5 N** across **0-8 N**; normalized RMSE across five users spans **0.041** to **0.070** after brief recalibration.
- Corduroy sliding perpendicular to ridges yields roughly **four times** larger vibration (RMS **0.0014 nm** vs **0.0005 nm**); tape-edge timing error falls to **0.005 s** and **1.0 mm** at **0.8 N** press.
- Vision-plus-FBG fusion cuts grasp-dimension standard deviation from **2.13 cm** to **0.83 cm** on a box; underwater spring slope matches air (**-0.0254** vs **-0.0241 nm/mm**) after temperature calibration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sensing around rather than under the contact patch preserves the human's own perceptual channel, a design principle for any demonstration hardware relying on operator tactile skill.
> - The human fingertip, not the transducer, sets the bandwidth ceiling: coherent response only holds to roughly 20-30 Hz because soft tissue low-passes vibration.
> - A single strain channel tracks total load magnitude, not normal force, since viscoelastic deformation conflates shear and normal components and adds hysteresis.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

TacClip addresses a persistent problem in tactile sensing for robotics and human motion capture: contact forces and dynamic touch events are important for manipulation, but many sensing systems interfere with the user’s ability to feel the environment naturally. Robot-learning systems increasingly use human demonstrations to learn manipulation strategies. Visual observations and hand trajectories provide information about object geometry and motion, but they often omit contact timing, force modulation, friction changes, slip, and vibration. These missing signals can be important for tasks such as grasping compliant objects, aligning fabric, finding edges, or controlling contact during insertion and palpation.

Existing approaches include tactile gloves, fingertip pressure sensors, exoskeletal force sensors, vision-based deformation estimation, and instrumented tools. Dense tactile gloves can measure contact distributions across the hand, while fingertip devices can provide more direct force or pressure measurements. However, these systems commonly cover the fingerpad with a sensor layer, glove material, or protective structure. This can modify friction, reduce sensitivity to fine texture and temperature, and make dexterous bare-finger exploration more difficult.

TacClip belongs to a related group of systems that infer contact from deformation elsewhere on the finger. It is particularly connected to fingernail-based force sensing, which estimates fingertip forces without obstructing the pad, and to Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) sensors used in robotic fingers and wearable hand-pose systems. Its mechanical distinction is that it does not require adhesive attachment to the nail or a sensing element on the contact surface. Instead, it uses a small removable clip positioned over the distal fingertip. The clip deforms when the fingertip expands or changes shape under load, and an embedded optical fiber measures that deformation.

The work also contributes to research on dynamic tactile sensing. Vibrations generated during sliding provide information about texture direction, roughness, friction, and edge transitions. TacClip is designed to record these signals while allowing the wearer to perform natural exploratory movements. The paper therefore occupies an intersection of wearable sensing, contact-rich robot learning, optical strain sensing, and human-centered tactile interfaces.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to develop a minimally encumbering fingertip sensor that measures contact-related deformation and dynamic vibrations while leaving the fingerpad completely exposed. The authors are motivated by situations in which the user’s own tactile perception is part of the sensing process. Examples include detecting a fabric ridge, locating the edge of transparent tape, palpating a compliant or vascular region, and interacting with wet surfaces.

A second objective is to determine whether deformation measured around the fingertip can provide useful estimates of contact force. TacClip is not intended to function as a conventional three-axis force transducer. The authors instead investigate whether its wavelength signal can estimate normal force and, more generally, the magnitude of the total contact load after calibration. This distinction is important because fingertip tissue is viscoelastic and because the clip responds to the combined mechanical behavior of the finger and sensor.

A third objective is to characterize the dynamic bandwidth of the finger–clip system. The optical interrogator samples at 2 kHz, but the effective bandwidth is expected to be limited by the mechanical filtering of soft fingertip tissue. The authors therefore evaluate how well the sensor captures vibrations at different frequencies.

The paper also explores two extensions. First, it tests whether the sensor can distinguish directional texture cues and detect small surface discontinuities. Second, it examines whether additional FBGs placed on a dorsal polyimide strip can supplement camera-based hand tracking when fingers are partially occluded. Finally, the authors assess whether the optical sensor remains useful underwater, where electronics and sealed wearable systems can be difficult to deploy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Mechanical and Optical Design

The fingertip unit is 3D printed from Rigid 4000 resin and slides over the distal fingertip without adhesive. Three nominal inner diameters—12, 13, and 14.4 mm—were fabricated for tested fingertip widths of approximately 13.5–17 mm. A snug fit is necessary because clip preload affects the FBG wavelength baseline and the force calibration. Removable high-friction silicone inserts can be added to improve retention without covering the pad.

A single-mode optical fiber containing an FBG is bonded to the clip’s central beam. The clip is designed so that lateral expansion of the fingertip bends the beam, producing strain in the fiber. The FBG converts strain into a shift in reflected Bragg wavelength according to the standard relation \(\lambda_B = 2n_{\mathrm{eff}}\Lambda\). Short-term contact signals are attributed mainly to mechanical strain, whereas temperature changes produce slower shifts that require compensation.

The optional hand-tracking configuration extends the same fiber along a thin Kapton strip on the back of the finger and hand. Four FBGs are placed at approximately 3 cm intervals. Their local strain measurements provide estimates of strip curvature and finger flexion. The authors emphasize that these measurements are intended to augment, rather than replace, full visual pose estimation.

#### 3.2. Force Calibration

For quasi-static testing, a wearer pressed and slid the TacClip-equipped finger against an ATI force/torque sensor. The ATI system measured the three force components, \(f_x\), \(f_y\), and \(f_z\), and served as ground truth. The TacClip wavelength signal and recent signal history were input to a multilayer perceptron. The model was trained to estimate both normal force and total force magnitude.

The use of signal history reflects the viscoelastic behavior of fingertip tissue. Loading and unloading can produce hysteresis and residual deformation, so an instantaneous wavelength-to-force mapping is insufficient. Cross-user generalization was evaluated across five users, with brief calibration used to account for individual tissue properties and preload differences.

#### 3.3. Dynamic Characterization

The optical interrogator sampled the sensor at 2 kHz. A muscle-lever system applied a swept-frequency, approximately constant-amplitude force to the fingerpad while the clip was worn. The chirp increased from near 0 to 100 Hz. The authors calculated a transfer function from applied force to wavelength shift using frequency-domain analysis and evaluated magnitude, phase, and magnitude-squared coherence.

#### 3.4. Application Experiments

The authors conducted four application studies. In the first, a finger slid over corduroy fabric either perpendicular or parallel to the ridges. High-pass filtering above 30 Hz was used to emphasize vibration while reducing the influence of baseline force.

In the second, a finger contacted a 78 μm transparent packing-tape edge mounted on a turntable. The turntable rotated at 45 RPM, producing a surface speed of 21.25 cm/s. Tests used nominal pressing forces of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.8 N. Edge events were detected through adaptive thresholding.

In the third, the sensor was tested during compression of a spring in air and underwater. A side-view camera measured spring length as a proxy for applied force. Wavelength responses were compared after temperature calibration.

In the fourth, the finger was moved across vascular and non-vascular regions of a tissue phantom. The objective was to compare dynamic signals associated with an embedded feature against smooth signals from a region without the feature.

For hand-pose augmentation, synchronized multi-camera motion capture and EasyMocap provided training ground truth. A neural network mapped dorsal FBG strain to joint flexion. During inference, a head-mounted GoPro and a visual pose system such as MediaPipe supplied the primary hand estimate. When visual confidence decreased, the system used FBG-based flexion estimates in a weighted fusion process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The quasi-static experiments showed a monotonic but nonlinear relationship between wavelength shift and contact loading. After supervised calibration, TacClip estimated force magnitude over a 0–8 N range with typical errors below 0.5 N. The single-FBG design responded more closely to total contact-force magnitude than to normal force alone. When shear was added while normal force remained approximately constant, the wavelength shift increased, indicating sensitivity to combined fingertip deformation.

Normalized wavelength-force curves were reasonably consistent across five users. Cross-user prediction produced normalized relative root-mean-square errors of approximately 0.041–0.070 after brief calibration. These results indicate that the sensor can be adapted across users, although calibration remains necessary.

The dynamic characterization showed relatively stable magnitude and phase response from near DC to approximately 20–30 Hz, with high coherence and limited phase lag. Above this range, coherence and transfer-function consistency declined, although measurable responses remained. The limiting factor was identified as the fingertip’s soft tissue, which mechanically attenuates higher-frequency vibrations before they reach the clip.

The corduroy experiment demonstrated directional texture sensitivity. Perpendicular sliding generated wavelength variations of approximately ±0.004 nm, compared with approximately ±0.001 nm during parallel sliding. The power spectral density also differed between the two directions. Thus, TacClip recorded dynamic patterns that corresponded to the fabric’s ridge orientation while leaving the wearer’s fingertip available for direct tactile perception.

For transparent tape detection, repeated signal peaks occurred as the finger crossed the tape boundary. Detection improved with increased pressing force because the signal-to-noise ratio increased. At 0.1 N, the mean timing error was +0.051 s, with a timing standard deviation of 0.340 s and a mean edge-location error of 10.7 mm. At 0.3 N, the timing error was −0.012 s, with a standard deviation of 0.104 s and a mean location error of −2.7 mm. At 0.8 N, the timing error was +0.005 s, with a standard deviation of 0.013 s and a mean location error of 1.0 mm. These results show that low-force detection is possible but less precise.

Underwater testing produced similar calibrated wavelength-versus-spring-length slopes in air and water: −0.0241 nm/mm in air and −0.0254 nm/mm underwater. This supports the feasibility of optical fingertip sensing in wet environments, provided temperature effects are compensated.

In the tissue-phantom experiment, sliding over the vascular region produced higher-frequency signal variations superimposed on the slower motion profile. Sliding over a non-vascular area produced smoother, primarily low-frequency changes. This result is an initial demonstration of dynamic palpation sensing rather than evidence of clinical diagnostic performance.

The vision–FBG fusion system reduced variation and improved agreement with known object dimensions during grasping. For cylinder, box, and card tests, the mean thumb–middle-finger distances changed from 4.69 to 4.32 cm, 10.29 to 9.11 cm, and 3.91 to 3.53 cm, respectively. Standard deviations decreased from 1.30 to 0.74 cm, 2.13 to 0.83 cm, and 1.06 to 0.53 cm. The results suggest that dorsal FBG measurements can compensate for some visual occlusion and tracking instability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TacClip’s main significance is architectural: it obtains contact-related signals without placing a material layer between the fingerpad and the environment. This makes it complementary to visual tracking, tactile gloves, force/torque sensors, and instrumented tools. Potential applications include collecting force-aware human demonstrations for robot learning, recognizing texture and edge events, manipulating fabrics, interacting with wet objects, and supplementing hand tracking during occluded grasps.

The device is mechanically simple, removable, and inexpensive at the sensor-head level. The reported prototype cost is approximately $20 per finger for the clip and FBG, excluding labor and the optical interrogator. FBGs also offer multiplexing, electromagnetic immunity, and inherent compatibility with wet environments.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. The current single-FBG configuration cannot independently recover the three force components or determine the contact location. Measurements depend on fingertip geometry, tissue stiffness, clip fit, preload, temperature, and contact history. High-frequency signals are attenuated by the fingertip, and the optical interrogator remains more expensive than conventional electronic strain-measurement hardware. The surgical-palpation results use a phantom, and the paper does not yet demonstrate improvements in a complete robot imitation-learning or control pipeline. Further work is therefore needed on standardized calibration, multi-axis sensing, retention, user variability, clinical validation, and downstream robotic performance.
