# Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Robot Learning: A Dexterous Manipulation Study on Reactive Catching

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel learning framework called Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS) was developed to enable robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for dexterous manipulation. This approach explicitly models and propagates uncertainty in simulation, achieving a 68% success rate in challenging flat-plate reactive catching on a real robot without any real-world fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Bridging the "sim-to-real" gap for dexterous manipulation is difficult due to physics-intensive contact interactions and high sensitivity to modeling errors and perception noise.
- Conventional domain randomization, which perturbs only one parameter instance per episode, is insufficient for robust learning as it does not allow policies to explicitly account for how uncertainties affect multiple possible manipulation outcomes.
- Reactive catching with a flat, non-stabilizing surface presents an extremely challenging problem, requiring precise coordination and force modulation under uncertainty, making zero-shot sim-to-real transfer difficult.

## Method

- Introduced **Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS)**, a learning paradigm that simultaneously represents and propagates a set of 'N' randomized instances with independently sampled physical parameters, all evolving under a shared robot action.
- Developed a policy learning pipeline consisting of a pre-trained ==point cloud-based Autoencoder== for encoding the DRIS state into a latent vector, and a ==FiLM-conditioned policy network== trained with ==PPO== using the average reward across all instances.
- Instantiated the problem for a ==flat-plate reactive catching== task, defining ball states, plate actions, and a reward function, and implemented a ==joint-space torque controller== for ==zero-shot real-world deployment==.

## Results

- DRIS policies achieved a **68%** success rate on real-world flat-plate reactive catching in zero-shot transfer, outperforming a hand-crafted baseline (**5%**) and an end-to-end sim-trained baseline (**13%**).
- Simulation experiments demonstrated that DRIS policies significantly improved robustness against observation noise, execution errors, and showed better generalization to out-of-distribution physical parameters compared to baselines.
- The DRIS approach showed computational efficiency, achieving superior robustness with considerably less VRAM usage compared to brute-force massively parallelized single-instance randomization, and also exhibited emergent generalizability to human-thrown and irregularly shaped objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling and propagating uncertainty through a set of randomized instances (DRIS) allows policies to learn actions that inherently account for a distribution of possible outcomes, leading to significantly more robust learning than conventional single-instance randomization.
> - DRIS acts as an exact particle approximation for system belief propagation, which theoretically reduces gradient variance and promotes implicit robustness by exposing the policy to a more comprehensive representation of dynamic variability.
> - Training a policy on a highly sensitive, flat-plate reactive catching task, without mechanical stabilization, serves as a strong testbed for evaluating the robustness and generalizability of sim-to-real transfer methods for dexterous manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a central challenge in robotics: bridging the "sim-to-real" gap, particularly for dexterous manipulation tasks. Dexterous manipulation, involving coordinated and intentional contact interactions (e.g., in-hand manipulation, tool use, catching), is characterized by its physics-intensive nature and high sensitivity to modeling errors and perception noise. This sensitivity often makes direct transfer of policies learned in simulation to real-world robots difficult.

Traditional model-based frameworks struggle with the need for precisely identified system parameters like contact stiffness, friction coefficients, and object geometries, which are often uncertain or variable in real-world scenarios. Learning-based methods, particularly those employing Reinforcement Learning (RL), have emerged as an alternative, capable of learning policies directly from data. However, these methods are data-intensive and typically rely on extensive simulation or expert demonstrations.

The standard approach to enhance robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer in RL is Domain Randomization (DR). Conventional DR perturbs physical parameters by sampling a single parameter instance per episode. While this exposes the policy to diverse experiences, it offers limited exposure to the full variability of real-world dynamics and lacks a structured mechanism for the policy to explicitly account for how uncertainties might affect multiple possible manipulation outcomes. Recent advancements in DR include active DR, which adapts sampling distributions using real-world data, and methods that strategically scale randomization or use entropy maximization to enable zero-shot transfer.

Beyond DR, other techniques for sim-to-real transfer include using high-fidelity simulators, adaptive system identification, and hybrid dynamics learning. The representation of uncertainty in robot states and dynamics is also a related field, with classical methods using belief-space planning (e.g., POMDPs) and learning-based approaches employing probabilistic latent dynamics, history-based parameter inference, or ensembles for model-based RL.

Regarding the specific task of robot catching, prior research has often relied on mechanically assisting end-effectors such such as cups, nets, or articulated hands that provide passive stabilization. Catching with a flat, non-stabilizing surface, which is the focus of this work, presents a significantly more challenging problem due to its high sensitivity to precise contact timing, plate orientation, and force modulation under uncertainty.

This research contributes to the landscape by proposing a novel DR method, Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS), that aims to explicitly model and propagate uncertainty more comprehensively than conventional DR. It seeks to achieve robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for highly dynamic and contact-rich tasks like flat-plate reactive catching, without requiring real-world fine-tuning or relying on mechanically stabilizing end-effectors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are threefold:

1. **Develop a novel robust learning strategy for dynamic manipulation under uncertainty:** The core motivation stems from the inherent difficulty of dexterous manipulation tasks, which are sensitive to discrepancies between simulation and reality, particularly concerning contact dynamics and physical properties. Conventional domain randomization, which randomizes only one instance per episode, is identified as insufficient for robust learning, as it does not enable policies to explicitly reason about how uncertainties affect multiple possible manipulation outcomes. The goal is to propose a method that provides a richer approximation of uncertain dynamics.

2. **Formulate and analyze the Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS) paradigm:** The researchers aim to introduce DRIS as a representation that captures the uncertainty in system dynamics by simultaneously representing and propagating a set of randomized instances. The motivation is that by training a policy on the joint evolution of multiple parallel instances, each with different randomized physical properties, the policy can learn actions that account for a distribution of possible outcomes. This explicit modeling of uncertainty is hypothesized to lead to more stable policy learning, greater robustness, and improved zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. A theoretical analysis is provided to support these claims.

3. **Validate the DRIS framework on a challenging reactive catching task with a flat plate:** The motivation for choosing this specific task is its extreme sensitivity to physical uncertainties and perception errors. Unlike traditional catching setups that use mechanically stabilizing end-effectors (e.g., curved surfaces or enclosing grippers), this work employs a flat, low-friction plate. This design amplifies the difficulty, requiring precise coordination of contact timing and force modulation without passive mechanical aid, thereby serving as a strong testbed for the robustness capabilities of the proposed DRIS approach. The objective is to demonstrate that policies learned with DRIS can achieve reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on such a demanding task.

In summary, the research is motivated by the limitations of existing sim-to-real transfer techniques for highly sensitive dexterous manipulation, and it aims to address these by introducing a novel uncertainty-aware learning framework (DRIS) and validating its effectiveness on a task that pushes the boundaries of robot dexterity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers around the proposed Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS) for policy learning in simulation, followed by zero-shot transfer to a real robot.

**A. Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS)**
The core idea is to move beyond conventional domain randomization (one randomized instance per episode) by representing and propagating a *set* of `N` randomized instances simultaneously. Each instance `c^(i)` in the set `ˆC = {c^(1)..., c^(N)}` has independently sampled physical parameters (e.g., ball radius, friction, restitution coefficients) from a domain space `C`. The DRIS, `D_t = { (s^(i)_t, c^(i)) }_i=1^N`, evolves under a *shared* robot action `a_t`. The dynamics of the entire instance set `D_t+1 = F(D_t, a_t)` are evaluated in parallel, and their collective state evolutions inform the policy update. This approach is theoretically supported as an exact particle approximation for system belief propagation, aiming for reduced gradient variance and improved robustness.

**B. Policy Learning Pipeline**
The learning process consists of two main parts:

1. **DRIS Encoder Pre-training (Step 1):**
 - **DRIS State Representation:** The DRIS state `S_t` is a collection of `N` individual ball states, `S_t = {s^(1)_t..., s^(N)_t}`, where each `s^(i)_t` is a 6D vector (displacement and velocity relative to the plate).
 - **Encoder Architecture:** A point cloud-based Autoencoder (AE) is used to map the variable-size set `S_t` into a fixed-dimensional latent vector `z_t ∈ R^(d_z)`. The encoder comprises 1D convolutional layers followed by a feature-wise max-pooling layer, similar to PointNet. An MLP-based decoder reconstructs the original set from `z_t`.
 - **Training:** The AE is pre-trained by collecting a dataset of DRIS samples via random robot actions. The reconstruction loss is the Chamfer distance between the input `S_t` and the reconstructed set `ˆS`.
 - **Output:** The trained encoder `ψ(·)` is then frozen and used to generate `z_t` for policy learning.

2. **FiLM-Conditioned Policy Network Learning (Step 2):**
 - **Policy Architecture:** The policy `π_θ` consists of a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) module and an MLP-based action generation network.
 - **FiLM Conditioning:** The encoded latent feature `z_t` is modulated by the observed plate tilting orientation `u_t` using `~z_t = FiLM(z_t, u_t) = λ(u_t) ⊙ z_t + μ(u_t)`. `λ(u_t)` and `μ(u_t)` are generated by small neural networks, applying scaling and bias coefficients. This allows the policy to adapt to how plate orientation influences gravity and contact forces.
 - **Action Generation:** The modulated feature `~z_t` is passed to an MLP to produce the action `a_t = MLP(~z_t)`.
 - **Reinforcement Learning:** The policy `π_θ` is trained using a policy gradient algorithm, specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), in simulation. The reward signal for training is the average reward across all `N` instances in the DRIS: `(1/N) * Σ_(s∈S_t) r(s, a_t)`.

**C. Reactive Catching Problem Instantiation**

- **Motion Frame:** A fixed motion frame is defined relative to the incoming ball at the start of manipulation. Its origin is at the plate's initial position, Z-axis vertically upward, and X-Z plane passes through the ball's center. This ensures invariance to the ball's incoming direction.
- **State (`s_t`):** Comprises the ball's 3D displacement `d_t` relative to the plate and its 3D linear velocity `v_t`, both expressed in the motion frame (6D total).
- **Action (`a_t`):** Consists of a 3D displacement command `δ_t` for the plate's center and a 2D plate tilting configuration `u_t = (α_t, β_t)`. `α_t` defines a horizontal rotation axis, and `β_t` is the tilt angle.
- **Reward Function:** The reward `r_t` combines a velocity term `r_v` (higher for lower ball velocity, decaying exponentially) and a constant penalty `r_p` if the ball is beneath or outside the plate. It uses components of displacement and velocity perpendicular (`d_⊥, v_⊥`) and parallel (`d_∥, v_∥`) to the plate's surface.

**D. Control Implementation on High-DoF Manipulator**
For real-world deployment, the learned action `a_t` is converted into a desired plate pose (orientation `^e R_t` and position `~p_e_t`) in the robot's base frame using Rodrigues' formula. This desired pose is then converted into a desired joint configuration `~q_t` via inverse kinematics. A joint-space torque controller tracks `~q_t` using feedback from observed joint angles and velocities, along with Coriolis and gravity compensation.

**E. Zero-Shot Inference in Real World**
When deployed on the real robot, the system observes a single real-world ball state `s`. This single state is treated as a DRIS of size `N=1` and is encoded by the pre-trained `ψ` to produce `z`. The trained policy `π_θ(z)` then generates the corresponding action `a`. This process is repeated without any real-world data collection or fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both simulation and real-world experimental results to validate the DRIS approach.

**A. Simulation Evaluation**

1. **Effect of DRIS Size:** Policies trained with DRIS, even with a modest number of instances (e.g., N=10), demonstrated substantial performance improvements over conventional Domain Randomization (DRIS with N=1) and an end-to-end (E2E) baseline (which directly maps single-ball state to action). As the DRIS size increased (N=50, N=200), robustness generally improved.

2. **Robustness to Observation Noise:** Under varying levels of injected Gaussian noise in the observed ball states, the E2E baseline and DRIS(1) showed significant performance degradation and were prone to overfitting. In contrast, DRIS policies trained with N ≥ 10 exhibited substantially improved robustness, maintaining higher rewards and success rates even under high noise levels (up to 4σ).

3. **Robustness to Execution Error:** When noise was added to desired joint positions to simulate robot execution errors, the E2E baseline was notably less robust than policies trained with DRIS. Increasing DRIS size generally enhanced robustness, with a clear performance boost observed starting from N=10.

4. **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Physics:** Policies were tested on balls with higher restitution coefficients (outside the training range). Policies trained with larger DRIS sizes showed better generalization capabilities to these unseen physical properties compared to the E2E baseline and DRIS(1), achieving higher rewards and success rates.

5. **Scalability and Computational Efficiency:** A comparison with an E2E baseline using massive parallelization (matching total ball interactions) indicated that DRIS provides superior robustness with considerably less VRAM usage. For instance, DRIS(50) achieved a 0.89 success rate with 0.71 GB VRAM, while a massively parallelized E2E achieved 0.73 success with 2 GB VRAM.

**B. Real-Robot Deployment (Zero-Shot Transfer)**

1. **System Setup:** Experiments were conducted on a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 robot manipulator with a 3D-printed flat plate covered in neoprene foam. A ramp system with interchangeable sections was used to release four different types of balls (wiffle, rubber, ping-pong, foam) with varying initial speeds, ensuring consistent initial states for comparative evaluation.

2. **Quantitative Evaluation:**
 - A single DRIS-trained policy (N=200) was deployed zero-shot, without any real-world fine-tuning.
 - **VelTrack Baseline (hand-crafted):** Achieved a 0.05 success rate (3/60 trials), often failing due to insufficient reactivity and inaccurate ball velocity estimates.
 - **E2E Baseline (sim-trained):** Achieved a 0.13 success rate (8/60 trials), showing sensitivity to observation noise and leading to jerky robot movements.
 - **DRIS-trained Policy (Ours):** Achieved a 0.68 success rate (41/60 trials). It exhibited high robustness and successfully caught all four types of balls tested under various ramp release conditions. Failures typically occurred when initial contact was insufficient to dissipate momentum or when tracking latency/noise caused unfavorable redirection.

3. **Qualitative Evaluation:**
 - The DRIS-trained policy successfully caught balls thrown by humans, demonstrating robustness to increased uncertainty and inconsistency.
 - It generalized directly to a pure balancing task, despite being trained for reactive catching.
 - The policy was also able to catch irregularly shaped objects, including toy apples, strawberries, and wooden cubes, though the cube was caught only occasionally due to its unpredictable impact dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and sim-to-real transfer, particularly for dexterous manipulation tasks characterized by high sensitivity and physical uncertainty.

The introduction of **Domain-Randomized Instance Set (DRIS)** as a learning paradigm offers a more robust and efficient alternative to conventional domain randomization. By explicitly modeling and propagating multiple randomized instances, DRIS enables policies to learn actions that inherently account for a distribution of possible outcomes. This moves beyond simply exposing the policy to varied experiences, allowing it to "reason" about uncertainty, which is crucial for tasks where small modeling errors can lead to large deviations in behavior.

The **theoretical analysis** supporting DRIS, demonstrating its role as an exact particle approximation for system belief propagation, reduced gradient variance, and implicit promotion of robustness, provides a foundational understanding of its effectiveness.

The most prominent impact is the demonstration of **reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer** for a highly challenging reactive catching task with a flat, non-stabilizing plate. This is a substantial achievement, as previous efforts often relied on mechanically assistive end-effectors or extensive real-world fine-tuning. The ability to directly deploy a policy trained purely in simulation onto a physical robot for such a dynamic, contact-rich task indicates a reduced sim-to-real gap and improved generalizability.

The findings related to **improved robustness** against observation noise, execution errors, and out-of-distribution physical parameters highlight DRIS's practical utility. This robustness is critical for real-world deployment where perfect sensing and execution are not guaranteed, and object properties may vary. Furthermore, the demonstrated **computational efficiency** of DRIS compared to brute-force parallelization suggests that it offers a more scalable approach for robust learning.

The **qualitative results**, including catching human-thrown objects, performing balancing tasks, and manipulating irregularly shaped objects, underscore the policy's emergent generalizability beyond its specific training objective. This suggests that the learned policy captures fundamental principles of reactive contact manipulation.

In terms of potential impact, this work could facilitate the development and deployment of autonomous robots in complex, unstructured environments where objects are varied and dynamics are uncertain. It could lead to more capable and adaptable robotic systems for applications in manufacturing, logistics, service robotics, and even assistive technologies. The DRIS framework provides a methodology that could be adapted to other challenging dexterous manipulation problems, contributing to the broader goal of making robots more intelligent and versatile in interacting with the physical world.
