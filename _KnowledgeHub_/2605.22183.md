# Action with Visual Primitives

> [!summary] Summary
> Action with Visual Primitives (AVP) introduces an end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architecture that uses explicit visual primitives as an interface, decoupling high-level reasoning from low-level motor control. This design significantly improves generalization and success rates across various real-world robotic manipulation tasks, including challenging spatial-compositional and cross-domain scenarios.

## Problem

- Existing VLA models implicitly couple cognitive, perceptual, and motor control capabilities, forcing the action expert to relearn spatial understanding already present in pre-trained VLMs.
- Current VLA policies exhibit weak generalization to new objects, task layouts, or environments due to the entanglement of transferable motor skills with task-specific visual context.
- Prior intermediate representations, like planning-centric or visual-prompting methods, suffer from either a lack of spatial precision or reliance on external, cascaded modules that introduce latency and error propagation.

## Method

- **AVP** proposes an end-to-end VLA architecture with an explicit visual-primitive-centric interface where the VLM predicts discretized visual primitives (e.g., 2D spatial anchors) representing next-stage subtasks.
- The action expert fuses these projected visual-primitive tokens with multimodal context to predict robot actions, allowing it to focus solely on motion execution, offloading spatial reasoning to the VLM.
- Ground-truth visual primitive supervision is automatically generated from end-effector kinematics (keyframe extraction, 3D pose retrieval, 2D image-plane projection) for robust and physically grounded training.

## Results

- AVP achieved **90.28%** success on Chinese chess, **88.19%** on domino placement, and **86.18%** on general object pick-and-place, consistently outperforming baselines like π₀.₅ by up to **21.22%** (general pick-and-place).
- Demonstrated strong spatial-compositional generalization with an **83%** success rate on unseen direct board-to-board transitions, where the π₀.₅ baseline failed completely (**0%** success).
- Exhibited robust zero-shot generalization to unseen, out-of-domain objects and varying background conditions, maintaining high success rates (e.g., **7/8** or **8/8** in cross-domain tests) where baselines degraded significantly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the VLM's high-level spatial reasoning from the action expert's motor execution through explicit visual primitives enhances learning efficiency and generalization capabilities.
> - Action-centric visual primitive supervision, derived directly from robot kinematics, provides a reliable and scalable method for spatial grounding without manual annotation or external perception models.
> - The explicit visual primitive interface implicitly encodes world-model-like predictions of future states, enabling better compositional generalization and robustness to visual domain shifts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robots capable of performing diverse manipulation tasks in unstructured environments represents a significant objective within the field of embodied artificial intelligence. Recent advancements have seen the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which fine-tune Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on extensive robotic datasets to integrate language understanding, visual perception, and action generation within a unified framework. Pioneering works such as RT-2, OpenVLA, and π
0.5
have demonstrated the efficacy of this paradigm across various complex multi-task scenarios.

However, a recurring challenge in current VLA architectures is the insufficient leverage of the semantic and spatial reasoning capabilities inherent in pretrained VLMs. Existing designs often map raw VLM features directly to motor commands, thereby coupling perception, reasoning, and control within a single learning objective. This coupling can necessitate the action expert to implicitly re-acquire cognitive and perceptual capacities already present in the VLM, potentially limiting learning efficiency and generalization, particularly when confronting variations in objects, layouts, or environments during testing.

Prior efforts to address this issue have explored several directions:
1. **Planning-centric approaches** (e.g., π
 0.5
 ): These methods decompose instructions into subtask descriptions, enabling the action expert to concentrate on atomic motor skills. While this can enhance compositional generalization, the purely linguistic interface may not adequately convey fine-grained spatial distinctions, especially in visually complex scenes or among geometrically similar entities.
2. **Subgoal image or predicted future frame methods** (e.g., π
 0.7, world-action models): These approaches provide visual targets to the action expert through subgoal images or predicted future frames. Although spatially informative, dense pixel predictions still require the action expert to infer the task-relevant elements within the scene.
3. **Visual-prompt methods** (e.g., Point-VLA, VP-VLA, TraceVLA): These techniques translate VLM outputs into spatial markers (such as points or bounding boxes) overlaid on the input image, offering direct spatial grounding. However, these methods typically depend on external models for prompt generation, resulting in multi-stage pipelines that can introduce inference latency and propagate errors between stages.

This work, AVP (Action with Visual Primitives), positions itself within this landscape by proposing an explicit, internally generated visual-primitive interface to address the limitations of existing VLA models. It aims to establish a clear division of responsibilities between the VLM and the action expert, where the VLM handles "what to do and where," and the action expert focuses on "how to execute," thereby seeking to improve learning efficiency and generalization without the drawbacks of external dependencies or fragmented pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized limitations of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in fully leveraging the advanced semantic and spatial reasoning capabilities of pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs). The authors identify a core problem: existing VLA architectures typically entangle instruction comprehension, spatial scene understanding, and motor control within a single, monolithic learning objective. This integration necessitates the action expert component to implicitly re-learn perceptual and cognitive skills already acquired by the VLM through extensive pre-training. Such redundancy can lead to several performance impediments:
- **Limited Learning Efficiency:** The action expert is burdened with processing complex multimodal inputs that contain information redundant to the VLM's existing knowledge, slowing down the learning process for motor control.
- **Weak Generalization:** When confronted with novel objects, varied scene layouts, or altered environments during deployment, the tightly coupled learning objective makes it challenging for the action expert to disentangle transferable motor skills from task-specific visual context. This can result in a diminished ability to generalize to new scenarios.
- **Inadequate Spatial Precision:** Purely linguistic interfaces in some VLA approaches may lack the fidelity to convey fine-grained spatial distinctions required for precise manipulation in visually dense or geometrically challenging environments. Meanwhile, pixel-level visual targets still leave a significant interpretative burden on the action expert.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this research are formulated as follows:
1. **Revisit and Redefine Responsibilities:** To fundamentally rethink the partition of responsibilities between the VLM and the action expert, establishing a more explicit and efficient division. The VLM should be responsible for reasoning "what to do and where," while the action expert should concentrate solely on "how to execute" the derived instructions.
2. **Introduce an Explicit Communication Medium:** To propose and implement visual primitives as an explicit, spatially grounded communication interface between the VLM and the action expert. This aims to replace the implicit, unstructured boundaries prevalent in current VLA designs, thereby facilitating clearer information transfer.
3. **Develop an End-to-End Architecture:** To design and realize AVP (Action with Visual Primitives) as an end-to-end VLA architecture that integrates this visual-primitive-centric interface. This design seeks to internalize spatial grounding capabilities directly within the VLA policy, circumventing the need for external perception modules and their associated issues of inference latency and error propagation.
4. **Automate Primitive Supervision:** To develop a supervision mechanism for visual primitives that is derived directly from end-effector kinematics. This approach aims to eliminate the requirement for manual spatial annotation or reliance on external visual prompt generators, thereby enhancing scalability and reducing annotation costs.
5. **Demonstrate Performance and Generalization:** To empirically validate that AVP improves manipulation success rates, data efficiency, spatial-compositional generalization, and object-level transfer capabilities across various real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to advance the state of generalist robotic manipulation by enabling more efficient learning and robust generalization in complex, open-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The AVP (Action with Visual Primitives) framework is designed as an end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architecture that employs visual primitives as an explicit interface between high-level understanding and low-level execution. This methodology aims to decouple the spatial reasoning tasks from the motor control tasks within a single, integrated model.

#### 3.1. AVP Architecture Overview

The AVP architecture comprises three primary components:
1. **Pretrained Vision-Language Model (VLM):** This module receives a language instruction and multi-view visual observations of the robot's workspace. Its role is to parse the current subtask and generate multimodal context tokens.
2. **Visual-Primitive Decoder:** Operating autoregressively, this decoder takes the multimodal context tokens from the VLM as input. It predicts a discretized visual primitive, which represents the next-stage target and its associated spatial information. This prediction constitutes the explicit condition for subsequent action generation.
3. **Flow-Matching Action Expert:** This module is responsible for producing robot actions. It receives an augmented multimodal representation, which is created by fusing the predicted visual primitive with the original multimodal tokens, along with the robot's proprioceptive state. The action expert uses a flow-matching approach for action prediction.

The workflow proceeds as follows: Given a language instruction ($l$) and observations ($o_t$), the VLM generates multimodal context tokens. The visual-primitive decoder then predicts a visual primitive ($p_t = D_{\psi}(\text{VLM}(o_t, l))$). This predicted primitive is subsequently projected into the visual token space ($z_{vp_t} = \text{Proj}(p_t, o_t)$) to make it directly consumable by the action expert. These projected visual-primitive tokens are fused with the original multimodal tokens to form an augmented multimodal representation ($z_{aug_t}$). Finally, the action expert uses $z_{aug_t}$ and the proprioceptive state ($s_t$) to predict a sequence of actions ($a_{t:t+h} = \pi_{\theta}(z_{aug_t}, s_t)$). This design establishes visual primitives as a clear communication channel, allowing the VLM to focus on "what and where" to act, while the action expert concentrates on "how to execute."

#### 3.2. Action-Centric Visual-Primitive Supervision

A critical aspect of AVP is its method for generating supervision for visual primitives. Unlike methods relying on manual annotation or external perception models, AVP derives primitive labels directly from end-effector kinematics, ensuring consistency with robot interaction and minimizing annotation overhead. The supervision construction involves three steps:
1. **Kinematic Keyframe Extraction:** Critical interaction keyframes are identified by detecting significant transitions in the gripper state (e.g., when the gripper significantly opens or closes, indicated by $| \Delta g_t | > \delta$). These keyframes correspond to physical interaction events such as grasping or releasing an object.
2. **End-effector Pose Acquisition:** For each identified keyframe, the corresponding 3D end-effector position ($P_t = [X_t, Y_t, Z_t]^T$) is acquired from the robot's proprioceptive sensors. This provides a physically grounded estimate of the interaction location in the robot's base coordinate frame.
3. **Image-Plane Projection and Discretization:** The 3D end-effector position is then projected into the image plane of each camera view to obtain a 2D spatial anchor ($m_t = (u_t, v_t)$). This projection utilizes standard camera intrinsic ($K$) and extrinsic ($T_C^R$) matrices. The resulting 2D point is subsequently discretized into visual-primitive labels ($y_{vp_t}$), which serve as ground-truth targets for training the visual-primitive decoder.

#### 3.3. Training Objective

The AVP model is trained with a combined loss function:
- **Visual-Primitive Prediction Loss ($L_{vp}$):** A cross-entropy loss ($L_{CE}$) is used to supervise the visual-primitive decoder, comparing the predicted primitive ($p_t$) with the ground-truth primitive ($y_{vp_t}$).
- **Action Prediction Objective ($L_{act}$):** This is a standard loss function for action prediction, applied to the action expert.
The overall training objective is a weighted sum of these two losses: $L = L_{act} + \lambda L_{vp}$, where $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter balancing the learning of action and primitive supervision.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform:** Experiments were conducted on an AgileX Piper tabletop platform featuring two 6-DoF manipulators with parallel grippers, forming a dual-arm system with a unified 14-dimensional action space.
- **Vision System:** Each arm was equipped with a RealSense D435i wrist camera, and an additional Hikvision camera was positioned overhead. All cameras provided synchronized RGB streams at 640 × 480 pixels resolution at 30 fps.
- **Tasks:** The evaluation included three real-world manipulation tasks:
 1. **Chinese chess manipulation:** A benchmark for precise manipulation in a structured, visually ambiguous, and densely arranged environment.
 2. **Domino placement:** Requires high-precision bimanual manipulation with strict orientation constraints.
 3. **General object pick-and-place:** Assesses generalization across objects with diverse visual appearances and geometric shapes.
- **Data and Training:** Policies were trained using real-world expert demonstrations (e.g., 11.2 hours for Chinese chess). Training was typically structured in two stages within the same total step count: initial optimization of the visual-primitive decoder, followed by joint training of the full model.
- **Baselines:** AVP was compared against several methods, including π
 0.5
 (an end-to-end VLA baseline), π
 0, Point-VLA (a cascaded visual-prompting method adapted with Kimi-K2.5), DM0, and LDA (world-model based approaches). Baselines were reproduced under similar robot embodiment, training data, and evaluation protocols where applicable.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across multiple real-world robotic manipulation tasks demonstrated the performance and generalization capabilities of the AVP framework.

#### 4.1. Main Evaluation on Manipulation Tasks

**Experiment I: Chinese Chess Manipulation**
This task involved precise manipulation of densely arranged and visually similar chess pieces on a 9 × 10 intersection grid. AVP achieved a 90.28% average success rate over 72 non-capture moves, representing an absolute gain of 37.04% compared to the π
0.5
baseline (53.24%). The most notable improvement was observed in placement success, with AVP achieving 81.94% versus π
0.5
's 20.83% (+61.11%).
Other baselines, including the cascaded visual-prompting method Point-VLA (Kimi), performed lower (48.15% average success) and exhibited significantly higher inference latency (37.32 s per step). World-model baselines like DM0 (45.37%) and LDA (49.07%) also demonstrated lower success rates, indicating that pixel-level future-state prediction provided limited guidance for the task's dense spatial reasoning requirements. AVP, in contrast, maintained a competitive inference latency of 0.27 s per step.

**Experiment II: Domino Placement**
This task required placing a domino with specific positional and orientational accuracy. AVP improved both pick (100.00% vs. 87.50% for π
0.5
) and orientation success rates (100.00% vs. 93.75% for π
0.5
), while maintaining comparable placement accuracy (64.58%). Overall, AVP achieved an 88.19% average success rate, a 6.25% improvement over π
0.5
(81.94%), and exhibited more stable manipulation execution.

**Experiment III: General Object Pick-and-Place**
Evaluating manipulation generalization across diverse objects, AVP showed substantial improvements. It achieved an 86.18% average success rate, compared to π
0.5
's 64.96%. Specifically, AVP improved pick success by 18.45% (90.24% vs. 71.79%) and placement success by 45.21% (68.29% vs. 23.08%) while maintaining perfect instruction following accuracy.

Across all three tasks, the consistent improvements observed suggest that the visual-primitive interface provides a spatially grounded means of communication between the VLM and the action expert, enabling the latter to more efficiently learn executable motion patterns by reducing the burden of joint localization and action generation.

#### 4.2. Generalization and Ablation Studies

**Spatial-Compositional Generalization:**
AVP was evaluated on unseen "Direct" board-to-board transitions (A → B) after being trained exclusively on "Indirect" transitions involving an intermediate waypoint (A → C → B). The π
0.5
baseline showed poor performance, often failing to execute valid direct transitions (20.67% average success). AVP, conversely, demonstrated strong spatial-compositional generalization, achieving an 83.33% average success rate, suggesting that explicit visual primitives allow the action expert to reuse learned motions in novel spatial configurations.

**Cross-Domain Generalization:**
AVP's ability to generalize zero-shot to unseen objects under different background conditions and execution modes (Direct vs. Indirect) was assessed. AVP maintained high success rates across these variations (e.g., 8/8 for Chessboard Direct, 7/8 for White cloth Direct). In contrast, the π
0.5
baseline degraded significantly under distribution shifts, particularly in the "Direct" setting (0/8 for both Chessboard and White cloth Direct). These results indicate that visual primitives aid in decoupling motion execution from incidental visual factors, allowing for more robust generalization.

**Visual Primitives Ablation:**
An ablation study on the Chinese chess task examined the impact of different visual primitive components:
- **Raw (no primitives):** 78% average success.
- **Box-based spatial highlighting:** Improved performance to 83% average success.
- **Background masking (Box + Mask):** Provided additional gains by suppressing irrelevant visual context, reaching 85% average success.
- **Memory-enhanced primitives (Box + Mask + Mem.):** Achieved the best overall performance at 91% average success, indicating that retaining primitives from previous subtasks enhances cross-step spatial context.
Further analysis showed that `point` primitives (86% avg) and `box-mask` primitives (86% avg) achieved strong performance, while higher mask opacity (e.g., α=0.9) improved placement accuracy in box-mask prompts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AVP framework presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly within the context of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, by introducing an explicit visual-primitive interface. This work revisits and proposes a structured division of responsibilities between the Vision-Language Model (VLM) and the action expert, which has several implications for advancing generalist robotics.

**Key Contributions and Technical Advancement:**
- **Redefined VLM-Action Expert Interface:** The core significance lies in conceptualizing and implementing visual primitives as a direct, spatially grounded communication channel. This departure from implicit, unstructured interfaces in existing VLAs allows for a clearer separation of concerns: the VLM determines "what to do and where," while the action expert focuses solely on "how to execute."
- **End-to-End Internalization of Spatial Reasoning:** AVP's end-to-end architecture internalizes spatial reasoning directly within the VLA model. This eliminates the need for external perception modules, which often introduce inference latency and suffer from cascading error propagation in multi-stage pipelines.
- **Automated and Scalable Supervision:** The novel action-centric visual-primitive supervision pipeline, which derives primitive labels directly from end-effector kinematics, addresses a critical bottleneck in robotic learning. By avoiding manual spatial annotation or reliance on external visual prompt generators, this approach offers a scalable and physically grounded method for acquiring supervision data, potentially reducing the cost and effort associated with dataset creation for complex manipulation tasks.

**Impact on Performance and Generalization:**
- **Enhanced Manipulation Performance:** Demonstrated substantial improvements in success rates across diverse and challenging real-world tasks, including Chinese chess manipulation, domino placement, and general object pick-and-place. For instance, an absolute gain of 37.04% over the π
 0.5
 baseline in Chinese chess manipulation is a notable result. This suggests that the explicit spatial guidance provided by visual primitives leads to more accurate and stable robot execution.
- **Improved Generalization Capabilities:** AVP exhibits strong spatial-compositional generalization, enabling successful execution of unseen task structures (e.g., direct transitions not present in training data). Furthermore, it demonstrates robust zero-shot cross-domain generalization to novel objects and varying backgrounds, highlighting its ability to decouple motion execution from incidental visual factors. This enhanced generalization is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured and dynamic real-world environments.
- **Increased Data Efficiency:** By explicitly guiding the action expert with spatial primitives, the approach can potentially reduce the amount of demonstration data required for learning new manipulation skills, as the action expert can focus on learning generalizable motion patterns rather than re-learning scene understanding.

**Potential Broader Impact:**
The advancements presented by AVP contribute to the long-term goal of building more capable and autonomous general-purpose robots. By addressing the limitations of learning efficiency and generalization, AVP could facilitate the development of robotic systems that are more adaptable to diverse tasks and environments without extensive re-training. This could accelerate progress in various applications, from industrial automation to domestic assistance, where robust and generalizable manipulation skills are essential.

**Acknowledged Limitations:**
The paper also identifies several limitations. The sequential, two-stage inference process (primitive prediction followed by action generation) can introduce additional runtime latency compared to single-pass policies. Furthermore, the reliance on precise hand-eye calibration for the supervision pipeline makes the system sensitive to camera extrinsic drifts or physical disturbances, indicating areas for future research into robustness and real-time calibration.
