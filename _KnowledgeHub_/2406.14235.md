# Mitigating the Human-Robot Domain Discrepancy in Visual Pre-training for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> HR-Align adapts human-data pretrained visual encoders (R3M, D4R) to robots using paired human-robot demonstration videos as a bridge. A parameter-efficient adapter plus a human-robot contrastive alignment loss aligns robot video features with frozen human features, boosting manipulation success rate by over 7% across 20 simulated and 5 real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Human-data pretrained visual backbones suffer large domain discrepancy from robots due to morphological differences, hurting downstream manipulation transfer.
- Proxy-task pretraining on human data alone lacks access to robot data, making it hard to directly close the human-robot semantic gap.
- Full fine-tuning on downstream robot data requires per-environment customization, sacrificing the versatility of pretrained visual representations.

## Method

- **HR-Align** inserts lightweight ==adapter modules== (down/up convolution projections with ==residual connection==) into a frozen pretrained encoder to adapt it to robot video input.
- A ==human-robot contrastive alignment loss== pulls adapted robot features toward frozen paired human features while pushing apart unpaired human-robot feature pairs in a batch.
- A ==task-aware attention== module uses frozen ==BERT== task-description embeddings as queries to aggregate video features into task-conditioned representations for both streams.

## Results

- On Adroit, R3M-Align improves average success rate by **7.3 points** and D4R-Align by **2.0 points** over unadapted pretrained backbones.
- On 18 RLBench multi-task benchmarks, D4R-Align gains **4.6 points** and R3M-Align gains **8.9 points** average success rate versus unadapted models.
- R3M-Align tunes only **1.6M** parameters yet outperforms full fine-tuning baselines R3M-PreT and R3M-ClS (**25M** parameters, **+3.7** and **+3.3** points respectively).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Paired human-robot demonstrations, not more human data or full fine-tuning, are the efficient bridge for closing cross-embodiment domain gaps in pretraining.
> - Parameter-efficient adaptation guided by a well-chosen alignment objective can outperform full-parameter fine-tuning while preserving pretrained model versatility.
> - Inserting adapters at a single well-chosen layer beats inserting them at multiple layers, suggesting distributed adaptation can conflict rather than compound.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing towards enabling robots to acquire a diverse range of skills for real-world application. A significant challenge in this area is the accurate modeling of sequential semantics from visual observations to predict appropriate end-effector actions. This challenge is exacerbated by the limited scale and diversity of readily available robot demonstration data.

To address the data scarcity, a prominent research direction has involved leveraging large-scale human activity datasets, such as Ego4D and Kinetics, for visual model pre-training. The underlying intuition is that human-object interactions in these datasets share dynamic similarities with robot actions, facilitating knowledge transfer. These pre-trained models are then often used as frozen visual backbones to support the learning of manipulation policies for downstream robotic tasks.

However, a fundamental issue arises from the inherent morphological differences between humans and robots, leading to a substantial "human-robot domain discrepancy." This discrepancy hinders the effective generalization of models pre-trained on human data to the robot domain. Existing approaches to tackle this problem fall into two main categories:
1. **Manipulation-oriented proxy tasks:** Some methods introduce proxy tasks (e.g., hand detection) during pre-training on human data to adapt representations for robotic policies. A limitation of this approach is the lack of explicit access to robot data during pre-training, which can make direct mitigation of the domain discrepancy difficult. Defining suitable manipulation-oriented tasks on human data can also be complex.
2. **Fine-tuning on downstream robot data:** Another strategy involves integrating learnable modules into pre-trained models and fine-tuning them directly on downstream robot data. While this can lead to more discriminative representations, it often requires customizing the pre-trained models for each distinct environment, thereby potentially reducing the versatility and generalizability of the pre-trained models across different tasks.

This work positions itself within this landscape by proposing an alternative adaptation paradigm that aims to mitigate the human-robot domain discrepancy while simultaneously preserving the versatility of pre-trained models. It seeks to bridge the gap using a small amount of readily available paired human-robot demonstration data, moving beyond previous strategies that either rely solely on human data or fine-tune extensively on specific downstream robot data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective method for adapting visual models pre-trained on human activity data for robust performance in robotic manipulation tasks, specifically addressing the "human-robot domain discrepancy."

The motivation stems from several factors:
- **Need for Generalizable Visual Representations:** Effective robotic manipulation in real-world scenarios requires visual representations that can generalize across different embodied environments. Large-scale human data offers a rich source for pre-training such representations.
- **Limitations of Robot Data:** Collecting large and diverse datasets of robot demonstrations is resource-intensive and costly. This scarcity necessitates alternative approaches for training robust robot policies.
- **Challenges of Existing Pre-training Paradigms:** While leveraging human data for pre-training is promising, the significant morphological differences between humans and robots create a domain gap. This gap limits the direct transferability and generalization of human-data pre-trained models to robot manipulation tasks.
- **Deficiencies of Current Mitigation Strategies:**
 - Methods that rely on manipulation-oriented proxy tasks on human data lack explicit access to robot data, making it difficult to directly align human and robot visual semantics.
 - Direct fine-tuning on downstream robot data, while improving performance for specific tasks, can compromise the versatility of pre-trained models by requiring custom modifications for each new environment.
- **Leveraging Paired Human-Robot Data:** The authors identify the potential of readily available paired human-robot demonstration data (e.g., RH20T dataset). Such data inherently possesses two key characteristics:
 - Semantic Alignment: Each human and robot demonstration pair exhibits well-aligned dynamic semantics for the same task.
 - Reduced Domain Gap: Although the robot demonstrations in these pairs may differ visually from downstream robot data, the domain discrepancy between them is reduced due to homogeneous robot structures.
- **Maintaining Model Versatility:** A crucial motivation is to develop an adaptation strategy that not only reduces the domain gap but also maintains the generalizability of the adapted models, eliminating the need for extensive customization for every new downstream environment.

In response to these motivations, the paper proposes a novel adaptation paradigm and introduces the Human-Robot Semantic Alignment (HR-Align) method. This method aims to efficiently adapt pre-trained models by leveraging paired human-robot demonstrations as semantic bridges, thereby mitigating the domain discrepancy while preserving model versatility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a new adaptation paradigm to mitigate the human-robot domain discrepancy in visual pre-training. This paradigm, termed Human-Robot Semantic Alignment (HR-Align), uses paired human-robot video data to guide the adaptation of existing human-data pre-trained models.

### 4. Problem Formulation

The method assumes access to a dataset `D` of `N` human-robot video pairs: `D = {H_i, R_i, L_i}_{i=1}^N`. `H_i` is a human video, `R_i` is a robot video imitating `H_i` via teleoperation, and `L_i` is a sentence describing the task. The core assumption is that these pairs have well-aligned dynamic semantics. The goal is to adapt a pre-trained visual encoder `F` (trained on human data) into an adapted model `T` that can extract more effective state representations (`T(I)`) for robot observations `I` in downstream manipulation tasks, instead of using `F(I)`.

### 5. Overview of HR-Align

HR-Align processes paired human and robot videos. It initially uses a frozen pre-trained model to extract features from both. To address the domain discrepancy, learnable adapter modules are injected into the pre-trained model when processing robot videos, enabling the model to adapt to robot-specific semantics. Task descriptions (`L`) serve as queries to extract task-aware semantics from these features. A human-robot contrastive alignment loss ensures semantic consistency between the representations of paired human and adapted robot data, guiding the adaptation process.

### 6. HR-Align Learning Details

- **Frozen Human and Robot Streams:** Given a model `F` pre-trained on human data, it receives a batch of `M` human-robot video pairs `(H_i, R_i, L_i)`. For each video (`H` and `R`), `T` frames are randomly sampled (`Υ`). The frozen pre-trained model `F` then extracts spatial-temporal features: `h_f = F(Υ(H))` and `r_f = F(Υ(R))`. While `h_f` is expected to capture human video semantics effectively, `r_f` from the robot video is expected to suffer from domain discrepancy due to `F` being trained solely on human data.

- **Adapted Robot Stream:** To alleviate this discrepancy, HR-Align introduces an adapted pre-trained model `T` by inserting learnable adapter modules into `F`. The adapted robot video features are obtained as `r_t = T(Υ(R)) = F_Adapter(Υ(R))`. An adapter module (Equation 3) takes an intermediate feature `r_{f,inter}` from `F` and applies a down-projection (`Conv_down`), an activation function `g`, an up-projection (`Conv_up`), and adds this result residually back to `r_{f,inter}`. This design allows for parameter-efficient adaptation, where only the adapter modules are learned, while the main body of `F` remains frozen or largely unchanged.

- **Task-aware Feature Modeling:** To enhance the extracted features with task-specific information, a feature enhancement module is applied. The task description `L` is first processed by a frozen BERT model followed by a learnable fully connected layer to obtain a task description feature `l`. This `l` acts as a query in an attention-based aggregation mechanism. For example, for the adapted robot feature `r_t`, an attention score `A_r` is computed based on `r_t` and `l`. This `A_r` is then used to weight `r_t` to produce a task-aware adapted robot feature `¯r_t`. Similar processes yield `¯h_f` and `¯r_f` for the human and unadapted robot streams, respectively.

- **Human-Robot Contrastive Alignment Loss:** The core of the adaptation is the contrastive alignment loss (Equation 6), designed to modulate the adapter modules. It operates on a batch of `M` video pairs and their task-aware features (`¯h_{f,i}`, `¯r_{f,i}`, `¯r_{t,i}`). The loss is structured around two principles:
 1. For a given human feature `¯h_{f,i}`, the adapted robot feature `¯r_{t,i}` from the same pair should have higher semantic similarity than the unadapted robot feature `¯r_{f,i}`.
 2. For any given pair (`¯h_{f,i}`, `¯r_{t,i}`), their semantic similarity should be higher compared to any unpaired combination (e.g., `¯h_{f,i}` with `¯r_{t,j}` where `j ≠ i`). The loss is symmetrical, applying the same principle from the robot's perspective (`¯r_{t,i}` with `¯h_{f,i}` vs. `¯r_{f,i}` or `¯h_{f,j}` where `j ≠ i`). The similarity function `S` uses `exp(x^T y / τ)`, where `τ` is a temperature factor. This loss encourages the adapted model to learn discriminative visual semantics in robot demonstrations that are consistent with their paired human counterparts.

### 7. Experimental Setups

The experimental validation proceeds in two stages:
1. **Pre-trained Model Adaptation:** Two widely used human-data pre-trained models are adapted: R3M (pre-trained on Ego4D using vision-language contrastive learning) and D4R (pre-trained on Kinetics using MoCo). The adaptation uses a subset of 56k human-robot video pairs from the RH20T dataset. Adaptation parameters include random sampling of 5 frames, frozen DistilBert for task descriptions, Adam optimizer, learning rate `1 × 10^-4`, batch size of 200, and `τ = 0.1`. Adapters are typically inserted after the last layer of the backbone.
2. **Downstream Policy Learning:** The adapted models (with frozen visual backbones) are used to extract state representations for robot visual observations. Action prediction models are built on these features to predict end-effector actions.
 - **Simulated Tasks:** Evaluated on 20 tasks: 2 single-tasks from Adroit (pen, relocate) using behavior cloning with top-view inputs and largest demo dataset sizes, and 18 language-conditioned multi-tasks from RLBench (using an RVT-like policy but with one self-attention layer for feature fusion).
 - **Real-world Tasks:** Evaluated on 5 custom tasks (put fruit in plate, stack cups, put tennis in mug, hang mug, put item in box) using an xArm7 robot, Inspire gripper, and Orbbec Femto Bolt camera. 40 teleoperation demonstrations per task are collected. Policies are trained under a single-task setting using the ACT framework to predict key-actions.

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the HR-Align method across simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks using two different human-data pre-trained models: D4R and R3M.

### 9. Generalization Across Different Tasks

- **Simulated Single-Task Setting (Adroit):**
 - For the D4R model, HR-Align (D4R-Align) improved the average success rate by 2.0% (from 63.0% to 65.0%) across the "pen" and "relocate" tasks.
 - For the R3M model, HR-Align (R3M-Align) achieved a more substantial average improvement of 7.3% (from 74.0% to 81.3%) on these tasks, with notable gains in the "relocate" task.

- **Simulated Multi-Task Setting (RLBench):**
 - On 18 language-conditioned multi-tasks, D4R-Align demonstrated superior performance on 10 tasks, leading to a 4.6% improvement in the averaged success rate across all 18 tasks (from 55.3% to 59.9%) compared to the unadapted D4R model.
 - R3M-Align also improved success rates on 10 tasks, achieving an 8.9% increase in the averaged success rate across all 18 tasks (from 50.3% to 59.2%) compared to the unadapted R3M model. This outcome indicates the necessity of mitigating the human-robot domain discrepancy for multi-task motor-control pre-training.

- **Real-world Task Setting:**
 - On five real-world tasks, D4R-Align showed an average 13% improvement in success rate over the pre-trained D4R model.
 - R3M-Align similarly demonstrated an 11% improvement over the pre-trained R3M model. These results indicate the method's effectiveness in real-world scenarios.

### 10. Effectiveness of Human-Robot Cross-Domain Alignment

To evaluate the design of the semantic alignment, HR-Align (R3M-Align) was compared against two strong baselines that involved fine-tuning the full R3M model on the robot videos from the paired human-robot dataset:
- **R3M-PreT:** Fine-tuned R3M using its original pre-training method.
- **R3M-ClS:** Fine-tuned R3M by classifying robot videos into action categories.
Both R3M-PreT and R3M-ClS involved training all 25 million parameters of the visual backbone.
- On the Adroit tasks, R3M-PreT achieved an average success rate of 77.7% (+3.7% over R3M), and R3M-ClS achieved 77.3% (+3.3% over R3M).
- In contrast, R3M-Align, by tuning only 1.6 million parameters (6.4% of the R3M backbone), achieved a higher average success rate of 81.3% (+7.3% over R3M). This demonstrates that the human-robot semantic alignment design is more effective in addressing the domain discrepancy while being significantly more parameter-efficient.
- Additional ablations in the appendix also showed HR-Align outperforming R3M-PreT and R3M-ClS, even when the baselines were trained with full data or adapted with similar parameter-efficient adapters.

### 11. Component Contribution to HR-Align Method

Ablation studies investigated the impact of inserting adapter modules at different positions within the R3M backbone network (before the first block, in the middle, and after the last block).
- Compared to the R3M model, all adapter placements (R3M-Align-E, R3M-Align-M, R3M-Align-L) significantly improved success rates on Adroit tasks while utilizing a small number of learnable parameters (0.1M, 3.5M, and 1.6M, respectively).
- R3M-Align-L (adapter after the last layer) achieved the highest improvement (+7.3%) with 1.6M parameters.
- Inserting adapters at all three locations (R3M-Align-E.M.L) with 5.2M parameters did not yield the best performance, suggesting potential challenges in harmoniously adapting representations across multiple positions.

### 12. Effectiveness of the Adapted Representation

t-SNE visualizations of feature distributions for randomly selected RLBench tasks were used to compare the unadapted R3M and adapted R3M-Align models.
- The feature distribution of the R3M model appeared significantly more dispersed.
- The R3M-Align model exhibited a more clustered and discriminative feature distribution, indicating that the adaptation process effectively learns more distinct representations for downstream tasks by reducing the human-robot domain discrepancy.

### 13. Additional Ablations (Language-guided feature enhancement)

Removing the language-guided feature enhancement from R3M-Align-L resulted in a performance degradation (80.0% average success rate compared to 81.3% for R3M-Align-L), demonstrating the contribution of language descriptions to capturing task-aware semantics and promoting human-robot semantic alignment.

### 14. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a parameter-efficient approach to mitigate a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: the domain discrepancy between human demonstration data used for pre-training and the robot execution environment. The proposed HR-Align method and its underlying adaptation paradigm offer several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Generalizability:** By leveraging paired human-robot demonstrations, the method allows pre-trained visual models to explicitly adapt to the robot domain. This results in significantly improved performance across a diverse range of robotic manipulation tasks, including single-task, language-conditioned multi-task, and real-world scenarios. This suggests a step towards more generalizable robotic skills learned from readily available human data.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** The use of lightweight adapter modules ensures that the adaptation process is parameter-efficient. This is a practical advantage, as it avoids the need for extensive fine-tuning of large pre-trained models for each new robot environment, thereby reducing computational costs and training time.
- **Maintenance of Model Versatility:** Unlike approaches that customize models for specific downstream environments, HR-Align aims to adapt pre-trained models in a way that preserves their versatility, making them more broadly applicable without requiring task-specific modifications.
- **Bridging the Human-Robot Gap:** The core human-robot contrastive alignment loss provides a mechanism to explicitly align the semantics between human and robot video data. This direct approach to mitigating the domain gap, guided by semantically consistent paired data, has been demonstrated to be more effective than alternative fine-tuning strategies.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work highlights several avenues for future exploration, such as developing quantitative measures for human-robot domain discrepancies, integrating paired human-robot data more deeply into the pre-training stage, and systematically studying the impact of data scale and diversity on adaptation outcomes. These limitations indicate directions for advancing the robustness and applicability of visual pre-training for robotics.

In summary, this work provides a method for making human-data pre-trained visual models more effective and practical for robotic manipulation tasks, contributing to the development of more capable and versatile robotic systems.
