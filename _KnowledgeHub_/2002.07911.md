# Generating Automatic Curricula via Self-Supervised Active Domain Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Supervised Active Domain Randomization (SS-ADR) extends asymmetric self-play to jointly evolve goal and environment curricula for goal-directed RL. Alice sets goals in a reference environment while Bob solves them in randomized ones; Alice's time-difference reward trains both her stopping policy and the SVPG environment-sampling particles. The single self-supervised signal yields robust zero-shot sim2real policies with low variance.

## Problem

- Self-play curriculum methods evolve only goals within one fixed environment, so learned policies never face varied dynamics and transfer poorly to real robots.
- Uniform domain randomization samples the parameter space blindly, producing unstructured task spaces, unstable convergence, and high variance across seeds.
- Active domain randomization ranks environments regardless of the goal inside them, ignoring that goal difficulty and environment difficulty interact in goal-directed settings.

## Method

- **SS-ADR** poses a bilevel optimization: the inner loop maximizes expected return over environment-goal pairs, the outer loop maximizes a self-play metric driving a joint curriculum.
- ==Asymmetric self-play== with Alice acting in the reference environment and Bob in the randomized one; Alice's stopping policy trained by vanilla policy gradient, both actors by **DDPG**.
- Alice's time-difference reward also trains the **SVPG** particles of active domain randomization, so one self-supervised signal co-evolves goal and environment sampling.

## Results

- On ErgoReacher (**8** randomized dimensions) and ErgoPusher (puck friction), SS-ADR and self-play both beat uniform randomization in-distribution; only SS-ADR converges with low variance on held-out hard environments.
- Zero-shot transfer to real Poppy Ergo Jr robots, **4** seeds by **25** trials each, no fine-tuning: SS-ADR gives the lowest average final distance and tightest spread across torque and friction variants.
- With uncalibrated randomization ranges, SS-ADR learns to avoid physically unstable settings below roughly **0.05** randomization coefficient, while uniform randomization keeps sampling unsolvable environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Coupling goal and environment curricula gives multiplicative benefit; evolving either alone risks degenerate proposals like impossible goals or physically unrealizable dynamics.
> - One self-supervised horizon signal, rewarding what the setter does fast but the solver cannot, can drive two curriculum spaces without extra reward engineering.
> - Learned samplers self-calibrate randomization ranges, reducing the trial-and-error tuning that uniform randomization demands; consistency, not just mean accuracy, matters for deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Reinforcement Learning (RL) frequently encounters challenges related to reward engineering, which involves designing reward functions that induce desired agent behaviors. This process is often difficult, prone to issues like reward hacking, and can hinder the development of general artificial intelligence. Curriculum learning offers a strategy to mitigate these difficulties by training agents on progressively more complex tasks. However, traditional curriculum learning often relies on human-designed heuristics to manage task progression, which can merely shift the burden of design.

To address the limitations of external reward functions and manual curriculum design, intrinsic motivation methods have emerged, where agents generate their own rewards, thereby fostering automatic curricula. Self-play is a notable intrinsic motivation technique, particularly effective in goal-directed RL, where agents learn by setting and achieving goals for themselves. While successful in generating goal curricula, existing self-play methods have primarily focused on learning within a single environment.

Concurrently, in robotic learning, the "sim2real" problem addresses the difficulty of transferring policies trained in simulation to real-world robots due to discrepancies between simulated and physical environments, known as the "reality gap." Domain Randomization (DR) is a prominent approach to bridge this gap, involving the perturbation of simulator parameters during training to expose the agent to a diverse set of environments. Active Domain Randomization (ADR) refines DR by framing the selection of environment parameters as an RL problem, learning to sample environments that are most informative for the agent's learning process. ADR, however, focuses solely on the environment curriculum, not considering the goal curriculum within these varied environments.

This paper addresses a gap in the existing research by integrating the strengths of self-play for goal curriculum generation with ADR for environment curriculum generation. The work proposes a unified framework that co-evolves both goal and environment curricula, driven by a single self-supervised reward signal. This integration aims to create agents that are both proficient in specific tasks and robust to environmental variations, facilitating more effective zero-shot transfer from simulation to reality. By doing so, the authors aim to overcome the limitations of learning curricula independently in either goal or environment space, which could lead to suboptimal or degenerate solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that can automatically generate a coupled curriculum in both goal and environment spaces, utilizing only a single self-supervised reward signal. This method aims to produce robust and generalizable policies, particularly for sim2real transfer tasks in robotics.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several challenges in contemporary Reinforcement Learning:

- **Reward Engineering Difficulty:** Designing effective reward functions for complex tasks is a significant hurdle in RL. The process is often arduous, requires extensive trial and error, and can lead to unintended behaviors (reward hacking) or difficult optimization landscapes for the agent. This design burden impedes the scalability and applicability of RL to real-world problems.
- **Limitations of Traditional Curriculum Learning:** While curriculum learning aims to simplify learning by presenting progressively harder tasks, it frequently necessitates manual heuristics for sequencing tasks. This can merely displace the reward design problem to a higher level of abstraction, rather than fundamentally solving it.
- **Independent Curriculum Evolution Issues:** Existing approaches either focus on generating goal curricula (e.g., via self-play) or environment curricula (e.g., via ADR). The authors hypothesize that evolving these curricula independently could lead to suboptimal or "degenerate solutions." For instance, an agent might learn to propose impossible goals in any environment, or the curriculum might include environments with unrealistic physical parameters that offer no learning utility. A coupled approach is posited to mitigate these issues by ensuring that the selected goals are achievable within the chosen environmental contexts, and vice versa.
- **Enhancing Sim2Real Transfer Robustness:** In robotic learning, policies trained in simulation often struggle to perform effectively in the real world due to the "reality gap." Domain Randomization (DR) and Active Domain Randomization (ADR) address this by varying simulation parameters. However, uniformly sampled DR can be inefficient, and ADR, while learning an environment curriculum, does not inherently learn an appropriate goal curriculum within these varied environments. The goal is to develop an approach that yields policies robust enough for zero-shot transfer, meaning they perform well on real robots without additional fine-tuning.
- **Reduction of Human Intervention:** By enabling the automatic co-evolution of both goal and environment curricula through self-supervision, the research seeks to reduce the need for human intervention in designing reward functions, curriculum heuristics, and even the calibration of randomization parameters, making the training process more autonomous and efficient. The paper specifically highlights that SS-ADR can self-calibrate, avoiding environments that are physically unstable or impossible to solve within an uncalibrated randomization space.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to create a more efficient, autonomous, and robust RL training paradigm that effectively bridges the simulation-to-reality gap by intelligently managing both the complexity of tasks and the variability of environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Self-Supervised Active Domain Randomization (SS-ADR), extends the asymmetric self-play framework by Sukhbaatar et al. and integrates it with Active Domain Randomization (ADR) principles. The core idea is to jointly learn a curriculum for goals and environments using a single self-supervised reward signal.

#### 3.1. Bilevel Optimization Formulation

The problem is conceptualized as a bilevel optimization. The inner loop focuses on maximizing the expected return for an agent given specific environment-goal pairs. The outer loop utilizes a self-play-based metric to enforce a naturally evolving curriculum across both environment and goal dimensions.

#### 3.2. Integration of Asymmetric Self-Play (Alice and Bob)

SS-ADR utilizes an Alice-Bob self-play mechanism:
- **Alice (Goal Setter):** Alice's role is to generate a challenging but achievable goal for Bob. Alice operates in a *reference environment* ($E_{ref}$), which is a standard, non-randomized version of the task. Alice takes actions, exploring the environment, until a "STOP" action is signaled by her stopping policy ($\pi_s^a$). The state at which Alice stops becomes Bob's target goal state ($s^*$). Alice's acting policy ($\pi_a$) is designed to be a time-delayed copy of Bob's policy, aiming to ensure that the goals Alice sets are within the general reach of Bob's current capabilities.
- **Bob (Task Completer):** After Alice sets a goal, the environment is reset to its starting state, and Bob's task is to reach the goal $s^*$. Crucially, Bob operates in a *randomized environment* ($E_{rand}$), whose parameters are determined by the ADR component. Bob's policy ($\pi_b$) takes the current state and Alice's goal state as input.

#### 3.3. Self-Supervised Reward for Curriculum Generation

The reward structure, derived from Sukhbaatar et al., is central to driving the curriculum:
- **Alice's Reward ($r_a$):** $r_a = \upsilon * \max(0, t_b - t_a)$, where $t_a$ is the time steps Alice takes to set a task, $t_b$ is the time steps Bob takes to complete it, and $\upsilon$ is a scaling factor. Alice is maximally rewarded when she can quickly set a task ($t_a$ is small) that Bob takes a long time to complete or cannot complete at all ($t_b$ is large). This encourages Alice to find goals that are "just beyond" Bob's current skill level.
- **Bob's Reward ($r_b$):** $r_b = -\upsilon * t_b$. Bob is rewarded for minimizing the time taken to reach the goal.

Alice's stopping policy ($\pi_s^a$) is trained using Vanilla Policy Gradients with $r_a$. Bob's acting policy ($\pi_b$) and Alice's acting policy ($\pi_a$) are trained using Deep Deterministic Policy Gradients (DDPG) with standard environment rewards (implicitly, as Bob tries to minimize $t_b$, which corresponds to completing the task).

#### 3.4. Active Domain Randomization (ADR) for Environment Curriculum

- **Randomization Space ($\Xi$):** A predefined space of simulation parameters (e.g., friction, gravity, joint torques) that can be varied.
- **SVPG Particles ($\mu_{\phi_i}$):** A set of $N$ "particles" parameterized by $\phi_i$ are used to explore and sample environments from the randomization space. These particles represent specific environment configurations ($\xi_i \in \Xi$).
- **Coupling Mechanism:** The key innovation of SS-ADR is that these SVPG particles, which determine the randomized environment $E_{rand}$ for Bob, are trained using *Alice's self-supervised reward* ($r_a$). Specifically, Equation 1 (Alice's reward) is evaluated for each environment randomization prescribed by an individual ADR particle. This means the environment curriculum is directly influenced by the difficulty Alice perceives in setting goals for Bob within those randomized environments. If an environment makes it too easy or too hard for Bob relative to Alice's setup time, the SVPG particles will adjust to find more fruitful randomization parameters.
- **SVPG Update Rule:** The particles are updated using the Stein's Variational Policy Gradient (SVPG) rule, which includes a term maximizing return (here, Alice's reward) and a kernel-based term encouraging diversity among particles, preventing them from collapsing to a single environment.

#### 3.5. Overall Training Loop

The training alternates between Alice setting a goal in $E_{ref}$, an environment $E_{rand}$ being sampled by SVPG particles, Bob attempting the goal in $E_{rand}$, and then updating policies and environment parameters based on the observed times ($t_a, t_b$) and their derived rewards. This continuous feedback loop drives the co-evolution of both the goal and environment curricula, with Alice challenging Bob in progressively harder randomized environments and Bob learning to generalize across them.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Two continuous control robotic tasks were used: ErgoReacher (4DoF arm, reach end-effector to goal) and ErgoPusher (3DoF arm, push puck to goal).
- **Randomization Parameters:** For ErgoPusher, puck friction ($N_{rand}=1$) was varied. For ErgoReacher, eight randomization dimensions ($N_{rand}=8$) related to joint torques and gains were used.
- **Evaluation:** Policies were evaluated every 5000 timesteps for 1 million total timesteps. Performance was measured by the final distance to the goal in both default (in-distribution) and intuitively hard (out-of-distribution) environments. Sim-to-real transfer involved deploying policies on real Poppy Ergo Jr. robots without fine-tuning, with 25 trials per policy/seed.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR):** Uniformly samples environment parameters from a fixed range; the goal remains constant.
 - **Unsupervised Default:** Uses the self-play framework to generate a goal curriculum, but only within the default, non-randomized environment.

#### 3.7. Implementation Details (from Appendix)

All networks utilized the same architecture and hyperparameters. Deep Deterministic Policy Gradients (DDPG) was used for Alice and Bob's acting policies, with actor and critic networks each having two hidden layers (400 and 300 neurons respectively) and ReLU activation. Alice's stopping policy used a multi-layered perceptron with two hidden layers of 300 neurons. The Adam optimizer was employed for all networks. The discount factor ($\gamma$) was 0.99, and the reward scaling factor ($\upsilon$) was 0.2.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence supporting the efficacy of Self-Supervised Active Domain Randomization (SS-ADR) in generating robust policies through coupled goal and environment curricula.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted on two robotic tasks, ErgoPusher and ErgoReacher, assessing performance in both default (in-distribution) and intuitively hard (out-of-distribution) environments.

- **Performance in Default Environments:** On the default environments, both SS-ADR and the Unsupervised-Default baseline (self-play for goal curriculum only) significantly outperformed Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR). SS-ADR and Unsupervised-Default demonstrated strong performance, faster convergence, and lower variance in achieving the goal, as measured by the final distance to the goal. UDR, in contrast, exhibited unstable performance and inconsistent convergence throughout training.
- **Performance in Intuitively Hard Environments:** When evaluated on intuitively hard environments, which were constructed with parameters outside the typical training distribution (e.g., extremely low puck friction for ErgoPusher, or very low joint torques/gains for ErgoReacher), SS-ADR demonstrated superior performance. SS-ADR consistently converged with low variance and strong performance, indicating effective generalization to challenging, unseen conditions. The Unsupervised-Default method, while generally better than UDR, showed higher variance and struggled more in these difficult environments. UDR continued to exhibit poor performance and high variance.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-Real Transfer Experiments

To assess real-world applicability, policies trained in simulation were transferred zero-shot (without any fine-tuning) to real Poppy Ergo Jr. robots.

- **Accuracy and Consistency:** SS-ADR policies consistently achieved lower final distances to the goal and exhibited lower performance spread across various real-world task instantiations (varying puck friction and joint torques) compared to both UDR and Unsupervised-Default. This indicates that SS-ADR generated more accurate and robust policies for real robotic systems.
- **Outperformance of Baselines:** SS-ADR demonstrated clear superiority over UDR across all tested real-world environment variants. In comparison to Unsupervised-Default, SS-ADR's performance was consistently better or comparable, particularly showcasing a notable advantage in the Pusher environment where it clearly outperformed all other baselines. The low spread observed with SS-ADR is highlighted as critical for the practical deployment of robotic RL agents, where consistent performance is essential.

#### 4.3. Self-Calibration of SS-ADR

The research also explored SS-ADR's ability to operate effectively within uncalibrated randomization ranges, which often contain physically unstable or unsolvable tasks.

- **Adaptation to Uncalibrated Ranges:** When trained with uncalibrated randomization ranges that included physically unstable environments (e.g., randomization coefficients below 0.05 for ErgoPusher leading to unstable dynamics), SS-ADR demonstrated the ability to adapt. It learned to avoid sampling parameters from these unsolvable regions, thereby preventing training on unproductive or detrimental tasks.
- **UDR Limitations:** UDR, due to its static uniform sampling strategy, continued to sample from these physically unstable environments within the uncalibrated range. This indiscriminate sampling hindered its training process and overall performance, reinforcing the benefits of SS-ADR's dynamic and adaptive environment curriculum.
- **Calibrated Ranges:** With carefully calibrated ranges, both SS-ADR and UDR could sample from "harder" task ranges. However, SS-ADR still showed evidence of actively learning a curriculum within these ranges, unlike UDR's static approach.

#### 4.4. Variance Reduction (Appendix A)

An additional finding presented in the appendix indicates that SS-ADR leads to lower variance across different random seeds when compared to Active Domain Randomization (ADR) alone in the ErgoReacher environment. This suggests improved stability of the learning process with the coupled curriculum approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Self-Supervised Active Domain Randomization (SS-ADR) represents a notable advancement in Reinforcement Learning, particularly for robotics and generalization. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** SS-ADR demonstrates the ability to generate policies that are highly robust and generalizable. By co-evolving curricula in both goal and environment spaces, agents learn to perform well across a wide range of tasks and environmental variations, which is crucial for real-world deployment.
- **Improved Sim2Real Transfer:** A primary challenge in robotic learning is bridging the "reality gap" between simulation and physical hardware. SS-ADR provides a more effective solution for zero-shot sim2real transfer, outperforming existing baselines by yielding policies that consistently perform well on real robots without additional fine-tuning. This could significantly accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous robotic systems.
- **Reduced Human Effort in RL Development:** SS-ADR diminishes the need for laborious human intervention in several key areas:
 - **Reward Engineering:** The method relies solely on a self-supervised reward signal, circumventing the complex and error-prone process of designing explicit reward functions.
 - **Curriculum Design:** It automatically generates both goal and environment curricula, eliminating the need for manually designed heuristics to control task progression.
 - **Parameter Calibration:** The self-calibrating nature of SS-ADR, which allows it to avoid sampling from physically unstable or unsolvable environments even with uncalibrated randomization ranges, reduces the trial-and-error often associated with setting simulator parameters.
- **Foundation for Autonomous Learning:** By enabling agents to autonomously generate their own learning challenges (goals) within intelligently selected varied environments, SS-ADR contributes to a paradigm of more autonomous and self-sufficient RL systems. This aligns with broader goals of artificial intelligence to learn without extensive human oversight.
- **Addressing Degenerate Solutions:** The coupled curriculum approach directly addresses the theoretical concern that independently evolving goal and environment curricula could lead to degenerate or unproductive learning. By synchronizing the difficulty of goals with the complexity of environments, SS-ADR ensures a more coherent and efficient learning trajectory.
- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated in robotic control tasks, the principle of co-evolving task and environment curricula using self-supervision could potentially be applied to other domains within RL where robust generalization across varying conditions is critical, such as complex game environments or adaptive control systems.

In summary, SS-ADR offers a practical and principled method for training highly capable and adaptable RL agents, mitigating some of the most persistent challenges in the field and paving the way for more robust and autonomous intelligent systems.
