# Belt-Finger: An Affordable Soft Belt-Driven Gripper for Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Tübingen and Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems developed the "Belt-Finger," an affordable soft belt-driven gripper that upgrades standard parallel grippers with three active in-hand degrees of freedom (translation, pitch, and roll). This design, costing approximately 10 euros per finger, enables dexterous manipulation of diverse objects, reducing task failure rates and execution times compared to conventional grippers, and successfully integrates with teleoperation, Model Predictive Control, and Vision-Language-Action policies.

## Problem

- Conventional parallel-jaw grippers have limited in-hand dexterity, requiring extensive robot arm movements and restricting utility in confined spaces.
- Existing dexterous multi-fingered robotic hands are often expensive, complex, and lack robustness, limiting widespread adoption.
- Soft grippers struggle with precise control and robustness, while rigid-support belt designs have difficulty conforming to complex object geometries.

## Method

- The **Belt-Finger** is a modular, soft belt-driven finger designed as an upgrade for parallel grippers, adding three active in-hand degrees of freedom (translation, pitch, roll).
- It utilizes soft, deformable TPU95 belts with high-friction coating and 3D-printable components, ensuring affordability and passive conformity to irregular object surfaces.
- The gripper integrates with diverse control paradigms, including intuitive teleoperation, an iCEM-based ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) for precise autonomous manipulation, and fine-tuned Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies.

## Results

- The Belt-Finger achieved approximately 45 mm translation, 85° pitch, and continuous roll, with payload capacities up to 9 N for translation and 100 N·mm for roll.
- iCEM-based MPC successfully reoriented and translated objects, achieving average final pose errors of **4.8** ± **1.5** mm and **9.1** ± **3.3**° for translation-only tasks and **6.7** ± **4.1** mm and **12.1** ± **7.3**° for medium combined tasks.
- Comparative benchmarks showed a **100%** success rate for the Belt-Finger across 10 manipulation tasks, in contrast to a **50%** failure rate for conventional parallel grippers, significantly reducing task failure rates and execution times, and VLA models achieved high success rates (e.g., **100%** for "lego_reorientation").

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Soft, deformable belts with a non-parallel arrangement enable passive conformity to complex object surfaces and induce extra lifting force, improving grasp robustness and reducing motor strain.
> - An iCEM-based MPC can effectively manage the control challenges of non-differentiable contact dynamics and non-constant sliding contacts introduced by soft belts for precise, autonomous manipulation.
> - The multi-DoF Belt-Finger is compatible with advanced learning models like VLA policies, allowing them to leverage low-level dexterity for high-level reasoning tasks and exhibit emergent closed-loop corrections.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation widely relies on end-effectors, with parallel-jaw grippers being a common choice due to their simplicity, robustness, and cost-effectiveness. However, these grippers possess limited in-hand mobility, often necessitating extensive robot arm movements and restricting their utility in confined or complex workspaces. This limitation becomes a significant bottleneck for dexterous manipulation tasks that require fine object reorientation or translation without re-grasping.

Current alternatives for achieving in-hand manipulation predominantly include multi-fingered robotic hands, such as the Shadow hand, LEAP Hand, or the Everyday finger. While these hands offer high dexterity with numerous actively controllable joints and complex kinematic chains, they typically incur substantial manufacturing costs, pose significant challenges in terms of robustness, and require sophisticated control strategies. This trade-off between dexterity and cost/complexity creates a dilemma for practical robotic applications.

Another class of end-effectors, soft grippers, provides intriguing continuous bendable fingers. However, achieving accurate control and consistent robustness with these designs remains an ongoing research challenge.

A more direct area of related work focuses on fingers with active surfaces. These designs aim to provide continuous in-hand manipulation without the need for complex multi-body kinematics found in articulated hands. Examples include grippers that use two driven belts to achieve effective rolling and translational control, such as the VF finger, Velvet Fingers, Band-Based Gripper, and RoTipBot. Some designs further increase in-hand degrees of freedom by incorporating rotatable fingertips, as seen in the Roller Grasper and DTactive, though this often leads to overly complex or over-actuated systems. Specific designs like the tri-finger belt and rigid-support double-belt utilize belts with rigid support, which simplify control but can compromise dexterity when dealing with complex object geometries.

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of conventional parallel grippers while seeking to avoid the high costs and complexities of multi-fingered hands or advanced active surface designs. The Belt-Finger aims to provide a "drop-in replacement" or upgrade for parallel grippers, enhancing their manipulation capabilities by integrating active belts. By focusing on an affordable, simple, and robust design with soft, deformable belts, the authors seek to overcome the dexterity limitations of basic parallel grippers and the geometric handling constraints of rigid-support belt designs, making dexterous in-hand manipulation more accessible and broadly applicable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized limitations of conventional parallel-jaw grippers in robotic systems. While widely adopted across industrial and household applications due to their simplicity, robustness, and affordability, these grippers inherently lack in-hand dexterity. This deficiency often compels the robot arm to perform large, compensatory motions for object reorientation or precise positioning, which is inefficient, time-consuming, and restrictive in confined or cluttered environments.

Conversely, existing solutions that offer substantial in-hand manipulation capabilities, such as advanced anthropomorphic robotic hands, present significant barriers. These devices are typically expensive to manufacture, involve intricate mechanical designs, and demand complex control algorithms, limiting their widespread adoption and practical deployment. There is a need for a solution that bridges the gap between the simplicity and cost-effectiveness of parallel grippers and the dexterous capabilities of more advanced robotic hands.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Dexterity-Enhancing Gripper Module:** The central objective is to design a finger module that serves as an upgrade for standard parallel grippers. This module is intended to add three active in-hand degrees of freedom (translation, pitch, and roll) while preserving the fundamental opening and closing functionality of traditional parallel grippers.
2. **Ensure Affordability and Simplicity:** A crucial objective is to engineer the mechanism for inexpensive manufacturing and straightforward integration. This includes prioritizing the use of 3D-printable and inexpensive off-the-shelf components to maintain a low overall cost and facilitate accessibility.
3. **Maintain Reliability and Control Precision:** The design aims to integrate enhanced manipulation capabilities without compromising the reliability and precise control characteristics associated with traditional parallel grippers.
4. **Broaden Manipulation Capabilities:** By adding in-hand DoFs, the work intends to significantly expand the range of manipulation tasks that can be performed by robots equipped with this gripper, particularly those requiring fine adjustments or reorientations.
5. **Demonstrate Versatile Control Paradigms:** To showcase the utility and adaptability of the proposed gripper, the authors aim to integrate and evaluate it within diverse control frameworks:
 - **Intuitive Teleoperation:** To enable human operators to perform complex demonstrations and tasks through an intuitive interface, facilitating rapid data collection for learning.
 - **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** To achieve precise, autonomous, and zero-shot in-hand manipulation of known objects, addressing the challenges of controlling non-constant sliding contacts inherent in the soft-belt design.
 - **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Policies:** To demonstrate the gripper's compatibility with advanced learning models, enabling it to execute complex, high-level reasoning tasks that require fine-grained low-level dexterity.

Overall, the motivation is to offer an accessible and effective solution for dexterous in-hand manipulation that addresses the limitations of current robotic end-effectors, making advanced manipulation capabilities more attainable for a broader range of applications and research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the design and evaluation of the "Belt-Finger," a soft belt-driven gripper, integrated with various control strategies.

**Hardware Design (Belt-Finger)**
The Belt-Finger is conceived as a modular, plug-and-play finger for parallel grippers. It enhances manipulation by actively shifting contact points to enable in-hand translation, roll, and pitch. Each finger module incorporates three continuous-rotation servo motors that drive synchronous wheels.

- **Belt Design:** A key innovation is the use of soft, deformable TPU95 belts with a high-friction coating and circular arc teeth. This soft design allows the belts to passively conform to and grasp irregular object surfaces, a departure from prior rigid-support belt designs which struggled with complex geometries. The compliance, along with narrow, pointed fingertips, aims to improve grasping robustness, access in cluttered spaces, and keep costs low through 3D-printable and off-the-shelf components. The internal volume also supports future integration of tactile sensors. The soft belt structure introduces a complex control problem due to non-constant sliding contacts.
- **Gripper Base & Fabrication:** The gripper base is largely 3D-printed (FFF), contributing to a low material cost (approximately 10 euros per finger). The gripper opening and closing are driven by a Dynamixel MX28 motor with a plastic rack-and-pinion mechanism (100 mm range).
- **Control (Low-level):** The gripper utilizes semi-impedance control for its opening and closing motion. For in-hand manipulation, theoretical object translation, roll, and pitch commands are directly converted into individual belt velocities via a scaling constant and a fixed transformation matrix. A non-parallel arrangement of the belts is employed, inducing an extra lifting force during gripper closing, which can alleviate motor strain during heavy load translation and allows for pointed fingertips.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC)**
An iCEM-based MPC framework is developed to manage the control challenges arising from the soft belts' non-constant sliding contacts, specifically for manipulating known object shapes.

- **Belt Finger Model:** Given the mixed rigid, deformable, and driven structure, an exact dynamic model is complex. Therefore, a simplified local, short-term approximation is used. Assumptions include sticky contacts (no slip), belts approximated by centerlines, sufficient motor power, and contacts treated as points. The model predicts the next object pose based on the current pose, object mesh, and the commanded in-hand action (translation, roll, pitch). Contact points are approximated by averaging mesh-belt intersections, and object rotational and translational velocities are derived from belt velocities using rigid body dynamics.
- **Zero-order Optimizer (iCEM):** The ray-tracing process in the model renders it non-differentiable, making traditional gradient-based optimization difficult. The iCEM planner is employed to iteratively refine a normal distribution of low-cost action sequences. It samples action sequences over a defined horizon, evaluates trajectories using a weighted distance cost function (prioritizing closer states to the target), and updates the sampling distribution based on the highest-scoring sequences. Only the first action from the optimal sequence is executed before replanning.
- **Gripper Opening Estimator:** To maintain grasp stability without overcomplicating the iCEM optimization, the gripper opening width is determined separately. It combines a feedforward term (object's average segment length between fingers at the predicted next pose) with an adaptive compensation term. This adaptive term adjusts for modeling errors that could lead to under-pressure (lacking friction) or over-pressure (restricting movement). The compensation term is updated based on comparing the observed object motion to the model's prediction of contact points; under-pressure is inferred if fewer active contacts than predicted are found, and over-pressure if the object moves less than minimum predicted.

**Teleoperation System**
To enable intuitive human control and efficient data collection for complex tasks, a teleoperation system is designed for simultaneous control of the robot arm (6-DoF) and the Belt-Finger gripper (4-DoF).

- **Interface:** A standard controller (e.g., gamepad) is used in conjunction with an external camera that tracks a marker array on the controller.
- **Arm Control:** Cartesian impedance control is used for the robotic arm. The target arm pose is calculated based on the current pose and a delta pose derived from the controller's relative pose via a second-order function, which promotes smoother trajectories compared to direct global pose mapping.
- **Gripper Control:** The right joystick dictates in-hand object roll and pitch, the left joystick's y-axis controls translation, and the left trigger adjusts the gripper opening size. This intuitive mapping facilitated rapid human adaptation and demonstration data collection.

**Experimental Evaluation**
The methodology includes five distinct experiments:
1. Benchmarking in-hand motion to confirm the independence and range of the added DoFs.
2. Evaluating the MPC performance for precise object manipulation.
3. Demonstrating teleoperated manipulation of everyday objects in challenging scenarios.
4. Assessing the fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on the gripper for complex tasks.
5. Comparative benchmarking against conventional parallel grippers to quantify the advantages of the additional in-hand DoFs in terms of success rate, efficiency, and user experience.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings derived from the evaluation of the Belt-Finger gripper across various tasks and control paradigms.

**Motion Benchmarking for In-Hand Movement:**
The Belt-Finger successfully demonstrated three independent in-hand degrees of freedom: translation, pitch, and roll. Across seven diverse objects, the gripper achieved approximately 45 mm of translation, 85° of pitch, and continuous roll. Minor variations in speed and precision were observed, attributed to teleoperation, alongside some pitch jitter from restricted camera views. Payload measurements indicated maximum capabilities of 9 N for translation, 100 N·mm for roll, and 50 N·mm for pitch. Asymmetric translational forces were observed, which the authors attribute to the non-parallel finger design. The gripper demonstrated adaptability to tapered surfaces, showing similar force-torque values for cylinder and cone shapes. After moderate usage (approximately 40 hours), a mean payload decay of 7.5% was measured. The gripper is reported to effectively handle most objects under 400g.

**Object Manipulation Through iCEM-based MPC:**
The iCEM-based MPC framework was evaluated for its ability to manipulate objects in-hand. Using a stationary robotic arm, the gripper successfully reoriented and translated benchmark objects towards target poses. The MPC demonstrated effective performance in "translation-only," "easy combi," and "medium combi" target difficulties, with errors predominantly minimized against orientation. For instance, in translation-only tasks, the final pose error averaged 4.8 ± 1.5mm for position and 9.1 ± 3.3° for orientation. In the "medium combi" tasks requiring all three motions, errors were 6.7 ± 4.1mm and 12.1 ± 7.3°. Even for "impossible combi" targets, which violate force-closure and are theoretically unreachable, the MPC minimized errors (10.9 ± 4.5mm, 42.1 ± 7.0°). Further tests on five complex objects yielded average pose errors of approximately 9.4-14 mm for translation and 13-33° for rotation across different movements. The soft belt's wrapping effect was identified as crucial for enabling these complex in-hand rotations.

**Manipulation of Daily Objects (Teleoperation):**
Teleoperation demonstrations showcased the gripper's capabilities in two challenging daily tasks:

- **Table Cleaning:** The gripper efficiently picked, reoriented, and placed irregularly shaped objects (e.g., LEGOs, pens, tweezers) in constrained spaces, often requiring combined translational and pitch motions. This task highlighted the gripper's ability to perform reorientations without necessitating large robot arm movements.
- **Card Insertion:** For tasks like picking and inserting a bank card into vertical and 15° sloped slots, the gripper's fine-grained pitch motion enabled precise alignment, thereby reducing the need for significant arm adjustments.

**Object Manipulation Through Imitation Learning (IL) with VLA Models:**
The Belt-Finger was successfully integrated with and evaluated using fine-tuned Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Despite the novelty of adapting pre-trained VLAs to a multi-DoF mechanism like the Belt-Finger, the system achieved notable results. Using the teleoperation setup, 150 diverse trajectories per task were collected for five daily tasks requiring complex in-hand manipulation. Three state-of-the-art VLA models (π0.5, GR00T N1.7, and ACT) were fine-tuned. GR00T N1.7 and π0.5 achieved high success rates across these tasks, including 100% for "lego_reorientation" and 85% for "pick_and_insert_tea_bag." The policies exhibited emergent, closed-loop correction behaviors using the active belts during inference, demonstrating successful leverage of the gripper's dexterity by the learning models.

**Benchmarking for Manipulation with and without In-Hand Motion:**
A comparative study involving a pick-rotate-insert task (inserting a pen into a holder) with five participants revealed that using the Belt-Finger significantly reduced both task failure rates and execution times compared to a conventional parallel gripper. All participants reportedly preferred the Belt-Finger, citing its intuitive in-hand reorientation capabilities over the complex and singularity-prone arm motions required by traditional grippers. A broader 10-task benchmark showed a 100% success rate for the Belt-Finger, in contrast to a 50% failure rate for conventional parallel fingers, particularly for tasks requiring in-hand pitch and roll. The Belt-Finger also demonstrated increased grasping stability and efficiency, for instance, by enabling single-step flipping motions and addressing "insufficient grasping" for small objects.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- The double-belt layout restricts pitch for small objects and limits the usable workspace for very thin, large, or concave shapes.
- Manipulability is dependent on friction, adhesion of the coating, and pressure distribution, leading to non-linear and less repeatable rotations across different surfaces, contamination, and wear.
- TPU belts introduce elasticity and hysteresis, potentially reducing precision, and long-term wear (stretching, tooth wear, coating degradation) is not fully characterized.
- Force/torque limits are imposed by belt traction and compact actuators.
- In-hand motion is conditioned on robot dynamics and object properties, which are partially simplified in the MPC controller.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the Belt-Finger gripper, an affordable, soft belt-driven end-effector designed to enhance the dexterous in-hand manipulation capabilities of conventional parallel two-finger grippers. The work holds several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of robotics.

Firstly, the Belt-Finger addresses a critical gap in robotic manipulation capabilities. By providing three additional in-hand degrees of freedom (translation, pitch, and roll) to a standard parallel gripper, it offers a cost-effective solution for tasks that traditionally demand either extensive robot arm movements or significantly more expensive and complex multi-fingered robotic hands. This design allows robots to perform fine reorientations and adjustments without requiring re-grasps or large arm motions, thereby increasing operational efficiency and enabling manipulation in confined spaces.

Secondly, the emphasis on affordability and open-source design is notable. Comprising 3D-printable and inexpensive off-the-shelf components, the Belt-Finger has a low material cost. This accessibility can facilitate broader adoption in academic research, small-scale industrial applications, and potentially even household robotics, making advanced manipulation capabilities available to a wider community.

Thirdly, the versatility demonstrated through various control paradigms underscores the gripper's robust utility.
- The intuitive and robust teleoperation system allows for efficient human-in-the-loop control, which is valuable for complex tasks (e.g., card insertion, table cleaning) and for generating human demonstrations to train learning models.
- The implementation of an iCEM-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) system enables precise, autonomous in-hand manipulation of known objects. This demonstrates a path toward autonomous dexterity even with the complexities introduced by soft, non-constant sliding contacts.
- Crucially, the successful fine-tuning of state-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (π0.5, GR00T N1.7, ACT) to leverage the Belt-Finger's multi-DoF capabilities represents an advancement in generalist robotics. This compatibility suggests that the Belt-Finger can bridge high-level reasoning from VLA models with low-level dexterity, enabling robots to tackle complex, real-world tasks that are otherwise inaccessible to standard parallel grippers. The observation of emergent, closed-loop correction behaviors from these policies further validates this integration.

Finally, comparative evaluations highlight tangible improvements over conventional parallel grippers. User studies and task benchmarks confirm significant reductions in task failure rates and execution times, coupled with improved user satisfaction due to more intuitive in-hand object reorientation. This tangible performance enhancement across various metrics positions the Belt-Finger as a practical upgrade for many robotic systems.

Looking ahead, the research points towards promising future directions, such as employing data-driven model-based reinforcement learning to address current modeling complexities and integrating additional sensing capabilities into the fingers. These future developments could further enhance the gripper's autonomy, adaptability, and precision, solidifying its potential to influence the development of more dexterous, capable, and accessible robotic manipulators.
