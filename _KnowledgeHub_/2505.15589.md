# World Models as Reference Trajectories for Rapid Motor Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Reflexive World Models (RWM) framework combines model-based reinforcement learning with adaptive control by interpreting learned world model predictions as implicit reference trajectories. This dual-component system enables rapid online adaptation of learned policies to unexpected changes in system dynamics, demonstrating performance recovery in robotic tasks with significantly reduced computational cost.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies, especially model-based ones, degrade performance when real-world system dynamics unexpectedly change.
- Current online adaptation methods in RL are computationally expensive, hindering real-time deployment in resource-constrained systems.
- Integrating the data-driven flexibility of RL with the rapid, formally guaranteed adaptation of classical control requires defining implicit reference trajectories for complex learned systems.

## Method

- A dual-architecture framework, **Reflexive World Models** (RWM), separates a pre-trained RL base policy and world model from a lightweight, online adaptive control module.
- The learned forward model's predictions of future latent states are used as implicit, task-relevant reference trajectories for the adaptive controller.
- The adaptive controller minimizes the squared error between the world model's predictions and actual observed latent states by adjusting its corrective actions in real-time through gradient-based updates.

## Results

- RWM achieved rapid adaptation to step perturbations and sustained performance under continuous nonstationary dynamics, outperforming TD-MPC2 and unadapted baselines in DeepMind Control Suite tasks.
- The framework demonstrated significant computational efficiency, with online adaptation requiring 1.8 hours compared to 16.5 hours for full TD-MPC2 retraining over 1 million environment steps.
- RWM's explicit online adaptation yielded higher median normalized reward (**0.6003**) during active perturbations compared to domain-randomized baselines (**0.3600** for TD-MPC2), highlighting its effectiveness over offline robustness alone.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World model predictions, which capture expected system behavior, can effectively serve as implicit reference trajectories, enabling classical adaptive control within an RL framework.
> - Decomposing the control problem into slow policy learning and rapid error correction allows for more efficient and robust adaptation to dynamic changes.
> - Minimizing the world model's prediction error by adjusting actions (adaptive control) rather than model parameters (model learning) enables a principled way to force the system to conform to learned expectations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research operates at the intersection of model-based reinforcement learning (RL) and classical adaptive control. Model-based RL methods have demonstrated capabilities in continuous control by employing learned world models to predict future states, facilitating efficient planning and policy optimization. However, a persistent challenge in deploying these systems is their degradation in performance when system dynamics change unexpectedly, often necessitating extensive retraining or specialized online adaptation mechanisms.

Conversely, classical adaptive control offers robust solutions for changing dynamics with formal stability guarantees, achieved by continuously adjusting control parameters based on tracking errors relative to explicit reference trajectories. However, these methods typically require predefined objectives, structured dynamics models, and hand-engineered cost functions, limiting their applicability to complex, high-dimensional problems where such explicit definitions are often unavailable.

Prior efforts to bridge these domains have included techniques like Deep Model Reference Adaptive Control, Rapid Motor Adaptation, Residual Policy Learning, meta-learning, and domain randomization. While these approaches have introduced some forms of online adaptation or improved robustness, they have often involved complex architectures, specific architectural constraints, or relied on extensive offline training to generalize to known variations. A general framework that combines the flexibility of learned policies with the rapid adaptation and robustness of control theory, without sacrificing computational efficiency, has remained an area of ongoing research. This work addresses this gap by proposing a method that leverages learned world model predictions as implicit reference trajectories, thereby enabling classical control techniques within an RL framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework for rapid motor adaptation that enables learned control policies to maintain performance in real-world environments with unexpectedly changing system dynamics, without necessitating extensive retraining or high online computational costs.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in deploying modern RL agents:
- **Performance Degradation under Dynamic Shifts:** Learned control policies, particularly those derived from model-based RL, often exhibit significant performance degradation when environmental conditions, actuator properties, or other system dynamics change post-training. This necessitates computationally intensive retraining or specialized robustness procedures.
- **Computational Cost of Adaptation:** Existing adaptation strategies in RL, such as online model retraining or complex planning horizons in Model Predictive Control, can incur substantial per-step operational costs, making them impractical for real-time deployment in resource-constrained or fast-reacting systems.
- **Bridging RL and Adaptive Control:** There is a recognized gap between the data-driven flexibility and high-dimensional capabilities of modern RL and the formal stability guarantees and rapid adaptation of classical adaptive control. RL excels at learning from abstract rewards and high-dimensional observations but often lacks fast, provable adaptation. Adaptive control offers speed and guarantees but requires explicit models and reference trajectories.
- **Need for Implicit Reference Trajectories:** Classical adaptive control relies on explicit reference trajectories. In complex, learned systems, defining these references manually is difficult. The authors hypothesize that world model predictions, which already capture expected system behavior, could serve as implicit, task-relevant reference trajectories.
- **Decomposition of Control Problems:** The work is motivated by the observation that motor control involves processes operating at different timescales: slower policy learning for long-term reward maximization and rapid error correction for maintaining execution fidelity under perturbations. Decomposing these objectives into distinct modules could lead to more efficient and robust adaptation.

To address these motivations, the research aims to establish a dual control framework, Reflexive World Models (RWM), that uses world model predictions as implicit reference trajectories for a rapid adaptation module. This framework seeks to achieve significantly faster adaptation with low online computational cost, while maintaining near-optimal performance under varying dynamics, and providing theoretical performance bounds.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Reflexive World Models (RWM) framework introduces a dual control architecture designed to separate long-term reward maximization from rapid motor execution and error correction.

**Dual Architecture:**
The RWM framework consists of two main modules:
1. **Reinforcement Learning Module (Base Policy, $\pi_0$):** This module learns a base policy, $\pi_0(z)$, that optimizes long-term reward in a learned continuous, normalized latent space $z = e(s)$. An encoder $e$ maps high-dimensional observations $s$ to this latent space. A forward model $F$ is also learned, predicting future latent states $\hat{z}_{t+1} = F(z_t, \pi_0(z_t))$. This module's parameters (encoder, base policy, forward model) are pre-trained using model-based RL methods, specifically drawing inspiration from TD-MPC2.
2. **Adaptive Control Module ($\pi_c$):** This module is responsible for rapid error correction. It generates corrective actions, $a_c$, that are added to the base policy's actions, such that the total action executed is $a_t = \pi_0(z_t) + \pi_c(z_t)$. This module operates in real-time to minimize discrepancies between the world model's predictions and actual observed latent states.

**Core Mechanism: World Model Predictions as Reference Trajectories:**
A central conceptual novelty of RWM is the interpretation of the forward model's predictions ($\hat{z}_{t+1}$) as target or reference states for the adaptive controller. The forward model itself learns to predict observed outcomes, while the control module adapts actions to make the system behave *as predicted* by the world model. Both the forward model and the control module share the same error function:
$L = \lVert \hat{z}_{t+1} - z_{t+1} \rVert^2$
This Mean Squared Error (MSE) is used as a discrepancy measure in the normalized latent space. The forward model minimizes this error by adjusting its internal parameters to improve predictions, following standard gradient descent. In contrast, the control module minimizes this error by adjusting its *actions* to align the system's actual behavior ($z_{t+1}$) with the forward model's predictions ($\hat{z}_{t+1}$).

**Adaptive Control Gradient:**
The adaptive control policy $\pi_c$ is updated by leveraging gradients through the world model to determine how actions should change to reduce the prediction error. This represents an inversion of the standard gradient flow in model learning. The update rule for the control module's parameters $\theta_c$ is given by:
$\theta_c \leftarrow \theta_c - \eta_c \left[ -\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_0} \frac{\partial a_c}{\partial \theta_c} \right]$
Here, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_0}$ determines the sensitivity of the prediction error to the base policy's action, effectively guiding the corrective action $a_c$ to reduce the error. The adaptive controller treats the world model's predictions as fixed targets and adapts its actions to achieve them. The operation in latent space ensures the controller focuses on task-relevant features and provides an interface between the RL policy and the adaptation module.

**Value Function Decomposition and Theoretical Guarantees:**
The framework formalizes the separation of policy learning and rapid adaptation by decomposing the Taylor expansion of the value function around optimal trajectories in latent space:
$V(z_{t+1} + \Delta z) = V(z_{t+1}) - \frac{1}{2} \Delta z^T H \Delta z + O(\lVert \Delta z \rVert^3)$
where $H = \nabla^2 V(z_{t+1})$ is positive definite near the optimum. This decomposition links maximizing mean value (policy optimization) with minimizing deviations (rapid adaptation). The paper also provides theoretical guarantees (Theorems 4.2 and 4.3) on the control error and value bounds under certain assumptions on system properties (Lipschitz control, control authority, model accuracy, bounded perturbation). These theorems establish quantitative relationships between world model accuracy, control authority, perturbation magnitude, and performance bounds.

**Experimental Setup:**
Simulations were conducted using the DeepMind Control Suite (e.g., humanoid-walk, walker-walk).
- **Baseline Policy Pre-training:** A TD-MPC2 agent was pre-trained for 1 million environment steps to provide the encoder $e$, base policy $\pi_0$, and forward dynamics model $F$.
- **Thresholded Action Cost:** To ensure the base policy $\pi_0$ provided differentiable, non-saturated actions suitable for the adaptive controller, a thresholded quadratic action cost, $\lambda \sum_i (\max(0, |a_i| - c))^2$, was incorporated during its pre-training. This prevents action saturation and policy inaction.
- **RWM Controller Training:** The RWM controller $\pi_c$ is an MLP trained online to minimize the squared error between the forward model's latent state predictions and observed next latent states. During this phase, parameters of the pre-trained TD-MPC2 components are frozen. The learning rate for $\pi_c$ was set to $3 \times 10^{-4}$.
- **Perturbation Protocols:** Adaptation was evaluated by introducing multiplicative step perturbations (sudden changes) and continuous filtered noise perturbations (gradual shifts) to actuator gains, with values sampled uniformly from $[-0.5, 0.5]$. These perturbations altered actuator outputs for periods, followed by perturbation-free intervals.
- **Performance Metrics:** Task performance (reward) and forward model error (as an implicit measure of latent trajectory deviation) were tracked. Normalized median reward and control error aggregated across perturbation cycles were used for comparative analysis.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Execution times for RWM's online adaptation phase were compared against full TD-MPC2 training and inference without adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several key findings across various continuous control tasks:

**Rapid Adaptation to Step Perturbations:**
- In the 2D point mass task, the RWM system demonstrated its core mechanism by correcting trajectory deviations under angular perturbations. When perturbations were removed, characteristic aftereffects were observed (Fig. 1-C), indicating adaptation through internal model formation rather than purely reactive control.
- In the Walker2D environment, under step perturbations to actuator gains, RWM consistently maintained higher reward and lower control error compared to the "No Adaptation" baseline and a full TD-MPC2 baseline (Fig. 2). RWM exhibited rapid recovery, reducing the error between predicted and actual latent states shortly after perturbation onset. Normalized median reward and control error metrics, aggregated across perturbation cycles, quantitatively demonstrated RWM's superior adaptation capabilities, showing effective and rapid recovery.

**Robustness to Nonstationary Dynamics:**
- A crucial test involved continuously varying perturbations (filtered noise) to actuator gains in the Walker2D environment, simulating gradual deterioration or drift. RWM maintained a performance of 360.56, outperforming TD-MPC2 (311.67) and the fixed baseline (233.42) (Fig. 3A). This performance advantage was associated with systematically lower control error for RWM throughout the adaptation process. These results suggest RWM's ability to maintain stable performance under unpredictable and evolving system dynamics without policy retraining.

**High-Dimensional Coordination:**
- In the 17-actuator Humanoid environment, successful locomotion requires synchronized multi-joint movements. Under perturbations, the unadapted system showed degraded coordination. RWM, by tracking latent predictions that capture coordinated multi-joint relationships, maintained coherent movement patterns and overall balance (Fig. 3B), indicating its effectiveness in complex, high-dimensional control scenarios.

**Computational Efficiency:**
- RWM's online adaptation phase exhibited significantly lower computational cost compared to full TD-MPC2 training. For 1 million environment steps under perturbation, RWM required approximately 1.8 hours, while full TD-MPC2 training took 16.5 hours on a single NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (Table 1). Inference without adaptation took 1.4 hours. This efficiency stems from RWM's lightweight controller updates using pre-trained components, avoiding expensive planning rollouts or complete model retraining.

**Comparison with Domain-Randomized Baselines:**
- To assess the unique benefits of RWM's online adaptation, a comparison was made with baseline agents (TD-MPC2 and "No Adaptation") pre-trained with exposure to randomized actuator perturbations. While this pre-training improved the baseline's nominal performance, RWM's online adaptation still provided significant advantages during active perturbation phases.
- During perturbation ON periods, RWM maintained a median normalized reward of 0.6003 and a median normalized control error of 0.7307 (Table 2, Fig. 5). In contrast, the domain-randomized TD-MPC2 baseline achieved a median normalized reward of 0.3600 and a median normalized control error of 1.4162. The "No Adaptation" policy, even with domain randomization, performed worst (median reward 0.1150, control error 0.9676 during perturbation). These results suggest that RWM's explicit online adaptation mechanism offers more effective real-time compensation for unmodeled dynamics than offline robustness techniques alone.

**Impact of Thresholded Action Cost:**
- The inclusion of a thresholded quadratic action cost during base policy pre-training was found to be crucial. It prevented action saturation, which would otherwise lead to vanishing gradients for the adaptive controller, and mitigated "policy inaction" (the "dead problem") caused by naive quadratic penalties, thus ensuring a differentiable and responsive base policy (Fig. 4).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Reflexive World Models (RWM) framework contributes to the fields of reinforcement learning and adaptive control by presenting a method for rapid motor adaptation under varying dynamics with notable properties:

**Theoretical Foundations:** The work provides a formal analysis through value function decomposition and theoretical guarantees (Theorems 4.2 and 4.3). These theorems establish quantitative bounds linking world model accuracy, control authority, and perturbation magnitude to performance, bridging between flexible learning and provable robustness. This analytical tractability, facilitated by RWM's dual-component architecture, addresses a limitation often present in complex, end-to-end learned RL systems where deriving such formal guarantees is challenging.

**Bridge Between RL and Adaptive Control:** RWM integrates the benefits of flexible policy learning from reinforcement learning (learning from abstract rewards in high-dimensional spaces) with the rapid error correction capabilities and robustness principles of classical adaptive control (trajectory tracking). By transforming world model predictions into implicit reference trajectories, it offers a principled approach to maintaining performance without relying on explicit, hand-engineered reference models.

**Enhanced Adaptability and Robustness:** The empirical results demonstrate RWM's ability to achieve significantly faster adaptation and maintain higher performance than model-based RL baselines under various dynamic changes, including step perturbations and continuous, nonstationary actuator gain variations. Its capability to handle high-dimensional coordination in complex environments like the Humanoid suggests broad applicability.

**Computational Efficiency:** A significant practical implication is the low online computational cost of RWM's adaptation phase. By using lightweight controller updates driven by single-step latent predictions, it avoids the substantial per-step operational costs associated with planning rollouts or extensive model retraining, which are common in other adaptive strategies. This efficiency positions RWM for potential deployment in real-world systems with real-time constraints.

**Potential for Understanding Biological Motor Control:** The framework's inherent dual-timescale operation, with slower policy learning and rapid error correction, aligns with hierarchical control architectures observed in biological motor control. This separation of intentional variability from execution variability could offer new avenues for understanding and modeling biological motor adaptation.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors identify several limitations and directions for future research:
- **Latent Representation Optimization:** Future work could focus on learning latent representations specifically optimized for RWM's adaptive control objective, potentially extending its applicability to arbitrary pre-trained policies.
- **End-to-End Joint Learning:** While the current phased training simplifies analysis, developing methods for end-to-end joint learning of the base policy, world model, and adaptive controller could present challenges related to controller exploitation of model inaccuracies.
- **Broader Perturbation Handling:** Expanding RWM's capabilities beyond actuator perturbations to address morphological changes, environmental shifts, or sensor noise would broaden its real-world applicability. This may require incorporating uncertainty estimation into the world model or developing techniques to disambiguate different error sources.

Overall, RWM offers a novel and effective approach to real-time adaptation in continuous control, combining theoretical rigor with strong empirical performance and practical efficiency.
