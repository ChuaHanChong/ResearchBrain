# DemoDiffusion: One-Shot Human Imitation using pre-trained Diffusion Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Carnegie Mellon University researchers developed DemoDiffusion, a method that allows robots to execute manipulation tasks by imitating a single human demonstration. It integrates kinematically retargeted human movements with a pre-trained generalist diffusion policy, achieving an 83.8% average success rate across eight real-world tasks, outperforming direct policy deployment (13.8%) and open-loop retargeting (52.5%).

## Problem

- Existing generalist robot policies struggle with zero-shot deployment to novel environments or unseen tasks, often requiring costly robot-specific fine-tuning.
- Current one-shot imitation from human demonstrations often fails due to embodiment mismatches or necessitates extensive online interaction for adaptation.
- There is a need for a practical, user-friendly method to program robots for new manipulation tasks in unstructured environments.

## Method

- A methodology is proposed that combines kinematic retargeting of human hand motions with a pre-trained diffusion policy.
- 3D human hand poses from a single demonstration are extracted and geometrically mapped to an initial open-loop robot end-effector trajectory.
- This trajectory is then refined through a closed-loop denoising process by a pre-trained generalist diffusion policy, starting from an intermediate noise level, to generate feasible robot actions.

## Results

- Achieved an average **83.8%** success rate across eight real-world manipulation tasks with a two-finger gripper, significantly exceeding direct generalist policy deployment (**13.8%**) and open-loop kinematic retargeting (**52.5%**).
- Demonstrated superior performance in dexterous grasping simulation for unseen objects with a 16-DOF hand, yielding **31.0%** success compared to **1.6%** for retargeting and **26.5%** for the base policy.
- Exhibited robustness to noisy human motion capture data and variations in kinematic retargeting, while also generalizing to new objects within the same category.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human hand trajectories provide a useful approximate prior for robot end-effector motions, which can be extracted via kinematic retargeting.
> - Pre-trained generalist diffusion policies are capable of refining these approximate trajectories to overcome embodiment mismatches and generate robust, closed-loop robot actions.
> - Initiating the diffusion policy's denoising process from an intermediate noise level applied to the retargeted trajectory effectively blends human guidance with the policy's learned behavior manifold.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is actively pursuing the development of "generalist" policies capable of performing a wide array of tasks in unstructured human environments. Recent efforts have focused on training such policies using large-scale demonstration datasets, often combining behavior cloning with vision-language pre-training-. While these generalist policies have demonstrated versatility, they frequently encounter difficulties when deployed zero-shot to novel environments or tasked with unseen operations, typically necessitating additional fine-tuning with task-and-scene-specific robot demonstrations. This fine-tuning process represents a significant logistical and temporal overhead, particularly as collecting robot demonstrations in real-world settings can be labor-intensive and beyond the capabilities of an average user.

This work proposes an alternative deployment mechanism that leverages pre-trained generalist policies to perform tasks by imitating a single human demonstration. This approach situates itself within the broader context of human-to-robot imitation, an area that has explored several methodologies:

- **Kinematic Retargeting:** Some methods directly map human hand poses to robot end-effector configurations-. While straightforward and enabling one-shot deployment, these approaches often suffer from human-robot embodiment mismatches, leading to imprecise actions. Their open-loop execution also renders them brittle to noise and variations in the environment.
- **Online Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Another line of research attempts to learn robot policies through online reinforcement learning, where human demonstrations help define reward functions-. This can address the embodiment gap, but it typically demands hours of online interaction and resets during test time, limiting its practicality for generic, real-world manipulation tasks, especially in safety-critical contexts.
- **Manipulation with Non-Robot Datasets:** A growing body of work utilizes human videos and large-scale web videos for robotics, leveraging advances in computer vision for representation learning, tracking, and reconstructing hand-object interactions-. These methods primarily use human videos for *training* robot policies (e.g., learning visual representations or predicting manipulation-relevant cues), often combined with limited robot interaction data. DemoDiffusion is distinct in that it uses a *single human demonstration during deployment* to guide a pre-trained policy, rather than for policy training.
- **One-Shot Imitation from Robot Demonstrations:** Prior works have also explored one-shot imitation using a single robot demonstration provided at test time-. However, requiring a robot demonstration can be restrictive for ubiquitous deployment by end-users.

DemoDiffusion distinguishes itself by aiming for a method that, similar to kinematic retargeting, can be deployed one-shot (without test-time training), yet benefits from learned priors for precise, closed-loop interaction, thereby circumventing the need for extensive paired human-robot data collection or cumbersome test-time fine-tuning via interaction. It seeks to unlock the potential of pre-trained generalist diffusion policies by providing them with task-specific guidance from an easily acquired human demonstration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic manipulation system that can be readily deployed in unstructured human environments with minimal effort. Specifically, the authors aim to enable robots to perform novel manipulation tasks by imitating a *single human demonstration*, without the need for task-specific training or the collection of paired human-robot data.

This objective is motivated by several limitations observed in current robot manipulation paradigms:

1. **Limited Generalization of Pre-trained Policies:** While large-scale datasets have enabled the training of "generalist" robot policies, these policies often struggle with zero-shot generalization to tasks or environments not encountered during training. Successful deployment frequently requires costly and time-consuming fine-tuning with robot-specific demonstrations for each new task or scene. The goal is to make these generalist policies more readily applicable to novel situations.
2. **Brittleness of Kinematic Retargeting:** Traditional kinematic retargeting approaches, which translate human hand motions directly to robot end-effector movements, are simple and one-shot. However, they are inherently limited by the significant embodiment mismatch between humans and robots, as well as the absence of closed-loop feedback during execution. This leads to brittle performance, especially in dynamic or variable environments. The motivation is to retain the one-shot nature while enhancing robustness and precision.
3. **Impracticality of Online Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Generic Tasks:** While online RL can potentially overcome the embodiment gap by learning from interaction, it typically requires extensive online experimentation and numerous resets during deployment (often hours), rendering it impractical for rapid, generic task deployment in real-world, safety-critical scenarios. The authors seek a method that avoids such prolonged test-time interaction.

To address these challenges, DemoDiffusion is built upon two core insights:

1. **Human Hand Motion as a Useful Prior:** The trajectory of a human hand during a demonstration provides valuable high-level information about the desired end-effector motion for the robot. This information can be extracted and converted into a rough, open-loop robot motion trajectory through kinematic retargeting. This serves as an initial approximation of the task intent.
2. **Refinement with Pre-trained Diffusion Policies:** Although the kinematically retargeted trajectory captures the overall structure of the task, it may not represent "plausible robot actions in-context" due to embodiment differences and the lack of real-time environmental feedback. Pre-trained generalist diffusion policies, which model the distribution of feasible robot actions, can be leveraged to refine this approximate trajectory. By guiding the denoising process of the diffusion policy with the human-derived motion, the robot can generate actions that are both faithful to the human demonstration and consistent with its own physical capabilities and environmental interactions in a closed-loop manner.

Therefore, the key motivation is to combine the efficiency of one-shot learning from human demonstrations with the robustness and precision offered by learned robot priors, effectively bridging the human-robot embodiment gap without additional policy training or extensive online interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DemoDiffusion addresses the problem of one-shot visual imitation, where a robot must perform a manipulation task (T) demonstrated by a human (D). The approach relies on a pre-trained, closed-loop diffusion policy, ¯πθ(a_t | o_≤t, T), which has been trained on a diverse dataset of robot interaction data (D_robot). The human demonstration is assumed to be minimal, such as an RGBD or multi-view video, from which 3D hand poses can be reliably extracted.

The methodology consists of two main steps:

#### 3.1. A. Kinematic Retargeting of Human Hand Trajectories to Robot End-Effector Poses

1. **Human Hand Pose Extraction:** From the given human demonstration video, the 3D hand pose trajectory {h_t}^T_t=0 is extracted. Each hand pose h_t is represented by the 3D locations of *J* keypoints (e.g., wrist and fingertips). These keypoints are estimated using an off-the-shelf pre-trained monocular hand pose estimator, such as Hamer.
2. **Geometric Mapping (f_retarget):** A simple geometric function, f_retarget: R^(3xJ) → R^6, is defined to convert the human hand pose h_t into a corresponding robot configuration ˆa_t.
 - The mapping aligns the human's wrist pose (position and orientation) to the robot's end-effector pose.
 - For a two-finger gripper, the distance between the human's thumb and the average of other fingertips is used to infer a binary robot grasp action (open/close).
 - For dexterous robotic hands, the fingertip positions of the robot hand are matched to those of the human hand using inverse kinematics.
3. **Trajectory Generation:** This procedure yields an open-loop robot end-effector trajectory {ˆa_t}^T_t=0 in the robot's configuration space. The initial robot configuration is set to the kinematically retargeted initial configuration from the human demonstration.

While this open-loop trajectory captures the overall motion, it often proves suboptimal or unstable during execution due to inherent differences in human and robot morphology (embodiment mismatch), the absence of real-time environmental feedback during retargeting, and potential inaccuracies in the initial hand pose estimation. Therefore, this retargeted trajectory serves as an approximate initial estimate for the next stage.

#### 3.2. B. Closed-Loop Denoising of Robot Actions with a Pre-Trained Diffusion Policy

The second step refines the kinematically retargeted trajectory using a pre-trained generalist diffusion policy, ¯πθ(a_t | o_≤t, T). This policy reliably models the distribution of plausible robot actions given the current and past observations (o_≤t) and the task description (T).

1. **Modified Reverse Diffusion Process:** Inspired by SDEdit, DemoDiffusion modifies the standard reverse diffusion process. Instead of starting with pure Gaussian noise at the maximum diffusion step *S*, the process begins from an intermediate diffusion step *s*∗ (where 0 < *s*∗ < *S*).
2. **Noisy Initialization:** A noisy version of the kinematically retargeted trajectory, {˜a_t^(s*)} is generated by perturbing the original {ˆa_t} with Gaussian noise:
 ˜a_t^(s*) = √(α_s*) ˆa_t + √(1 - α_s*) ε_t, where ε_t ∼ N(0, I).
 Here, α_s* corresponds to the diffusion schedule of the pre-trained policy. This step intuitively projects the potentially unfeasible but approximately correct retargeted trajectory closer to the manifold of plausible robot actions.
3. **Iterative Denoising and Refinement:** The pre-trained diffusion policy ¯πθ then iteratively denoises this noisy trajectory from step *s*∗ down to 0. Crucially, this denoising process is conditioned on the robot's real-time observations (o_≤t) from its onboard cameras. This closed-loop mechanism allows the policy to adapt the human-derived trajectory to the robot's own embodiment and the current environment, compensating for any embodiment mismatches or external perturbations (e.g., object slippage, occlusion).
4. **Final Action Output:** After *s*∗ denoising steps, the final output a_t = ˜a_t^(0) represents the refined, executable sequence of robot actions, which is then deployed on the robot.

The key hyperparameter in this process is *s*∗, which dictates the trade-off between faithfulness to the human demonstration and adherence to the robot policy's distribution of plausible actions. A lower *s*∗ emphasizes the human demonstration, while a higher *s*∗ allows the robot policy more influence. The authors note that this iterative refinement concept is also applicable to flow-matching models, which is relevant as their real-world experiments utilize a flow-matching policy (Pi-0).

#### 3.3. Real-World Implementation Details

- **Hardware:** Franka Emika Panda arm with a Robotiq two-finger gripper.
- **Pre-trained Policy:** Pi-0, a publicly available flow-matching policy trained on the DROID dataset. It takes a language task specification and observations from two cameras, outputting per-timestep joint velocity. This policy is used without fine-tuning.
- **Vision System:** Four external Realsense cameras are used for triangulating 3D human hand poses (using Hamer). A Zed-Mini wrist camera is also used. The Pi-0 policy utilizes observations from two specific external cameras.
- **Action Space:** The kinematically retargeted end-effector poses are converted to joint velocities via inverse kinematics before adding noise and denoising with Pi-0. Gripper actions are set to fully closed if the inferred grip width (from thumb-to-finger distance) is below 80% of the maximum.
- **Hyperparameter `s*/S` Selection:** For real-world tasks, `s*/S` is heuristically chosen: 0.2 when the base Pi-0 policy has a zero success rate, and 0.4 otherwise, reflecting a balance between human guidance and policy adherence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DemoDiffusion was evaluated through experiments in both a simulated dexterous grasping environment and real-world manipulation tasks. The investigations aimed to assess its performance against baselines, its ability to tackle tasks where the pre-trained policy fails, the impact of the `s*`/`S` hyperparameter, and its robustness to input imperfections.

#### 4.1. A. Dexterous Grasping in Simulation

**Setup:**
A simulation environment was used for picking up generic objects with a 16-DOF Allegro hand. A diffusion policy was trained on 985 grasping trajectories across 58 generic training objects (from ShapeNet and PartNet). The method was tested on 1220 unseen objects from the Objaverse dataset, utilizing ground-truth human grasping trajectories (from GraspXL), thereby eliminating errors from hand estimation or initial object configuration.

**Baselines:**
1. Open-loop kinematic retargeting from the human demonstration.
2. Direct deployment of the trained robot policy without human demonstration guidance.

**Results (Table I and Fig. 3):**
- **DemoDiffusion** achieved an average success rate of **31.0%**.
- The **robot policy** alone achieved **26.5%**.
- **Kinematic retargeting** alone achieved **1.6%**.
- DemoDiffusion consistently outperformed both baselines across small, medium, and large object sizes, with the most significant gains observed on smaller objects.
- This demonstrates that while kinematic retargeting on its own is largely insufficient, using it to initiate refinement with a generalist grasping policy significantly improves performance.
- Furthermore, initiating the denoising process with a kinematically retargeted trajectory (DemoDiffusion) proved more effective than relying solely on the pre-trained robot policy, indicating that the human demonstration provides a valuable prior.
- DemoDiffusion also exhibited higher inference speed compared to the full denoising process of the base policy, proportional to `S`/`s*`.

**Ablation Study on `s*`/`S` (Fig. 3):**
- Analysis of the `s*`/`S` ratio showed a consistent trend of performance gain from `s*`/`S = 1` (pure robot policy) down to `s*`/`S = 0.2`. This indicates that DemoDiffusion is robust to the choice of this hyperparameter within a reasonable range, with an optimal performance observed around `s*`/`S = 0.2`.

#### 4.2. B. Real-World Manipulation

**Setup:**
Experiments were conducted using a Franka Emika Panda arm with a two-finger Robotiq gripper. The publicly available flow-matching policy Pi-0 was used as the pre-trained generalist policy. Eight diverse manipulation tasks (e.g., "Shut Down Laptop", "Wipe Table", "Pick up Bear") were evaluated (Fig. 4). A single human demonstration was provided per task, with natural variations in object placement and the inclusion of visual distractors during robot execution.

**Baselines:**
1. Open-loop kinematic retargeting.
2. Direct deployment of the Pi-0 model with language instructions.

**Qualitative Visualizations (Fig. 6):**
- **Pi-0** typically performed general reaching behaviors but often failed to follow the specific manipulation trajectory required or confused the target object with distractors.
- **Kinematic Retargeting** produced plausible general motions but frequently failed at crucial contact points (e.g., missed grasping the iron, lost contact with the laptop before full closure).
- **DemoDiffusion** successfully reached the target object, followed the approximate motion of the retargeted trajectory, and maintained contact to complete the tasks effectively.

**Quantitative Comparisons (Table II):**
- **DemoDiffusion** achieved an average success rate of **83.8%**.
- **Kinematic Retargeting** achieved **52.5%**.
- **Pi-0** achieved **13.8%**.
- DemoDiffusion either matched or significantly outperformed both baselines across all 8 tasks.
- Its performance gains were particularly notable in tasks requiring precise contact and sustained interaction, such as "Shut Down Laptop" (60% vs. 10-20%) and "Wipe Table" (100% vs. 0-50%).
- Notably, DemoDiffusion succeeded even on tasks where both Pi-0 and kinematic retargeting completely failed, demonstrating its ability to leverage the strengths of both components.

**Robustness to Noisy Human Demonstrations (Table III):**
- To evaluate robustness, a random 5cm shift was introduced to the tracked 3D human hand keypoints.
- While performance decreased (e.g., "Shut Laptop" from 60% to 40%, "Pick Place Banana" from 90% to 70%), DemoDiffusion maintained a high level of performance, suggesting resilience to inaccuracies in human motion tracking.

**Robustness to Different Kinematic Retargeting Methods (Table III):**
- An alternative kinematic retargeting method ("Thumb+Index," using only thumb-index distance for grasp inference) was tested. This alternative itself showed degraded performance compared to the primary retargeting method.
- Despite this suboptimal initial retargeting, DemoDiffusion still consistently improved task performance, highlighting its ability to refine imperfect inputs.

**Additional Results with Different `s*`/`S` Hyperparameters (Table VI):**
- Further analysis showed that a higher `s*`/`S` (e.g., 0.4) generally performed better when the base Pi-0 policy already achieved a non-zero success rate.
- Conversely, a lower `s*`/`S` (e.g., 0.2) tended to perform better when Pi-0 had a zero success rate. This suggests an adaptive strategy for selecting `s*`/`S` based on the perceived reliability of the base policy for a given task.

**Zero-Shot Generalization to New Objects (Fig. 8):**
- DemoDiffusion demonstrated the ability to generalize zero-shot to new objects (with different colors and shapes) within the same task category, using the same human demonstration and text prompt. This indicates its adaptability beyond the specific object shown in the demonstration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DemoDiffusion presents a method for robotic manipulation that addresses a critical challenge in deploying robots in dynamic, unstructured human environments: the ability to perform novel tasks with minimal prior knowledge or effort. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Robot Deployment Accessibility:** By enabling robots to learn from a single human demonstration without requiring task-specific training, paired human-robot data, or online fine-tuning, DemoDiffusion significantly lowers the barrier to entry for robot deployment. This could empower non-expert users to teach robots new skills, accelerating the adoption of robotics in various settings, including homes, offices, and small businesses, where expert roboticists are often unavailable.
2. **Effective Utilization of Generalist Policies:** The work demonstrates a practical mechanism to leverage the capabilities of pre-trained "generalist" diffusion policies, even in scenarios where these policies might otherwise struggle with zero-shot generalization. By guiding these policies with human demonstrations, DemoDiffusion unlocks their potential for a broader range of real-world tasks. This approach capitalizes on existing, powerful models, maximizing their utility without additional specialized training.
3. **Robust One-Shot Imitation:** DemoDiffusion effectively bridges the human-robot embodiment gap. It combines the high-level intent provided by kinematic retargeting with the low-level, closed-loop precision and contextual awareness of a pre-trained diffusion policy. This fusion results in robust, one-shot imitation capabilities that outperform brittle open-loop methods and avoid the computational and time costs of online reinforcement learning.
4. **Resilience to Input Imperfections:** The demonstrated robustness to noisy human demonstrations (e.g., tracking errors) and variations in kinematic retargeting enhances the method's practical applicability. In real-world scenarios, perfect data is rare, and the ability to function effectively with imperfect inputs is crucial for widespread adoption.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** DemoDiffusion can serve as a foundational approach for future efforts in human imitation. It suggests new directions for research, such as exploring adaptive methods for selecting the `s*`/`S` hyperparameter dynamically based on task characteristics or policy confidence. It could also inform improved exploration strategies for methods pursuing policy adaptation with online RL, providing a robust initialization for subsequent learning.

Despite its current limitations, such as the assumption that human and robot strategies should align, the inability to produce reusable task policies, and the challenges of accurate 3D human motion capture, DemoDiffusion represents a step toward more adaptable and user-friendly robotic systems. Its capacity for rapid, task-agnostic deployment from simple human guidance holds considerable promise for advancing autonomous manipulation in diverse and unstructured environments.
