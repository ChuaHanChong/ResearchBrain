# Open X-Embodiment: Robotic Learning Datasets and RT-X Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The OpenX-Embodiment Collaboration released the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) Dataset, a consolidated collection of over 1 million real robot trajectories from 22 embodiments. This work demonstrates that large RT-X models trained on such diverse data achieve positive transfer and emergent skills across different robot platforms.

## Problem

- Robotics lacks large-scale, diverse datasets comparable to those in NLP and computer vision, limiting the development of general-purpose, pre-trained models.
- Existing robotics datasets are typically small, specialized, and narrow in scope, which hinders generalization and requires extensive retraining for new applications.
- The 'embodiment gap' makes data transfer difficult between different robot platforms due to variations in kinematics, sensors, and action spaces.

## Method

- Consolidating 60 existing robot datasets from 21 institutions into the **Open X-Embodiment (OXE) Dataset**, comprising over 1 million real robot trajectories from 22 distinct embodiments.
- Standardizing all aggregated data into a consistent ==RLDS== data format to enable unified training.
- Training high-capacity ==Transformer==-based **RT-X** policies (**RT-1-X** and **RT-2-X**) on this combined dataset, with a focus on standardizing action and observation spaces.

## Results

- RT-1-X achieved notable positive transfer, outperforming original methods on **4 out of 5** small-scale datasets.
- The larger RT-2-X model (**55B** parameters) outperformed original methods and baselines even on data-rich domains, demonstrating the importance of model capacity for large-scale data utilization.
- RT-2-X showed approximately **3x** higher success rates in emergent skills, demonstrating robust knowledge transfer and the ability to perform tasks previously unseen by a specific robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pooling and standardizing diverse, large-scale robotics data is crucial for developing generalizable robot policies, mirroring successes in NLP and CV.
> - High-capacity models, like RT-2-X, are necessary to effectively leverage vast and heterogeneous robotics datasets for positive transfer.
> - Co-training on multi-embodiment data facilitates the emergence of novel skills and knowledge transfer across different robot platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper is positioned at the forefront of the growing movement to bring large-scale machine learning paradigms, which have revolutionized fields like natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV), to robotics.

In NLP, the rise of large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4 and PaLM 2, trained on billions of text tokens scraped from the web, has led to remarkable generalization capabilities and the emergence of "generalist" models. Similarly, in computer vision, models like CLIP, trained on vast image-text pairs, have demonstrated impressive open-vocabulary classification and zero-shot transfer. These successes are largely attributed to the immense scale and diversity of their training data, enabling the models to learn robust and transferable representations.

Robotics, however, has traditionally lagged in this regard. As the authors correctly point out:
- **Data Scarcity:** Compared to the billions of data points available for NLP and CV, even the largest robotics datasets are orders of magnitude smaller (5-18 million observations at best).
- **Data Narrowness:** Most existing robotics datasets are highly specialized, focusing on a single robot, a limited set of environments, or a narrow range of tasks. This leads to models that are often brittle, lack generalization, and require significant retraining for new applications, robots, or environments.
- **Embodiment Gap:** The physical nature of robotics means that data collected on one robot often does not directly transfer to another due to differences in kinematics, dynamics, sensors, and action spaces.

Prior work has attempted to address these challenges through various approaches:
- **Transfer Across Embodiments:** Research has explored methods to facilitate transfer between different robot platforms, both in simulation and real-world settings. These often involve shared action representations, learning transferable features, or adapting policies based on embodiment information. RoboNet was an early effort to collect multi-robot data, but the Open X-Embodiment dataset is significantly larger and more diverse in terms of number of robots and institutions.
- **Large-Scale Robot Learning Datasets:** Efforts have been made to create open-source datasets for specific tasks like grasping, pushing, or teleoperation. However, most of these focus on a single type of robot.
- **Language-Conditioned Robot Learning:** More recently, researchers have integrated language models into robotics to allow for natural language instruction following, often by leveraging pre-trained language or vision-language models.

This paper distinguishes itself by directly addressing the *fundamental data limitation* in robotics. Instead of focusing solely on new architectures or more complex transfer mechanisms, it posits that the *union* of existing, disparate robotics datasets can collectively provide the necessary scale and diversity for generalizable policies. By creating the **Open X-Embodiment (OXE) Dataset**, it aims to establish a de facto standard and a critical resource, mirroring the foundational datasets in other AI fields. The empirical validation with RT-X models then demonstrates that existing high-capacity architectures (like Transformers) *can* effectively leverage this pooled multi-embodiment data to achieve positive transfer and emergent capabilities, thereby validating the "large data" hypothesis for robotics. It is a significant step towards developing "foundation models" or "generalist agents" for robotics, a concept gaining rapid traction in the field (e.g., RoboCat, GNM, UniGrasp).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the Open X-Embodiment project stems from the observation that large-scale learning on diverse datasets has been a cornerstone of success in other AI domains like NLP and computer vision. The authors ask: **"Can such a consolidation [of pretrained models] happen in robotics?"** They aim to challenge the conventional robotics paradigm where a separate model is trained for every application, robot, and environment.

The core problem they seek to address is the **lack of diverse, large-scale datasets comparable to those in NLP and CV**, which limits the development of general-purpose, pre-trained models in robotics. While individual robotic datasets exist, they are often narrow in scope and suffer from limited coverage of variations in environments and robots.

To overcome these challenges and pursue the vision of "generalist" X-robot policies, the paper sets out two main objectives:

1. **Evaluate the Benefits of X-Embodiment Training:** To empirically determine if policies trained on a consolidated dataset from many different robots and environments can achieve **positive transfer**. This means assessing whether co-training on diverse data leads to better performance on individual robots compared to training on robot-specific data alone, and if it enables generalization to new, unseen tasks.
2. **Organize and Provide Large Robotic Datasets:** To build and release a comprehensive, standardized, and open-source dataset that enables the broader robotics community to conduct research on X-embodiment models. This involves creating the **Open X-Embodiment (OXE) Repository**, which includes both the massive dataset and pre-trained model checkpoints.

In essence, the project is motivated by the hypothesis that **data diversity and scale are key to achieving generalization in robotics**, and its objective is to provide both the evidence and the essential infrastructure (data and models) to accelerate research in this direction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of the Open X-Embodiment project revolves around two synergistic components: the creation of a large, standardized, multi-robot dataset, and the training and evaluation of high-capacity transformer-based policies on this consolidated data.

#### 3.1. The Open X-Embodiment (OXE) Repository & Dataset Creation

- **Data Aggregation:** The OXE Dataset is an unprecedented collection, pooling **60 existing robot datasets** from **21 different institutions** (34 labs in total). This includes 1M+ real robot trajectories, spanning 22 distinct robot embodiments, from single arms to bi-manual robots and quadrupeds.
- **Standardization:** A crucial step was converting these heterogeneous datasets into a **consistent data format**. The authors adopted the **RLDS (Reinforcement Learning Dataset) data format**, which stores data in serialized `tfrecord` files. This format is designed to accommodate various action spaces, input modalities (e.g., multiple RGB cameras, depth cameras, point clouds), and supports efficient, parallelized data loading across major deep learning frameworks.
- **Data Characteristics:** An analysis of the dataset reveals:
 - **Robot Diversity:** Franka robots are the most common, contributing to a large diversity in visually distinct scenes. xArm and Google Robot contribute the most trajectories.
 - **Skill Diversity:** While pick-place tasks are prevalent, the dataset includes a long tail of diverse skills such as "wiping," "assembling," "navigating," and "turning on." In total, it demonstrates 527 skills across 160,266 tasks.
 - **Object Diversity:** The dataset covers a wide range of household objects, from appliances to food items and utensils, further enhancing the data's heterogeneity.

#### 3.2. RT-X Policy Design (Models and Training)

- **Foundation Models:** The project leverages existing, high-capacity Transformer-based architectures, RT-1 and RT-2, adapted for the X-embodiment setting (hence "RT-X"). The choice of these architectures is strategic, as they have demonstrated a capacity to learn from large-scale data.
- **Action & Observation Space Consolidation:** A key challenge in multi-robot training is the variation in observation and action spaces. The approach involves:
 - **Standardized Output:** Models predict a 7-dimensional end-effector action vector (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw, gripper opening/rate) or their rates. This vector is discretized into 256 uniform bins for each dimension.
 - **Normalization:** Actions from different datasets are normalized prior to discretization, allowing the model's output to be de-normalized appropriately for each specific embodiment at inference time.
 - **Coarse Alignment:** While a canonical camera view is selected and resized, the observations still vary significantly (e.g., camera poses, properties). Crucially, action coordinate frames are *not* globally aligned, meaning the same action vector can induce very different motions across robots, forcing the model to learn to adapt.
- **Policy Architectures:**
 - **RT-1-X:** A 35M parameter Transformer-based network. It takes a history of 15 images (processed by ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet) and a natural language instruction (processed by USE embedding). FiLM layers intertwine visual and language representations, which are then fed to a decoder-only Transformer to output tokenized actions. RT-1-X is trained solely on the "robotics mixture data."
 - **RT-2-X:** A large Vision-Language Model (VLM) adapted for robotic control by casting actions as natural language tokens. The specific variant is RT-2-PaLI-X, built on a ViT visual model and UL2 language model, pre-trained primarily on the WebLI dataset. RT-2-X is co-fine-tuned with an approximately 1:1 split of the original VLM data and the robotics mixture data.
- **Training Details:** Both models are trained using a standard categorical cross-entropy objective. The "robotics data mixture" used for experiments consists of data from 9 manipulators (a subset of the full OXE dataset, reflecting its ongoing expansion during the project).
- **Inference:** Models run at robot-specific rates (3-10 Hz), with RT-1 locally and RT-2 typically via a cloud service.

#### 3.3. Experimental Evaluation

- **Scope:** 3600 evaluation trials across 6 different robots.
- **Evaluation Types:**
 - **In-Distribution Performance:**
 - **Small-scale datasets:** Evaluation on domains with limited robot-specific data (e.g., Kitchen Manipulation, Cable Routing). Expected to benefit most from transfer.
 - **Large-scale datasets:** Evaluation on data-rich domains (e.g., Bridge, RT-1 paper data). Expected to be more challenging to show further improvement.
 - **Baselines:** Performance is compared against the "Original Method" (models developed by dataset creators) and an "RT-1" baseline trained solely on the robot-specific dataset.
 - **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Settings:**
 - **Unseen Objects/Environments:** Testing the ability to manipulate novel objects in new environments (similar to original RT-2 evaluation).
 - **Emergent Skills Evaluation:** Crucially, this assesses knowledge transfer across robots. For example, testing the Google Robot on skills present in the WidowX (Bridge dataset) but *not* in the Google Robot's native dataset.
- **Ablation Studies:** To understand the impact of design choices, experiments vary model size, history length, inclusion of web-pretraining, and dataset composition.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted provide compelling evidence for the efficacy of X-embodiment training in robotics, particularly when combined with high-capacity models.

#### 4.1. In-Distribution Performance Across Different Embodiments

- **Small-Scale Dataset Domains:** For domains with limited robot-specific data, **RT-1-X demonstrated significant positive transfer**. It outperformed the "Original Method" (models optimized by the dataset creators for their specific data) on 4 out of 5 datasets, showing a large average improvement. This indicates that co-training on the diverse X-embodiment data mixture substantially benefits robots with scarce dedicated training data.
- **Large-Scale Dataset Domains:** The results here highlight the importance of model capacity.
 - The smaller **RT-1-X model *underperformed*** the RT-1 baseline trained solely on the embodiment-specific dataset. This suggests that RT-1-X, despite its design for robotics, had insufficient capacity to effectively utilize and benefit from the vast, heterogeneous X-embodiment dataset in data-rich domains. It essentially "underfit" the complexity of the combined data.
 - In contrast, the much larger **RT-2-X model (55B parameters) *outperformed* both the "Original Method" and the RT-1 baseline**. This is a critical finding: X-robot training *can* improve performance even in data-rich domains, but only when a sufficiently high-capacity architecture is employed to absorb and leverage the diverse information.

#### 4.2. Improved Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Settings

- **Unseen Objects, Backgrounds, and Environments:** On tasks involving novel objects and environments, RT-2-X performed roughly on par with the original RT-2. This is expected, as RT-2 already possesses strong generalization capabilities along these dimensions due to its underlying vision-language model (VLM) backbone, which is pre-trained on massive web-scale vision and language data.
- **Emergent Skills Evaluation (Cross-Robot Transfer):** This is where RT-X models, particularly RT-2-X, showed remarkable capabilities.
 - When evaluating the Google Robot on tasks/skills present in *other* robot datasets (e.g., from the WidowX robot in the Bridge dataset) but *not* in Google Robot's own training data, **RT-2-X significantly outperformed RT-2 by approximately 3x**. This is a powerful demonstration of **knowledge transfer across embodiments**. The RT-2-X controller successfully acquired skills from one robot and applied them to a different, albeit related, platform.
 - Ablation studies further confirmed this: removing the Bridge dataset (containing the WidowX data) from RT-2-X's training significantly reduced its performance on these emergent skills, strongly suggesting that the positive transfer was indeed due to learning from the diverse multi-robot data.

#### 4.3. Influence of Design Decisions

Ablation studies on the RT-2-X model provided insights into critical architectural and training choices:
- **History Length:** Including a short history of images in the model's input significantly improved generalization performance.
- **Web-Based Pre-training:** As observed in previous VLM work, pre-training the model on web-scale vision and language data (as done with RT-2's VLM backbone) was **critical** for achieving high performance. Without it, performance dropped drastically.
- **Model Capacity:** The larger RT-2-X model (55B parameters) achieved a significantly higher success rate in emergent skills compared to its smaller 5B parameter counterpart. This strongly indicates that **higher model capacity enables a greater degree of transfer** and the emergence of new skills from diverse data.
- **Co-fine-tuning vs. Fine-tuning:** Unlike some prior RT-2 findings, co-fine-tuning (training on VLM data + robotics data simultaneously) and pure fine-tuning (on robotics data after VLM pre-training) yielded similar performance. The authors attribute this to the vastly increased diversity of the robotics data mixture in this work, which might mitigate some of the specific benefits of co-fine-tuning seen with narrower robotics datasets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Open X-Embodiment" project represents a pivotal contribution to the field of robotic learning, with significant immediate and long-term implications:

#### 5.1. Foundational Resource for Robotic Learning

The most tangible and immediate impact is the creation and public release of the **Open X-Embodiment (OXE) Repository**. This includes:
- **The largest and most diverse real-world robot dataset to date:** Consolidating 1M+ trajectories from 22 embodiments across 21 institutions into a standardized format is a monumental engineering and coordination feat. This directly addresses the chronic data scarcity and diversity issues plaguing robot learning.
- **Open-source tools and pre-trained models (RT-X checkpoints):** These resources significantly lower the barrier to entry for researchers to explore X-embodiment learning, facilitating replication, extension, and new lines of inquiry.
By providing this open infrastructure, the project serves as a catalyst, enabling the entire research community to advance towards more generalizable robot policies without needing to collect such vast datasets individually.

#### 5.2. Empirical Validation of the "Large Data" Paradigm in Robotics

The experimental results provide strong empirical evidence that the "large-scale, diverse data" paradigm, successful in NLP and CV, is also applicable and highly beneficial in robotics.
- **Positive Transfer:** Demonstrating that training on multi-robot data leads to improved performance on individual robots, especially those with limited existing data, validates the core hypothesis of the work.
- **Emergent Skills:** The ability of RT-2-X to perform tasks (emergent skills) not seen in a robot's specific training data but learned from other robots is a powerful testament to the value of shared, diverse experience. This suggests that generalist policies can indeed learn a broader repertoire of behaviors.
- **Importance of Model Capacity:** The finding that larger models (RT-2-X 55B) are necessary to fully leverage the benefits of such diverse data parallels observations in LLMs and VLMs, reinforcing the scaling laws hypothesis for robotics.

#### 5.3. Laying the Groundwork for Generalist Robot Policies

This work is a crucial step towards the long-sought goal of creating "generalist" robot policies or "foundation models" for robotics. Such models would ideally be able to adapt quickly to new robots, tasks, and environments, significantly reducing the development time and cost for new robotic applications. By showing that policies can learn across different robot morphologies and task settings, the paper pushes the field closer to this vision.

#### 5.4. Fostering Community and Collaboration

The sheer number of collaborators and institutions involved underscores a growing trend towards "big science" in robotics. This collective effort to share and standardize data is vital for accelerating progress, moving beyond fragmented, lab-specific datasets. The open-source nature of the dataset and models is designed to encourage further community contributions and research.

#### 5.5. Future Impact and Open Problems

While a significant achievement, the authors acknowledge several open problems and future work directions, which themselves highlight the project's impact by defining the next frontier:
- **Generalization to Truly Novel Robots:** The current work evaluates transfer among robots *present* in the training data. A critical next step is demonstrating zero-shot or few-shot adaptation to entirely *new* robotic embodiments not seen during training.
- **Diverse Modalities:** The current dataset primarily focuses on end-effector control and visual inputs. Future work needs to explore incorporating and transferring knowledge across vastly different sensing (e.g., haptics, force-torque, audio) and actuation (e.g., legged locomotion, mobile manipulation) modalities.
- **Understanding Transfer Mechanisms:** Delving deeper into *why* and *when* positive transfer occurs, and conversely, when it fails (negative transfer), will be crucial for developing more robust and predictable generalist policies.

In conclusion, "Open X-Embodiment" is not just a research paper; it's a foundational initiative. By providing both the empirical evidence and the essential data infrastructure, it empowers the robotics community to pursue the development of truly generalist robot AI, promising to unlock new capabilities and accelerate the deployment of robots in diverse real-world scenarios.
