# DMC-VB: A Benchmark for Representation Learning for Control with Visual Distractors

> [!summary] Summary
> DMC-VB (DeepMind Control Visual Benchmark) is a large offline-RL dataset built in the DeepMind Control Suite to measure how robust visual control agents are to control-irrelevant distractors such as background and camera viewpoint. It spans locomotion and 3D navigation tasks of varying difficulty, static and dynamic visual variations, multiple behavioral-policy skill levels, paired state/pixel observations, and tasks with hidden goals, and is an order of magnitude larger than prior datasets. Three accompanying benchmarks evaluate representation-learning methods for pretraining, revealing that off-the-shelf pretrained representations do not help on DMC-VB, that a large pixel-vs-state representation gap persists, and that pretraining on suboptimal data or on stochastic-hidden-goal tasks can help when expert data is scarce.

## Problem

- Agents trained by behavioral cloning or offline RL generalize well in some respects but are remarkably brittle to minor visual variations in control-irrelevant factors such as background and camera viewpoint.
- Prior offline-RL datasets for visual control are limited in scale and do not jointly cover task diversity, static and dynamic distractors, multiple policy skill levels, paired state/pixel observations, and hidden-goal tasks, making systematic study of visual representation learning hard.
- It is unclear whether pretrained visual representations actually help downstream policy learning under visual distractors, and how much of the gap to state-based control they close.

## Method

- Introduces **DMC-VB**, an offline-RL dataset in the DeepMind Control Suite combining three ==locomotion tasks== (walker-walk, cheetah-run, humanoid-walk) and seven ==3D navigation tasks== over maze layouts of three complexity levels (empty, single-obstacle, multi-obstacle).
- Data is generated by rolling out ==MPO== behavioral policies trained on states for 2M steps, selecting checkpoints at different skill levels so each task carries expert, mixed, and suboptimal trajectories; every timestep returns paired state and pixel observations.
- Injects both ==static and dynamic visual distractors== (backgrounds, camera viewpoint) and includes navigation tasks with hidden goals to stress control-irrelevant invariance.
- Defines three benchmarks: (B1) whether visual representation learning makes policies robust to distractors; (B2) whether pretraining on mixed-quality data helps few-shot policy learning with limited expert data; (B3) whether pretraining on stochastic-hidden-goal tasks transfers to a new fixed-hidden-goal task.
- Evaluates representation-learning methods including ==inverse dynamics (ID)==, ==behavioral cloning (BC)== encoders, and other recently proposed pretraining objectives, using a two-stage frozen-encoder-then-policy training protocol.

## Results

- Off-the-shelf pretrained representations do **not** help policy learning on DMC-VB, and a **large representation gap** persists between policies learned on pixels versus on states.
- When expert data is limited (policy trained on only **1%** of the expert dataset, ~**20** trajectories), policy learning benefits from representations pretrained on **suboptimal (mixed-quality)** data.
- Pretraining on tasks with **stochastic hidden goals** improves policy learning on a new task with a fixed hidden goal under scarce expert data.
- Merely combining mixed and limited expert data without representation pretraining is insufficient, isolating the value of the pretraining stage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robustness to control-irrelevant visual distractors is a distinct, measurable axis that standard offline-RL benchmarks miss; DMC-VB makes it a first-class evaluation target.
> - The persistent pixel-vs-state gap shows current visual representation learning still leaves substantial control performance on the table under distractors.
> - Suboptimal-data and hidden-goal pretraining are the regimes where representation learning pays off, pointing future work toward data-composition and goal-structure rather than bigger generic encoders.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) has seen significant advancements, particularly with the rise of offline RL and behavioral cloning, which enable agents to learn from large, previously collected datasets without costly online interaction. This approach is instrumental in scaling generalist agents. However, a notable challenge in this area is the lack of robustness and generalization of agents when encountering minor visual variations in control-irrelevant factors, such as backgrounds or camera viewpoints. Agents trained on offline visual data often exhibit brittle performance under these visual distribution shifts.

The theoretical context for this brittleness is often framed within Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) where the state is factorized into control-relevant (endogenous) and control-irrelevant (exogenous) variables. To achieve robust generalization in novel visual domains, agents must learn "control-sufficient" latent representations that capture essential information for control while being minimal enough to disregard irrelevant visual distractors.

Prior research has explored various methods for learning such latent representations. These include generative models like autoencoders, which reconstruct inputs, but often fail to discard distractors and generalize effectively. Non-generative alternatives have also been proposed, such as contrastive learning, latent forward models, and inverse dynamics models, aiming to learn more minimal and robust representations.

Despite these algorithmic developments, the systematic evaluation of representation learning techniques for control in the presence of visual distractors has been hindered by limitations in existing datasets. Datasets like D4RL provide states but lack visual observations with distractors. VD4RL introduces visual distractors but does not include states for measuring the representation gap, offers limited distractor diversity, and may be of insufficient size. Other datasets such as RL Unplugged, Libero, and Colosseum also lack comprehensive visual distractor diversity or different behavioral policy qualities. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a new benchmark dataset designed to facilitate rigorous and systematic evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce the DeepMind Control Vision Benchmark (DMC-VB), a novel dataset designed to systematically and rigorously evaluate the robustness of offline RL agents learning continuous control tasks from visual input in the presence of visual distractors. The motivation for creating DMC-VB stems from the identified shortcomings of existing datasets, which do not collectively possess the necessary properties for a thorough investigation of visual representation learning for control.

The authors detail six key desiderata that motivated the design of DMC-VB:

1. **Task Diversity:** The benchmark aims to include a range of tasks, from simpler locomotion tasks (walker-walk, cheetah-run, humanoid-walk) to more complex 3D navigation tasks (Ant Maze with varying layouts: empty, single-obstacle, multi-obstacle). This diversity is intended to drive the development of algorithms capable of addressing different levels of complexity and control challenges.
2. **Distractor Diversity:** DMC-VB incorporates various types of visual distractors to evaluate robustness against realistic visual variations. For locomotion tasks, these include changes in agent color, camera viewpoint (static or dynamically moving), and background (static images or dynamic videos from the Kubric dataset). For navigation tasks, distractors involve different textures and colors for the maze floors and walls.
3. **Demonstration Quality Diversity:** The dataset includes trajectories generated by behavioral policies of different skill levels, ranging from random to expert (defined by achieving 95% of convergent reward), as well as "medium" (50% reward) and "mixed" (0-50% reward) qualities. This feature allows researchers to investigate how effectively policies can be derived from suboptimal demonstrations and whether pretraining on such data is beneficial.
4. **State and Visual Observations:** DMC-VB systematically provides both pixel observations and corresponding ground-truth states (proprioceptive and exteroceptive measurements). This inclusion is crucial for quantifying the "representation gap" between policies trained on visual inputs and those trained on privileged state information, and for establishing an upper bound on performance.
5. **Large Size:** Each dataset within DMC-VB is an order of magnitude larger than some previous benchmarks (e.g., 1M steps compared to 100k steps in VD4RL). This larger scale is motivated by empirical findings suggesting that smaller datasets can significantly harm the performance of RL algorithms, particularly for harder tasks or in the presence of visual distractors.
6. **Hidden Goal:** The benchmark includes a variant of the navigation tasks where the goal is not visually observable. This is inspired by prior work suggesting that pretraining representations can be critical in scenarios where the goal is a hidden variable or inferred from context, enabling the study of goal-free exploration and representation transfer.

Accompanying the dataset, the paper also aims to propose three specific benchmarks (B1, B2, B3) that leverage these unique properties to evaluate various representation learning methods for control, thereby providing a structured framework for future research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research methodology revolves around the creation of the DMC-VB dataset and the design of benchmark experiments to evaluate visual representation learning methods for continuous control.

#### 3.1. DMC-VB Dataset Generation

The dataset is collected within the DeepMind Control Suite, including standard locomotion tasks (walker-walk, cheetah-run, humanoid-walk) and custom Ant Maze navigation tasks.
- **Tasks:** Locomotion tasks aim for target velocity achievement. Navigation tasks involve a quadruped ant reaching a visible green sphere goal in various maze layouts (empty, single-obstacle, multi-obstacle). A dense reward is defined as the negative normalized shortest path length to the goal, computed using the Fast Marching Method.
- **Distractors:**
 - **Locomotion:** Utilizes the Distracting Control Suite to introduce variations in agent color, camera viewpoint (static random or dynamically moving), and background (static images or dynamic videos from Kubric). Three settings are used: 'none', 'static', and 'dynamic'.
 - **Navigation:** Visual distractors are applied as different textures and colors to the maze floors and walls.
- **Demonstration Data Quality:** Datasets are generated using behavioral policies with varying skill levels:
 - 'Expert': Policy achieving 95% of the reward at convergence.
 - 'Medium': Policy achieving 50% of the reward at convergence.
 - 'Mixed': Trajectories from eight evenly spaced checkpoints achieving 0-50% of the reward.
 - 'Random': Actions sampled uniformly from the action space.
- **Observations:** For each timestep, both pixel observations (RGB images) and corresponding privileged states (proprioceptive and exteroceptive measurements) are systematically collected. For navigation tasks, three camera views are collected: top-down, egocentric, and a follow-up view, with additional variants where the goal sphere is hidden.
- **Size:** Each dataset contains 1 million steps, significantly larger than some previous benchmarks.
- **Training Behavioral Policies:** Online MPO (Maximum A Posteriori Policy Optimisation) agents are trained using state information for 2 million steps to generate the expert and other policy checkpoints. Action repeat of two is used in all environments.

#### 3.2. Agent Architecture and Training

A two-stage procedure is adopted for training agents:
1. **Pretraining Stage:** A visual encoder network ($\phi$) is trained to minimize a representation learning loss.
2. **Policy Learning Stage:** A policy network ($\pi$) is trained with the pretrained visual encoder ($\phi^*$) held constant.
- **Architectures:**
 - **Visual Encoder ($\phi$):** A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with four layers (3x3 filters, (32, 64, 128, 256) channels, strides (2, 2, 1, 1), GELU activations), followed by a linear projection to a 64-dimensional latent space, layer normalization, and a hyperbolic tangent activation.
 - **Policy Network ($\pi$):** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers (256 units, ReLU activations) and a hyperbolic tangent activation for the output actions (normalized to [-1, 1]).
 - **Frame Stacking:** Three consecutive observations are stacked as input to the encoder ($ \tilde{o}_t = (o_{t-\ell+1}, \ldots, o_t)$).
- **Training Details:** All models are trained for 400,000 Adam iterations on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of 0.001. Online evaluations are performed every 20,000 steps over 30 rollouts in evaluation environments with unseen distractors.

#### 3.3. Policy Learning Methods

Two primary policy learning objectives are used:
- **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** Minimizes the L2 error between the predicted actions from the policy network and the actions in the dataset ($L_{BC}$). This is a supervised learning approach.
- **TD3-BC:** An offline RL algorithm that combines the TD3 critic objective with a BC term added to the actor objective to regularize the policy towards actions present in the dataset ($L_{TD3-BC, Actor}$).

#### 3.4. Visual Representation Learning Methods (for Pretraining $\phi$)

Several established representation learning methods are benchmarked for pretraining the encoder:
- **Inverse Dynamics (ID):** Trains a model to predict the action taken from the current observation and a future observation *k* steps ahead ($L_{ID}$). The paper uses *k*=1 by default.
- **Latent Forward Dynamics (LFD):** Predicts the next latent representation from the current latent representation and action, using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) target network to prevent latent collapse ($L_{LFD}$).
- **AutoEncoder (AE):** Trains the encoder ($\phi$) and a decoder ($\psi$) to reconstruct the input pixel observations ($L_{AE}$).
- **Pretrained DINO Encoder:** Leverages an off-the-shelf DINO Vision Transformer pretrained on large image datasets. DMC-VB observations are padded to match DINO's input size requirements.

#### 3.5. Baselines using Privileged States

To provide upper bounds and context, two state-based baselines are included:
- **BC (state):** A BC agent trained directly on the ground-truth state information (using an MLP for the encoder $\tilde{\phi}$) at both training and evaluation times.
- **State Prediction Pretraining:** The visual encoder is pretrained by minimizing the L2 error between the predicted state from the observation embedding and the true state ($L_{state}$).

#### 3.6. Benchmark Experiments

Three distinct benchmark evaluations are designed:
- **Benchmark 1 (B1): Policy learning with visual distractors.** Evaluates the degradation of policy learning and quantifies the representation gap. Agents are trained on full expert and medium datasets across different distractor types (none, static, dynamic) and compared against state-based baselines.
- **Benchmark 2 (B2): Pretraining on mixed data for few-shot policy learning.** Investigates whether pretraining on large, mixed-quality datasets improves policy learning when expert demonstrations are limited (1% of the expert dataset).
- **Benchmark 3 (B3): Pretraining on stochastic hidden goals for few-shot policy learning.** Explores whether pretraining on tasks with random hidden goals benefits few-shot policy learning on new tasks with fixed hidden goals, using limited expert data for the policy stage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of different representation learning methods on the DMC-VB benchmark.

#### 4.1. Policy Learning with Visual Distractors (Benchmark 1)

- **Performance of Pretraining Methods:** The simple Behavioral Cloning (BC) method, where the encoder and policy are trained end-to-end without separate pretraining (denoted NULL+BC), emerged as the top-performing method overall for locomotion tasks. Inverse Dynamics (ID) pretraining followed by BC (ID+BC) performed comparably to NULL+BC but did not consistently exceed it. Other pretraining methods, including Latent Forward Dynamics (LFD+BC), AutoEncoder (AE+BC), and DINO pretrained encoder (DINO+BC), generally performed worse than NULL+BC. This suggests that the investigated pretraining methods do not universally improve BC policy learning on DMC-VB.
- **Robustness to Distractors:** In the presence of distractors, NULL+BC and ID+BC maintained high rewards on several locomotion tasks (walker-expert, walker-medium, cheetah-medium), indicating a degree of robustness to visual variations. For navigation (Ant Maze) tasks, NULL+BC also performed best, and pretrained visual representations generally harmed performance.
- **Offline RL vs. BC:** The offline RL algorithm, TD3-BC (NULL+TD3-BC), was mostly outperformed by NULL+BC and did not demonstrate superior performance.
- **Representation Gap:** Policies trained on ground-truth states (NULL+BC (state)) were unaffected by visual distractors and established an upper bound. The performance gap between state-trained and pixel-trained policies was minimal without distractors but significantly widened with dynamic distractors, highlighting the challenge of learning robust visual representations.
- **Analysis of Learned Representations:**
 - Methods that achieved low state reconstruction error (e.g., BC and ID) generally performed better in downstream policy learning. These methods appeared to discard control-irrelevant information (like background and agent color), leading to higher observation reconstruction errors but lower state reconstruction errors.
 - Conversely, methods with low observation reconstruction error (e.g., AE, DINO) tended to perform worse in policy learning, likely due to their failure to filter out distractors and higher state reconstruction errors. LFD exhibited the highest state reconstruction errors, correlating with its poor policy performance.
- **State Prediction Pretraining:** Pretraining the encoder to predict states (State+BC) did not offer an advantage over NULL+BC. This was attributed to the difficulty in accurately predicting certain state components, such as velocities, from visual observations alone.

#### 4.2. Pretraining on Mixed Data Helps Few-Shot Policy Learning (Benchmark 2)

- **Impact of Limited Expert Data:** Restricting the amount of expert data (to 1%) significantly degraded policy learning performance.
- **Benefit of Pretraining on Mixed Data:** Merely combining mixed-quality data with limited expert data for end-to-end BC led to a performance drop. However, pretraining a visual representation on the full mixed-quality dataset, followed by policy learning on only 1% of expert data (ID (mixed) + BC (1% expert) or BC (mixed) + BC (1% expert)), provided a performance boost. This effect was particularly noticeable in the presence of visual distractors. ID and BC pretraining methods showed similar benefits in this setting.

#### 4.3. Pretraining on Stochastic Hidden Goals Helps Few-Shot Policy Learning (Benchmark 3)

- **Utility for Hidden Goals:** Pretraining representations on a large dataset of Ant Maze tasks with stochastic (random and unseen) hidden goals, subsequently improved few-shot policy learning on new tasks with fixed hidden goals and limited expert data.
- **Comparison of Methods:** Contrary to some previous findings, BC pretraining on stochastic hidden goal tasks (BC (stochastic goals) + BC (fixed goal)) outperformed ID pretraining (ID (stochastic goals) + BC (fixed goal)) in this specific benchmark scenario.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of DMC-VB represents a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning, particularly concerning robust learning from visual observations. Its design, adhering to six key desiderata, establishes it as a comprehensive and systematic benchmark for evaluating visual representation learning methods in the context of continuous control with visual distractors.

The findings from the benchmark experiments highlight several critical areas for future research:
- **Challenges for Current Pretraining:** The observation that current visual representation pretraining methods, both generative and non-generative, do not consistently improve Behavioral Cloning policy learning on DMC-VB is a notable result. This finding poses an open question for researchers to investigate the underlying reasons and to develop more effective pretraining strategies tailored for robust control from pixels.
- **The Representation Gap:** The paper clearly demonstrates a substantial "representation gap" between agents trained on ground-truth states and those trained on pixels, especially in dynamic distractor environments. This gap underscores the need for new methods that can learn truly "control-sufficient," minimal, and robust representations from high-dimensional visual inputs, effectively filtering out control-irrelevant information.
- **Leveraging Suboptimal and Goal-Free Data:** The work shows the practical value of leveraging suboptimal (mixed-quality) datasets and data collected via goal-free exploration (stochastic hidden goals) for pretraining. This can enable more effective policy learning in scenarios with limited expert data, a common constraint in real-world applications.

DMC-VB is poised to accelerate research toward creating robust and generalist agents that can operate reliably in visually complex and variable environments. By providing a standardized and challenging evaluation platform, it will facilitate the development and systematic comparison of novel representation learning algorithms.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge two primary limitations of the current work:
1. **Scope of Environments:** DMC-VB currently focuses on locomotion and navigation tasks. Extending the benchmark to a broader range of environments, such as those with sparse rewards, multiple agents, complex manipulation tasks, or stochastic dynamics, could provide a more comprehensive assessment.
2. **Synthetic Distractors:** The visual distractors in DMC-VB are synthetic. While these are relevant to certain robotics tasks, their generalization to real-world scenarios might be questioned. Incorporating more diverse and realistic distractors could further strengthen the findings and broaden the applicability of the benchmark.
