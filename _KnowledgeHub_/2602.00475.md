# Parallel Stochastic Gradient-Based Planning for World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> GRASP, a novel gradient-based planning algorithm, enables world models to effectively solve long-horizon control tasks from high-dimensional visual inputs by combining parallelized planning, Langevin-style exploration, and a robust gradient-cutting mechanism. It achieves a 43.4% success rate in the Push-T environment at a 50-step horizon, significantly outperforming prior methods.

## Problem

- Existing planning methods struggle with long-horizon control tasks when leveraging learned visual world models, particularly in high-dimensional observation spaces.
- Zero-order (sampling-based) algorithms face scalability issues due to serial rollouts and diminished performance with increasing planning horizons and action dimensions.
- Traditional gradient-based methods are hindered by susceptibility to local minima and gradient instability arising from differentiating through long sequences of deep neural network dynamics.

## Method

- GRASP introduces ==parallelized planning using auxiliary "virtual states"== alongside actions, reformulating the problem to allow parallel world model evaluations across the entire horizon.
- It incorporates ==Langevin-style stochastic updates== by injecting Gaussian noise directly into state iterates to encourage exploration and escape local minima in the non-convex optimization landscape.
- A novel ==Grad-cut Dynamics Loss== detaches gradients through world model state inputs to prevent brittle gradient exploitation, complemented by dense goal shaping for a global, task-aligned signal.
- Periodic synchronization steps with full-gradient descent on the original serial rollout objective refine trajectories, balancing exploration with precise convergence.

## Results

- GRASP achieves superior performance on long-horizon tasks, demonstrating a **43.4%** success rate in the challenging Push-T environment at a 50-step horizon, outperforming CEM (**30.2%**), vanilla GD (**37.6%**), and Latent Collocation (**4.2%**).
- The method consistently exhibits faster convergence and lower median planning times for successful trials across various environments, indicating enhanced computational efficiency.
- Ablation studies confirm that the robust grad-cut dynamics and periodic full-rollout synchronization steps are critical components for GRASP's effectiveness in navigating complex loss landscapes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling world model dynamics through "virtual states" allows for significant parallelization in planning, transforming the serial optimization challenge into a lifted problem.
> - Directly injecting Langevin-style noise into state iterates effectively facilitates exploration of the lifted state space, enabling escape from local minima and discovery of non-greedy solutions.
> - Gradients with respect to state inputs in vision-based world models are often brittle and can be exploited; detaching these gradients, combined with dense goal shaping, yields more robust and physically meaningful plans.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of World Models, Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL), and Gradient-Based Optimization in Artificial Intelligence. The field of AI has seen rapid advancements in building "world models" – neural networks trained to simulate environment dynamics directly from raw sensory inputs, such as video. These models represent a crucial step towards intelligent agents that can predict the consequences of their actions, enabling more sophisticated planning and reasoning. Recent successes in applications ranging from computer games (Valevski et al., 2024) to real-world navigation (Bar et al., 2025; Ball et al., 2025) and robotic manipulation (Goswami et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025) underscore the growing importance and potential of world models.

Despite their promise, using world models for planning, especially over long horizons and in high-dimensional, unstructured observation spaces (like vision), presents significant challenges. Traditional planning algorithms often fall into two categories:
1. **Zero-order (sampling-based) methods:** Such as the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) or Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI). These methods are robust and simple but suffer from performance degradation with longer planning horizons and higher action dimensionality due to their reliance on serial trajectory rollouts and brute-force exploration (Bharadhwaj et al., 2020).
2. **Gradient-based methods:** These leverage the differentiability of modern deep neural network world models to optimize action sequences directly. While offering greater sample efficiency and finer-grained control, they face major hurdles. Key issues include susceptibility to local minima in non-convex loss landscapes (Jyothir et al., 2023) and instability arising from differentiating through long sequences of world model applications, akin to "backpropagation through time" (Werbos, 2002). This can lead to vanishing or exploding gradients and poorly conditioned optimization problems.

Prior work has attempted to address these gradient-based challenges through techniques like multiple shooting and direct collocation from classical optimal control (Von Stryk, 1993; Tamimi and Li, 2009). However, these methods were primarily designed for systems with known, analytical dynamics and often do not scale effectively to complex, learned deep neural network dynamics, especially in high-dimensional latent spaces where Jacobians can be brittle.

This paper explicitly addresses the gap by proposing a novel gradient-based planner that aims to combine the benefits of gradient information (efficiency, fine-grained control) with enhanced robustness and scalability for visual world models. It positions itself as a "stochastic version of a non-condensed or collocation-based optimal controller," demonstrating an evolution of classical control principles to the modern deep learning paradigm. By tackling issues like gradient sensitivity and local minima in learned, high-dimensional dynamics, this work contributes to making world models more practical and effective tools for long-horizon decision-making in complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, efficient, and highly parallelizable planning algorithm for world models that can effectively solve long-horizon control tasks from high-dimensional visual inputs. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and aims to address specific limitations of existing planning approaches:

1. **Overcoming Limitations of Zero-Order Methods:** The paper seeks to move beyond the inherent scalability issues of zero-order optimization methods (like CEM) that suffer from serial evaluation bottlenecks and poor performance as planning horizons and action dimensions increase. The motivation is to leverage the differentiability of modern deep world models for more efficient and precise planning.
2. **Mitigating Gradient-Based Planning Challenges:** While gradient-based methods offer promise, they are plagued by two major problems in the context of learned world models:
- **Local Minima:** The complex and often "jagged" loss landscapes (as illustrated in Figure 1) can trap deterministic gradient descent algorithms in suboptimal solutions, especially when non-greedy paths are required to reach the goal. The motivation here is to introduce mechanisms that promote exploration and escape from these traps.
- **Gradient Instability and Sensitivity:** Differentiating through long sequences of deep world models can lead to unstable gradients (similar to backpropagation through time issues). Furthermore, in high-dimensional latent state spaces learned by vision-based world models, gradients with respect to *state inputs* can be "brittle" or "adversarial." This means the optimizer might exploit sensitive Jacobian structures to achieve seemingly low loss values by creating unphysical state transitions, rather than discovering dynamically meaningful plans (Figure 3). The motivation is to design a gradient structure that is robust to these issues.
3. **Enabling Parallel Computation:** Standard planning formulations often rely on serial rollouts of the world model, which is computationally expensive. A key motivation is to decouple the temporal dynamics, allowing for parallel evaluation of the world model across different time steps to improve computational efficiency.
4. **Scaling to Long Horizons and High-Dimensional States:** The ultimate goal is to enable world models to plan effectively for significantly longer horizons and in environments characterized by high-dimensional visual observations, where existing methods typically struggle. The paper aims to demonstrate that its proposed method remains stable and performs well under these challenging conditions.

In essence, the authors are motivated to create a "best of both worlds" planner, combining the exploration capabilities of stochastic methods with the efficiency and precision of gradient-based optimization, specifically tailored for the complexities of modern, differentiable world models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed planning algorithm, named GRASP (Gradient RelAxed Stochastic Planner), introduces a novel combination of techniques to address the challenges of long-horizon planning with vision-based world models. Its methodology revolves around three core components: parallelized planning with "virtual states," exploration through Langevin-style state updates, and a novel gradient-cutting mechanism with dense goal shaping, all orchestrated by periodic full-rollout synchronization.

1. **Parallelized Planning with "Virtual States" (Decoupling Dynamics):**
- **Concept:** Instead of optimizing only an action sequence `a` by serially rolling out the world model `F_θ(s_0, a)`, GRASP introduces auxiliary "virtual states" `s = (s_1..., s_T)` as independent optimization variables alongside actions `a = (a_0..., a_{T-1})`.
- **Objective:** The original planning problem (Equation 3) is reformulated into a dynamics consistency loss (Equation 4) that penalizes deviations from `s_{t+1} = F_θ(s_t, a_t)` for all `t`.
- **Benefit:** This reformulation allows all world model evaluations (`F_θ(s_t, a_t)`) across the entire horizon to be computed in parallel, significantly improving computational efficiency compared to serial rollouts. It also frames the problem as a "lifted" optimization over states and actions, drawing inspiration from multiple shooting and direct collocation methods in optimal control.

2. **Exploration via Langevin State Updates:**
- **Problem:** The lifted optimization landscape, while better conditioned than serial rollouts, is still non-convex and prone to local minima. Deterministic updates can get stuck in "unphysical" regions (e.g., a path going straight through a wall, as in Figure 7).
- **Approach:** GRASP injects isotropic Gaussian noise directly into the state iterates (`s_t`) during each optimization step (Equation 5), similar to Langevin dynamics. This stochasticity encourages exploration of the lifted state space, allowing the planner to "hop" between nearby basins and escape unfavorable local minima.
- **Rationale:** The authors argue that noising states directly (rather than actions) is more effective for finding intermediate states crucial for solutions, while still guiding towards dynamically feasible trajectories.

3. **Sensitivity to State Gradients and Grad-cut Dynamics Loss:**
- **Problem:** A critical observation in vision-based world models is that gradients with respect to *state inputs* (`∇_s F_θ(s, a)`) can be brittle and easily exploited by the optimizer. Instead of finding genuine dynamic transitions, the optimizer might find minuscule, adversarial perturbations to a state that drastically change the world model's output in a misleading way (Figure 3).
- **Solution (Grad-cut Dynamics Loss):** To mitigate this, GRASP deliberately *detaches gradients* through the state inputs (`s_t`) of the world model when calculating the dynamics loss (Equation 8). It uses a `stop-gradient` operator (`¯s_t`), meaning `¯s_t = s_t` in value, but its gradient is treated as zero. This ensures that gradients through `F_θ(¯s_t, a_t)` flow only with respect to actions `a_t` and the target next state `s_{t+1}`, making the optimization more robust.
- **Dense Goal Shaping:** Detaching state gradients, however, could lead trajectories to gravitate towards the current rollout regardless of the goal. To provide a global, task-aligned signal, a dense goal loss is added for *each one-step prediction* (Equation 9): `||F_θ(¯s_t, a_t) - g||^2`. This term provides direct gradient information to every action `a_t` (via `F_θ`'s action-input Jacobian) while maintaining the grad-cut on `s_t`.
- **Total Loss:** The final objective `L(s, a)` (Equation 10) combines the grad-cut dynamics consistency and the dense goal shaping.

4. **Full-Rollout Synchronization:**
- **Problem:** While the no-state-gradient updates are robust and the stochasticity aids exploration, the resulting dynamics in the `(s,a)` space do not follow a "true" gradient vector field. They bias towards goal-oriented trajectories but might lack the precision of full backpropagation for fine-tuning.
- **Approach:** Periodically (every `K_sync` iterations), GRASP performs a few steps (`J_sync`) of standard, full-gradient descent (Equation 15) on the *original serial rollout objective* (Equation 13). During this phase, actions are updated using backpropagation through the entire `T`-step rollout, taking advantage of its sharp but brittle landscape for refinement.
- **Benefit:** This balances the smoothed, explorative landscape of the GRASP updates (Figure 1c) with the sharp, precise landscape of traditional gradient descent (Figure 1b), allowing for both wide exploration and accurate convergence.

**Theoretical Justification:** The paper provides theoretical insights, particularly for linear systems. It demonstrates that the Lipschitz constant of the gradient for the shooting method (serial rollout) can grow exponentially with the horizon `T` (Theorem 2), leading to ill-conditioning. In contrast, the lifted state method's gradient Lipschitz constant is bounded, independent of `T` (Theorem 3), suggesting better conditioning. It also provides theoretical support for Gaussian noise regularization (Theorem 4) and analyzes how the state-gradient-free dynamics lead to a Gaussian "tube" around one-step predictions, with goal shaping inducing a goal-directed drift of the tube center (Theorems 7 & 8).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

GRASP demonstrates significant improvements in long-horizon planning for visual world models, while remaining competitive in shorter-horizon tasks. The core findings are presented through extensive experiments on D4RL and DeepMind control suite environments with learned visual dynamics (PointMaze, Wall-Single, and Push-T).

1. **Superior Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks:**
- GRASP consistently and substantially outperforms existing planning algorithms—namely the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), Latent Collocation (LatCo), and vanilla Gradient Descent (GD)—in long-horizon settings (H=40 to H=80), particularly in the challenging Push-T environment.
- As shown in Table 1, for Push-T at horizon H=50, GRASP achieves a 43.4% success rate with a median success time of 15.2 seconds. This contrasts sharply with CEM (30.2% success, 96.2s time), GD (37.6% success, 76.3s time), and LatCo (4.2% success, 1114.7s time).
- This superiority becomes even more pronounced at longer horizons (e.g., H=80), where GRASP achieves a 10.4% success rate compared to 2.8% for CEM, 6.4% for GD, and 0.0% for LatCo. This clearly demonstrates GRASP's robustness and ability to scale.
- Figure 5 further illustrates GRASP's faster convergence, showing a higher cumulative success rate over time compared to baselines. Figure 6 visualizes how GRASP successfully finds non-greedy, realistic paths for long horizons, which often involve moving away from the goal initially to find an optimal trajectory.

2. **Robustness to Local Minima and Non-Greedy Paths:**
- A key finding is GRASP's ability to navigate non-convex loss landscapes and discover non-greedy solutions. The combination of stochastic state updates and the modified gradient structure enables it to escape local optima that trap deterministic or sampling-based planners.

3. **Efficiency and Faster Convergence:**
- Beyond higher success rates, GRASP consistently achieves lower planning times, especially for successful trials, underscoring its computational efficiency (Tables 1 and 4). This is attributed to the parallelized world model evaluations and the focused exploration provided by its stochastic gradient updates.

4. **Competitiveness on Short-Horizon Tasks:**
- For shorter planning horizons (H=10 to H=30), GRASP maintains success rates comparable to (or slightly better than) strong baselines across all environments (Table 3). Importantly, it does so with consistently low planning times, often competitive with or faster than other gradient-based methods and significantly faster than sampling-based approaches (Table 4). This indicates that the benefits of GRASP are not limited to long-horizon problems but also translate to efficient performance on easier tasks.

5. **Validation of Core Components via Ablation Studies:**
- The ablation study (Table 2) confirms the critical role of GRASP's unique components. Removing the GD sync steps ("No Sync") or allowing gradients to flow through state inputs ("Flow" vs. "Stop" for `∇_s F(s,a)`) drastically reduces performance, highlighting the necessity of both synchronization for refinement and gradient detaching for robustness. Similarly, optimizing the noise level (`σ`) is crucial for optimal exploration.

In summary, GRASP sets a new benchmark for planning with world models by offering a robust, efficient, and scalable solution for long-horizon control, effectively addressing the traditional challenges of gradient instability and local minima in learned high-dimensional dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of GRASP holds significant implications for the field of AI and its applications, particularly in areas involving intelligent agents, robotics, and complex decision-making.

1. **Advancing World Model Utility:** GRASP fundamentally enhances the practicality and utility of learned world models. By providing a planning algorithm that is robust, efficient, and scalable to long horizons and high-dimensional visual inputs, it moves world models closer to fulfilling their potential as core components for intelligent agents that can reason about the future.

2. **Bridging Gaps in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL):** MBRL agents rely heavily on effective planning mechanisms. GRASP offers a more powerful planning module for these agents, enabling them to tackle more complex tasks with longer decision horizons and potentially reducing the sample complexity required for learning. This could lead to more efficient and capable AI agents in various domains.

3. **Overcoming Gradient Challenges in Deep Dynamics:** The paper's innovative approach to handling brittle state gradients in learned deep dynamics (the "grad-cut" mechanism with dense goal shaping) is a significant methodological contribution. This technique could inspire similar solutions in other deep learning contexts where gradient sensitivity or adversarial examples pose challenges to optimization and control. It offers a practical way to harness the power of gradients while sidestepping their notorious pathologies in high-dimensional, learned spaces.

4. **Enhanced Exploration and Local Minima Escape:** The integration of Langevin-style stochasticity into state updates provides a powerful, principled mechanism for exploration. This allows planners to escape suboptimal local minima and discover non-greedy, more effective trajectories, which is crucial for complex, real-world problems. This also offers insights into how to inject useful noise into gradient-based systems beyond simple parameter perturbation.

5. **Accelerating Development of Autonomous Systems:** For applications like robotics, autonomous navigation, and medical simulations (as mentioned in the introduction), the ability to plan accurately and efficiently over long horizons from raw sensory input is paramount. GRASP's improved performance and speed can accelerate the development and deployment of such autonomous systems, making them more reliable and capable.

6. **Inspiring Future Research:** The work opens several promising avenues for future research.
- **Hybrid Planners:** The demonstrated benefit of periodically synchronizing with full-gradient rollouts suggests further exploration of sophisticated hybrid planning strategies that dynamically balance different optimization techniques.
- **Improved State Representations:** The paper notes that ideally, if world model state representations were smoother and geometrically well-behaved, some of GRASP's stabilization mechanisms might become unnecessary. This motivates future work in representation learning, potentially through adversarial training, diffusion models, or explicit regularization of the latent space geometry, to create more robust and interpretable world models.
- **Theoretical Generalization:** Extending the theoretical analysis beyond linear systems to general nonlinear world models would further solidify the understanding and provide more direct guidance for future improvements.

In conclusion, GRASP represents a substantial methodological leap for planning in world models, particularly those operating on visual data. By combining parallelized computation, targeted stochasticity, and a robust gradient handling strategy, it pushes the boundaries of what is achievable in long-horizon planning, thereby significantly impacting the development of more intelligent and capable AI agents.
