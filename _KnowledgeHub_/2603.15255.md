# SAGE: Multi-Agent Self-Evolution for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The SAGE framework introduces a multi-agent self-evolution system enabling large language models to autonomously improve reasoning capabilities in mathematics and code generation. It achieves up to a 2.5% overall average accuracy improvement on various Qwen models and demonstrates strong out-of-distribution generalization, relying only on a small seed set and automatic verification signals.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning methods for LLM reasoning heavily depend on large, human-curated datasets for verifiable rewards, creating scalability and autonomy bottlenecks.
- Prior self-play and multi-agent systems often suffer from instability, curriculum drift, and a lack of explicit planning or robust quality control in complex, multi-step reasoning tasks.
- Current multi-agent frameworks struggle to integrate structured planning and consistent quality assurance for intricate problem decomposition and solution generation.

## Method

- SAGE implements a ==closed-loop, multi-agent system== where a shared LLM backbone instantiates four specialized roles: ==Challenger== (generates tasks), ==Planner== (creates solution plans), ==Solver== (executes plans), and ==Critic== (assesses task/plan quality).
- It utilizes a ==dual-role Critic== agent to filter self-generated tasks and validate solution plans, ensuring high-quality training signals and preventing curriculum degradation.
- The framework employs ==Task-Relative REINFORCE++== for synchronized parameter updates across all agents, coordinating heterogeneous objectives for stable, continuous co-evolution with minimal human supervision.

## Results

- SAGE consistently improved reasoning performance across Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B, and Qwen3-4B models, achieving up to **2.5%** higher overall average accuracy compared to base models.
- The framework demonstrated strong out-of-distribution generalization, achieving the best or near-best OOD Average performance across all tested backbones and notably improving LiveCodeBench scores.
- Ablation studies confirmed that all three trainable agents (Challenger, Planner, Solver) are critical, with the Critic's quality filtering being particularly important for code generation and the Challenger crucial for OOD generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit planning (by the Planner) and rigorous quality control (by the Critic) are essential for stable and effective self-evolution in complex, multi-step reasoning domains.
> - A self-generated curriculum, continuously refined by a quality-aware Critic and an adversarial Challenger, can lead to substantial out-of-distribution generalization capabilities in LLMs.
> - Specialized multi-agent collaboration within a unified LLM backbone, coupled with coordinated reward mechanisms, fosters effective division of labor and synergistic improvement in reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of reasoning capabilities in large language models (LLMs) represents a significant area of research. Reinforcement learning (RL) techniques have been instrumental in advancing LLM performance in domains such as mathematics and code generation. However, many existing RL methods for LLMs typically rely on large volumes of human-curated datasets to provide verifiable rewards. This dependency introduces scalability challenges and limits the autonomous adaptation of LLMs, particularly as models approach or exceed human-level capabilities in certain tasks.

Recent research has explored alternative paradigms to reduce this reliance on extensive human labeling. Self-play frameworks, such as SPIRAL and Absolute Zero, utilize verifiable environments to enable autonomous model improvement. While these approaches offer a pathway to self-evolution, they often lack explicit planning mechanisms and robust quality control, which can lead to instability and curriculum drift, especially when addressing complex, multi-step reasoning problems.

Concurrently, multi-agent systems have emerged as a method for enhancing LLM performance on intricate tasks through role specialization and collaboration. Frameworks like MetaGPT, CAMEL, MARS, and MAE leverage multiple LLM agents that interact to decompose and solve complex problems. However, a common challenge for these multi-agent systems includes difficulties in open-ended domains that lack strong verification mechanisms, and a limited integration of structured planning for tasks requiring sequential multi-step reasoning.

The SAGE framework is positioned within this landscape by aiming to address these identified gaps. It seeks to develop a closed-loop, self-evolving multi-agent system that minimizes the dependency on large human-labeled datasets. By integrating explicit planning and a rigorous quality control mechanism through a specialized "Critic" agent, SAGE endeavors to provide a more stable and effective approach to self-training for long-horizon, multi-step reasoning tasks in verifiable domains, building upon and extending prior work in self-play and multi-agent LLM systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable large language models (LLMs) to autonomously improve their reasoning capabilities in verifiable domains, such as mathematics and code generation, with minimal reliance on external human-curated data. The authors propose the SAGE framework to achieve this through a multi-agent self-evolutionary process.

The motivation for developing SAGE stems from several limitations observed in current LLM reasoning advancements:

1. **Dependency on Human-Curated Data**: Existing reinforcement learning (RL) techniques, while effective for LLM reasoning, frequently necessitate large-scale, human-labeled datasets to provide accurate and verifiable rewards. This dependency is a bottleneck for scalability, as it requires significant human effort and financial resources to generate and maintain these datasets. As LLMs become more capable, the generation of increasingly complex and diverse human-curated data becomes challenging, limiting the models' ability to adapt autonomously.

2. **Instability in Self-Play and Multi-Agent Systems**: While self-play and multi-agent frameworks offer promise for autonomous learning, they often encounter issues with stability, particularly in complex, multi-step reasoning tasks. Many self-play paradigms lack explicit planning mechanisms, making it difficult for models to systematically approach long-horizon problems. Additionally, a lack of strong quality control can lead to "curriculum drift," where the self-generated training data degrades in quality or relevance, thereby hindering stable and continuous improvement.

3. **Lack of Integrated Planning and Quality Assurance**: Current multi-agent systems, despite specializing roles, frequently struggle to integrate robust planning strategies for decomposing complex tasks or consistently ensuring the quality of generated tasks and solutions. This can result in suboptimal learning trajectories or the propagation of errors throughout the self-evolutionary loop.

To address these motivations, SAGE pursues the following specific objectives:
- To design and implement a scalable multi-agent framework that facilitates the self-evolution of LLMs in reasoning tasks.
- To introduce a dual-role Critic mechanism that actively filters generated tasks for quality and validates solution plans, thereby maintaining the integrity and effectiveness of the training signal.
- To empirically demonstrate that this framework can enable effective co-evolution of agents in mathematical and code generation domains, achieving significant performance improvements even when initialized with only a small seed set of examples.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to pave a path toward more autonomous, scalable, and stable LLM reasoning systems that can adapt and improve without extensive human intervention.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SAGE (Self-evolving Agents for Generalized reasoning Evolution) is a closed-loop, multi-agent framework designed for the autonomous improvement of LLM reasoning in verifiable domains like mathematics and coding. The framework requires only a small initial seed set and leverages automatic verification signals for continuous self-iteration.

#### 3.1. Framework Architecture and Agent Roles

SAGE instantiates four specialized agents from a shared Large Language Model (LLM) backbone, denoted as M_θ:
1. **Challenger Agent (π_c)**: Responsible for generating new, increasingly challenging tasks (questions) along with their corresponding verifiers. It is rewarded for creating difficult problems that the Solver initially struggles with but are ultimately solvable.
2. **Planner Agent (π_p)**: Upon receiving a question, the Planner generates a structured, multi-step plan to address it.
3. **Solver Agent (π_s)**: Follows the plan generated by the Planner (if deemed acceptable) to produce a final answer to the given task. Its primary optimization signal comes from external verifiers that assess the correctness of its solutions.
4. **Critic Agent (π_cr)**: Serves a dual role:
 - Evaluates the quality and well-formedness of questions generated by the Challenger.
 - Assesses the clarity, completeness, and logical soundness of plans produced by the Planner.
 - Provides format rewards to ensure adherence to specified output structures for all agents.
 The Critic's score is a deterministic output, while correctness for Solver's answer is determined by an external, domain-specific verifier.

#### 3.2. Training Workflow

The SAGE training pipeline operates in a continuous co-evolutionary loop:
1. **Challenger Phase**: The Challenger samples reference problems from a small human-curated seed set (D_0) and generates a new problem `q` and its verifier `v`. The Critic assesses the quality of `q` (s_q). The Solver attempts to solve `q` multiple times (N_s samples), and the average success rate (¯s_gt) is used to compute a difficulty reward (r_d = 1 - ¯s_gt). The Challenger's reward (r_c) is a composite of `s_q`, `r_d`, and a format reward (r_f). Generated tasks are filtered: only questions with `s_q ≥ α` (quality threshold) and valid verifiers are added to the growing dataset `D`. If `s_q < α`, the difficulty reward component is suppressed to prevent training on ill-posed "hard" tasks.
2. **Plan-Solve Phase**: A question `q` (with its verifier `v`) is sampled from the dataset `D`. The Planner generates a plan `p`. The Critic evaluates `p`, providing a quality score `s_p`. If `s_p` meets a gating threshold `β`, the plan is provided to the Solver; otherwise, the Solver attempts to answer directly without a plan. The Solver generates an answer `a`. An external domain-specific verifier `V_gt` assesses the correctness of `a` against `v`, yielding `s_gt`. The Planner's reward (r_p) combines `s_p` and `r_f`. The Solver's reward (r_s) combines `s_p` (if plan was used), `s_gt`, and `r_f`.
3. **Critic Phase**: The Critic itself receives a format reward (r_cr = r_f) to ensure its outputs (scores) adhere to the required format, enhancing the stability of downstream reward computation.
4. **Synchronized Update**: All agents share the same LLM backbone. The parameters `θ` of this backbone are jointly updated using Task-Relative REINFORCE++, a critic-free policy gradient method. This method applies per-role advantage normalization to coordinate agents with heterogeneous objectives, stabilizing training across different reward signals.

#### 3.3. Reward Design and Normalization

- **Format Reward (r_f)**: A soft score ranging from 0 to 1, awarded based on the strict adherence to required output tags (e.g., `<question>`, `<plan>`, `<score>`). Partial credit is given for partial adherence, and low scores for missing or malformed tags.
- **Score Normalization**: Critic-generated scores, typically on a 1-10 scale, are normalized to a range using a defined piecewise function for consistent reward scaling.
- **Weighting Coefficients**: Specific weights (`w_p`, `w_c`, `w_f` for Solver, `λ_plan`, `λ_f` for Planner) are used to combine different reward components, allowing for flexible prioritization (e.g., Solver's default weights are 0.2 for plan quality, 0.6 for correctness, and 0.2 for format).

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Base Models**: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Qwen3-4B-Base models were used as the shared LLM backbones, initialized from their corresponding base checkpoints. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) was applied for efficient fine-tuning.
- **Training Data**: A small seed set of 500 instances was sampled from existing datasets: MATH, GSM8K, HumanEval, and MBPP.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks**:
 - **Mathematical Reasoning**: In-distribution (ID) benchmarks included GSM8K and MATH. Out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks comprised AIME’24, AIME’25, OlympiadBench, and AMC’23.
 - **Code Generation**: ID benchmarks included HumanEval+ and MBPP+ (evaluated via Evalplus). OOD assessment used LiveCodeBench v1–v5.
- **Metric**: Pass@1 accuracy based on greedy decoding was reported for all benchmarks.
- **Baselines**: Performance was compared against the original base models, Absolute-Zero-Reasoning (AZR), and Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the SAGE framework across various large language models and reasoning benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Consistent Improvements Across Model Scales

SAGE consistently improved the reasoning capabilities of the tested LLMs:
- On the Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct model, SAGE achieved an Overall Average accuracy of 42.0%, representing a 1.6% improvement over the base model. Notable gains were observed in-distribution benchmarks such as GSM8K (from 84.6% to 85.5%) and MATH (from 60.4% to 66.2%).
- For the Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct model, SAGE yielded an Overall Average accuracy of 50.1%, a 2.5% improvement over its base model. This indicates consistent effectiveness across different model scales.
- Even with the stronger Qwen-3-4B-Base model, which already had high baseline performance (Overall Avg. 55.7%), SAGE attained the highest Code Average (56.2%) and remained competitive overall (55.9%). Specifically, it showed a 9.1% gain on LiveCodeBench (from 21.5% to 30.6%).

#### 4.2. Strong Out-of-Distribution Generalization

A notable strength of SAGE is its ability to generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks:
- SAGE achieved the best or near-best OOD Average performance across all three backbones (19.0% on Qwen-2.5-3B, 28.8% on Qwen-2.5-7B, and 36.0% on Qwen-3-4B).
- For the Qwen-2.5-7B model, SAGE improved the OOD Average by 4.2% while maintaining competitive in-distribution (ID) accuracy.
- On the LiveCodeBench, SAGE consistently achieved the best performance across all three backbones, demonstrating its effectiveness in OOD code generation scenarios.
- In mathematical reasoning, SAGE maintained competitive performance on competition-level benchmarks like OlympiadBench, showing a 10.7% improvement over the base Qwen-2.5-7B model (from 28.0% to 38.7%).

#### 4.3. Comparison with Baselines

In comparison to other self-training baselines:
- Absolute-Zero-Reasoning (AZR) and Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE) demonstrated inconsistent gains and, in some cases, performance degradation on specific benchmarks (e.g., AZR on Qwen-3-4B-Base showed a significant drop in Math Average from 56.3% to 46.7%).
- SAGE, in contrast, consistently provided more balanced improvements across both code generation and mathematical reasoning domains without significant performance sacrifices in any benchmark group.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

An ablation study on the Qwen-2.5-3B model revealed the contribution of each agent:
- **Full SAGE** (42.0% O Avg.) outperformed all ablated configurations.
- **Without Challenger Training**: A significant drop in code benchmarks, especially LiveCodeBench (from 16.9% to 9.0%), indicated the Challenger's importance for curriculum generation and OOD generalization.
- **Without Solver Training**: This resulted in the largest overall decline (O Avg. 38.2%), with substantial drops in both GSM8K (from 85.5% to 81.2%) and MATH (from 66.2% to 60.4%), confirming the Solver's role as the primary driver of reasoning capability.
- **Without Critic Training**: This led to a degradation in code benchmarks (C Avg. 44.8%) while maintaining competitive math performance. This suggests the Critic's quality filtering is particularly crucial for code generation, where output format and correctness are tightly linked.
These findings confirm that all three trainable agents (Challenger, Planner, Solver) complement each other, with the Challenger–Solver interaction forming the core co-evolutionary loop and the Critic providing essential quality control.

#### 4.5. Training Dynamics Analysis

Analysis of the training dynamics on Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct showed:
- Validation accuracy rapidly improved initially, peaking around steps 100–140 (69.5%), before a gradual decline (to 61.6% by step 240). This suggests potential over-specialization on the self-generated curriculum in later stages of training.
- The cumulative number of valid questions expanded steadily throughout training, increasing 18-fold from the seed set (from 1,136 to 20,532 by step 250). The acceleration of this growth coincided with the peak validation accuracy, indicating that a well-trained Challenger, with effective Critic filtering, generated high-quality tasks at an increasing rate.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Analysis

A qualitative case study demonstrated the collaborative reasoning process. The Challenger generated a math word problem. The Planner decomposed it into four structured steps. The Solver systematically executed these steps to derive the correct answer. The Critic provided quality scores for both the generated question and the plan, illustrating how specialized roles facilitate effective division of labor and coordination within the unified training loop.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SAGE framework presents a significant contribution to the field of large language model (LLM) reasoning by offering a scalable and effective approach to autonomous self-evolution.

**Reduced Dependency on Human Supervision**: A primary impact of SAGE is its ability to reduce the reliance on extensive human-curated datasets for verifiable rewards. By leveraging a multi-agent closed-loop system, SAGE enables LLMs to generate their own challenging tasks and solutions, supervised only by a small initial seed set and domain-specific external verifiers. This addresses a critical scalability bottleneck in training advanced LLM reasoning capabilities, allowing for more autonomous and cost-effective development.

**Enhanced Reasoning and Generalization**: The framework consistently improves LLMs' performance across complex reasoning tasks in mathematics and code generation. Notably, SAGE demonstrates strong out-of-distribution generalization, enabling models to perform effectively on unseen, competition-level benchmarks. This suggests that the self-generated curriculum and multi-agent interactions foster a deeper understanding and more robust reasoning capabilities, moving beyond rote memorization of in-distribution examples.

**Improved Training Stability and Quality Control**: The integration of a dual-role Critic agent for quality filtering of generated tasks and plans is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the training process. This mechanism prevents curriculum degradation and ensures that the self-generated challenges remain relevant, solvable, and of high quality. The explicit planning agent further contributes to stable learning by guiding the Solver through structured, multi-step problem-solving. This robust quality control differentiates SAGE from some prior self-play approaches that struggled with instability.

**Multi-Agent Collaboration for Complex Tasks**: SAGE exemplifies the effectiveness of specialized multi-agent collaboration, where distinct roles (Challenger, Planner, Solver, Critic) work in concert within a unified LLM backbone. This architecture demonstrates how complex reasoning can be decomposed and tackled more effectively through an adversarial yet collaborative dynamic, providing insights into designing sophisticated AI systems.

**Potential Future Directions (informed by limitations)**:
- **Expansion to Non-Verifiable Domains**: Future work could explore extending SAGE to open-ended tasks where objective, external verification is challenging. This might involve developing learned reward models or human-in-the-loop validation mechanisms.
- **Minimizing Seed Requirements**: Further research into reducing the initial seed set requirement could broaden the applicability of SAGE to extremely low-resource scenarios, making it more accessible.
- **Exploration of Other Reasoning Domains**: Applying SAGE to other structured reasoning domains, such as logical reasoning, scientific problem-solving, or knowledge graph completion, could validate its generalizability and uncover new insights.
- **Mitigating Over-Specialization**: The observed decline in validation accuracy during prolonged training suggests potential over-specialization on the self-generated curriculum. Future work could investigate dynamic curriculum strategies, early stopping mechanisms, or diversity-promoting rewards to maintain optimal performance over longer training durations.

Overall, SAGE represents a scalable and effective pathway for evolving capable reasoning agents, significantly reducing dependency on extensive human supervision while enhancing robustness and generalization in challenging AI tasks.
