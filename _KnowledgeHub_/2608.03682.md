# PhyAI: Real-Time Physical AI at the Edge, Scalable Rollouts in the Cloud

> [!summary] Summary
> PhyAI introduces a unified inference runtime designed for Physical AI policies, supporting both Vision-Language-Action and World-Action models across edge, cloud, and onboard deployments. The system demonstrates speedups ranging from 1.4x to 4.65x over official implementations and provides a 'control-time Roofline' metric to guide optimization efforts for real-time robotic control.

## Problem

- Existing Physical AI policy deployments rely on separate, fragmented inference programs for different lifecycle stages (offline evaluation, cloud RL, edge serving, onboard robots), leading to inconsistent behavior and duplicated optimization efforts.
- Current inference systems are specialized for specific models/devices or focused on token-oriented decoding for LLMs, failing to comprehensively support the complex, multimodal execution structures required by embodied AI policies (e.g., iterative action solvers, evolving world latents).
- Meeting diverse performance requirements (minimal latency for real-time control, high throughput for shared servers, large batch processing for cloud rollouts) consistently for varied model architectures within a single, coherent framework is challenging.

## Method

- Developed **PhyAI**, a unified inference runtime architecture that separates model-specific logic from shared execution services, allowing for adaptable execution policies across diverse embodied AI models (VLA, WAM) and hardware architectures.
- Integrated a suite of optimization techniques including custom ==kernel fusion== (e.g., fused AdaRMSNorm, combined QKV projections), ==CUDA Graph== replay for iterative policy loops, profiled operator selection, and offline model quantization.
- Implemented a scheduler to orchestrate various parallelism strategies such as ==Data Parallelism==, ==Tensor Parallelism==, and Classifier-Free Guidance parallelism, tailoring execution to specific deployment demands.

## Results

- Achieved significant speedups over official implementations for various Physical AI models, including **2.64x** for "pi_0" on Thor, **4.31x** for GR00T N1.7 on A40, and **4.65x** for MiniCPM-Robot on H100.
- Improved batching throughput (e.g., "pi_0.5" from **44.26** to **100.02 samples/s** at batch 32) and cut cloud RL rollout's inference-time share by **16.9 percentage points**, projecting **1.36x** higher training throughput.
- Application of the control-time Roofline revealed that some robotic control tasks (e.g., "pi_0.5" on LIBERO) were environment-bound, while others (Cosmos3 generation path) remained inference-bound.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, consistent inference path can effectively unify the deployment lifecycle of Physical AI policies, significantly reducing engineering overhead and ensuring predictable model behavior across diverse environments.
> - The 'control-time Roofline' model provides a critical diagnostic tool, distinguishing between inference-bound and environment-bound robotic control scenarios to guide more efficient resource allocation and optimization strategies.
> - High-performance runtime design for embodied AI must move beyond token-oriented LLM serving, specifically accommodating complex multimodal execution patterns like iterative action solvers, evolving world latents, and classifier-free guidance branches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Physical AI policies, encompassing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g., RT-1, RT-2, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, GR00T) and World-Action Models (WAMs) (e.g., Cosmos3, DreamZero), has led to a requirement for these policies to operate across various stages of their lifecycle. These stages include offline evaluation, cloud-based reinforcement learning (RL) rollouts, shared edge serving, and onboard robotic deployment.

A significant challenge in this landscape is the current reliance on separate, often disparate, inference programs for each of these settings, even when using the same model checkpoint and action semantics. This fragmentation necessitates independent optimization and validation efforts for each path, potentially leading to inconsistencies in behavior across deployment environments.

Existing inference systems address aspects of this problem but do not provide a comprehensive solution. Systems like FlashRT, vla.cpp, and realtime-vla offer optimizations for specific models or devices. LeRobot provides a generalized asynchronous policy-inference stack but does not extend to the kernel-level, multi-architecture execution engine required for a unified approach. Large Language Model (LLM) serving systems such as vLLM and SGLang focus on token-oriented decoding, which does not directly translate to the execution models of embodied AI, which involve multimodal conditions, iterative action solvers, evolving world latents, or classifier-free guidance branches.

PhyAI positions itself to fill this gap by providing a unified inference runtime. It aims to offer a single, consistent inference path that preserves model semantics while adapting execution policies to different architectures and deployment scenarios. This approach seeks to streamline the development and deployment process for embodied AI, ensuring consistent model behavior and reducing engineering overhead across the entire model lifecycle. The introduction of the control-time Roofline further contributes to the landscape by offering a metric to evaluate the actual impact of inference latency on overall control performance, distinguishing between inference-bound and environment-bound scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of PhyAI is to establish a unified inference runtime for Physical AI policies. This runtime aims to provide a single, consistent inference path that can be used across various deployment settings, including onboard robots, edge servers, and cloud infrastructure, for both Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World-Action Models (WAMs). The system's design seeks to maintain model semantics while allowing for adaptable execution policies tailored to specific hardware architectures and deployment demands.

This central objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing Inconsistent Inference Paths:** Current practice in Physical AI involves using separate inference programs for different stages of a model's lifecycle. For instance, a policy might have one program for offline evaluation, another for cloud reinforcement learning (RL) rollouts, and distinct implementations for edge GPU serving and onboard deployment. Even though these programs utilize the same model checkpoint and action semantics, their separation leads to duplicated effort in optimization and validation. This fragmentation can also result in behavioral discrepancies, where a policy's performance or characteristics observed in one setting do not directly translate to another. PhyAI seeks to eliminate this issue by offering a single, validated codebase for inference.

2. **Managing Diverse Model Architectures:** Embodied AI models exhibit varied execution structures. VLA models, such as the $\pi$-family, typically involve a vision-language backbone followed by an iterative action expert. In contrast, WAMs like Cosmos3 evolve video and action states conjointly. These structural differences require distinct approaches to optimize execution, memory management, and parallelization. A unified runtime must accommodate these architectural nuances without forcing all models into a single, suboptimal pattern.

3. **Meeting Varied Deployment Requirements:** Different deployment contexts impose distinct performance criteria. Onboard deployments prioritize minimal single-request latency for real-time control. Shared edge servers must balance latency with high throughput to serve multiple robots efficiently. Cloud rollouts often leverage large batch sizes and distributed execution for rapid data generation. These diverse workloads demand tailored scheduling and parallel strategies, which existing fragmented systems struggle to provide consistently under a single policy interface.

4. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Runtimes:** While some specialized runtimes (e.g., FlashRT, vla.cpp, realtime-vla) optimize specific models or devices, and LLM serving systems (e.g., vLLM, SGLang) exist, none offer a comprehensive, kernel-level execution engine that can universally support the complex requirements of multimodal embodied AI. These requirements include reusable multimodal conditions, iterative action solvers, evolving world latents, and classifier-free guidance branches. PhyAI aims to bridge this gap by offering a runtime capable of preserving these model semantics across multiple architectures and deployment settings.

5. **Establishing a Relevant Performance Metric:** The authors identify that simply reducing inference latency does not always equate to a proportional improvement in control rate. In sequential control loops, inference time is on the critical path, but with techniques like real-time chunking (RTC), inference can be overlapped with action execution. This motivates the development of the "control-time Roofline," a conceptual model that distinguishes between inference-bound and environment-bound control. The objective here is to provide a framework for evaluating inference performance in the context of robotic control deadlines, guiding whether further inference acceleration is beneficial or if resources could be reallocated (e.g., to larger models or cheaper hardware).

In summary, PhyAI is motivated by the need for efficiency, consistency, and adaptability in the deployment of Physical AI policies, aiming to consolidate the inference lifecycle onto a single, high-performance, and semantically consistent platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PhyAI's methodology centers on a unified inference runtime architecture designed to separate model-specific logic from shared execution services. This separation allows for adaptability across diverse embodied AI models and deployment environments.

#### 3.1. PhyAI Architecture

The system is structured in a layered hierarchy:
- **Scheduler:** Manages multiple model runners and orchestrates parallel execution strategies such as Data Parallelism (DP), Tensor Parallelism (TP), and Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) parallelism. It determines device grouping and work partitioning.
- **Model Runner:** Owns the mutable state for individual requests, including intermediate buffers, KV caches, CUDA Graph buckets, and their memory lifetimes. It is responsible for advancing solver or generation loops and assembling action chunks.
- **Stateless Modeling Modules:** Implement architecture-specific computations (e.g., multimodal prefix encoding, action experts, coupled video-action generation) without retaining request-specific state between calls.
- **Layers Interface:** Provides a uniform way for modeling modules to express computation, offering fused and distributed operators. An operator selector dispatches appropriate kernels based on matrix shape, data type, accelerator, and execution configuration.
- **phyai-kernel, Framework/Library Kernels:** Low-level kernel implementations selected by the Layers interface.
- **phyai-utils-tools:** Handles preprocessing and post-processing steps.
- **phyai-model-optimizer:** Performs offline model quantization.

#### 3.2. Optimization Methods

PhyAI integrates several optimization techniques within its runtime:
- **Kernel Fusion:** Custom CUDA kernels are developed to fuse multiple small operations into larger ones, reducing launch overhead and intermediate data movement. Examples include fused AdaRMSNorm and residual-add plus RMSNorm, combined QKV projections in one GEMM, and fused MLP gate/up projections with GeGLU/SwiGLU activations. For MiniCPM-Robot, a Triton kernel for RMSNorm with SiLU gate and another for depthwise causal Conv1d, SiLU, and Q/K/V split are used.
- **Graph Replay (CUDA Graphs):** For iterative policies that repeat identical computation patterns with minimal state changes, PhyAI captures these loops as CUDA Graphs. The model runner manages reusable tensors (e.g., KV caches, attention metadata) and fixed buffers. This approach reduces CPU overhead by eliminating redundant kernel launches for stable operations.
- **Profiled Operator Selection:** Instead of statically binding an operation to a single kernel, PhyAI's operator selector dynamically chooses the most efficient kernel based on real-time profiling. This selection considers tensor shape, data type, target accelerator, and current execution configuration (e.g., using FlashInfer FA2 for specific attention patterns in $\pi_{0.5}$).
- **Quantization:** Models are quantized offline using round-to-nearest (RTN) via `phyai-model-optimizer`. High-performance operators from libraries like FlashInfer and Humming are utilized for quantized execution, supporting W4A16, W8A16, W8A8, and W4A8 precisions.

#### 3.3. Parallel Execution

PhyAI supports multiple parallelism strategies configured by the scheduler:
- **Data Parallelism (DP):** Independent requests are assigned to separate model runners, each executing a full single-GPU model path. This is suitable for scenarios where requests are independent, such as with $\pi_{0.5}$.
- **Tensor Parallelism (TP) and Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) Parallelism:** For models like Cosmos3-Nano-Policy-DROID, TP partitions computations within the model's layers (e.g., QKV and MLP projections), while CFG parallelism runs conditional and unconditional branches independently on separate TP groups. These groups communicate through collective operations like `all-gather` to combine intermediate results (e.g., guided velocities) before proceeding to the next denoising step. The system can also reuse ranks for spatially tiled VAE decoding if required.

#### 3.4. Control-Time Roofline

The paper introduces the control-time Roofline model to provide a more nuanced evaluation of inference latency in robotic control. This model defines the full critical path for an action as $L_{critical} = L_{observe} + L_{transfer} + L_{queue} + L_{inference} + L_{actuate}$. The control period under ideal overlap is $L_{overlap} = \max(L_{inference}, L_{env})$, where $L_{env}$ is the environment execution time. This framework helps identify whether a system is "inference-bound" (inference is the bottleneck) or "environment-bound" (environment execution is the bottleneck), guiding further optimization efforts. The Roofline uses normalized axes: $X = L_{env} / L_{inference}$ (relative inference rate) and $Y = L_{env} / L_{control}$ (relative control rate), where $L_{control}$ is either sequential ($L_{inference} + L_{env}$) or overlapped ($\max(L_{inference}, L_{env})$).

#### 3.5. Measurement Settings

Evaluation involves two main sets of measurements:
- **Single-request latency:** Measures the model-runner execution time ($L_{inference}$) across various models (e.g., $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, GR00T N1.7, MiniCPM-Robot, Cosmos3) and hardware (e.g., RTX 5090, Thor, A40, H100, H20).
- **Batch scaling and phase analysis:** Analyzes throughput, full-batch latency, and the contribution of different execution phases (e.g., vision tower, language prefix, action expert for $\pi_{0.5}$; Prepare, Backbone, Action Head for GR00T; VAE encode, Condition, Generation for Cosmos3) as batch size increases. This includes calculating achieved throughput, logical arithmetic intensity, and comparing against device-specific Roofline models.
- **Cloud RL Rollout Simulation:** Simulates an integrated RL rollout workload using PhyAI as the inference backend to assess its impact on overall training throughput.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents detailed findings across single-request latency, batch scaling, phase analysis, control-time analysis, and cloud RL rollout simulations.

#### 4.1. Single-Request Latency

PhyAI consistently demonstrated speedups over the official implementations across 11 measured model-device pairs.
- **$\pi_0$**: Achieved a 2.64x speedup on Thor and 4.05x on RTX 5090.
- **$\pi_{0.5}$**: Demonstrated a 1.67x speedup on Thor and 1.82x on RTX 5090.
- **GR00T N1.7**: Showed speedups of 1.40x on Thor, 2.28x on RTX 5090, and 4.31x on A40.
- **MiniCPM-Robot**: Achieved 2.78x on Thor, 2.02x on RTX 5090, and 4.65x on H100.
- **Cosmos3-Nano-Policy-DROID**: Reduced latency from 2.46s to 1.18s (a 2.08x speedup) on eight H20 GPUs with CFG=2 and TP=4.

The report notes that these comparisons were not always fully precision-matched (e.g., some FlashRT results used FP8 while PhyAI used BF16), and other specialized runtimes (FlashRT, realtime-vla) could be faster in certain specific configurations. The measured advantage for PhyAI is primarily relative to the official implementations.

#### 4.2. Batch Scaling and Phase Analysis

The study analyzed how performance scales with batch size and identified bottlenecks within models:
- **$\pi_{0.5}$ (Hopper-series GPU):** At batch size one, the action expert contributed only 8.8% of estimated FLOPs but accounted for 57.2% of profiled latency. This indicated it was a bottleneck due to numerous small kernel launches (left of the Roofline ridge at 50.8 FLOP/byte). As batch size increased to 32, its latency share dropped to 13.5%, while the vision and language phases (51.4% and 35.1%, respectively) became dominant. Throughput increased from 44.26 samples/s (batch 1) to 100.02 samples/s (batch 32), with a full batch latency of 319.94 ms.
- **GR00T N1.7 (RTX 5090):** Throughput improved from 48.8 samples/s (batch 1) to 138.8 samples/s (batch 32). However, 92.2% of the maximum throughput was already achieved by batch size eight. The bottleneck shifted from the Action Head (59.2% of time at batch 1) to the Backbone (69.7% at batch 32).
- **Cosmos3 (Hopper-series GPU):** This model exhibited limited throughput gains with batching, increasing only 14.3% from batch size 1 (0.883 chunks/s) to batch size 16 (1.010 chunks/s). The generation phase was identified as already compute-bound at batch size one, operating to the right of the Roofline ridge (926.2 FLOP/byte and 68.5%–73.9% MFU), meaning larger batches offered little additional compute utilization and primarily increased overall request latency.

#### 4.3. Control-Time Roofline Application

- **$\pi_{0.5}$ on LIBERO suites:** The measured inference times for $\pi_{0.5}$ on four LIBERO suites (spatial, object, goal, LIBERO-10) were found to be shorter than the environment execution time. This indicates that these scenarios were **environment-bound**, meaning that further reducing inference latency would primarily create timing margin rather than directly increasing the ideal control rate under ideal overlap.
- **Cosmos3 generation path:** In contrast, the Cosmos3 generation path remained **inference-bound**, suggesting that improvements in its inference speed would directly translate to a faster control rate.

#### 4.4. Cloud RL Rollout Simulation

A simulated $\pi_{0.5}$ GRPO training workload using PhyAI as the inference backend showed:
- The inference component's share of the total RL step time decreased from 53.1% (baseline) to 36.2% (with PhyAI), representing a 16.9 percentage point reduction or a 31.8% relative decrease.
- This change corresponded to a projected 26.5% reduction in rollout-step latency and approximately 1.36x higher training throughput.
- Under configurations with more inference-intensive workloads (e.g., more denoising steps), PhyAI's speedup could lead to an estimated 1.32x–1.44x end-to-end speedup in RL training throughput.

In summary, PhyAI demonstrates significant performance improvements over official implementations and provides detailed insights into model bottlenecks and control performance dynamics across different deployment scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PhyAI runtime presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of Physical AI:

1. **Unified and Streamlined Development Workflow:** By providing a single inference path for both VLA and WAM models across onboard, edge, and cloud environments, PhyAI simplifies the development, optimization, and validation processes. This unification reduces the engineering effort associated with maintaining separate inference implementations for different stages of the model lifecycle, potentially accelerating research and development cycles for embodied AI. The consistent behavior across deployments ensures that policies trained and validated in one setting will function predictably in another.

2. **Enhanced Performance and Efficiency:** The demonstrated speedups (1.40x to 4.65x over official implementations) for various Physical AI models translate directly into practical benefits. Faster inference allows for:
 - **Quicker Robot Control Loops:** Reduced latency enables robots to react more swiftly to environmental changes, potentially improving task success rates and safety.
 - **Higher Throughput:** For shared edge servers and cloud RL rollouts, increased throughput means more samples can be processed per unit time, supporting larger-scale experimentation and training.
 - **Resource Optimization:** The ability to achieve competitive latency on less powerful or less expensive hardware (e.g., Jetson Thor) or to run more policies concurrently on existing hardware leads to more efficient resource utilization and potentially lower operational costs for robotic deployments.

3. **Informed Optimization Strategies through Control-Time Roofline:** The introduction of the control-time Roofline provides a critical diagnostic tool. It allows researchers and developers to ascertain whether a system's control rate is limited by inference speed (inference-bound) or by the time required for physical environment execution (environment-bound). This distinction guides optimization efforts, preventing unnecessary investment in further inference acceleration when it would not yield a proportional improvement in overall control performance. Instead, resources could be redirected to other aspects, such as deploying larger models, increasing visual resolution, or using more robust but slower devices.

4. **Acceleration of Reinforcement Learning Training:** The simulated results demonstrating a significant reduction in the inference portion of cloud RL rollout steps indicate a substantial impact on the efficiency of RL training. Faster policy inference directly shortens the wall-clock time required for training, enabling quicker iteration on models and potentially leading to the development of more capable Physical AI agents within reduced timelines.

5. **Foundation for Future Embodied AI Systems:** PhyAI's architecture, which cleanly separates model semantics from execution policy, offers a robust and adaptable foundation. This design supports the integration of new model architectures and diverse hardware accelerators (e.g., Ascend, Kunlunxin, Zhenwu, AMD Instinct), ensuring the runtime remains relevant as the field evolves. The project's open-source nature further invites community contributions, fostering shared infrastructure for Physical AI.

6. **Towards Production-Grade Robot-as-a-Service (MaaS):** The report discusses the potential for flexible deployment models, including local MaaS for factory fleets. PhyAI's ability to maintain consistent model behavior across onboard, edge, and cloud placements, while adapting execution for efficiency, is foundational for developing a reliable and scalable robot MaaS infrastructure. This could allow organizations to centralize compute resources for robotic fleets, optimizing for cost, utilization, and critical-path latency based on specific operational needs. The proposed "production-grade inference serving protocol" for stateful, timed streams of observations and actions would be a significant step towards this vision.

In conclusion, PhyAI offers a unified, high-performance runtime that addresses critical challenges in the deployment and scaling of Physical AI. Its contributions have the potential to significantly streamline development workflows, enhance operational efficiency, and accelerate the progress of embodied AI research toward real-world applications.
