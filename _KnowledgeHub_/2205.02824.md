# Rapid Locomotion via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> MIT Improbable AI Lab researchers developed an end-to-end reinforcement learning controller that enabled the Mini Cheetah robot to achieve rapid, agile, and robust locomotion, setting a new indoor speed record of 3.9 m/s and demonstrating high agility (Froude number 5.1) on diverse natural terrains.

## Problem

- Model-based control (MBC) for legged robots is limited by the accuracy and computational cost of hand-designed dynamic models, especially for complex, high-speed behaviors on unpredictable terrains.
- Previous reinforcement learning (RL) controllers for legged robots often lacked the agility and speed to match MBC benchmarks or operate across broad velocity ranges on diverse natural terrains.
- Training RL policies for complex, multi-task locomotion (e.g., simultaneous high linear and angular velocities) is challenging due to physical constraints and the difficulty of exploration in high-dimensional command spaces.

## Method

- A 5-layer neural network policy was trained using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== in IsaacGym, mapping sensory inputs and velocity commands to joint position commands for a ==low-gain PD controller==.
- A ==teacher-student training paradigm==, coupled with ==domain randomization==, enabled robust ==sim-to-real transfer== and ==online system identification== by inferring unknown environment parameters from sensory history.
- A novel ==Grid Adaptive Curriculum== strategy was introduced to gradually expand the command space for linear and angular velocities, effectively navigating the physical feasibility boundaries for high-speed, agile locomotion.

## Results

- The Mini Cheetah achieved a new indoor speed record of **3.9 m/s**, surpassing the previous **3.7 m/s** benchmark set by model-based controllers, and demonstrated a Froude number of **5.1**, the highest reported for the robot.
- The learned policy exhibited robust performance on diverse natural terrains (e.g., grass, pebbles, ice) at high speeds (**3.4 m/s** on grass) and maintained stability despite mechanical blockages, trips, and steep inclines, outperforming a baseline model-predictive controller.
- Ablation studies confirmed the Grid Adaptive Curriculum's superiority over other curriculum strategies and highlighted the critical role of online system identification for high-speed performance, with policies trained via teacher-student learning significantly outperforming those with only domain randomization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An adaptive curriculum, particularly the Grid Adaptive strategy, is crucial for successfully learning complex, high-speed, and agile locomotion policies, allowing the agent to explore feasible command combinations.
> - Teacher-student training with online system identification effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap, enabling learned policies to adapt to unknown real-world dynamics and achieve high performance comparable to privileged teacher policies.
> - Reinforcement learning can produce robust, agile locomotion controllers that can surpass model-based methods in specific performance metrics and exhibit emergent robustness to disturbances and varied terrains with minimal sensing requirements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot locomotion has historically been dominated by **model-based control (MBC)** approaches. MBC relies on simplified, hand-designed dynamic models and real-time trajectory optimization to enable robots to balance and move. While impressive progress has been made, allowing robots to achieve dynamic behaviors on various terrains (e.g.,), these methods are inherently limited by the human designer's ability to create accurate and efficient reduced-order models. As tasks become more complex, such as high-speed running and agile maneuvering on unpredictable natural terrains, the fidelity and computational cost of these models become significant bottlenecks. The robot's robustness and adaptability are directly tied to the "creativity and investment of the human designer."

In recent years, **reinforcement learning (RL)** has emerged as a powerful alternative, offering a scalable scheme for synthesizing controllers for complex tasks in challenging environments. RL allows robots to learn a direct mapping from sensory observations to actions (a policy) by maximizing a reward function over numerous simulated interactions. This approach reduces the reliance on explicit human-designed models, enabling the discovery of complex, often counter-intuitive, behaviors. Recent works have successfully employed RL to learn various locomotion controllers for quadrupedal robots like ANYmal, and Unitree A1, demonstrating robust walking and basic traversing capabilities.

However, a significant gap remained: achieving **record-breaking high-speed and agile locomotion** (both linear and angular velocity) on **diverse natural terrains** using RL. Previous RL-based controllers often exhibited lower agility (measured by Froude number) or were restricted to narrower ranges of commanded velocities compared to their model-based counterparts or the robots' physical limits. Training multi-task RL policies for broad velocity ranges, especially those involving simultaneous high linear and angular speeds, often failed because the combination of high speeds and agility introduced physical constraints (like centrifugal force) that made many tasks challenging or infeasible from scratch.

This paper directly addresses this gap. It aims to demonstrate that, by leveraging advanced RL techniques, an end-to-end learned controller can achieve agility levels competitive with, or even exceeding, state-of-the-art model-based controllers on the same hardware. It pushes the boundaries of RL applications in legged locomotion by focusing on extreme agility, high speeds, and real-world deployment on complex terrains, integrating established techniques like domain randomization, teacher-student training for online system identification, and introducing a novel adaptive curriculum strategy to overcome training difficulties. The work is also notably concurrent with Ji et al., which similarly uses RL for Mini Cheetah agility, further highlighting the growing interest and capability of RL in this domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end learned controller capable of achieving **rapid, agile, and robust locomotion** for the MIT Mini Cheetah robot across a wide range of linear and angular velocities, especially on diverse natural terrains.

The specific key objectives include:
- **Achieving Record Agility:** To enable the MIT Mini Cheetah to sustain speeds and execute maneuvers (e.g., high-speed turning) at or above previously reported benchmarks, particularly those achieved by model-based controllers.
- **Robustness on Natural Terrains:** To demonstrate reliable and stable performance on challenging, unstructured outdoor terrains such as grass, ice, and gravel, in addition to flat indoor surfaces.
- **Robustness to Disturbances and Failures:** To show the controller's ability to recover from unexpected events like tripping, mechanical blockages, or slips.
- **Minimal Sensing Requirements:** To develop a policy that operates with a minimal sensor suite (gyroscope and joint encoders), making it suitable for deployment on a broader range of robots, including commercially available and less expensive platforms.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To ensure that a policy trained purely in simulation can be directly transferred to the real world without further fine-tuning, demonstrating the efficacy of chosen randomization and adaptation techniques.
- **Scalable Controller Synthesis:** To provide a scalable alternative to model-based control that reduces the human effort required to design and refine complex models, especially for nuanced high-speed dynamics.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several challenges in robotics:
- The inherent limitations of human-designed models in capturing the full complexity of robot-environment interactions at high speeds and on varied terrains.
- The difficulty of training RL agents for highly dynamic tasks over broad command ranges, where naive sampling often leads to training failure due to task infeasibility or sparse rewards.
- The desire for legged robots to operate autonomously and effectively in unpredictable, human-centric environments, necessitating both high performance and adaptability.
- The potential of reinforcement learning to unlock emergent behaviors and levels of robustness that are difficult to engineer explicitly.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology involves training a neural network controller using reinforcement learning in a high-fidelity simulation and then transferring it directly to the real world. The approach is structured around three key components: a robust control architecture, a teacher-student training paradigm for sim-to-real transfer, and an adaptive curriculum strategy.

**Hardware and Simulation:**
- **Hardware:** The **MIT Mini Cheetah** quadruped robot is used, featuring 12 quasi-direct-drive actuators, joint position encoders, and an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU). The neural network controller runs onboard at 50 Hz.
- **Simulation:** The **IsaacGym simulator** is used for massively parallel training. The authors collected 400 million simulated timesteps using 4000 parallel agents, achieving this in under three hours on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. This high-throughput simulation environment is crucial for the data-intensive nature of RL.

**Control Architecture:**
- **Controller Design:** A 5-layer neural network $\pi_\theta$ directly maps sensory inputs and velocity commands to desired joint position commands. These commands are then sent to a low-gain PD controller on the robot's joints.
- **Observation Space:** The policy input $x_t$ consists of a history (15 timesteps) of previous observations $o_t$ (joint angles, joint velocities, gravity vector orientation from IMU, previous actions) concatenated with the current body velocity command $v^{cmd}_t$ (longitudinal, lateral, and yaw rate).
- **Action Space:** 12 joint position commands for the PD controller.
- **Reward Function:** A rich reward function is designed, closely following prior work, to encourage desirable behaviors:
 - **Task Rewards:** For tracking linear ($v^{cmd}_x, v^{cmd}_y$) and yaw ($\omega^{cmd}_z$) velocity commands.
 - **Stability Penalties:** For vertical velocity, roll-pitch velocity, base height deviation, and base orientation deviation.
 - **Smoothness Penalties:** For joint torques, joint accelerations, and action rate changes, along with a bonus for foot airtime.
 - **Safety Penalties:** For self-collision and joint limit violations. Specific penalties for body height and orientation were introduced to counter tendencies at high speeds.

**Teacher-Student Training for Sim-to-Real Transfer (Online System Identification):**
- To bridge the **sim-to-real gap** and enable adaptive behavior in the real world where certain parameters (e.g., ground friction, payload mass, motor strength) are unknown or vary, the authors employ a teacher-student training approach, a form of online system identification.
- **Domain Randomization:** During training, simulation parameters ($d_t$) like body mass, center of mass, motor strength, ground friction, and restitution are randomized within defined ranges (Table I).
- **Teacher Policy ($\pi_T$):** This policy is trained with **privileged information**, meaning it has direct access to the randomized domain parameters $d_t$ in addition to standard sensor data $x_t$. It's composed of an encoder $g_{\theta_d}$ that compresses $d_t$ into a latent vector $z_t$, and a policy body $\pi_{\theta_b}$ that uses $x_t$ and $z_t$ to produce actions. It is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Student Policy ($\pi_S$):** For real-world deployment, $d_t$ is unavailable. The student policy replaces the teacher's encoder $g_{\theta_d}$ with an **online identification module** $h_{\theta_a}$. This module learns to infer the latent representation $\hat{z}_t$ from a history of past observations $x_{[t-h:t-1]}$ (where $h=15$ timesteps). The student reuses the teacher's policy body $\pi_{\theta_b}$ and is trained via supervised learning to match $\hat{z}_t$ to the teacher's $z_t$ output, effectively learning to implicitly identify the environment dynamics from sensory history.

**Curriculum Strategy for High-Speed Locomotion:**
- Learning high-speed, omnidirectional locomotion across a broad range of linear and angular velocities is challenging. Training without a curriculum fails because most high-speed commands are difficult or infeasible from scratch, yielding little reward. The authors propose an automatic reward-based curriculum:
 - **Initialization:** The curriculum starts with a uniform distribution of easy-to-track, low-velocity commands ($v^{cmd}_x, \omega^{cmd}_z \in [-1.0, 1.0]$).
 - **Box Adaptive Curriculum:** Commands $v^{cmd}_x$ and $\omega^{cmd}_z$ are sampled independently. If the agent successfully tracks a command (based on individual reward thresholds $r_{v_x}$ and $r_{\omega_z}$), the sampling distribution expands to neighboring command regions for each dimension independently.
 - **Grid Adaptive Curriculum:** This novel strategy samples commands from a *joint* probability distribution over $v^{cmd}_x$ and $\omega^{cmd}_z$. Expansion of the command space (to 4-connected grid neighbors) only occurs if *both* linear and angular velocity tracking rewards exceed their respective thresholds. This approach inherently models the interaction between linear and angular speeds, allowing the curriculum to avoid combinations that are physically infeasible due to effects like centrifugal force (e.g., very high linear and angular velocity simultaneously).

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Tracking Error:** Defined as the root mean square deviation between commanded and actual linear ($v_x$) and yaw ($\omega_z$) velocities, averaged over trials within discretized grid cells of the command space.
- **Command Area:** An aggregate metric representing the area in the $v^{cmd}_x$-$\omega^{cmd}_z$ plane where the sum of linear and angular tracking errors is below a specified threshold $\epsilon_0$. This metric quantifies the diversity and range of agile commands the controller can reliably actuate.
- **Froude Number:** Used to compare agility across different robots by normalizing speed by leg length and gravity ($Fr = v^2/gl$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the efficacy of the proposed methodology in achieving rapid and agile locomotion.

**Curriculum Learning Enables High-Speed Locomotion:**
- **No Curriculum:** Training without a curriculum entirely failed to learn meaningful velocity control, highlighting the necessity of structured task progression for complex behaviors.
- **Box Adaptive Curriculum:** Showed substantial improvement, enabling the agent to learn and gradually expand its command tracking capabilities.
- **Grid Adaptive Curriculum:** Outperformed the Box Adaptive curriculum, achieving a significantly larger "command area" (Figure 3a). This demonstrates its superiority in handling the coupled nature of high linear and angular velocities, effectively exploring the feasible joint command space while respecting physical constraints. The observed inverse relationship between maximum linear and angular velocity at the command area boundary (Figure 3b) visually confirms the robot reaching its physical limits, which the Grid Adaptive curriculum successfully navigates.

**Real-world Testing and Record Agility:**
- **Indoor Running Speed:** The Mini Cheetah, controlled by the learned policy, sustained an average speed of **3.8 m/s**, with a peak recorded speed of **3.9 m/s** indoors. This surpasses the previous record of 3.7 m/s for the same robot achieved with a model-predictive controller.
- **Agility (Froude Number):** The achieved performance corresponds to a Froude number of **5.1**, which is the highest reported for the Mini Cheetah and is significantly more agile than previous reinforcement learning applications in legged locomotion (Table III). This puts the RL-based controller in a competitive class with highly specialized model-based controllers.
- **Outdoor Running:** The robot successfully completed a 10-meter dash on uneven grassy terrain at an average speed of **3.4 m/s**, demonstrating robust performance on natural, varied surfaces.
- **Yaw Control:** The policy enabled the robot to achieve a maximum yaw rate of **5.7 rad/s**, which is 90% of the fastest rate reported by model-based controllers. Crucially, this was achieved by a *single* policy that also handles linear locomotion, unlike model-based approaches that often use separate controllers. The robot also demonstrated robust spinning on challenging icy terrain (Figure 1d).
- **Emergent Robustness:** Qualitative experiments highlighted the controller's remarkable robustness:
 - Successful ascent of a steep incline made of small pebbles.
 - Maintaining balance despite a mechanical blockage in one motor.
 - Self-righting after tripping at high speed, flying upside down, and landing on its feet.
 - Recovery and gait adaptation after tripping over a small barrier.
 - In contrast, a baseline model-predictive controller failed in similar gravelly incline and barrier-tripping scenarios.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Impact of Online System Identification:** Policies trained with the teacher-student paradigm (online system identification) significantly outperformed those trained solely with domain randomization, particularly at high speeds (Figure 4, Table IV). The student policy, inferring domain parameters from observation history, nearly matched the performance of the privileged teacher policy, confirming the effectiveness of implicit system identification in reducing the sim-to-real gap for agile behaviors.
- **Impact of Rough-Terrain Training:** The study revealed a trade-off: while training on varied terrains can enhance robustness, a policy trained *only on flat ground* can still exhibit remarkable robustness on diverse outdoor terrains. Furthermore, explicitly introducing rough terrain randomization during training *decreased* the maximum speed/command area achievable on flat ground (Figure 5). This suggests that for maximal speed on ideal surfaces, specializing the training environment can be beneficial, and the learned policy’s flat-ground training still generalized well to mild outdoor roughness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a significant milestone in the field of legged robotics and reinforcement learning, with several profound implications:

- **Pushes the Boundaries of RL in Legged Locomotion:** The paper conclusively demonstrates that end-to-end reinforcement learning can produce controllers for small quadruped robots that achieve levels of agility (speed, turning radius, Froude number) and robustness on diverse natural terrains that are competitive with, and in some aspects superior to, highly-tuned model-predictive control approaches. This challenges the long-held belief that precise model-based methods are indispensable for extreme dynamic performance.
- **Validation of Advanced RL Techniques:** The success of this work strongly validates the effectiveness of an adaptive curriculum (especially the Grid Adaptive strategy) for learning complex, multi-task policies in physically constrained environments. It also reinforces the critical role of teacher-student training with online system identification for robust sim-to-real transfer, particularly for high-speed and adaptive behaviors.
- **Practical Accessibility and Scalability:** By achieving high performance with a minimal sensing suite (IMU and joint encoders) and on a relatively accessible robot platform (Mini Cheetah, with commercially available analogues), the methodology significantly lowers the barrier for other researchers to reproduce, build upon, and deploy advanced legged robot controllers. The RL paradigm offers a scalable pathway to synthesize complex behaviors without extensive manual model engineering.
- **Enabling Future Robot Applications:** The demonstrated ability to run at high speeds, execute agile turns, and robustly traverse varied, unstructured outdoor terrains moves legged robots closer to real-world deployment in dynamic, unpredictable environments. This opens up possibilities for applications in inspection, search and rescue, logistics, and exploration where agility and robustness are paramount.
- **Direction for Future Research:** The paper identifies several promising avenues:
 - **Multi-Objective Optimization:** Combining agile locomotion with other objectives like energy efficiency, wear minimization, or incorporating human preferences.
 - **Vision Integration:** Extending the system to use vision for predictive tasks like stair climbing, obstacle avoidance, and path planning.
 - **Complex Behaviors:** Expanding beyond ground-plane velocity control to encompass behaviors like jumping, crouching, or loco-manipulation.
 - **Understanding Trade-offs:** Further investigation into the trade-offs between specialization for speed on flat ground versus generalization to rough terrains, potentially leading to adaptive policies that can dynamically choose their operating modes.

In essence, Margolis et al. provide compelling evidence that advanced reinforcement learning, when carefully engineered with suitable curriculum and sim-to-real transfer strategies, is not just a viable alternative but a leading contender for realizing highly dynamic and robust legged robot locomotion in the real world.
