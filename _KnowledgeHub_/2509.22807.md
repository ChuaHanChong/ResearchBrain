# MTRec: Learning to Align with User Preferences via Mental Reward Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from South China University of Technology, Dalian University of Technology, and Huawei Noah's Ark Lab developed MTRec, a framework that infers users' "mental rewards" from observed behavior using a novel distributional Inverse Reinforcement Learning approach. This method provides complementary supervision to existing recommender models, yielding a 7% increase in user viewing time on a real-world short-video platform and enhancing performance on public datasets and a simulated environment.

## Problem

- Recommender systems primarily rely on implicit user feedback, which frequently misrepresents actual user satisfaction.
- This discrepancy leads to models optimizing for surrogate objectives that do not align with true user preferences.
- Traditional methods for handling erroneous feedback are often insufficient for systematic misalignments.

## Method

- Model users as active agents within a ==User-Centric Markov Decision== Process, aiming to maximize their internal "mental rewards".
- Introduce Quantile Regression Inverse Q-learning (QR-IQL) to infer a distributional mental reward function from observed user behaviors, accounting for stochastic satisfaction.
- Integrate the learned mental reward model as an auxiliary supervision signal to enhance both classification-based and reinforcement learning-based recommender systems.

## Results

- Consistently improved AUC and Normalised Capped Importance Sampling (NCIS) across various classification-based sequential recommendation models on Amazon datasets.
- Boosted episodic Click-Through-Rate (eCTR) for RL-based models like PPO and SAC in the Virtual Taobao simulation, with SAC's eCTR increasing from **0.7055** to **0.909**.
- Achieved a stable **7%** increase in average user viewing time during a 7-day online A/B test on a large-scale industrial short-video recommendation platform.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Users' internal satisfaction, termed "mental reward," is a stochastic quantity that influences subsequent interactions and can be inferred from observed behavior.
> - Implicit feedback signals often misalign with true user satisfaction, as evidenced by user actions sometimes corresponding to low mental rewards.
> - Learning a distributional mental reward function, rather than a deterministic one, is crucial for accurately capturing the variability of user preferences and experiences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recommender systems predominantly rely on implicit user feedback, such as clicks or viewing duration, for training due to the inherent sparsity and cost of obtaining explicit feedback (e.g., ratings). However, a significant challenge within the field is the observed misalignment between these implicit signals and users' actual preferences or satisfaction. For instance, a user might click on an item due to an appealing preview but find the content unsatisfactory, or skip an item not due to dislike but prior exposure. This discrepancy can lead recommender systems to optimize for surrogate objectives that do not genuinely reflect user satisfaction.

Previous research efforts to mitigate this misalignment have included treating erroneous feedback as noise and applying denoising techniques, or fusing multiple feedback signals. However, these methods often exhibit limited effectiveness, particularly when erroneous feedback is systematic rather than random, or when conflicting signals are present. These approaches typically focus on data mining solutions without deeply addressing the fundamental mismatch in user preference representation.

This work draws inspiration from the field of AI alignment, particularly in Natural Language Processing, where reward models are used to guide Large Language Models (LLMs) towards aligning with human values. Methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reward RAnked FineTuning (RAFT) exemplify this. The paper extends this concept to recommender systems, proposing a "reward model" for user preferences.

Within the context of Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) for sequential recommendation, existing RL-based approaches typically model the recommender system as an agent seeking to maximize system-centric rewards, often suffering from biases in user simulators. IRL has been applied to infer system reward functions from expert recommendation policies. This paper diverges by adopting a user-centric perspective, modeling users as active agents whose behaviors are driven by internal "mental rewards." This shift allows for the inference of a user-specific reward function from observed behaviors, addressing a gap in previous system-centric IRL applications in recommendation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance sequential recommendation models by aligning them more closely with users' true preferences, moving beyond the limitations of implicit feedback. This goal is motivated by the observation that implicit feedback frequently fails to capture actual user satisfaction, leading to a fundamental misalignment in the optimization targets of current recommendation systems.

The core motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

- **Quantify User Internal Satisfaction:** The central motivation is to develop a method to quantify and uncover users' "internal satisfaction" with recommended items. This internal satisfaction is conceptualized as a "mental reward" that is generated each time a user interacts with an item. This mental reward is distinct from observed implicit feedback (e.g., clicks), which may not accurately reflect true feelings.
- **Bridge the Gap between Models and Preferences:** The research aims to bridge the gap between what recommendation models optimize for (surrogate objectives like click-through rate) and users' real preferences. The hypothesis is that by understanding and optimizing for mental rewards, recommendation models can achieve better alignment with user satisfaction.
- **Leverage User Behavioral Dynamics:** The authors observe two key characteristics of this mental reward:
 - **Influence on Short-term Interests and Subsequent Behaviors:** Mental rewards are posited to influence a user's immediate interests and future actions. For example, a negative mental reward from an item could lead to a loss of interest in similar items.
 - **User Reward Maximization:** Users are assumed to be implicitly maximizing their accumulated mental rewards during interactions with a recommender system, naturally seeking positive experiences. These observations suggest that if mental rewards can be accurately inferred, they can serve as a potent signal for guiding recommendation models.
- **Develop a Novel Framework (MTRec):** To achieve these objectives, the paper proposes MTRec, a sequential recommendation framework designed to learn and utilize a mental reward model.
- **Address Stochasticity in User Preferences:** Acknowledging that user preferences and environmental factors can introduce randomness, the objective extends to learning a *distributional* mental reward function rather than a deterministic one. This is crucial for capturing the intrinsic variability of user satisfaction.
- **Improve Existing Recommendation Models:** The ultimate objective is to demonstrate that the learned mental reward model can provide complementary supervision signals to improve the performance of both classification-based and reinforcement learning-based recommendation models, leading to increased user engagement and satisfaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MTRec framework consists of three primary components: a User-Centric Markov Decision Process (MDP), a distributional Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) method for learning mental rewards, and strategies for applying the learned mental reward model to guide existing recommendation models.

#### 3.1. User-Centric Markov Decision Process (MDP)

Unlike traditional RL models in recommendation where the system is the agent, MTRec models the *user* as the active agent within an MDP, seeking to maximize her own accumulated mental rewards. The MDP is defined by:
- **State Space (S):** A state $s_t = (h_t, i_t)$ encapsulates the interaction history $h_t$ up to time $t$ and the item $i_t$ currently being considered or displayed. An interaction is a tuple $<u, i, a>_t$ (user, item, action).
- **Action Space (A):** Represents the user's response to an item, simplified here to positive or negative actions (e.g., click/skip, watch/skip).
- **Transition Function (P):** Defines how the state evolves after a user takes an action, forming a new state $s_{t+1}$.
- **Mental Reward Function (R):** This is a key departure. It maps a state-action pair $(s, a)$ to a *distribution* of mental rewards $r \in \mathbb{R}$, acknowledging that satisfaction can be stochastic due to various influencing factors.
- **User's Behavioral Policy ($\pi$):** Maps a state to a distribution over actions, reflecting the user's strategy to maximize accumulated mental rewards.

#### 3.2. Uncovering the Mental Reward Model (Quantile Regression Inverse Q-learning - QR-IQL)

Given the assumption that users act to maximize their mental rewards, Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) is employed to infer this reward function from observed user behavior (expert trajectories).
- **Limitation of Conventional IRL:** Standard IRL methods typically recover a deterministic reward function, which is insufficient for capturing the stochastic nature of mental rewards.
- **Distributional Approach:** To address this, MTRec introduces **Quantile Regression Inverse Q-learning (QR-IQL)**, a novel distributional variant of IRL. Instead of learning a scalar Q-value, QR-IQL learns a distributional Q-function, where the distribution of Q is characterized by a set of quantiles.
- **Quantile Representation:** The Q-function, $Q(s, a)$, is approximated by the expectation of a random variable $Z(s, a)$, whose distribution is represented by $N$ quantiles, $\{\lambda_i(s, a)\}_{i=1}^N$. The mean $Q_{\lambda}(s, a) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i(s, a)$.
- **Objective Formulation:** QR-IQL reformulates the Maximum Entropy IRL objective (Problem 4 in the paper) to optimize for a distributional Q-function. It incorporates a specific convex regularizer $\psi(x) = x - \frac{1}{4\alpha}x^2$ and modifies the objective to directly optimize the Q-network that outputs quantile values.
- **Optimization:** The optimization process for learning the optimal quantile distribution supports $\{\lambda_i(s, a)\}_{i=1}^N$ utilizes the Pinball loss, which is suitable for quantile regression. This loss encourages the predicted quantile values to match the target distribution. The mental rewards $r^*(s, a)$ are then recovered from the optimal Q-function using a derived relationship.

#### 3.3. Applications of the Mental Reward Model

The learned mental reward model $r^*(s, a)$ is not directly used for recommendation due to its potential lack of fine-grained feature modeling. Instead, it provides a complementary supervision signal to guide the training of existing recommendation models ($F_{\zeta}$).
- **Classification-based Models:** For models optimized via Cross-Entropy loss ($L_{CE}$), an **alignment loss ($L_{Align}$)** is introduced. This loss encourages the model to assign higher probabilities to actions that yield higher mental rewards. The final loss is a weighted sum: $L_{Final}(\zeta) = L_{CE}(\zeta) + \kappa \cdot L_{Align}(\zeta)$, where $\kappa$ balances the two objectives.
- **RL-based Models:** For recommender systems modeled as RL agents maximizing accumulated system rewards ($\hat{r}(h, i, a)$), the mental reward $r^*(h, i, a)$ is simply added to the existing environment reward: $L_{Final}(\zeta) = -\mathbb{E}_{i_t \sim F_{\zeta}}[\sum_t (\hat{r}(h_{t-1}, i_t, a_t) + \kappa \cdot r^*(h_{t-1}, i_t, a_t))]$.
- **Implementation Details:** The Q-network architecture for learning mental rewards can be aligned with the recommendation model architecture (except the output layer) to ensure consistent feature processing. All training is conducted offline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of MTRec was evaluated through extensive experiments across offline public datasets, a simulated online environment, and a real-world industrial platform.

#### 4.1. Experiments on Public Datasets (RQ1: Classification-based Models)

- **Datasets:** Amazon Books and Electronics, treating user reviews as sequential behaviors.
- **Baselines:** Eight established classification-based sequential recommendation models: Wide&Deep, PNN, DeepFM, SASRec, DIN, DIEN, LinRec, and SIGMA. Each baseline was tested individually and augmented with MTRec (denoted as "-MTRec") or a non-distributional IRL approach ("-IRL").
- **Metrics:** Area Under Curve (AUC) for ranking ability, and Normalised Capped Importance Sampling (NCIS) for approximating online performance and long-term engagement.
- **Results:** MTRec consistently improved both AUC and NCIS across nearly all baseline models on both datasets. The improvements in NCIS were notably more significant than those in AUC, suggesting that MTRec effectively enhances overall user satisfaction and long-term engagement without sacrificing ranking accuracy. Comparing MTRec with the non-distributional IRL variant demonstrated the benefit of learning a distributional mental reward model.

#### 4.2. Experiments on Virtual Taobao (RQ2: RL-based Models)

- **Environment:** Virtual Taobao, a simulated interactive recommendation platform.
- **Baselines:** Two prominent RL algorithms, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC).
- **Metric:** Episodic Click-Through-Rate (eCTR).
- **Results:** By incorporating mental rewards into the reward function (with $\kappa = 0.2$), MTRec significantly boosted the average eCTR for both PPO (from 0.5435 to 0.678) and SAC (from 0.7055 to 0.909). This indicates that mental rewards provide valuable signals for improving RL-based recommendation policies in maximizing user engagement in sequential interactions.

#### 4.3. Evaluation of the Mental Reward Model (RQ3)

Direct evaluation of the mental reward model is challenging due to the lack of ground-truth labels. The authors conducted indirect evaluations based on its correlation with behavioral patterns:
- **Mental Rewards Over Time:** On the Amazon Book dataset, the averaged mental rewards were observed to decrease with an increasing number of interaction steps within a trajectory. This aligns with the intuitive notion of user fatigue or decreasing satisfaction over prolonged interactions.
- **Distribution of Rewards given Actions:** Visualizations showed the distribution of expected mental rewards for actions actually taken by users, and counterfactual rewards for alternative actions. A non-negligible proportion of actions that users *actually took* (e.g., a click) were associated with *relatively low* mental rewards. This observation indirectly validates the "misalignment problem" (e.g., clicking on a headline but finding the content dissatisfying), demonstrating that implicit feedback does not always equate to high mental satisfaction, and that the mental reward model can capture this discrepancy.

#### 4.4. Online A/B test on Industrial Platform (RQ4)

- **Platform:** A large-scale industrial short-video recommendation platform (tens of millions of DAU).
- **Baseline:** A Deep Cross Network (DCN) model trained on binary click labels.
- **Metric:** Average user viewing time, a key indicator of user engagement and satisfaction in short-video platforms.
- **Results:** An online A/B test over a 7-day period showed a stable increase of approximately 7% in average user viewing time for the DCN model augmented with MTRec, compared to the baseline DCN. This result demonstrates the practical effectiveness of MTRec in a real-world setting, confirming its ability to improve overall recommendation quality and user engagement by aligning with deeper user preferences. The industrial friendliness of MTRec was highlighted due to its offline training and auxiliary loss integration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "MTRec: Learning to Align with User Preferences via Mental Reward Models" holds several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of recommender systems:

- **Addressing a Fundamental Problem:** The work directly tackles the long-standing challenge of misalignment between easily collectible implicit user feedback and users' true, underlying preferences or satisfaction. This is a crucial step beyond merely mitigating noise or biases in feedback signals, aiming for a deeper understanding of user intent.
- **Novel Conceptualization of "Mental Reward":** By formally introducing and quantifying the concept of a "mental reward"—users' internal satisfaction—the paper provides a new theoretical lens through which to analyze and optimize user-recommender system interactions. This shifts the paradigm from users as passive label providers to active agents with internal reward functions.
- **Technical Advancement in Inverse Reinforcement Learning:** The development of Quantile Regression Inverse Q-learning (QR-IQL) is a notable technical contribution. By enabling the learning of a *distributional* mental reward function, it allows for the capture of stochasticity and richer information about user satisfaction, which conventional, deterministic IRL methods cannot achieve. This method could potentially be applied to other domains requiring the inference of stochastic expert rewards.
- **Broad Applicability and Robustness:** The demonstrated improvements across a diverse range of recommendation models (both classification-based and RL-based) and different datasets (e-commerce, simulated, and industrial short-video) suggest the framework's versatility and robustness. It offers a general method to augment existing systems rather than requiring a complete overhaul.
- **Demonstrated Real-World Value:** The successful deployment and observed 7% increase in average user viewing time on an industrial short-video platform provide strong evidence of MTRec's practical utility and its capacity to drive tangible improvements in user engagement and satisfaction in real-world applications. This result is particularly significant given the scale and competitiveness of industrial recommendation systems.
- **Foundation for Future User Modeling:** The work opens new avenues for research into advanced user modeling. Future efforts could focus on further refining the evaluation of mental reward models, potentially through comprehensive human studies, and exploring how these intrinsic rewards interact with other psychological factors influencing user behavior. It encourages a deeper, more human-centric approach to understanding user interactions with AI systems.
- **Industrial Feasibility:** The framework's design, which leverages existing interaction data for offline training and integrates as an auxiliary loss, makes it highly compatible with existing industrial recommendation pipelines, facilitating adoption.
