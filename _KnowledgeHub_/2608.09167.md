# Intuitive Hand Positional Guidance Using McKibben-Based Surface Tactile Sensations to Shoulder and Elbow

> [!summary] Summary
> A wearable fabric suit uses thin McKibben artificial muscles placed over shoulder and elbow muscle groups to deform clothing, producing surface tactile sensations that intuitively induce six arm movements. A spherical-coordinate PID controller mapped through a Weber-Fechner exponential converts hand-position error into pneumatic pressure. Movement induction reached 92% accuracy, and hand guidance was faster than vibrotactile or speech cues with no prior instruction.

## Problem

- Hand-mounted tactile guidance devices intuitively convey cues but physically obstruct the hand and interfere with camera-based hand tracking in immersive environments.
- Wrist and forearm vibrotactile guidance coexists with tracking but requires users to first learn an arbitrary mapping between vibration patterns and target hand positions.
- Visual hand-guidance cues compete for limited human visual processing capacity, which is already saturated by the immersive task itself.

## Method

- Wearable fabric actuator arranges thin ==McKibben artificial muscles== over deltoid, latissimus dorsi, infraspinatus, and pectoralis major via Velcro, deforming cloth to induce four shoulder movements plus prior elbow flexion/extension.
- Hand position modeled in a ==shoulder-centered spherical coordinate system==, with radius, elevation, and azimuth each mapped one-to-one onto a positive and negative direction tactile channel.
- Two-stage controller: ==PID== on equilibrium-point shifts per the ==equilibrium point hypothesis==, then an exponential ==Weber-Fechner transform== to pneumatic pressure with per-user perceptual thresholds measured by the ==method of limits==.

## Results

- Across eight naive participants, movement identification reached **0.92** accuracy with per-movement F1 above **0.90** and no significant bias between the four shoulder movements.
- Correct responses and confidence rose sharply from **100** to **200 kPa** then saturated at **250-350 kPa**, empirically supporting the logarithmic perception assumption.
- Guidance accuracy matched vibrotactile (**21.8** vs **25.6 degrees** elevation error) despite no instruction, while speech was most accurate (**11.8 degrees**) but significantly slowest in target-touching throughput.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Placing actuators over the muscles that produce a motion makes the cue self-explanatory, removing the training phase that arbitrary vibration encodings demand.
> - Tactile and speech guidance trade off: use tactile channels for time-constrained tasks, speech when fine angular precision matters more than latency.
> - Choosing proximal joints as leading joints for distal-endpoint guidance can outperform the conventional wrist-led scheme while leaving hand tracking unobstructed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper addresses challenges within the field of human-computer interaction, particularly concerning immersive environments such as virtual reality and teleoperation systems. Over the past several decades, significant attention has been directed towards enhancing user experience and task performance in these environments, where a strong "sense of presence" is correlated with improved work efficiency. A key aspect of immersive interaction involves users manipulating virtual objects with their hands, necessitating effective hand positional guidance.

Current methods for hand positional guidance often rely on visual cues. However, human visual processing capacity is finite, and visual guidance can interfere with the user's primary visual tasks or hand tracking mechanisms. Consequently, researchers have explored tactile feedback as an alternative, aiming to provide guidance without visual distraction. Existing tactile guidance systems typically employ devices that provide sensations directly to the hands, wrists, or forearms. While these approaches can coexist with hand tracking, they often require users to undergo a learning phase to establish the relationship between a specific tactile sensation and the intended movement or target position. This necessity for prior instruction and training reduces the intuitiveness and immediate usability of such systems. For instance, some methods use the wrist as a "leading joint" for guidance, implying that users must interpret wrist sensations to infer broader arm movements.

This study positions itself by addressing the perceived lack of intuitiveness and the requirement for training inherent in many conventional tactile guidance systems. It introduces a novel approach utilizing McKibben-based surface tactile sensations, which are suggested to be more intuitively perceivable than traditional vibrotactile sensations, thereby potentially eliminating the need for pre-use instruction or extensive training. Furthermore, the paper diverges from conventional wrist-centric guidance by proposing the shoulder and elbow as "leading joints." This strategy aims to provide a more natural and intuitive form of guidance, as these joints are primary drivers of arm movement in human motor control. By developing a system that capitalizes on the intuitive nature of McKibben-based tactile feedback delivered to the shoulder and elbow, this work seeks to advance the design of user-friendly and efficient hand positional guidance systems for immersive environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a hand positional guidance system that offers intuitive perception, thereby enhancing user interaction and task performance in immersive environments without requiring prior instruction or training.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations identified in existing hand positional guidance methods:

- **Lack of Intuitiveness in Conventional Tactile Guidance:** Many current tactile guidance systems, including those employing vibrotactile feedback, necessitate that users learn the specific correlation between a given tactile sensation and the corresponding target position or desired movement. This learning curve reduces immediate usability and requires pre-operational training, hindering spontaneous and natural interaction.
- **Interference of Visual Guidance:** While visual cues are common, they can impose a cognitive load on users, as the human capacity for visual information processing is limited. Moreover, visual guidance might obstruct the user's view of the immersive environment or interfere with hand tracking technologies, which are crucial for interaction in virtual spaces.
- **Suboptimal "Leading Joint" Strategy:** Many tactile guidance systems leverage the wrist as a "leading joint," with the shoulder and elbow acting as subordinate joints. This approach may not align optimally with natural human motor control, potentially making the guidance less intuitive for broader arm movements.

To address these challenges, the study establishes several specific objectives:

1. **Develop a Wearable Fabric Actuator for Shoulder Movements:** The primary objective was to design and fabricate a wearable actuator capable of providing McKibben-based surface tactile sensations specifically tailored to induce four distinct shoulder movements: abduction, adduction, horizontal abduction, and horizontal adduction. This builds upon previous work that successfully induced elbow movements.
2. **Validate Actuator Effectiveness and Intuitiveness:** Experimentally evaluate the developed shoulder actuator to confirm its ability to accurately and intuitively induce the target shoulder movements. This evaluation was crucial to verify that users could perceive and correctly interpret the tactile sensations without prior instruction.
3. **Integrate and Construct a Comprehensive Guidance System:** Combine the newly developed shoulder actuator with a previously established McKibben-based actuator for elbow movements (flexion and extension). This integration aimed to create a unified system capable of inducing six specific arm movements (four shoulder, two elbow) to guide overall hand position indirectly.
4. **Implement a Psychophysics-Based Control Algorithm:** Develop an algorithm to regulate the intensity of the McKibben-based tactile sensations. This algorithm was designed to leverage principles from psychophysics, specifically the equilibrium point hypothesis for motor control and the Weber–Fechner law for sensory perception, to ensure that the pneumatic pressure applied to the actuators translated into intuitively perceivable and proportional guidance cues.
5. **Evaluate System Performance (Accuracy and Speed):** Conduct user studies to quantitatively assess the accuracy and speed of the proposed hand positional guidance system. This evaluation involved a comparative analysis against conventional guidance methods, including synthesized speech and a vibrotactile system, to highlight the advantages of the McKibben-based approach in terms of intuitiveness and performance.

By achieving these objectives, the researchers aimed to provide a more natural, intuitive, and effective hand positional guidance solution for immersive environments, mitigating the need for extensive user training and enhancing overall user experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study involved the development of a novel wearable fabric actuator, the integration of this actuator into a complete hand positional guidance system, the implementation of a psychophysics-based control algorithm, and a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the system's performance.

#### 3.1. Wearable Fabric Actuator Development

- **Principle:** The core of the tactile sensation mechanism is the McKibben artificial muscle. When pneumatic pressure is applied, these muscles contract and deform the fabric they are attached to, creating a surface tactile sensation on the wearer's skin.
- **Components:** The actuator consists of a standard motion capture suit (OptiTrack) with Velcro loops on its exterior and thin McKibben artificial muscles (EMM20 × 800 s-muscle) attached via Velcro tape.
- **Shoulder Actuator Design:** Building on prior work for elbow movements, the researchers focused on designing an actuator for four shoulder movements: abduction, adduction, horizontal abduction, and horizontal adduction. The arrangement of McKibben artificial muscles was critical. Based on anatomical knowledge of muscles contributing to these movements (deltoid, latissimus dorsi, infraspinatus, pectoralis major), the artificial muscles were strategically placed around these areas (e.g., across the deltoid for abduction, near the latissimus dorsi for adduction). The goal was to replicate deformation patterns similar to those of natural human muscle contraction during these movements, facilitating intuitive tactile perception.
- **Integrated System Actuator:** For the full hand positional guidance system, the newly developed shoulder actuator was combined with a previously designed McKibben-based actuator capable of inducing elbow flexion and extension. This integration resulted in a wearable fabric actuator capable of inducing six distinct movements (four shoulder, two elbow).

#### 3.2. Evaluation of Eliciting Shoulder Movements by McKibben-based Surface Tactile Sensations

- **Participants:** Eight healthy individuals participated in this preliminary evaluation.
- **Experimental Setup:** Participants wore the fabric actuator and earmuffs, sitting on a stool with their left elbow bent at 90 degrees and forearm placed horizontally in front of the body.
- **Procedure:** The actuator randomly provided one of the four target shoulder movements. Pneumatic pressure was varied across seven levels (100, 150, 175, 200, 250, 300, and 350 kPa). Each combination of movement and pressure was presented three times per participant. Participants identified the induced movement and rated their confidence (1-7 Likert scale). A key aspect was that participants received no prior instruction regarding the sensation-movement relationship, assessing the intrinsic intuitiveness. If no sensation was felt, no response was given.
- **Metrics:** The evaluation focused on the number of correct, wrong, and no responses, mean confidence for correct responses, and classification metrics including precision, recall, and F1-score for each shoulder movement. Statistical analysis utilized the χ² test with Bonferroni correction.

#### 3.3. Hand Positional Guidance System Configuration

- **Hardware:** The system comprised a control PC, a mixed-reality device with hand tracking (Hololens2), an air compressor, a pneumatic controller (programmable logic controller and electric pneumatic regulators), and the integrated wearable fabric actuator.
- **Operation Flow:** The Hololens2 tracked the user's hand position, transmitting this data to the control PC. The PC calculated required pneumatic pressure commands based on the target hand position and transmitted them to the pneumatic controller. The controller then adjusted air pressure to the McKibben artificial muscles, providing tactile sensations that guided the user to intuitively adjust their hand position.
- **Coordinate System:** Hand position was modeled using a spherical coordinate system (r, θ, ϕ) relative to the shoulder as the origin. Specific changes in r, θ, and ϕ were mapped to the six induced shoulder and elbow movements (e.g., positive r for elbow extension, negative r for elbow flexion, positive θ for shoulder adduction).

#### 3.4. Pneumatic Pressure Adjustment Algorithm

- **Assumptions:** The algorithm was built upon four assumptions: (1) independence of r, θ, and ϕ parameters; (2) induced movements following the equilibrium point hypothesis; (3) linear variation of equilibrium points with perceived tactile sensation; and (4) human perception of tactile sensation and pneumatic pressure following the Weber–Fechner law.
- **Control Mechanism:** A PID-like control strategy was applied to a vector `q` representing the six movement directions (e.g., `r`, `-r`, `θ`, `-θ`, `ϕ`, `-ϕ`). The equilibrium point vector `q_eq` was calculated based on the difference between the target equilibrium point `q_d_eq` and the current state `q`.
- **Psychophysical Mapping:** The calculated `q_eq` values were then transformed into pneumatic pressures `p_i` using the Weber–Fechner law: `p_i = p_0_i * exp(q_eq,i / k_i)`.
 - `p_0_i`: Minimum pneumatic pressure threshold for perceiving sensation, individually measured for each user via the method of limits.
 - `k_i`: Constant determined by the maximum desired equilibrium point `q_M_eq,i` and maximum pneumatic pressure `p_M_i` to ensure the full range of guidance.

#### 3.5. Evaluation of Hand Positional Guidance System (Accuracy and Speed)

- **Participants:** Nine healthy individuals participated in both accuracy and speed experiments.
- **Common Setup:** Participants wore the proposed fabric actuator, a vibrotactile device (Tactosy for Arms), Hololens2, and headphones, sitting on a stool with a keyboard on their lap.
- **Guidance Conditions (3 types):**
 1. **Proposed System:** McKibben-based surface tactile sensation (no prior instruction).
 2. **Synthesized Speech:** Speech-based guidance providing r, θ, and ϕ values (10 mm/1° resolution) once per task (informed about output method).
 3. **Vibrotactile Guidance:** Wrist-based "Phantom Sensation" method (informed about sensation-position relationship). This method used wrist- and elbow-side vibrators to guide movement along the y-axis, extended from previous 2D work.
- **Accuracy Experiment (Section V-A):**
 - **Task:** Manipulate a blue virtual sphere (50mm diameter) to a randomly positioned red target sphere in the immersive environment. Initial blue sphere at r=400mm, θ=90°, ϕ=90°. Target varied (r: 200-600mm, θ/ϕ: 40-140°). Participants pressed a keyboard upon completion.
 - **Metrics:** Absolute error between the virtual object and target position in elevation angle (θ), azimuth angle (ϕ), and radius (r). Each participant performed 10 tasks per condition. Statistical analysis used the Friedman test with Bonferroni correction.
- **Speed Experiment (Section V-B):**
 - **Task:** Repeatedly touch a randomly appearing red target virtual sphere with a blue virtual sphere using the left hand within a 40-second period. Upon touch, the red sphere moved to the initial blue sphere's position, then reappeared randomly.
 - **Metrics:** Number of red virtual objects touched within 40 seconds. Each participant performed 5 repetitions per condition. Statistical analysis used the Friedman test with Bonferroni correction.
 - **No prior practice was allowed for any condition in both experiments.**

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the performance of the McKibben-based surface tactile actuator and the integrated hand positional guidance system.

#### 4.1. Evaluation of Eliciting Shoulder Movements

- **High Accuracy:** The developed wearable fabric actuator demonstrated a high accuracy of 0.92 in inducing the four specific shoulder movements (abduction, adduction, horizontal abduction, horizontal adduction) when participants perceived a tactile sensation.
- **Absence of Bias:** Precision, recall, and F1-scores for each shoulder movement were consistently above 0.90, 0.89, and 0.90, respectively. Statistical analysis (χ² test with Bonferroni correction) indicated no significant differences in correct responses across the induced shoulder movements, suggesting the actuator could induce movements without bias.
- **Intuitive Perception:** Participants could intuitively perceive and correctly identify the induced movements without prior instruction regarding the sensation-movement relationship.
- **Pressure-Confidence Relationship:** As pneumatic pressure increased from 100 kPa to 200 kPa, both the number of correct responses and reported confidence levels improved substantially. Beyond 200 kPa (250–350 kPa), the effects diminished, a pattern noted to be consistent with the Weber–Fechner law, which describes a non-linear relationship between physical stimulus intensity and perceived sensation.

#### 4.2. Accuracy Evaluation of Hand Positional Guidance System

- **Speech vs. Tactile Accuracy:** Synthesized speech guidance exhibited higher accuracy for both elevation and azimuth angles compared to both the proposed McKibben-based system and vibrotactile guidance. Specifically, statistically significant differences were observed between synthesized speech and the proposed system (p=0.001 for elevation, p=0.022 for azimuth), and between synthesized speech and vibrotactile guidance (p<0.001 for elevation, p=0.001 for azimuth).
- **Proposed System vs. Vibrotactile Accuracy:** The proposed McKibben-based system achieved comparable accuracy to vibrotactile guidance for elevation and azimuth angles. While no statistically significant differences were found, the median absolute errors for the proposed system were smaller (Elevation: 21.8 deg vs 25.6 deg; Azimuth: 14.6 deg vs 26.1 deg).
- **Radius Accuracy:** No statistically significant differences were observed among any of the guidance methods regarding the absolute error in radius.
- **Median Errors (Table V):**
 - **Proposed System:** Elevation 21.8 deg, Azimuth 14.6 deg, Radius 82.8 mm.
 - **Synthesized Speech:** Elevation 11.8 deg, Azimuth 8.8 deg, Radius 71.8 mm.
 - **Vibrotactile:** Elevation 25.6 deg, Azimuth 26.1 deg, Radius 73.9 mm.

#### 4.3. Speed Evaluation of Hand Positional Guidance System

- **Proposed System's Superior Speed:** The proposed McKibben-based guidance system achieved the highest median and mean scores in terms of the number of target virtual objects touched within 40 seconds.
- **Statistically Significant Differences:** Statistical analysis (Friedman test with Bonferroni correction) revealed statistically significant differences in speed among all three guidance conditions. The proposed system was faster than both synthesized speech and vibrotactile guidance.
- These results collectively indicate that the proposed system can guide hand position with comparable accuracy to vibrotactile methods while demonstrating superior speed, and crucially, without requiring prior instruction on tactile sensation interpretation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the development of intuitive human-robot interaction and user interfaces within immersive environments, particularly concerning hand positional guidance. The primary significance lies in demonstrating the efficacy and inherent intuitiveness of McKibben-based surface tactile sensations for guiding complex limb movements.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

- **Enhanced Intuitiveness and Reduced Training Requirements:** The most prominent finding is that the McKibben-based surface tactile sensations, applied to the shoulder and elbow, can intuitively induce specific arm movements. Participants in the study accurately perceived these sensations and performed guided movements without any prior instruction or training on the relationship between the tactile feedback and the target position. This eliminates a major hurdle associated with conventional tactile guidance systems, which typically require users to learn and adapt to the specific feedback patterns. This instruction-free intuitiveness can significantly reduce onboarding time and cognitive load for users in various applications.
- **Improved User Experience in Immersive Environments:** By providing intuitive and fast hand positional guidance, the system has the potential to enhance the "sense of presence" and overall user experience in virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and teleoperation systems. Users can focus more on the task itself rather than on interpreting guidance cues, leading to more seamless and immersive interactions.
- **Optimized Guidance Strategy for Arm Movements:** The adoption of the shoulder and elbow as "leading joints," in contrast to wrist-based systems, appears to align more closely with natural human motor control. The comparable accuracy and superior speed of the proposed system over vibrotactile guidance (even with the latter providing prior instruction) suggest that McKibben-based sensations delivered to these proximal joints are an effective strategy for whole-arm guidance.
- **Trade-off Understanding for Application-Specific Design:** The comparative evaluation revealed a trade-off between guidance accuracy (higher with synthesized speech) and guidance speed (higher with tactile sensations). This finding is crucial for informing the design of future guidance systems. For time-critical tasks where quick responses are paramount, tactile feedback would be preferred. For tasks requiring extreme precision where time is less constrained, a speech-based or hybrid approach might be more suitable. This understanding allows for tailoring guidance modalities to specific application requirements.
- **Potential for Broad Application Areas:** Beyond immersive entertainment, the system holds significant promise for a range of practical applications:
 - **Teleoperation:** Enabling more intuitive control of remote robots or devices in hazardous or inaccessible environments (e.g., medical, space exploration, disaster response).
 - **Rehabilitation:** Providing intuitive guidance for motor learning and recovery in patients with neurological or physical impairments, allowing therapists to guide movements more naturally.
 - **Assembly and Training:** Facilitating intuitive guidance for complex manual tasks in manufacturing or for training personnel in procedural steps.
 - **Assisted Living:** Developing assistive devices that can guide users with limited mobility or vision to interact with their environment.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The study lays groundwork for future advancements, such as exploring guidance for dynamically moving target objects in immersive environments and applying the principles to active motor learning scenarios, as alluded to in the conclusion. Further refinement of the psychophysics-based control algorithm could also lead to even more nuanced and adaptive guidance.

In conclusion, this work significantly advances the development of intuitive and efficient human-machine interfaces by demonstrating the potential of McKibben-based surface tactile sensations for hand positional guidance. Its benefits in terms of intuitiveness, speed, and reduced training requirements offer substantial improvements for a wide array of immersive and assistive technologies.
