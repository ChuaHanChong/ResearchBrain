# Robometer: Scaling General-Purpose Robotic Reward Models via Trajectory Comparisons

> [!summary] Summary
> ROBOMETER scales general-purpose robot reward models by integrating absolute progress and relative preference supervision within a Vision-Language Model architecture. It achieved an average VOC Pearson r of 0.95 and Kendall τa of 0.66 on out-of-distribution data, significantly improving online reinforcement learning, offline learning, and data filtering applications.

## Problem

- Manually annotating dense, absolute progress labels for millions of frames in diverse, real-world robotic datasets is labor-intensive, ambiguous for suboptimal trajectories, and does not scale efficiently.
- Existing reward models struggle to generalize a consistent notion of progress across various robot embodiments, environments, and diverse trajectory qualities (successful, suboptimal, failed).
- Large quantities of valuable 'bad behavior' data (suboptimal and failed trajectories) generated in real-world robotics are underutilized due to the difficulty of precise labeling, hindering robust policy learning.

## Method

- A ==Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone== (QWEN3-VL-4B-INSTRUCT) is trained with a novel ==dual-objective approach==, combining ==absolute progress supervision== for expert trajectories with ==relative preference supervision== for trajectory comparisons.
- ==Preference supervision== leverages pairwise comparisons between trajectories (e.g., expert vs. unlabeled failure, different tasks, original vs. synthetically rewound) to learn global ordering constraints and distinguish quality differences in unlabeled data.
- The ==RBM-1M dataset== was curated, comprising over one million trajectories from 21 robot embodiments, including significant amounts of suboptimal and failed trajectories, to support scalable reward learning.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art reward evaluation, with an average VOC Pearson r of **0.95** and Kendall τa of **0.66** on out-of-distribution data, outperforming baselines like RoboReward-8B (**0.88** r, **0.47** τa).
- Enhanced online reinforcement learning, improving success rates by an average of **2.5x** over RoboReward, achieving **85%** for single-stage and **70%** for two-stage tasks, and enabling **2.4x** higher average success rates in offline RL.
- Demonstrated effective zero-shot failure detection, achieving an average F1 score of **0.81** across seven manipulation tasks on an unseen robot, and improved imitation learning policies by **4.5x** through ROBOMETER-based data filtering.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The integration of preference learning with 'bad behavior' data (suboptimal and failed trajectories) is crucial for training robust reward models that can effectively differentiate various levels of task performance.
> - Combining precise absolute progress labels on expert data with scalable relative preference labels on diverse, unlabeled data provides a powerful and generalized approach to reward function learning.
> - Leveraging a large-scale, pre-trained Vision-Language Model backbone is essential for learning generalizable multimodal reward representations across a wide array of robotic tasks and visual inputs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of effective reward functions is a central challenge in reinforcement learning (RL) and robotics. Traditional approaches to reward learning often rely on Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL), inferring rewards from task-specific expert demonstrations. While effective, these methods typically require new demonstrations for each task or reward specification, limiting their generalization and scalability.

More recently, research has explored preference-based reward learning, drawing inspiration from human cognition where comparative judgments are prevalent. This approach, central to Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), uses human preferences over trajectories to learn reward models. However, direct human preference annotation for robotics can be costly and time-consuming at scale.

Another significant trend involves leveraging large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) to generate more generalizable reward functions. These "foundation models" have been used for tasks like generating reward code, shaping functions, or acting as zero-shot progress/success estimators directly from images, videos, and language instructions. While promising, directly applying pre-trained VLMs can yield noisy signals, and task-specific fine-tuning may lead to overfitting, limiting generalization. Existing fine-tuning approaches for VLM-based reward models often depend on frame-level progress labels derived from expert demonstrations or require specific goal images or counterfactual instruction labeling, which can be expensive to obtain for large, diverse datasets, especially those containing suboptimal or failed trajectories.

This work, ROBOMETER, positions itself within this landscape by addressing the scalability and generalization limitations of current VLM-based reward models. It proposes a novel training framework that combines two types of supervision:
1. **Intra-trajectory progress supervision**: Anchoring reward magnitude to expert data, similar to prior work but designed for robustness.
2. **Inter-trajectory preference supervision**: Imposing global ordering constraints across diverse trajectories, including suboptimal and failed ones, without requiring additional human preference annotations.

By leveraging a dual-objective training strategy on a large, diverse dataset that explicitly includes failed trajectories, ROBOMETER aims to learn more generalizable and robust reward functions than methods relying solely on absolute progress labels or computationally expensive human preference data. This approach allows the effective utilization of abundant suboptimal data, which is typically discarded or underutilized in conventional reward modeling frameworks, thus improving scalability and generalization across embodiments, scenes, and trajectory quality.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable and generalizable framework for training robot reward models that can effectively learn from large, diverse datasets, including a significant proportion of suboptimal and failed trajectories. The core motivation stems from several limitations observed in current general-purpose robotic reward models:

1. **Reliance on Frame-Level Progress Labels from Expert Demonstrations**: Existing models predominantly depend on detailed, frame-level progress annotations derived from successful, expert demonstrations. While these labels are straightforward to obtain for perfect trajectories (e.g., linearly interpolating progress from 0 to 1), they become increasingly ambiguous, ill-defined, and expensive to annotate for suboptimal or failed attempts.
2. **Poor Scalability to Large Datasets with Suboptimal Data**: Real-world robot learning inherently generates vast amounts of suboptimal, incomplete, or failed trajectories. These data points are rich in information about "what not to do" or "how to recover," but their value is largely untapped because absolute progress labels are difficult or impossible to assign meaningfully. The inability to leverage this abundant failure data limits the scalability and robustness of reward models.
3. **Limited Generalization**: The reliance on trajectory-local, absolute progress supervision limits a reward model's ability to generalize across different robot embodiments, tasks, scenes, and varying trajectory qualities. A reward model should ideally internalize a calibrated scale of task progress that holds true regardless of the specific robot or environment, a capability that pointwise supervision struggles to achieve.
4. **Need for Calibrated Reward Signals for Downstream Applications**: Effective robot learning, whether through online RL, offline RL, imitation learning, or automated failure detection, critically depends on well-calibrated and consistent reward signals. Rewards must not only identify successful completion but also differentiate between various degrees of suboptimal performance and clearly penalize failures.

To address these motivations, the ROBOMETER project sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

- **Introduce a Scalable Reward Modeling Framework**: Develop a dual-objective training recipe that combines intra-trajectory progress supervision (anchoring reward magnitude) with inter-trajectory preference supervision (imposing global ordering constraints).
- **Enable Learning from Both Successful and Failed Trajectories**: Design the framework to effectively leverage both reward-labeled expert data and reward-unlabeled suboptimal/failed trajectories by formulating preference prediction over trajectory pairs. This allows learning relative quality judgments without requiring absolute progress scores for failures.
- **Curate a Large-Scale, Diverse Dataset (RBM-1M)**: Assemble a dataset of over one million multi-embodiment trajectories, specifically designed to include a substantial number of suboptimal and failed trajectories that are difficult to leverage with existing methods. This dataset is crucial for learning globally consistent preference relations.
- **Improve Generalization and Robustness**: Through the dual objective and diverse dataset, aim to train reward functions that generalize effectively to unseen tasks, robot embodiments, and execution qualities, outperforming current state-of-the-art baselines.
- **Demonstrate Utility in Downstream Robot Learning Applications**: Validate the learned reward functions by showing improved performance in practical robotic applications, including automatic online RL, offline RL with noisy data, dataset filtering for imitation learning, and zero-shot failure detection.

The central insight motivating ROBOMETER is that comparative judgments, analogous to human cognition, provide a powerful and scalable supervision signal. By learning to predict which of two trajectories better completes a task, the model can internalize a global understanding of task progress and quality, which is difficult to achieve with isolated, absolute progress labels alone.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ROBOMETER framework is built upon three main pillars: a large and diverse dataset, a pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone, and a composite training objective that integrates frame-level progress with trajectory-level preferences.

**A. RBM-1M Dataset**
ROBOMETER is trained on RBM-1M, a large-scale reward-learning dataset for robot manipulation, comprising over one million trajectories from 21 robot embodiments. The dataset is intentionally diverse in terms of viewpoints, scenes, and robot platforms, including single-arm, bimanual, and mobile manipulators, as well as human demonstrations. Crucially, RBM-1M incorporates a substantial amount of suboptimal and failed trajectories, which are challenging to leverage with purely absolute progress-based supervision.

RBM-1M aggregates data from:
- **Expert robot data**: From diverse sources like Open-X, AGIBotWorld, and MolmoACT, typically containing successful demonstrations.
- **Human videos**: From datasets like Epic-Kitchens for scene diversity, and human-robot paired datasets like RH20T for embodiment-invariant representations.
- **Simulation data**: From sources such as LIBERO.
- **Failed trajectories**: Collected from automated policy rollouts (e.g., SOAR), failure-detection datasets (e.g., FAILSafe), and self-generated failures (e.g., LIBERO with added noise).

For trajectories lacking explicit progress labels or having noisy termination points, they are primarily used for preference prediction. A lightweight manual annotation process for "task finished" thresholds is applied to 10 sampled trajectories per data source to adjust ground-truth progress and success targets for more reliable supervision.

**B. ROBOMETER Architecture and Tokenization**
The architecture is based on a causally masked VLM, QWEN3-VL-4B-INSTRUCT, which processes interleaved text and visual tokens using a single autoregressive decoder.
- **Hidden Embedding Extraction**: To enable both frame-level and trajectory-level predictions without altering the VLM's pre-trained representations, specific learned tokens are inserted:
 - `<|prog token|>`: Interleaved after each frame ($o_{1,t}$) of the *first* video in a pair. Its hidden state attends to the instruction and preceding frames of $o_1$, allowing dense, frame-level progress estimates.
 - `<|pref token|>`: Appended at the end of the multi-video prompt. Its hidden state attends to the instruction and *both* trajectories, enabling a relative judgment for preference prediction.
 - `<|split token|>`: A separator token to demarcate the boundary between the two video trajectories.
- **Input Structure**: The model takes an instruction ($l$) and up to two video trajectories ($o_1, o_2$). For inference with a single trajectory, only $o_1$ is input. Both trajectories are fixed to length T=8 frames to avoid biasing preference predictions based on video length.

**C. Training Objectives**
ROBOMETER is optimized using a composite loss function: $L = L_{pref} + L_{prog} + L_{succ}$. This combination allows anchoring rewards to absolute progress while learning fine-grained quality distinctions through trajectory comparisons.
- **Preference Prediction Loss ($L_{pref}$)**: A binary classifier (an MLP head) is attached to the hidden state of the `<|pref token|>`. It predicts which of the two trajectories ($o_1$ or $o_2$) better satisfies the language instruction ($l$). This is trained with binary cross-entropy.
- **Progress Prediction Loss ($L_{prog}$)**: For the first trajectory ($o_1$), an MLP head is attached to each `<|prog token|>,t` hidden state to predict continuous progress ($p_t$). Progress targets are discretized into 10 uniformly spaced bins over, and the loss is computed using cross-entropy, modeling progress as a categorical distribution. This supervision is applied primarily to successful trajectories, or trajectories with partial progress labels.
- **Success Prediction Loss ($L_{succ}$)**: Another MLP head is attached to each `<|prog token|>,t` to predict binary success ($s_t$). Success targets are defined as $s_t=0$ for $t<T$ and $s_t=1$ for $t=T$ (for successful trajectories), and trained with balanced binary cross-entropy.

**D. Data Sampling and Augmentation**
To construct training inputs $(l, o_1, o_2)$ and their ground-truth preference targets ($y$) from RBM-1M, three dynamic strategies are employed:
1. **Progress-Based Comparisons (Different Expertise)**: Samples two trajectories ($\tau_1, \tau_2$) with the same instruction but different outcomes (e.g., expert vs. unlabeled failure, or trajectories with different partial progress scores). The preference $y$ is set to the higher-quality trajectory. This enables leveraging unlabeled failures by contrasting them with successful demonstrations.
2. **Instruction Negatives (Different Tasks)**: Samples $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ with distinct instructions ($l_1 \ne l_2$). One instruction is randomly chosen as the conditioning text $l$, and $y$ is set to the trajectory matching $l$. The progress target for the other trajectory is set to 0, enforcing that correct behavior for the wrong task yields no reward. This grounds rewards in the language command.
3. **Video Rewind (Augmented Failures)**: Generates synthetic preferences from a single expert trajectory $\tau$ by reversing a segment of time. A "Chosen" forward sequence is compared against a "Rejected" rewound sequence. This explicitly models "undoing" progress, a common failure mode in RL.

Fixed-length subsampling is performed by randomly selecting start and end indices and uniformly sampling 8 frames, ensuring the first and current frames are always included.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate ROBOMETER's effectiveness in generating rewards, its internal components, and its impact on downstream robot learning.

**Q1: Reward Evaluation**
- **Trajectory Task Alignment**: On the RBM-EVAL-OOD dataset (out-of-distribution scenes, embodiments, and viewpoints), ROBOMETER produced the most "diagonal-heavy" confusion matrix, indicating strong alignment between unseen successful demonstrations and their corresponding language instructions (Figure 3). This suggests a superior ability to reward the correct task, even in cluttered, multi-task settings.
- **Reward Alignment (VOC Pearson r)**: ROBOMETER achieved the highest Value Order Correlation (VOC) Pearson `r` on both RBM-EVAL-ID (in-distribution) and RBM-EVAL-OOD datasets (Table I(a)), outperforming state-of-the-art baselines by an average of 14%. This indicates a strong correlation between predicted rewards and ground-truth task progress over time.
- **Relative Trajectory Rankings (Kendall τ_a)**: ROBOMETER demonstrated superior ability to distinguish between failed, suboptimal, and successful trajectories for the same task, achieving a Kendall-τ_a of 0.66 on RBM-EVAL-OOD (Table I(b)). This substantially outperformed baselines like RoboReward-4B (0.50) and RoboReward-8B (0.47), indicating a more accurate ordering of policy performance levels. Qualitative analysis (Figure 4) showed ROBOMETER's sharper separation in rewards and accurate reflection of task progress regression for suboptimal trajectories, unlike baselines.
- **Reward Fine-tuning**: ROBOMETER served as a strong initialization for domain-specific fine-tuning on the RoboFAC dataset. Both LoRA and full fine-tuning of ROBOMETER-4B significantly outperformed fine-tuning the base Qwen3-VL VLM from scratch, especially in Kendall τ and success-fail difference metrics (Table II).

**Q2: Ablations: Why ROBOMETER Performs Well**
Ablation studies on the LIBERO-90 benchmark and RBM-EVAL-OOD revealed the contribution of each component:
- **H1: Preference Prediction Improves Reward Performance**: Adding preference supervision to progress-only training consistently improved policy ranking performance (Kendall-τ_a) on both LIBERO-90 and RBM-EVAL-OOD (Table III). This suggests that global comparative constraints induce a more structured internal reward representation.
- **H2: Scaling with Failure Data Improves Performance**: Incorporating failed trajectories for preference training yielded the largest gains across all ranking-based metrics, significantly improving Kendall-τ and the average reward difference between successful and failed trajectories.
- **H3: Pre-trained VLMs are Crucial**: Replacing the pre-trained VLM backbone with a scaled ReWiND-like architecture resulted in severe degradation across all metrics, confirming the importance of large-scale multimodal pre-trained backbones for generalizable reward representations.
- **Ablation Results on RL Performance**: Policies trained with the full ROBOMETER model (H2 LIBERO model) demonstrated 2-4x better sample efficiency than sparse reward and other ablations on unseen LIBERO-90 tasks (Figure 5), confirming that improvements in reward evaluation metrics translate to better policy learning.
- **Preference Loss Formulation**: Using a dedicated `<|pref token|>` and binary cross-entropy (BCE) for preference prediction, processing both videos in a single forward pass, significantly outperformed the Bradley-Terry loss, which computes independent scores per trajectory (Table XI).
- **Data Sampling Strategies**: Each data sampling strategy (different-task negatives, rewind augmentation, suboptimal trajectory pairs) contributed complementary signals. Removing any component, especially video rewinding, degraded performance on held-out ranking and reward separation (Table XVIII).
- **Loss Weight Ablations**: Uniform weighting of preference and progress losses (λ_pref=1, λ_prog=1) yielded the best performance on LIBERO-90 (Table XIX).
- **Success Head Ablation**: Removing the success prediction head led to a small but consistent drop in reward generalization, indicating its utility as a training signal (Table XX).

**Q3: Accelerating Robot Learning with Generalizable Rewards**
ROBOMETER's dense and generalizable reward signals demonstrated improvements across various downstream applications:
- **Automatic Online RL**: Integrating ROBOMETER into DSRL on a DROID setup improved success rates from 20% to 85% on a single-stage task and from 20% to 70% on a two-stage task, outperforming RoboReward by an average of 2.5x (Figure 6). ROBOMETER’s accurate success predictions enabled autonomous episode termination and stage advancement, while RoboReward often assigned high rewards to incorrect behaviors.
- **Offline RL with Mixed-Expertise Data**: Using Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) on an SO-101 robot with a mix of expert and noisy demonstrations, ROBOMETER rewards consistently outperformed sparse rewards and RoboReward. It achieved an average 2.4x success rate improvement over the best baseline for each task (Figure 7), attributed to its dense, temporally aligned rewards.
- **Data Filtering & Retrieval**: For unsupervised filtering of a bimanual "play" dataset, ROBOMETER consistently achieved higher retrieval relevance for task-relevant subtrajectories compared to baselines like RoboReward, SigLIP, and STRAP (Figure 8(a)). Policies fine-tuned on ROBOMETER-filtered data achieved an average 4.5x higher success rate than those using baseline-retrieved data (Figure 8(b)), demonstrating efficacy for targeted imitation learning.
- **Failure Detection**: ROBOMETER achieved the highest average F1 score for zero-shot failure detection on a Franka Panda DROID robot across 7 tasks (Table IV). It robustly detected irreversible, insufficient-progress, and non-terminal failures by monitoring regressions or stagnation in predicted task progress (Figures 9, 15, 16), without requiring task-specific thresholds or calibration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ROBOMETER represents a notable advance in general-purpose robot reward modeling due to its scalable training framework and robust performance across various applications. Its key significance lies in:

1. **Scalable Reward Learning from Diverse Data**: The dual-objective training recipe, combining intra-trajectory progress and inter-trajectory preference supervision, enables effective learning from large, heterogeneous datasets including unlabeled suboptimal and failed trajectories. This allows for the full utilization of data typically discarded, which is crucial for real-world robot learning where failures are common.
2. **Enhanced Generalization**: By learning global ordering constraints through preference comparisons across diverse embodiments, scenes, and trajectory qualities, ROBOMETER produces reward functions that generalize well to out-of-distribution tasks and environments. This broad generalization capability reduces the need for task-specific reward engineering or extensive re-labeling for new scenarios.
3. **Improved Downstream Robot Learning**: The research demonstrates that ROBOMETER's dense, well-calibrated, and instruction-aligned reward signals translate into tangible improvements in various robot learning paradigms:
 - **Reinforcement Learning**: Higher sample efficiency and success rates in both automatic online RL and offline RL with mixed-expertise data, facilitating more autonomous robot learning.
 - **Imitation Learning**: Effective unsupervised data filtering and retrieval, enabling the curation of high-quality datasets for policy fine-tuning and leading to higher success rates for learned policies.
 - **Failure Detection**: Robust, zero-shot detection of diverse failure modes (irreversible, insufficient-progress, semantic) without task-specific calibration, contributing to safer and more reliable robot deployment.
4. **Strong Initialization for Fine-tuning**: ROBOMETER provides a powerful pre-trained initialization, allowing for efficient adaptation to new domains with minimal data through low-rank fine-tuning (LoRA), thus lowering the computational burden for specialized tasks.
5. **Public Resource Contribution**: The public release of ROBOMETER, the RBM-1M dataset, and training code fosters further research and development in general-purpose reward modeling for robotics.

**Potential Impact**:
The ability to train general-purpose reward models that are robust to failure data and generalize across tasks and robots has broad implications for the autonomy of robotic systems. It can accelerate the development and deployment of intelligent robots by:
- Reducing the manual effort required for reward specification.
- Enabling robots to learn more effectively from imperfect real-world interactions.
- Improving the reliability and safety of robotic operations through enhanced failure detection capabilities.
- Paving the way for more sophisticated, open-ended robot learning systems that can continuously improve from diverse data streams.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including ROBOMETER's current reliance on subsampled video inputs, which may limit its ability to model very long-horizon or partially observed tasks effectively. The diversity of real-world failure modes may not be fully captured by the current training data, leading to potential misrecognition of rare or subtle failures. As a vision-language-based model, it lacks direct access to latent physical states (e.g., contact forces), potentially missing failures until they become visually apparent. Future work could explore incorporating denser temporal modeling, VQA-style supervision for task structure, off-domain data for generalization, and systematically curated failure datasets. Additionally, extending ROBOMETER with a safety head to predict safety-critical states could enhance its utility in constrained environments.
