# K-Score: Kalman Filter as a Principled Alternative to Reward Normalization in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Bern introduced K-Score, a method that applies a one-dimensional Kalman filter for reward normalization in policy gradient reinforcement learning. This approach yielded faster convergence and more stable training, demonstrating a 3.97x speedup on CartPole and 1.32x on LunarLander compared to Z-score normalization.

## Problem

- Policy gradient methods suffer from high variance in gradient estimates, leading to training instability and reduced sample efficiency.
- Conventional reward normalization techniques, like Z-score, assume stationary reward distributions and require careful hyperparameter tuning, which often lags in non-stationary environments.
- These heuristic normalization methods do not formally account for the inherent uncertainty or noise in observed reward signals.

## Method

- Integrate a ==one-dimensional Kalman filter==, termed **K-Score**, directly into the policy gradient pipeline to adaptively estimate the reward signal — raw reward/return normalization, not TD-target or critic-support normalization.
- Model the true underlying reward mean as a ==latent stochastic process== that evolves over time, with observed returns treated as noisy measurements.
- Utilize the Kalman filter's recursive prediction and update steps to estimate the reward mean and its uncertainty, which are then used for dynamic return normalization; validated on **REINFORCE/Actor-Critic/GAE/PPO**, all on-policy.

## Results

- Kalman-based normalization consistently achieved faster convergence and more stable learning dynamics than Z-score normalization across various policy gradient methods.
- K-Score reduced episodes to convergence by **3.97×** on CartPole-v1 (from 306 to 77 episodes) and by **1.32×** on LunarLander-v2 (from 991 to 750 episodes) for PPO agents.
- The adaptive Kalman variant, which dynamically updates measurement noise, provided the fastest convergence and lowest variance among the tested normalization strategies.
- Validated only on toy control tasks (**CartPole-v1**, **LunarLander-v2**), not on off-policy or distributional-critic settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A Kalman filter offers a principled, uncertainty-aware method to adaptively estimate reward statistics, providing a robust alternative to heuristic normalization.
> - Modeling the reward mean as an evolving stochastic process enables "controlled forgetting," allowing the filter to track non-stationary changes in reward distributions effectively.
> - The lightweight, one-dimensional Kalman filter is architecture-agnostic, requiring no changes to policy network designs and introducing minimal computational overhead for broad compatibility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) serves as a paradigm for sequential decision-making, enabling agents to acquire optimal behaviors through interaction with environments. Within RL, policy gradient methods are recognized for their applicability in high-dimensional and continuous action spaces, areas where value-based methods may encounter challenges. However, a known limitation of policy gradient methods is the high variance in gradient estimates, which can stem from the stochastic nature of both the environment and the policy. This high variance can contribute to noisy updates, reduced sample efficiency, and training instability, particularly during initial learning phases.

To mitigate these issues, various variance reduction techniques have been developed. A commonly adopted approach is reward normalization, which involves standardizing observed returns using Z-score normalization based on running estimates of the mean and standard deviation. While empirically effective in smoothing reward signal fluctuations and improving the conditioning of policy gradient updates, these heuristic methods possess limitations. They are often sensitive to hyperparameters, necessitate careful tuning, and rely on an assumption of stationary reward statistics, a condition that is frequently not met in dynamic or partially observable environments.

Kalman filtering, a classical Bayesian estimation method, has a established history in applications such as robotics, tracking, and control for linear state estimation under uncertainty. In the context of reinforcement learning, Kalman-style methods have primarily been explored for tasks such as value estimation, uncertainty modeling, or belief-state inference in partially observable settings. Examples include tracking uncertainty in value-function approximation for trust-region policy optimization or using filtering-style estimation for local value modeling in sparse RL.

This work positions itself by proposing an application of Kalman filtering that deviates from its typical use in RL. Instead of estimating states or values, the authors apply a one-dimensional Kalman filter directly to the reward, return, or advantage normalization signal. The objective is to provide an uncertainty-aware, adaptive alternative to conventional running mean and standard deviation normalization, thereby addressing the limitations of heuristic normalization strategies within policy gradient methods. The paper also acknowledges a concurrent work, KRPO, which similarly integrates Kalman filtering into RL for language model reasoning, but notes differences in scope and application context. While KRPO focuses on GRPO-style RL for language models with group-relative objectives, the presented work investigates Kalman-based normalization as a general preprocessing mechanism for various policy gradient methods in classic control environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a principled and adaptive alternative to conventional reward normalization techniques used in policy gradient reinforcement learning. The authors aim to improve the stability and efficiency of RL training, particularly in environments characterized by high-variance or non-stationary reward signals.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations identified in widely adopted reward normalization methods, such as Z-score normalization:
- **Assumption of Stationarity:** Conventional methods typically assume that the reward distribution remains stationary throughout training. However, in online reinforcement learning, the policy continuously evolves, leading to non-stationary reward distributions. This mismatch can cause running statistics to lag behind the true reward scale, hindering effective learning.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** Z-score normalization often requires careful tuning of heuristic parameters, such as decay rates for moving averages. The performance of the agent can be sensitive to these parameters, necessitating extensive experimentation and domain-specific knowledge.
- **Lack of Uncertainty Treatment:** Standard normalization techniques do not formally account for the uncertainty or noise inherent in the observed reward signal. They provide point estimates of the mean and standard deviation without considering their confidence, which can lead to suboptimal adaptation in noisy environments.
- **Unstable Training and Slow Convergence:** The combination of high variance in reward signals and the aforementioned limitations of heuristic normalization can result in unstable policy updates, poor sample efficiency, and prolonged training times, especially during the critical early stages of learning or in environments with sparse rewards.

To address these issues, the authors propose a Bayesian estimation approach using a Kalman filter. Their motivation is to:
- **Provide a Principled Alternative:** Replace heuristic normalization with a method grounded in Bayesian estimation theory, offering a more robust and statistically sound approach to reward estimation.
- **Enable Adaptivity to Non-Stationarity:** Model the true underlying reward signal as a latent stochastic process, allowing the system to recursively estimate the reward mean and its uncertainty. This enables dynamic adaptation to changes in the reward distribution, which is a common characteristic of online RL.
- **Incorporate Uncertainty Awareness:** Leverage the Kalman filter's ability to fuse prior knowledge with new observations optimally, providing an uncertainty-aware estimate of the reward baseline. This helps the agent to better manage noisy feedback and respond appropriately to the variability of the learning environment.
- **Maintain Computational Efficiency and Compatibility:** Develop a lightweight, one-dimensional Kalman filter module that incurs minimal computational overhead and requires no modifications to existing policy network architectures. This ensures broad compatibility with a range of policy gradient algorithms such as REINFORCE, Actor-Critic, GAE, and PPO.

By integrating a Kalman filter, the authors aim to achieve smoother training dynamics, faster convergence, and improved stability compared to conventional normalization techniques, without introducing significant algorithmic or computational complexity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed K-Score, integrates a one-dimensional Kalman filter into the policy gradient reinforcement learning pipeline to provide an adaptive and principled alternative to reward normalization.

**Problem Formulation and Motivation:**
The approach addresses the limitations of Z-score normalization, specifically its reliance on a stationary reward distribution, the need for hyperparameter tuning, and the absence of formal uncertainty treatment. The core idea is to model the true underlying reward signal as a latent stochastic process, treating the reward mean as a hidden variable that evolves over time and must be estimated from noisy observations. This perspective naturally leads to the use of a Kalman filter.

**Kalman Filtering for Adaptive Reward Estimation:**
The method models the return signal $G_t$ as a noisy observation of a latent reward mean $x_t$. This latent mean is assumed to evolve over time according to a first-order Markov process. The dynamics are defined by two equations:
- **Latent Reward Dynamics (Process Model):** $x_t = x_{t-1} + w_t$, where $w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q)$. This equation describes the evolution of the true reward mean, with $w_t$ representing the process noise and $Q$ its variance.
- **Observation Model:** $G_t = x_t + v_t$, where $v_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R)$. This equation describes how the observed return $G_t$ relates to the true reward mean $x_t$, with $v_t$ representing the observation noise and $R$ its variance.

The Kalman filter then recursively estimates the reward mean $x_t$ and its uncertainty $P_t$ using a two-step update:
1. **Predict Step:**
 - Predicted mean: $x_{t|t-1} = x_{t-1}$
 - Predicted covariance: $P_{t|t-1} = P_{t-1} + Q$
2. **Update Step:**
 - Kalman Gain: $K_t = \frac{P_{t|t-1}}{P_{t|t-1} + R}$
 - Updated mean: $x_t = x_{t|t-1} + K_t(G_t - x_{t|t-1})$
 - Updated covariance: $P_t = (1 - K_t)P_{t|t-1}$

After computing $x_t$ and $P_t$, the normalized return $\hat{G}^{\text{Kalman}}_t$ is calculated as:
$\hat{G}^{\text{Kalman}}_t = \frac{G_t - x_t}{\sqrt{P_t} + \epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is a small constant to prevent division by zero. This formulation provides a dynamically updated baseline and scale, incorporating uncertainty into the normalization process. The Kalman gain $K_t$ adapts to the noise levels, balancing the influence of prior estimates and new observations.

**Integration into Policy Gradient Algorithms:**
The Kalman filtering mechanism is integrated into the policy gradient pipeline at the point where returns or advantages are typically normalized. For a vanilla policy gradient objective $\nabla_{\theta} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t|s_t) \cdot G_t]$, the original discounted return $G_t$ is replaced with the Kalman-normalized variant $\hat{G}_t$. The procedure involves:
1. Initializing policy parameters $\theta$, Kalman filter state $(x_0, P_0)$, and noise parameters $Q, R$.
2. In each training iteration, sampling a trajectory.
3. Computing discounted returns $G_t$ for the trajectory.
4. For each timestep, updating the Kalman filter with $G_t$ to obtain $(x_t, P_t)$.
5. Normalizing the return using the computed $x_t$ and $P_t$ to get $\hat{G}_t$.
6. Computing the gradient estimate using $\hat{G}_t$.
7. Updating the policy parameters $\theta$.
This integration is designed to be architecture-agnostic, requiring no changes to the policy network, and incurring minimal computational cost due to the scalar nature and constant-time updates of the 1D Kalman filter.

**Theoretical Analysis:**
The paper briefly analyzes the behavior of Kalman-based mean estimation compared to standard running-mean estimation. While running means consistently estimate a fixed latent mean under stationarity, they do not forget old observations, leading to lag in non-stationary environments. The Kalman filter, by modeling the latent mean as an evolving process ($Q > 0$), introduces "controlled forgetting" and an uncertainty-aware gain $K_t$. This allows the estimator to track non-stationary changes in return distribution while balancing smoothing and responsiveness. The steady-state variance $P_{\infty}$ of the Kalman filter, which depends on $Q$ and $R$, reflects this adaptive behavior.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation was conducted on two OpenAI Gym environments:
- **CartPole-v1:** A low-variance, dense reward task with a discrete action space. Solved at an average reward of 475 over 100 episodes.
- **LunarLander-v2:** A high-variance, sparse reward task with a discrete action space. Solved at an average reward above 200.

Implementations used PyTorch and OpenAI Gym. Separate training and evaluation environments with different random seeds were used. Training was terminated when the agent reached a predefined reward threshold in the evaluation environment.

Two variants of the Kalman reward normalization were implemented:
- **Simple Kalman:** Uses fixed process ($Q$) and measurement noise ($R$) values, determined via grid search per model and environment.
- **Adaptive Kalman:** Dynamically updates the measurement noise $R_t$ using an exponential moving average (EMA) of squared residuals, with an update rule $R_t = \alpha R_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)(G_t - x_{t-1})^2$ (where $\alpha=0.9$).

**Baselines and Variants:**
The Kalman-based normalization was applied to three common policy gradient algorithms:
- **Actor-Critic (AC):** Standard advantage $A_t = G_t - V(s_t)$.
- **Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE):** Advantage computed via a bias-variance trade-off.
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** An on-policy method with a clipped surrogate objective.
For each baseline, performance was compared against Z-score normalization and the Simple Kalman and Adaptive Kalman variants.

**Evaluation Metric:**
The primary metric was the "episodes to convergence," defined as the number of training episodes required for the agent to reach or exceed a specific reward threshold in the evaluation environment (475 for CartPole, 200 for LunarLander). This metric quantifies learning efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the effectiveness of Kalman-based reward normalization across various policy gradient algorithms and benchmark environments, primarily focusing on convergence speed and training stability.

**Overall Trend:**
Across all experiments, Kalman-based reward normalization consistently resulted in faster convergence and more stable learning dynamics compared to conventional Z-score normalization. These improvements were particularly evident during the early stages of training and in environments characterized by high reward variance.

**CartPole with Actor-Critic:**
- **Z-score Normalization (Figure 1):** The Actor-Critic agent using Z-score normalization required approximately 70 episodes to reach the target reward threshold of 475. The training and evaluation reward curves exhibited significant fluctuations, particularly in the initial 50 episodes, indicating unstable learning.
- **Kalman Normalization (Figure 2):** With Kalman-based normalization, the Actor-Critic agent achieved the reward threshold within approximately 25 episodes, maintaining stable performance thereafter. The training curve was smoother, and the test reward stabilized at optimal levels with reduced variance. This suggests the Kalman filter's ability to manage early outliers and adapt cautiously in high-variance regimes.

**CartPole with GAE:**
- **Z-score Normalization (Figure 3):** Similar to the Actor-Critic scenario, GAE with conventional normalization eventually converged but demonstrated unstable learning during initial episodes.
- **Kalman Normalization (Figure 4):** GAE augmented with Kalman-based normalization showed more consistent and rapid performance improvements, characterized by lower variance and more stable policy updates.

**LunarLander with Actor-Critic:**
- **Kalman Normalization (Figure 5):** In the LunarLander environment, known for its sparse and high-variance rewards, the Actor-Critic agent with Kalman-based normalization achieved faster convergence and significantly smoother training curves compared to mean-std normalization (results for mean-std not explicitly shown in Figure 5 but implied by textual comparison). This indicates superior handling of reward noise and non-stationarity in challenging settings.

**PPO on CartPole with Multiple Normalization Methods:**
Comparative evaluation of PPO with different normalization strategies provided quantitative results:
- **Adaptive Kalman (Figure 6):** Achieved the fastest convergence with minimal variance. The dynamic adjustment of measurement noise $R_t$ allowed for rapid adaptation to changing reward patterns.
- **Simple Kalman (Figure 8):** Showed intermediate performance. While it improved upon Z-score, it did not match the performance of the adaptive variant, indicating the benefit of dynamic noise estimation.
- **Z-score Normalization (Figure 7):** Exhibited slower and noisier convergence.

A summary table presented the episodes required to reach the target reward:
- **CartPole-v1:** Z-Score: 306 episodes; K-Score (Ours): 77 episodes. This represents a 3.97x improvement in convergence speed.
- **LunarLander-v2:** Z-Score: 991 episodes; K-Score (Ours): 750 episodes. This represents a 1.32x improvement.

**Ablation Study: Effect of Process Noise Q (PPO on CartPole, R=1 fixed):**
The study investigated the impact of the process noise parameter $Q$:
- **Q values ($1 \times 10^{-4}$, $1 \times 10^{-3}$, $1 \times 10^{-2}$):** Required 75, 71, and 69 episodes respectively to reach the threshold.
- **Q values ($1 \times 10^{-1}$, $1$):** Required 170 and 98 episodes respectively.
These results suggest that smaller values of $Q$, which imply greater trust in the prior estimate, generally accelerated convergence by mitigating overreaction to noisy returns. However, the study also indicated a potential trade-off, where excessively small $Q$ values might lead to under-adaptation in highly non-stationary environments, implying that adaptive noise tuning could be beneficial.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a lightweight, principled alternative to reward normalization in policy gradient reinforcement learning by integrating a one-dimensional Kalman filter for online reward estimation. This approach offers several significances and potential impacts:

**Significance:**
- **Principled Alternative:** By replacing heuristic normalization strategies with a Kalman filter grounded in Bayesian estimation theory, the work introduces a statistically robust method for adaptively estimating the latent reward mean and its uncertainty. This contrasts with conventional Z-score normalization which relies on fixed heuristics and assumes stationary reward statistics.
- **Improved Training Stability and Efficiency:** The integration of Kalman filtering helps to smooth high-variance returns and adapt to non-stationary environments, leading to more stable learning dynamics and faster convergence. Empirical results demonstrate substantial reductions in the number of training episodes required to reach performance thresholds, particularly in environments with high reward variance (e.g., 3.97x speedup on CartPole, 1.32x on LunarLander for PPO).
- **Architecture-Agnostic and Minimal Overhead:** The proposed method does not require modifications to existing policy network architectures, making it compatible with a wide range of policy gradient algorithms such as REINFORCE, Actor-Critic, GAE, and PPO. The computational overhead of the 1D Kalman filter is minimal, facilitating easy integration into existing RL pipelines.
- **Adaptive Uncertainty Modeling:** The Kalman filter's dynamic balance between prior knowledge and new observations, mediated by the Kalman gain $K_t$, allows for uncertainty-aware adaptation. The adaptive Kalman variant, which dynamically updates measurement noise, consistently demonstrated superior performance, underscoring the benefits of this feature.

**Limitations (as stated by the authors):**
- **Manual Parameter Initialization:** Both simple and adaptive Kalman variants still require manual initialization of the process noise ($Q$) and measurement noise ($R$) parameters. Although these can be tuned per task, the lack of an automated or learned mechanism for these parameters limits general applicability.
- **One-Dimensionality:** The current method employs a one-dimensional Kalman filter applied solely to the scalar return signal ($G_t$). This may limit its capacity to capture more complex temporal or structural dependencies that might be present in more intricate tasks or state spaces.

**Future Work (as suggested by the authors):**
- **Multi-dimensional or Structured Extensions:** Exploring more complex Kalman filter variants that can model richer representations of uncertainty, potentially considering state dynamics or policy structure.
- **Learnable Parameters:** Developing methods to make the Kalman filter fully differentiable and its noise parameters ($Q, R$) learnable within an end-to-end training framework, removing the need for manual tuning.
- **Broader Application in RL:** Extending Kalman filtering beyond reward normalization to other critical components of RL, such as value estimation or advantage calculation, to potentially further enhance learning stability.
- **Evaluation in Diverse Domains:** Testing the approach in continuous control, offline RL, and safety-critical domains to validate its robustness and broader utility.

**Potential Societal Impact:**
The primary goal of this work is to advance the field of machine learning by improving the sample efficiency and learning stability of RL algorithms in noisy or dynamic environments. Improved stability and faster learning in RL could accelerate progress in various applications, including robotics, autonomous systems, and intelligent decision-making under uncertainty.

The authors do not foresee any specific adverse societal consequences from this work. However, they acknowledge that, like many advancements in RL, care should be exercised when deploying such systems in safety-critical or ethically sensitive domains. Increased learning speed and adaptability, while beneficial, could potentially lead to unforeseen behaviors if not properly constrained or interpreted within their deployment contexts. Practitioners are encouraged to consider the broader implications of RL applications in areas such as surveillance, automation, and decision-making systems.
