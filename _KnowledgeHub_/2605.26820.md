# Can VLA Models Learn from Real-World Data Continually without Forgetting?

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from HKU and collaborators investigates catastrophic forgetting in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models when learning continually from real-world robotic data, demonstrating severe performance degradation in naive sequential fine-tuning. The study shows that experience replay, when properly configured and combined with consistent action normalization, effectively mitigates forgetting and enables VLA models to achieve performance comparable to or better than joint multi-task training on a novel real-world manipulation dataset.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle with catastrophic forgetting when learning new skills sequentially from diverse real-world data, impeding their deployment in dynamic environments.
- Previous studies on continual VLA learning relied heavily on simulated environments, potentially underestimating forgetting due to limited task heterogeneity and pretraining dataset overlap.
- Existing experimental protocols often pre-computed global statistics across all tasks for normalization, violating causality and masking real-world challenges where future task data is unavailable.

## Method

- The team constructed a novel real-world sequential manipulation dataset with four highly diverse tasks for a robotic arm, enabling a more realistic evaluation of continual learning.
- They systematically investigated experience replay (ER) as a continual learning strategy, varying replay buffer sizes and frequencies, and compared its performance against naive sequential fine-tuning and joint multi-task training.
- The study analyzed the critical impact of action normalization consistency, comparing a fixed normalization strategy against per-task normalization in a continual learning context.

## Results

- Naive sequential fine-tuning led to dramatic performance drops on earlier tasks, with the average score (excluding the last task) falling from 99.2 to 17.8 points.
- Experience replay with a 0.2 buffer ratio and 0.2 replay frequency maintained all task performances within **10** percentage points of their single-task baselines, reducing average NBT from +80.0 to +5.0.
- The continual learning pipeline with ER achieved 90.0 on the 'Press Button' task, significantly outperforming joint training which scored only 13.3, demonstrating superior performance in complex scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA models suffer severe catastrophic forgetting (average NBT of +80.0) when fine-tuned sequentially on real-world data, with forgetting patterns influenced by visual similarity and action primitive overlap between tasks.
> - Experience replay is highly effective at mitigating real-world VLA forgetting, even with modest replay budgets (e.g., 20% of a single task's data), significantly reducing average NBT to +5.0.
> - A well-configured continual learning pipeline with experience replay can match or outperform joint multi-task training, circumventing issues like gradient interference and representation domination.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an area of research aimed at developing general-purpose robotic systems capable of integrating visual perception, natural language understanding, and action generation within a unified policy. These models, often trained on extensive datasets of robot data, have demonstrated capabilities in transfer learning and cross-task generalization for embodied control. However, the practical deployment of such models in dynamic real-world environments necessitates the ability for continuous learning. Robots must incrementally acquire new skills from evolving data streams while preserving previously learned behaviors. This requirement directly confronts the issue of catastrophic forgetting, a known challenge in continual learning, where the acquisition of new knowledge can lead to the degradation or loss of prior knowledge. For VLA models, sequential fine-tuning on new data risks overwriting existing skills, and continuous retraining on an ever-growing accumulated dataset is often computationally prohibitive. Therefore, mitigating catastrophic forgetting is considered essential for achieving scalable and lifelong robot learning.

Prior research has initiated investigations into continual learning for VLA models. Some studies have suggested that large, pretrained VLAs may exhibit resilience to forgetting, with pretraining, model scale, and small replay buffers contributing to retention during sequential learning. Other approaches have explored on-policy reinforcement learning for maintaining a balance between retention, plasticity, and generalization. Additionally, adaptation methods have been developed to address forgetting when tasks arrive sequentially with limited access to historical data.

Despite these advancements, existing studies on continual learning in VLA models have primarily relied on simulated environments. These simulation-based benchmarks, such as LIBERO, often feature limited heterogeneity in embodiment, simulator, and action space across sub-tasks. Such confined distribution shifts may underestimate the actual extent of forgetting that would occur in diverse real-world deployment scenarios. Furthermore, the observed robustness to forgetting in some studies might be influenced by pretraining overlap, where benchmarks used for evaluation are also part of large-scale VLA pretraining datasets, potentially reactivating memorized priors rather than demonstrating true retention. A further limitation identified in previous protocols is the pre-computation of global statistics (e.g., normalization parameters) over entire task streams before sequential training, which introduces a form of information leakage from future tasks that is not available in real-world deployment. These issues collectively raise questions regarding the generalizability of current findings to realistic settings and highlight the need to understand whether forgetting in VLA models is primarily an algorithmic failure or influenced by factors such as distributional inconsistency, normalization drift, and optimization imbalance under real-world sequential data.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by directly addressing these identified limitations. It shifts the evaluation of continual learning in VLA models from simulated to physical world settings, constructing a real-world dataset and conducting a systematic empirical study under deployment-realistic constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models when they are required to learn new skills continually from real-world data. Specifically, the study aims to determine if VLA models can adapt to sequentially arriving real-world tasks without degrading previously acquired knowledge.

This overarching objective is motivated by several considerations:
1. **Addressing Limitations of Simulation-Based Studies:** Previous research on continual learning in VLA models has predominantly utilized simulated environments. The authors contend that these simulations, often characterized by limited heterogeneity in tasks, embodiments, and action spaces, may not accurately reflect the challenges of real-world deployment. They argue that such controlled settings could lead to an underestimation of catastrophic forgetting. The study aims to bridge this gap by conducting experiments directly in the physical world.
2. **Investigating Real-World Data Heterogeneity:** The deployment of robots in real-world scenarios involves exposure to a diverse range of tasks, objects, and environmental conditions. The research seeks to understand how VLA models cope with sequential learning across tasks that exhibit substantial variations in object geometry, grasping strategies, and motion primitives, which is more representative of real-world complexity than narrowly defined sub-tasks in benchmarks like LIBERO.
3. **Examining Confounding Factors in Previous Work:** The authors highlight concerns that observed forgetting resistance in prior studies might be confounded by pretraining overlap (where evaluation benchmarks are part of pretraining data) or by experimental protocols that violate causality (e.g., pre-computing global statistics from the entire task stream). This study is motivated to provide a more rigorous assessment by operating under constraints that mimic real deployment conditions.
4. **Identifying Practical Implementation Factors:** Beyond algorithmic design, the research is motivated to explore whether practical implementation details, often overlooked in prior work, significantly influence the success or failure of continual learning. Specifically, the study aims to investigate the roles of factors such as replay frequency and action normalization strategy.
5. **Benchmarking Continual Learning Against Joint Training:** The research aims to compare the performance of a continual learning approach, particularly with experience replay, against joint multi-task training under equivalent data budgets. This comparison seeks to determine if continual adaptation offers a competitive or even superior paradigm for VLA learning in real-world scenarios.

To achieve these objectives, the research formulated specific questions (RQs) that guided its experimental design:
- **RQ1:** Do VLA models suffer from catastrophic forgetting when trained on sequential real-world data?
- **RQ2:** What specific patterns of forgetting emerge across tasks, and what are their underlying drivers?
- **RQ3:** Can experience replay (ER) effectively mitigate forgetting in real-world continual VLA adaptation?
- **RQ4:** How do implementation details such as replay frequency and buffer size influence the effectiveness of ER in preventing forgetting?
- **RQ5:** How does a continual learning pipeline compare in performance to a joint multi-task training approach?
- **RQ6:** What is the importance of action normalization consistency in the context of continual VLA learning?

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic empirical approach to investigate continual learning in VLA models within a real-world setting.

**Problem Formulation:**
Robot learning is modeled as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). The objective of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is to learn a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that minimizes the negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss over a dataset of expert demonstrations. For continual learning, the model is exposed to a sequence of K tasks $\{T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_K\}$, each with its own dataset $D_k$. The goal is for the policy to perform well on all tasks seen up to the current training stage ($k$) without forgetting earlier knowledge.

**Robot Platform:**
Experiments were conducted using an AgileX PiPER robotic arm. This platform features six integrated joint motors and a gripper. Visual observations were captured by a multi-camera setup, including two wrist-mounted cameras and two external cameras, providing RGB images at 480x640 resolution and 30 Hz.

**Real-World Sequential Manipulation Dataset:**
A novel real-world dataset was constructed to facilitate the study of continual learning. The dataset comprises four distinct manipulation tasks, each with 500 expert trajectories, designed to maximize inter-task diversity across object geometry, grasping strategies, and motion primitives. The tasks are sequenced to introduce substantial distributional shifts:
1. **Stack Bowl (D1):** A rigid-object pick-and-place task, involving grasping a yellow bowl and placing it into a green bowl.
2. **Hang Cup (D2):** A spatial alignment and hook interaction task, requiring grasping a purple cup and hanging it on a mug rack.
3. **Press Button (D3):** A small-object localization and contact-rich task, where the robot locates and presses a green button with its end-effector.
4. **Fold Towel (D4):** A deformable-object manipulation task, involving grasping and folding a grey towel corner-to-corner.

**Model Baseline and Training:**
The $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA model was used as the base policy. Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) settings were applied:
- **Optimizer:** AdamW.
- **Learning Rate Schedule:** Cosine decay, with a peak learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$ and decay to $5 \times 10^{-6}$.
- **Warmup Steps:** 200 steps.
- **Batch Size:** 128.
- **EMA Decay:** 0.998.
- **Action Horizon:** 10.
- **Training Steps per Task:** Each task was trained for 4,000 steps.
- **Evaluation:** Model checkpoints were evaluated after each training stage by rolling out the policy for each task. Scores were computed using task-specific rubrics and normalized to a 0–100 scale.

**Action Normalization Strategies:**
Two strategies for action normalization were compared, addressing the challenge of unavailable global statistics in a continual learning setting:
- **Strategy-I (Default):** The mean ($\mu_1$) and standard deviation ($\sigma_1$) statistics from the first task ($D_1$) were fixed and used throughout the entire continual learning process, ensuring a consistent action space.
- **Strategy-II:** Individual statistics ($\mu_k$, $\sigma_k$) were computed and applied for each task $T_k$, leading to a shifting normalization reference.

**Continual Learning with Experience Replay (ER):**
The effectiveness of experience replay, a standard continual learning technique, was evaluated.
- **Replay Buffer Management:** A replay buffer $B$ with a fixed total capacity of $M$ episodes was maintained, where $M = B \cdot |D_k|$ and $B$ is a buffer ratio relative to a single-task dataset size. After training on task $T_k$, a subset of its demonstrations was added to $B$. To ensure balanced coverage, the buffer capacity was equally distributed among all previously seen tasks $\{D_1, \ldots, D_{k-1}\}$, with a minimum of one episode per task.
- **Replay Frequency ($f_r$):** A hyperparameter $f_r \in (0,1)$ controlled the proportion of replay samples in each mini-batch. At each training step, samples were drawn from the replay buffer with probability $f_r$ and from the current task's dataset with probability $1 - f_r$. To ensure 4,000 effective optimization steps on the current task, the total training budget for replay-based methods was increased to $4000 / (1 - f_r)$.
- **Hyperparameter Sweep:** Experiments systematically varied buffer ratio $B \in \{0.002, 0.02, 0.2\}$ and replay frequency $f_r \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5\}$. The default configuration for ER was $B = 0.2$ and $f_r = 0.2$.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The learning performance was quantified using three complementary metrics:
- **Average Score ($\bar{\rho}_K$):** The mean normalized score across all tasks evaluated at the final checkpoint.
- **Negative Backward Transfer (NBT$_i$):** Quantifies knowledge loss on task $i$ after learning subsequent tasks. A positive value indicates degradation. Also reported as per-task terminal score drop ($\rho_{i,i} - \rho_{i,K}$).
- **Forward Transfer (FT$_i$):** Assesses whether prior tasks facilitate or hinder the acquisition of new tasks. A positive value indicates facilitation.

**Detailed Task Scoring Rubrics:**
A fine-grained, multi-stage evaluation protocol was adopted for each task instead of binary success/failure. This captured partial task completion and provided more stable measurements under real-world noise. Each successfully completed checkpoint contributed one score unit, with maximum scores varying by task (e.g., Stack Bowl: 4, Hang Cup: 4, Press Button: 3, Fold Towel: 5). Scores were normalized to a 0-100 scale.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presented several key findings regarding continual learning of VLA models in real-world environments:

1. **Catastrophic Forgetting in Naive Sequential Fine-Tuning:**
 Without any anti-forgetting mechanisms, sequential fine-tuning of VLA models on the real-world dataset resulted in severe catastrophic forgetting. Performance on earlier tasks (D1, D2, D3) collapsed dramatically. For instance, the score on D1 (Stack Bowl) dropped from a single-task baseline of 100.0 to 15.0, D2 (Hang Cup) from 97.5 to 25.0, and D3 (Press Button) from 100.0 to 13.3. Excluding the last-trained task (D4), the average score dropped from a single-task baseline of 99.2 to 17.8. The calculated average Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) of +80.0 confirmed a near-total loss of prior task knowledge. This empirically established that real-world sequential adaptation poses a significant challenge for VLA deployment.

2. **Structured Forgetting Patterns:**
 Forgetting was not uniform across tasks. D3 (Press Button) exhibited the most severe forgetting, followed by D2 (Hang Cup). The analysis indicated that forgetting was influenced by axes of visual similarity and action primitive overlap. For example, visually similar attributes (e.g., "green" for a button and a target bowl) led to object confusion, and different manipulation primitives (e.g., grasping a cup versus a towel) could overwrite each other.

3. **Effectiveness of Experience Replay (ER) with Modest Budgets:**
 Experience replay proved highly effective in mitigating catastrophic forgetting. Under the default configuration (buffer ratio $B = 0.2$, replay frequency $f_r = 0.2$), all four tasks maintained performance within 10 percentage points of their single-task baselines. The average NBT decreased substantially from +80.0 (without ER) to +5.0. This indicated that a replay budget equivalent to 20% of a single task's data and 20% of training steps was sufficient to largely eliminate forgetting. Furthermore, the D4 (Fold Towel) task showed positive Forward Transfer (FT), improving from 52.0 (single-task) to 78–92 within the sequential learning setup, suggesting effective transfer from rigid-object to deformable-object manipulation.

4. **Impact of Buffer Size and Replay Frequency:**
 - **Buffer Size:** Generally, larger replay buffers led to improved retention, though sensitivity varied by task. Tasks requiring precise spatial localization, such as D2 (Hang Cup), were particularly vulnerable to smaller buffer sizes, experiencing drops from 97.5 to 60.0 as the buffer decreased. This suggested a need for diverse replay trajectories for such tasks.
 - **Replay Frequency:** The replay frequency exhibited a U-shaped trade-off. Low frequencies ($f_r = 0.05$) were insufficient to preserve old tasks, while excessively high frequencies ($f_r = 0.5$) impaired the acquisition of new tasks (e.g., D3 dropped to 33.3 at Task 3). The optimal balance between stability and plasticity was observed around $f_r = 0.2$.

5. **Continual Learning Outperforms Joint Training:**
 The well-configured continual learning pipeline with experience replay consistently matched or exceeded the performance of joint multi-task training across all four tasks. Notably, for D3 (Press Button), joint training failed significantly, achieving only 13.3, whereas continual learning with ER achieved 90.0. The study attributed the failures of joint training to gradient interference, representation domination by visually richer tasks, and imbalanced loss scales. Continual learning, by staging task acquisition and preserving prior knowledge via replay without destructive gradient competition, circumvented these issues.

6. **Critical Role of Action Normalization Consistency:**
 Action normalization consistency was identified as a critical factor for ER effectiveness. Using per-task normalization (Strategy-II) resulted in models failing on non-Stack Bowl tasks (scores of 0.0 for D2, D3, D4), even when task semantics might have been partially learned. This failure occurred because inconsistent gradient signals were generated within batches, as replay data normalized under prior tasks alternated with current data normalized under current task statistics. This interaction prevented the model from establishing a stable action space. In contrast, consistent normalization (Strategy-I) preserved a stable action space and enabled high performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an empirical study of real-world continual learning for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which is significant for several reasons:

1. **Bridging the Reality Gap:** The work provides the first empirical investigation into continual VLA learning using a real-world dataset and deployment-realistic constraints. This addresses a critical limitation of previous simulation-based studies, offering a more accurate assessment of VLA models' capabilities and challenges in practical scenarios.
2. **Highlighting Catastrophic Forgetting:** The findings definitively demonstrate that VLA models suffer significant catastrophic forgetting when exposed to heterogeneous, sequential real-world data. This establishes forgetting as a first-order problem for the deployment of long-lived robotic policies based on VLAs, underscoring the necessity for effective continual learning strategies.
3. **Providing Practical Guidance:** The research offers practical guidance for deploying VLA models in continual learning settings. It validates experience replay as an effective mechanism to mitigate forgetting and, crucially, identifies specific implementation factors—such as replay frequency, buffer size, and action normalization consistency—as determinants of success. These are not minor engineering choices but critical elements that can dictate whether continual adaptation succeeds or fails.
4. **Challenging Conventional Paradigms:** The demonstration that a well-configured sequential learning pipeline with a modest replay budget can outperform joint multi-task training is a notable finding. This suggests that acquiring skills incrementally with appropriate forgetting mitigation can be a more effective strategy than simultaneous learning across all tasks, especially when facing issues like gradient interference and task imbalance in joint training. This challenges the assumption that consolidating all data for joint training is always optimal and positions continual learning as a competitive and potentially superior paradigm for scalable real-world VLA learning.
5. **Inspiring Future Research:** By establishing a real-world benchmark and systematically analyzing key factors, this work is expected to inspire further research in the field. Future work could expand the evaluation to more diverse embodiments and task distributions, investigate other continual adaptation strategies, and explore larger-scale, long-horizon scenarios, thereby advancing the development of truly lifelong learning robots.

In summary, this paper contributes to the understanding of VLA model behavior under real-world continual learning constraints, identifies key challenges and effective mitigation strategies, and offers insights that can guide the development of robust, adaptable robotic systems.
