# ContactFlow: A video action conditioning that transfers across embodiments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Bonn and Lamarr Institute introduce ContactFlow, an embodiment-agnostic action representation, to condition a video generative world model for predicting physically plausible robot manipulation. This system improves cross-embodiment generalization for unseen tasks and enables a robot control pipeline that successfully verifies proposed trajectories before execution.

## Problem

- Existing video-based world models for robotics often generate visually plausible but physically impossible outcomes, particularly for complex contact and manipulation interactions.
- Action conditioning mechanisms in current world models are frequently embodiment-specific (e.g., joint-based), limiting their transferability across different robot morphologies or between robots and human demonstrations.
- The inability to leverage large, heterogeneous datasets of human interactions or diverse robot data due to incompatible action representations restricts scalable robot learning.

## Method

- Developed **Contact Flow**, an embodiment-agnostic action representation that encodes the trajectory of 3D contact points between an actor and an object, projected into image space as a sparse 7-channel control frame.
- Integrated Contact Flow as a conditioning signal into a ==latent video diffusion transformer== (**CF World Model**), based on ==DiT-like architectures==, to predict future visual frames of manipulation outcomes.
- Designed a "propose-imagine-verify-act" pipeline where a vision-language model (VLM) assesses imagined manipulation rollouts from the CF World Model to verify trajectory success before real-world robot execution.

## Results

- Contact Flow models achieved competitive performance on the DROID dataset, with the Wan 2.2 14B + ControlNet variant achieving a DreamSim of **0.035** and PSNR of **24.20**, outperforming baselines like CTRL-World.
- The models demonstrated strong zero-shot cross-embodiment generalization, consistently outperforming Kinema4D on unseen robot benchmarks (RLBench OOD, AgiBot OOD, GenieSim OOD) and human-hand manipulation datasets (EgoDex OOD).
- The full propose-imagine-verify-act pipeline successfully forecasted real-robot outcomes in **8 out of 10** unseen pick-and-place scenarios with a Franka Panda robot, enabling successful deployment where direct policy execution failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Abstracting manipulation dynamics to object-centric 3D contact point trajectories creates a minimal and universal action representation that effectively transfers across human hands and various robot grippers.
> - Conditioning a video generative model with Contact Flow enables the prediction of physically plausible manipulation outcomes, moving beyond mere visual coherence to respect underlying interaction mechanics.
> - A world model capable of predicting physically realistic interactions can serve as a zero-shot verifier for proposed robot trajectories, significantly enhancing the robustness and safety of robot control in unseen environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of robot learning and embodied intelligence, specifically focusing on the development and application of world models for robot planning and control. World models, which are learned simulators of environment dynamics, represent a foundational component for intelligent agents to anticipate and evaluate the consequences of their actions prior to execution.

Recent advancements in video generation, particularly large-scale generative models, have enabled the synthesis of temporally coherent and photorealistic future frames. These models have found various applications in robotics, including:
- **Planning and Control:** Rolling out candidate action sequences in imagination to select goal-achieving paths (e.g., UniPi).
- **Policy Learning:** Pretraining or distilling manipulation policies from generated visual dynamics (e.g., GR-1).
- **Data Augmentation:** Synthesizing novel demonstrations and experiences to enhance generalization (e.g., RoboDreamer, DreamGen).
- **Evaluation and Verification:** Acting as action-conditioned simulators to score or verify behaviors before real-world execution (e.g., IRASim, Wang et al.).

Despite these advances, a significant challenge remains in achieving physical faithfulness in video-based world models. Existing models often generate visually plausible but physically impossible outcomes, particularly concerning complex physical interactions like contact and manipulation. Furthermore, the action conditioning mechanisms used by many current world models are frequently embodiment-specific. Joint-based representations, while expressive, are tied to particular kinematic chains, limiting their transferability across robots with different morphologies. Actor mask or silhouette representations offer some generality but still encode the full actor shape, which may inadvertently emphasize the actor's appearance rather than the fundamental interaction with the object.

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing an interaction representation that is agnostic to the actor's embodiment and specifically focuses on the physically salient aspect of manipulation: contact. By doing so, it aims to create world models that not only generate visually convincing videos but also respect the underlying mechanics of interaction, thereby enhancing their utility for robust robot control and learning from diverse data sources, including human demonstrations. This places the work in a critical position to bridge the gap between high-fidelity video generation and physically grounded robot planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an embodiment-agnostic action representation, termed "Contact Flow," that can effectively condition a video generative world model to predict physically plausible manipulation outcomes. This representation is designed to enable seamless transfer of learned interaction dynamics across different robot embodiments and between human demonstrations and robotic execution.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current video-based world models for robotics:

- **Lack of Physical Plausibility, particularly Contact:** While modern generative models can produce visually convincing videos, they often fail to capture the physical constraints governing manipulation. Specifically, the mechanics of contact—where and how an agent physically engages with an object—are frequently not accurately modeled. A world model that predicts physically impossible contact interactions or object movements is of limited practical use for robot planning, as it would lead to erroneous action verification and execution.

- **Embodiment-Specific Action Conditioning:** Many existing action representations are intrinsically tied to a specific robot morphology or kinematic structure (e.g., joint angles, end-effector poses). This embodiment specificity severely restricts the reusability of learned policies and world models. It necessitates extensive retraining or adaptation when deploying to a new robot platform or when attempting to leverage large datasets of human demonstrations, which involve a different "embodiment" (the human hand). The lack of a shared, transferable conditioning signal between diverse actors (humans, different robots) prevents scalable data integration.

The authors propose that physical manipulation is fundamentally governed by contact. An object's motion is determined by the application of force at specific points on its surface, irrespective of the actor's overall shape or kinematics. This insight motivates the development of Contact Flow, which is designed to be:
- **Minimal:** It discards extraneous actor-specific details (appearance, full kinematics), focusing solely on the locus of interaction.
- **Local and Geometric:** It encodes the specific points and their trajectories on the object's surface where contact occurs.
- **Embodiment-Agnostic:** By defining interaction purely in terms of object-centric contact geometry, the representation becomes universal across human hands and various robot grippers.

By formalizing this intuition into Contact Flow, the researchers aim to provide a shared conditioning signal for both human demonstrations and robotic execution. This allows for training a large-scale video generative model on a heterogeneous mixture of human-object interaction and robot manipulation videos. The resulting world model would then be capable of predicting physically realistic manipulation outcomes. This model is integrated into a "propose-imagine-verify-act" pipeline, where candidate robot trajectories are first imagined by the world model and then verified by a vision-language model (VLM) for task success before actual execution, thus enhancing the robustness and safety of robot deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology revolves around "Contact Flow," an embodiment-agnostic action representation, and its integration into a video generative world model within a propose-imagine-verify-act pipeline.

#### 3.1. Contact Flow Encoding

Contact Flow is defined as the trajectory of 3D contact points between an actor (human hand or robot gripper) and a target object over time, projected into image space. It specifically captures the surface interface and its movement where contact occurs, focusing on the "active dynamics" of interaction while excluding subsequent "passive dynamics" (e.g., object movement after initial contact).

Formally, at each timestep *t*, Contact Flow is represented as a set of *N<sub>t</sub>* contact points *C<sub>t</sub>* = {*c<sup>(i)</sup><sub>t</sub>*}<sup>*N<sub>t</sub>*</sup><sub>*i*=1</sub>. Each contact point *c<sup>(i)</sup><sub>t</sub>* is a 7-dimensional vector [x, y, z, ∆x, ∆y, ∆z, w], where:
- (x, y, z) are the 3D coordinates of the contact point on the object surface in the camera frame.
- (∆x, ∆y, ∆z) represent the displacement (local contact motion or "flow") to the subsequent frame.
- w ∈ is a confidence weight indicating the certainty of contact.

These 3D points are projected into the image plane using camera intrinsics *K* to obtain 2D pixel coordinates *u<sup>(i)</sup><sub>t</sub>*. The 7 attributes of each point are written to its corresponding pixel, forming a sparse 7-channel control frame. Stacking these frames over time yields a spatiotemporal Contact Flow video *C<sub>1:T</sub>*, which serves as the conditioning signal for the generative model. The confidence weight *w* is derived from embodiment-specific measures (spatial closeness for robots, HaMeR hand-pose and HACO contact confidence for humans) and modulated by local spatio-temporal neighborhood consistency (temporal alignment and neighborhood density).

#### 3.2. CF World Model

The Contact Flow world model is instantiated using a latent video diffusion transformer, specifically based on recent DiT-based video generators (Wan).
- **Architecture:** Given an input video *x<sub>0:T</sub>*, frames are encoded into a latent sequence *z<sub>0:T</sub>* using a frozen video VAE encoder *E*. The model learns a conditional generative distribution *p<sub>θ</sub>*(z<sub>1:T</sub> | z<sub>0</sub>, C<sub>1:T</sub>), where *z<sub>0</sub>* is the latent of the initial frame and *C<sub>1:T</sub>* is the Contact Flow signal.
- **Training Objective:** Training employs a flow-matching objective in latent space. The denoising network *v<sub>θ</sub>* is trained to predict the target velocity field, conditioned on the initial latent *z<sub>0</sub>* and the Contact Flow *C<sub>1:T</sub>*.
- **Inference:** During inference, the model starts from latent noise and integrates the learned velocity field, conditioned on *z<sub>0</sub>* and *C<sub>1:T</sub>*, to generate future latents *ˆz<sub>1:T</sub>*, which are then decoded into predicted future frames *ˆx<sub>1:T</sub>*.
- **Control Injection:** To demonstrate the robustness of Contact Flow as a conditioning signal, two different control injection mechanisms popular in diffusion models are tested:
 - **ControlNet:** Contact Flow is rendered as a spatiotemporal control video and fed into a trainable parallel branch that injects contact-aware features into a frozen U-Net backbone.
 - **VACE:** The Contact Flow signal is encoded as an additional video condition via a Video Condition Unit (VCU) and injected into a frozen Diffusion Transformer using lightweight Context Adapter blocks.

#### 3.3. Data Processing

Training data is sourced from both human manipulation videos and teleoperated robot episodes, processed to extract object masks, hand/gripper poses, dense pointmaps, and contact regions.

- **Pointmap Acquisition:** For any video sequence, 3D pointmaps *P<sub>t</sub>* are acquired either from stereo pairs using FoundationStereo or directly from RGB frames with MapAnything.
- **Contact Flow from Human Demonstration:**
 - **Hand Mesh Estimation:** HaMeR estimates 2D hand poses, which are used to infer 3D keypoint coordinates and fit the MANO model to the scene.
 - **Object Mask Segmentation:** SAM3 (prompted by object names from spaCy or Gemini) or pre-provided masks segment the manipulated object. GroundingDINO is used for quality checks.
 - **Contact Estimation:** HACO estimates contact regions on the hand mesh, filtered by a binary contact estimation (distance threshold between hand and object, 2D mask overlap) and temporal smoothing. Datasets processed include TasteRob, TACO, and OakInk.
- **Contact Flow from Robotic Data:**
 - Assumes a calibrated, static, exocentric RGBD camera, URDF, and robot state vector.
 - **Segmentation:** WildDet3D (with ground-truth depth) estimates 3D bounding boxes for target objects, identified by checking robot tool center point entry. SAM3 (fine-tuned on RoboEngine) provides 2D robot masks.
 - **Contact Estimation:** A geometric approach uses the URDF and object pointcloud to distinguish between front and back gripper fingers. Overlap between object masks and rendered robot finger masks (or rendered depth for occluded fingers) yields contact points. For frames after gripper closing, a rigid transform assumption holds. Point correspondences are smoothed with a Hough-based filter. RobotInter is used for filtering.
- **Contact Flow at Inference:**
 - Requires an end-effector trajectory and a metrically accurate 3D model and pose of the target object, obtained from an external stereo camera (FoundationStereo).
 - A VLM (Gemini) reduces the task to an atomic instruction and proposes an object bounding box, refined by SAM3.
 - SAM 3D-Objects generates a 3D object geometry, which is then refined in pose and scale through a staged process involving PCA alignment, trimmed ICP, and a differentiable render-and-compare step using gsplat.
 - Finally, the end-effector trajectory is applied to the scene pointmap via the gripper URDF to synthesize the anticipated Contact Flow, which then conditions the world model.

#### 3.4. Propose-Imagine-Verify-Act Pipeline

The complete pipeline for real-world deployment:
1. **Propose:** A single RGBD view initializes a symbolic twin simulator. A π<sub>0.5</sub> vision-language-action policy proposes an end-effector trajectory within this twin.
2. **Imagine:** The proposed trajectory is converted into Contact Flow. The Contact Flow world model (conditioned on the initial frame and Contact Flow) generates a predicted visual rollout of the manipulation.
3. **Verify:** A VLM (Gemini) assesses the imagined rollout to judge whether the task is successfully completed.
4. **Act:** Only trajectories deemed successful by the VLM in simulation are executed open-loop on the real robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation addresses two main questions: (Q1) the effectiveness of Contact Flow as a conditioning signal for video prediction, and (Q2) the world model's ability to act as a zero-shot verifier for proposed trajectories in unseen scenes.

#### 4.1. Q1: Contact Flow as an Effective Conditioning Signal: Evaluation on DROID Dataset (Table 1)

Models were evaluated on held-out DROID scenes, generating future video frames conditioned on the initial frame and ground-truth Contact Flow. Performance was measured using DreamSim (lower is better), FID (lower), FVD (lower), PSNR (higher), SSIM (higher), and LPIPS (lower). The metrics were computed on the agent-masked region to focus on object manipulation rather than actor appearance.
- **Contact Flow variants (CF + VACE/CTRL-Net):** The Contact Flow models, especially the Wan 2.2 14B + ControlNet variant, demonstrated competitive performance. For instance, Wan 2.2 14B achieved a DreamSim of 0.035, an FID of 74.05, and a PSNR of 24.20. The Wan 2.1 14B + VACE model was also competitive.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **Kinema4D (Robot Pointmap):** Showed a DreamSim of 0.043, FID of 67.06, and PSNR of 20.17. Contact Flow models generally achieved better DreamSim and PSNR, while FID was sometimes lower for Kinema4D.
 - **CTRL-World (Action Embeddings):** Performed with a DreamSim of 0.059, FID of 72.90, and PSNR of 22.44. Contact Flow models consistently outperformed CTRL-World across most metrics.
 - **TesserAct (Language Conditioning):** Showed the lowest performance with a DreamSim of 0.106, FID of 164.54, and PSNR of 13.96, indicating that high-level language conditioning without geometric control is less effective for precise manipulation prediction.
- **Conclusion for DROID:** Contact Flow is demonstrated to be an effective conditioning signal, enabling accurate and coherent predictions of robot manipulation outcomes, outperforming or matching existing approaches in many key metrics for generation quality.

#### 4.1.2. Cross-Dataset Evaluation (Table 2)

This evaluation assessed generalization to entirely unseen embodiments, scenes, and rendering styles, including held-out splits of human and robot training data, and four datasets entirely unseen during training (RLBench OOD, AgiBot OOD, GenieSim OOD for robots; EgoDex OOD for human hands).
- **Robot Manipulation Benchmarks (RLBench OOD, AgiBot OOD, GenieSim OOD):**
 - Contact Flow models, particularly those trained on a mixed dataset (14B mix) or human-only data (14B human), generally outperformed Kinema4D in DreamSim and other visual quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) across these out-of-distribution robot tasks. For example, on RLBench OOD, 14B (mix) achieved a DreamSim of 0.040 compared to Kinema4D's 0.051. On GenieSim OOD, 5B achieved 0.050 compared to Kinema4D's 0.085.
 - Training primarily on DROID (14B DROID) sometimes led to slightly worse generalization compared to mixed or human-only training, suggesting the importance of diverse data for embodiment transfer.
- **Human-Hand Manipulation Benchmarks (TACO, Taste-ROB, OakInk, EgoDex OOD):**
 - Contact Flow models, especially those trained on human data (14B human) or mixed data (14B mix), consistently and significantly outperformed Kinema4D across all metrics for human-hand manipulation datasets, including the out-of-distribution EgoDex. For example, on Taste-ROB, 14B (human) achieved a DreamSim of 0.016 and a PSNR of 30.66, vastly superior to Kinema4D's 0.107 DreamSim and 20.68 PSNR.
- **Overall Generalization:** The results suggest that Contact Flow enables strong cross-embodiment generalization, particularly from human demonstrations to various manipulation tasks, including zero-shot scenarios. The mixed training data strategy and human-specific training appeared to yield robust generalization capabilities.

#### 4.2. Q2: World Model as a Zero-Shot Verifier

The full propose-imagine-verify-act pipeline was deployed on a Franka Panda robot for 10 unseen tabletop pick-and-place scenarios.
- **Verification Accuracy:** Using the Wan 2.2 14B backbone, the world model successfully forecasted the real-robot outcome correctly in 8 out of 10 cases. This indicates that the imagined rollouts were sufficiently accurate for the VLM to reliably predict the success or failure of a proposed trajectory.
- **Impact of Verification:** The verification step was crucial for successful robot deployment. Directly deploying the π<sub>0.5</sub> policy (a state-of-the-art VLA) into the real world without the intermediate imagination and verification step resulted in no successful runs. This highlights the value of the Contact Flow world model in validating trajectories generated by a high-level policy, even if the policy itself can propose motions within a symbolic twin, which is too coarse for reliable certification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper introduces Contact Flow, a novel action representation with several significant implications for robot learning and embodied AI.

**Significance:**
- **Embodiment-Agnostic Interaction Encoding:** Contact Flow's core contribution is its ability to abstract manipulation into a compact, object-centric, and embodiment-agnostic signal based on 3D contact point trajectories. This addresses a fundamental limitation of previous action representations that were tied to specific robot kinematics or full actor appearance.
- **Scalable Learning from Heterogeneous Data:** By providing a shared conditioning signal, Contact Flow enables the training of a single world model on diverse datasets comprising both human demonstrations and robotic manipulation videos. This capability is critical for leveraging the vast amount of human interaction data to teach robots complex skills, circumventing the need for expensive and embodiment-specific robot data collection.
- **Physically Plausible World Models:** The experiments demonstrate that conditioning on Contact Flow results in video predictions that are not only visually coherent but also physically plausible, particularly concerning object interaction. This moves beyond merely photorealistic generation to models that respect the mechanics of contact, a crucial step for practical robot control.
- **Zero-Shot Transfer and Generalization:** The Contact Flow world model exhibits strong generalization capabilities, transferring learned interaction dynamics to unseen robot embodiments, scenes, and objects without requiring per-dataset adaptation. This zero-shot deployment potential is a key enabler for rapid and flexible robot deployment in novel environments.
- **Robust Robot Control Pipeline:** The integration of Contact Flow into a "propose-imagine-verify-act" pipeline provides a mechanism for robust robot control. By allowing a VLM to evaluate imagined rollouts before real-world execution, the pipeline enhances the reliability and safety of robot operation, acting as a crucial filter for potentially problematic trajectories from high-level policies.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Robot Skill Acquisition:** The ability to learn from large-scale, diverse human demonstration data, regardless of embodiment, could significantly accelerate the development of robotic manipulation skills, making robots more capable and adaptable.
- **Enhanced Robot Safety and Reliability:** The imagine-verify loop could become a standard component in robot control architectures, reducing the risk of errors and failures in real-world scenarios by pre-validating actions in a learned simulation.
- **Reduced Data Dependency and Cost:** By enabling knowledge transfer across embodiments and facilitating zero-shot deployment, Contact Flow could reduce the extensive data collection and engineering effort typically required to adapt robots to new tasks or environments.
- **Advancement of General-Purpose World Models:** This work contributes to the broader goal of creating general-purpose world models for embodied AI, which can reason about and predict complex physical interactions in a robust and transferable manner.
- **New Research Avenues:** Contact Flow opens new research directions in contact-rich manipulation, exploring more physically grounded world models, and investigating the interplay between different levels of abstraction (e.g., symbolic planning and visual dynamics).

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as inference cost and reliance on upstream calibration and depth estimation, the proposed Contact Flow representation provides a foundational advancement for building more capable, adaptable, and reliable robotic systems.
