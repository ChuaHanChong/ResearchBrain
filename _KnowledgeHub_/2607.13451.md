# Learning Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics for Deformable Object Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A hybrid simulation framework, Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics (PGRD), accurately models deformable objects by combining an optimized spring-mass physics backbone with a neural network that learns residual velocity corrections. This approach consistently achieves lower 3D tracking errors and higher visual accuracy across diverse objects compared to both purely physics-based and purely learning-based methods, supporting robust robotic manipulation and photorealistic video prediction.

## Problem

- Physics-based simulations of deformable objects struggle with accurately identifying material parameters, suffer from high computational costs, and are limited by simplifying assumptions or discretization in capturing complex, non-linear deformations and contact dynamics.
- Purely learning-based methods for deformable object simulation often require extensive data, exhibit poor generalization to novel scenarios or materials, and can produce physically inconsistent or unstable predictions.
- Integrating learned corrections into physics simulations presents stability challenges, particularly when attempting direct position-based modifications, which can lead to simulation divergence.

## Method

- Developed **Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics (PGRD)**, a hybrid simulation framework that uses an optimizable ==spring-mass system== as a physics backbone and a learned neural network to predict per-particle ==residual velocity corrections==.
- Implemented a two-stage training process: first, optimizing physics parameters via ==CMA-ES==, then training the neural network to predict ==velocity-based residuals==, which are summed with physics-predicted velocities to ensure simulation stability.
- Designed a network architecture featuring a ==Point Transformer V3 (PTv3)== encoder for spatiotemporal features, a ==NeRF-style MLP decoder== for initial corrections, and a ==sliding-window transformer== with a ==gating mechanism== for temporal aggregation and stable blending of local and history-based refinements.

## Results

- **PGRD** consistently achieved the **lowest error** across all **six** diverse deformable objects and all **three** 3D tracking metrics (**Mean Distance Error**, **Chamfer Distance**, **Earth Mover’s Distance**), outperforming both optimized physics-based (e.g., Differentiable Spring-Mass, MPM) and state-of-the-art learning-based (e.g., PGND) baselines.
- In action-conditioned video prediction, PGRD demonstrated superior visual accuracy, achieving the best **J-Score** and **F-Score** for object mask prediction and the lowest **LPIPS** scores for perceptual similarity, confirming that improved 3D dynamics translate to more realistic visual outcomes.
- Ablation studies validated the critical contributions of key architectural components, including the PTv3 encoder, NeRF-style decoder, temporal aggregator with gating, internal particles for volumetric objects, and the necessity of the velocity-based residual formulation for maintaining stability and performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Employing velocity-based residual corrections, rather than position-based ones, is crucial for maintaining stability and physical consistency when integrating learned components into dynamic physics simulations.
> - Grounding the learned dynamics in an optimized physics backbone provides a strong inductive bias, reducing data requirements and enhancing generalization by allowing the neural network to focus on learning subtle, missing dynamics (e.g., non-linearities, heterogeneities) rather than the entire system from scratch.
> - Leveraging temporal context through a sliding-window transformer and an adaptive gating mechanism to blend initial and history-based corrections significantly improves accuracy and robustness over multi-step rollouts in deformable object simulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Accurate simulation of deformable objects is a recognized challenge in robotics and computer vision, impacting applications such as robotic manipulation. The existing research landscape for deformable object simulation is broadly categorized into two main paradigms: physics-based methods and learning-based methods.

Physics-based methods, which include approaches like spring-mass models, Finite Element Methods (FEM), Position-Based Dynamics (PBD), and Material Point Methods (MPM), aim to simulate object deformation using analytical equations derived from material properties and physical laws. These methods offer interpretability and physical plausibility. However, they are often limited by the difficulty of accurately obtaining material parameters for real-world objects, high computational costs, inherent limitations due to discretization (e.g., coarse meshes missing fine deformations), and reliance on simplifying modeling assumptions (e.g., linear elasticity). Recent advancements in differentiable physics simulation have enabled gradient-based optimization for parameter identification, but these methods struggle with discontinuities arising from contact, friction, and non-smooth geometries common in robotic tasks.

Learning-based methods, on the other hand, leverage data-driven models, such as neural networks, to learn deformation patterns directly from observations. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), transformers, and recurrent neural networks have been employed to capture complex nonlinear dynamics without explicit material parameters. While these approaches can achieve real-time performance and model intricate behaviors, they typically require large amounts of training data, may exhibit poor generalization to unseen scenarios or materials, and can lack the physical consistency necessary for robust real-world deployment, potentially learning spurious correlations.

This work, Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics (PGRD), positions itself as a hybrid simulation framework that seeks to combine the strengths of both paradigms while mitigating their respective weaknesses. It attempts to provide the interpretability and generalizability benefits of physics-based models with the flexibility and data-fitting capabilities of learning-based models. While the concept of residual learning has been applied to model robot dynamics (where discrepancies are simpler modeling errors), PGRD aims to extend this paradigm to the more complex, high-dimensional states of deformable objects, where residuals must account for phenomena like nonlinear stiffness and heterogeneous materials.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a hybrid simulation framework, Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics (PGRD), for accurate and stable simulation of deformable objects, specifically targeting challenges in robotic manipulation applications.

The motivation for developing PGRD stems from the identified limitations of existing simulation paradigms:

1. **Addressing limitations of physics-based methods:**
 - **Material parameter identification:** Physics-based simulators require precise material parameters (e.g., stiffness, damping), which are often difficult to obtain for real-world objects and can vary spatially. Existing methods typically rely on gradient-free optimization, which scales poorly, or differentiable simulation, which struggles with the non-differentiable nature of contacts and friction in realistic scenarios. PGRD aims to bypass the need for precise analytical modeling of these complexities by offloading their representation to a learned component.
 - **Modeling assumptions and discretization:** Traditional physics models are constrained by their underlying mathematical formulations (e.g., linear elasticity) and discretization choices, which may fail to capture fine-scale or highly nonlinear deformations. PGRD is motivated to use a neural network to predict corrections for these inherent modeling errors.
 - **Computational cost:** While spring-mass models are efficient, more complex physics simulators (like FEM or MPM) can be computationally expensive, hindering real-time applications and iterative planning. PGRD seeks to maintain the efficiency of a simpler physics backbone while enhancing accuracy through learning.

2. **Overcoming shortcomings of learning-based methods:**
 - **Data dependency and generalization:** Purely data-driven models are often data-hungry and prone to poor generalization when applied to scenarios outside their training distribution or objects with different material properties. By grounding the learning process in a physics backbone, PGRD is motivated to provide inductive biases that reduce data requirements and improve generalization by having the network learn only the *residual* dynamics, rather than the entire dynamics from scratch.
 - **Physical consistency and stability:** Learning-based models can sometimes produce physically inconsistent or unstable simulations without explicit physical priors. The physics backbone in PGRD is intended to provide inherent physical plausibility and stability, which the learned residuals then refine.

3. **Achieving stable hybrid dynamics:** A specific technical motivation is to ensure stability when injecting learned corrections into a physics simulation. Directly correcting positions can destabilize the simulation by creating mismatches between positions and velocities. Therefore, a key objective is to adopt a velocity-based residual formulation, motivated by impulse-based simulation, to enable smooth and stable integration of learned corrections.

4. **Capturing temporal dependencies:** Deformable object dynamics involve momentum and temporal effects. PGRD aims to incorporate a mechanism, specifically a sliding-window transformer, to capture these temporal dependencies, allowing the network to refine velocity corrections based on a history of past states and actions.

5. **Enabling downstream applications:** Ultimately, the motivation extends to demonstrating the practical utility of an improved deformable object simulator. This includes its application as a forward model for model predictive control (MPC) in robotic manipulation and for interactive, photorealistic simulation, including language-conditioned planning tasks.

In summary, PGRD aims to develop a robust, accurate, and stable hybrid simulation framework that leverages the strengths of both physics-based and learning-based approaches to address critical limitations in deformable object modeling, thereby facilitating advanced robotic manipulation and interactive virtual environments.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics (PGRD) framework operates as a hybrid simulation system, integrating an optimizable spring-mass physics backbone with a learned neural network for residual corrections. The approach involves a two-stage training procedure and a specifically designed network architecture.

**A. Physics Backbone**
The simulation backbone models deformable objects as a spring-mass system.
1. **Representation:** Objects are discretized into a graph of point masses (nodes) interconnected by springs (edges). Each node possesses a position (x) and velocity (v) that evolve over time using Newtonian mechanics solved with a semi-implicit Euler method.
2. **Manipulation Modeling:** For prehensile manipulation, particles near the gripper are treated as rigid and move with the gripper. For non-prehensile manipulation, the gripper acts as a spherical collision object that pushes penetrating particles.
3. **Parameter Optimization:** The fidelity of this spring-mass model relies on five physical parameters (θ): stiffness, damping, threshold (for spring creation), maximum springs per node, and ground friction. These parameters are optimized to match real-world observations.
 - **Objective:** Minimize the pointwise mean squared error between predicted and ground truth particle positions over a trajectory.
 - **Optimizer:** Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) is used, a gradient-free method chosen due to the non-differentiable nature of contacts and collisions in the simulator. This is the first stage of the two-stage training process.
4. **Volumetric Object Augmentation:** For volumetric objects (e.g., soft toys), the surface spring-mass model is augmented with internal particles. These particles are uniformly sampled within a watertight mesh (reconstructed from multi-view observations using RaySt3R and marching cubes) to prevent unrealistic collapse and maintain structural integrity.

**B. Residual Dynamics Framework**
Following the optimized physics backbone, a learned residual module predicts corrections to account for discrepancies between the physics model and real-world dynamics, such as nonlinear stiffness or heterogeneous materials.
1. **Velocity-Based Formulation:** At each timestep, the physics backbone provides an immediate prediction (X_sim, V_sim). A neural network then predicts a per-particle residual velocity (∆V_residual). This velocity-based formulation is critical for stability, as direct position corrections were found to cause simulation instability and blow-ups.
2. **Final State Update:** The final velocities (ˆV_t+1) are obtained by adding the learned residual velocities to the simulator’s predictions: ˆV_t+1 = V_sim_t+1 + ∆V_residual_t+1. These corrected velocities are then time-integrated to update positions: ˆX_t+1 = X_sim_t+1 + ∆V_residual_t+1 · dt.
3. **Boundary Conditions:** For particles rigidly held by grippers, their residual velocities are zeroed out.
4. **Training Objective:** The residual network is trained with a supervised objective to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between predicted (ˆX_t) and ground truth (X*_t) particle positions over a multi-step rollout. Crucially, during training, the simulator’s predicted state is fed back as input for the next step (multi-step rollout) rather than resetting to ground truth. This exposes the network to its own prediction errors, improving robustness to error accumulation over long horizons. This forms the second stage of the two-stage training.

**C. Network Architecture**
The residual network consists of three main components:
1. **Encoder (Point Transformer V3 - PTv3):** This component extracts per-particle spatiotemporal features. Input features are formed by concatenating the current state (X_t, V_t), historical states (X_his_t, V_his_t), and the physics simulator’s immediate predictions (X_sim_t+1, V_sim_t+1). PTv3 processes these inputs through patch-based self-attention layers, computing position-dependent features based on local geometric configurations.
2. **Decoder (NeRF-style MLP):** The encoder's features are passed to a decoder for initial velocity correction estimation (∆V_initial_t+1). This MLP decoder takes the encoder features concatenated with Fourier positional encodings of the particle positions.
3. **Temporal Aggregator (Sliding-Window Transformer):** This module refines the initial velocity correction using temporal context.
 - **Mechanism:** A buffer stores initial corrections (∆V_initial_t+1) from a sliding window of previous frames. This sequence is projected to a latent embedding, augmented with sinusoidal positional encodings, and processed by a transformer.
 - **Gating:** The transformer output for the final timestep (h_t) is used to compute a temporal offset (δ_t) via a tanh activation and gating weights (g_t) via a sigmoid activation. The final residual velocity (∆V_residual_t+1) is a blended combination of the initial prediction and the temporal offset, controlled by the gating weights: ∆V_residual_t+1 = 0.1 · [(1 − g_t) ⊙ ∆V_initial_t+1 + g_t ⊙ δ_t]. This gating mechanism adaptively blends local and history-based corrections, enhancing stability.

**D. Data Collection**
Data is collected from real-world manipulation tasks using a UFACTORY xArm 7. Four Intel RealSense D455 cameras capture synchronized RGBD observations. Object masks are obtained using Grounded SAM 2, and 2D image points are tracked with CoTracker. These 2D tracks are lifted to 3D using depth information. An iterative rollout procedure extracts temporally consistent 3D point cloud trajectories and robot gripper positions, forming the dataset for training and evaluation. The dataset consists of 120 episodes (approximately 4.07 minutes of unique interaction data) across six diverse deformable objects: rope, paper, sloth toy, duster, flag, and teddy toy, encompassing both prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research evaluates PGRD's performance in two primary settings: 3D tracking accuracy and action-conditioned video prediction, comparing it against both physics-based and learning-based baselines.

**A. Dynamics and Tracking Accuracy (3D Metrics)**
PGRD was evaluated on six diverse real-world deformable objects (rope, paper, sloth toy, duster, flag, teddy toy) using Mean Distance Error (MDE), Chamfer Distance (CD), and Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) as metrics for 3D tracking accuracy.
- **Superior Performance:** PGRD consistently achieved the lowest error across all objects and all three 3D tracking metrics (Table I). This indicates that the learned residual corrections effectively bridge the gap between the physics backbone and real-world dynamics, outperforming both purely physics-based and purely learning-based approaches.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **Physics-based:** The optimized spring-mass model generally outperformed MPM, particularly for coherent objects. The Differentiable (Diff.) Spring-Mass, which refines parameters through gradient-based optimization, showed improvements over the basic optimized spring-mass baseline on most objects, demonstrating the value of refined physics parameters. However, even with this refinement, Diff. Spring-Mass did not match PGRD's performance.
 - **Learning-based:** PGND, a state-of-the-art learning-based method, substantially outperformed GBND. However, PGRD still surpassed PGND across all tested objects.
- **Qualitative Analysis (Figure 4):**
 - **Heterogeneous Objects:** PGRD exhibited distinct advantages on challenging objects like the duster, which has a rigid stem and soft feathers. Only PGRD successfully simulated both the stem's rigidity and the feathers' deformation. Physics-based baselines like spring-mass caused unnatural bending of the rigid stem due to uniform material assumptions, while MPM struggled to maintain volume. Learning-based methods like GBND and PGND also failed, predicting erroneous motions or incorrect compressions.
 - **Thin Objects:** MPM performed poorly on thin objects such as paper and flag, causing them to lose structural cohesion.
 - **Volumetric Objects (non-prehensile):** For the teddy toy under non-prehensile manipulation, the optimized spring-mass model struggled with slippage, and PGND inaccurately compressed the object. PGRD maintained proper contact and realistic compression.
 - **General Observations:** The optimized spring-mass model often appeared too stiff, capturing overall motion but lacking local flexibility. Learning-based methods sometimes accumulated drift (PGND) or produced unphysical artifacts due to graph topology issues (GBND). PGRD maintained both plausibility from the physics backbone and accuracy from learned local dynamics.

**B. Action-Conditioned Video Prediction (Visual Metrics)**
PGRD's dynamics predictions were used to drive 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for rendering future video frames, which were then compared against ground truth frames using J-Score/IoU, F-Score, and LPIPS metrics.
- **Superior Visual Accuracy:** PGRD consistently achieved the best J-Score and F-Score across all objects (Table II), indicating superior spatial overlap and foreground detection of the predicted object masks. It also maintained the lowest LPIPS scores, signifying better perceptual similarity to ground truth frames.
- **Consistency:** This demonstrates that PGRD's improved 3D dynamics predictions translate into more visually accurate and realistic action-conditioned video predictions.
- **Baseline Performance:** Differentiable Spring-Mass generally improved over the basic optimized spring-mass in visual metrics. PGND’s performance was mixed compared to Diff. Spring-Mass.

**C. Ablation Studies (Table III)**
Ablation studies confirmed the importance of several architectural choices:
- **Encoder:** Replacing PTv3 with PointNet++ degraded performance, indicating the value of PTv3's stronger local feature extraction.
- **Decoder:** Removing the NeRF-style decoder, by directly projecting encoder features, also reduced accuracy, highlighting the utility of explicit position-conditioned decoding.
- **Temporal Aggregator:** Removing the temporal aggregator component, which refines current estimates using a sliding window of previous predictions, decreased accuracy, supporting the need for temporal context.
- **Gating Mechanism:** Replacing the gating mechanism in the temporal aggregator with direct addition led to slightly worse performance, suggesting its role in stable refinement.
- **Internal Particles:** For volumetric objects like the sloth toy, removing internal particles (which prevent collapse) significantly degraded performance.
- **Velocity vs. Position Residuals:** Directly predicting position residuals led to simulation divergence, validating the necessity of the velocity-based residual formulation for stability.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The Physics-Guided Residual Dynamics (PGRD) framework represents a notable advancement in deformable object simulation, carrying several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Simulation Accuracy and Robustness:** By combining the physical grounding of an optimized spring-mass model with the adaptability of learned residual corrections, PGRD achieves higher simulation accuracy than either purely physics-based or purely learning-based methods. Its ability to handle challenging scenarios, such as heterogeneous materials (e.g., the duster) and contact-rich interactions, where existing methods struggle, significantly broadens the scope of accurate deformable object simulation.

2. **Improved Generalization and Stability:** The hybrid nature of PGRD, with physics providing an inductive bias, is expected to improve generalization to unseen scenarios and material properties compared to purely data-driven models. The velocity-based residual formulation specifically addresses stability issues often encountered when integrating learned corrections into dynamic systems, making the simulation more robust over long horizons.

3. **Broader Applicability to Real-World Robotics:** A key advantage is that PGRD does not require the underlying physics simulator to be fully differentiable. This allows it to be applied to complex real-world manipulation tasks involving non-smooth phenomena like collisions and friction, which often cause issues for differentiable simulators. This expands its utility in practical robotic applications.

4. **Enabling Advanced Robotic Manipulation Planning:**
 - **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** PGRD serves as a more accurate forward model for model-based control strategies like Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI). This leads to more effective and reliable manipulation planning, as demonstrated by superior performance in rope manipulation tasks and a significantly higher success rate (8 out of 10 trials vs. 2 out of 10 for the baseline) in complex cable rerouting through a narrow slot. Such capabilities are crucial for automating tasks involving delicate or flexible objects.
 - **Language-Conditioned Planning:** The integration of PGRD with language-conditioned goal generation pipelines (using models like Nano Banana Pro and Depth Anything V2) eliminates the need for pre-collected target point clouds. This allows robots to execute manipulation tasks directly from natural language commands and generated goal images, opening up a richer, more intuitive interface for human-robot interaction and broader task definitions in unstructured environments.

5. **Interactive and Photorealistic Virtual Environments:** PGRD's capability to predict dynamics that can drive 3D Gaussian Splatting enables interactive, photorealistic simulations. Users can issue commands, and the system renders visually consistent deformations in real-time. This has significant potential impact in various fields:
 - **Virtual Prototyping and Design:** Engineers and designers can interactively test and visualize how deformable materials behave under various forces.
 - **Robotics Training and Simulation:** Creation of more realistic and robust simulation environments for training robotic policies, reducing the reliance on real-world data collection.
 - **Entertainment and VR/AR:** Development of more immersive and believable virtual experiences with deformable objects.

In conclusion, PGRD contributes a technically sound and empirically validated approach to deformable object simulation. Its advancements in accuracy, stability, and applicability are poised to significantly impact the development of more capable and adaptable robotic systems, as well as more realistic and interactive digital environments.
