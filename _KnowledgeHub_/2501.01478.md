# Enhancing Reasoning through Process Supervision with Monte Carlo Tree Search

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at AI Lab, Giant Network developed an iterative self-training approach that uses Monte Carlo Tree Search to automatically generate fine-grained process supervision for large language models. This method assigns nuanced 'relative correctness' scores to intermediate reasoning steps, leading to improved mathematical reasoning performance, demonstrating gains of up to 4.85% on the MATH dataset and 5.03% on the GSM8K dataset for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) often exhibit limitations in robust, multi-step logical reasoning, particularly in complex domains such as mathematics.
- Traditional outcome-based supervision for LLMs is insufficient as models can arrive at correct final answers via flawed intermediate steps, hindering the development of genuine reasoning skills.
- While process supervision (feedback on intermediate steps) is effective, human annotation for such feedback is prohibitively expensive and prior automated methods, like MCTS-DPO, use less informative binary preference signals and discard valuable data.

## Method

- An iterative self-training framework is proposed where the LLM generates its own process supervision data using ==Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)==.
- MCTS is applied at each partial solution step to explore candidate 'next steps' and assign a nuanced =='relative correctness' score== to each step based on its average reward (success rate) compared to sibling steps.
- The LLM is fine-tuned using a ==weighted negative log-likelihood loss== that incorporates these relative correctness scores, alongside a KL divergence penalty to prevent excessive drift from the previous iteration.

## Results

- The proposed method consistently outperformed baselines (Zero-shot-CoT, RFT, Step-level DPO) on both MATH and GSM8K datasets, for instance, improving Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct's accuracy on MATH from **47.07%** to **51.92%**.
- Performance generally improved with successive iterations of the self-training process, demonstrating the cumulative benefits of the self-supervision loop, although improvements converged within a few iterations.
- The MCTS-based scoring method proved significantly superior to the 'Step-level DPO' baseline, which yielded only marginal improvements, validating the effectiveness of nuanced, relative correctness scores for training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Assigning nuanced, relative correctness scores to intermediate reasoning steps through MCTS provides a more effective supervision signal than binary preference (chosen/rejected) methods, leading to greater performance improvements.
> - An iterative self-training loop, where LLMs generate and learn from their own MCTS-evaluated reasoning steps, effectively enhances multi-step reasoning capabilities without reliance on costly human annotations or complex reinforcement learning setups.
> - The enhanced reasoning ability learned through this process demonstrates positive transferability across different mathematical reasoning datasets, indicating the acquisition of generalized problem-solving skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in the field of Large Language Models: their limited ability to perform robust, multi-step reasoning, particularly in complex domains like mathematics. While LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capacity across a wide array of language tasks, achieving human-level performance, their reasoning capabilities often fall short, struggling with logical consistency and accurate step-by-step problem-solving.

The broader research landscape for enhancing LLM reasoning can be categorized into several approaches:

- **Inference-time Techniques:**
 - **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:** This widely adopted technique (e.g., Wei et al., Kojima et al.) encourages LLMs to decompose problems and "think step-by-step." While effective, the reasoning elicited is often still limited, and it primarily serves as an inference-time strategy rather than instilling deeper reasoning abilities through training.
 - **Tree Search Algorithms at Inference:** More advanced techniques like Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al.) and integration of MCTS (Hao et al., Zhang et al.) during inference explore multiple reasoning paths to find a correct solution. These methods can significantly improve performance but come at a substantial computational cost during deployment.

- **Training-time Techniques (Fine-tuning):**
 - **Outcome Supervision:** Early attempts focused on fine-tuning LLMs by supervising them to produce correct final answers. Examples include Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) (Yuan et al.) and Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) (Zelikman et al.). However, a key limitation of outcome supervision is that an LLM might arrive at a correct final answer through flawed or incorrect intermediate reasoning steps, leading to brittle problem-solving abilities. Iterative Reasoning Preference Optimization (Pang et al.) also falls into this category, forming preference pairs based on correct/incorrect CoTs, but still fundamentally driven by the final outcome.
 - **Process Supervision:** This paradigm aims to provide more precise, fine-grained feedback on the *intermediate steps* of a reasoning process, rather than just the final answer.
 - **Human-labeled Process Supervision:** Pioneering work like Lightman et al. demonstrated that Process Reward Models (PRMs) trained on human-annotated reasoning steps significantly outperform Outcome Reward Models (ORMs). However, human annotation of reasoning steps, especially for complex problems, is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.
 - **Automated Process Supervision:** To address the cost of human labeling, researchers have explored methods to automatically generate process supervision.
 - Some approaches use Monte Carlo Sampling to estimate step "correctness" and train PRMs (e.g., Wang et al., Luo et al.), often followed by reinforcement learning (RL) to train the LLM.
 - Other methods avoid PRMs, directly identifying incorrect steps for preference learning, like Step-DPO (Lai et al.) and Self-Explore (Hwang et al.).
 - **MCTS-DPO (Xie et al.):** This work is a direct precursor to the current paper. It utilizes MCTS to generate pairwise data for preference learning (e.g., using Direct Preference Optimization, DPO), with iterative data generation and training.

This paper builds directly upon the automated process supervision paradigm, specifically drawing inspiration from MCTS-DPO. However, it critically identifies and addresses limitations of MCTS-DPO:
1. **Binary Preference vs. Graded Scores:** MCTS-DPO labels reasoning steps only as "chosen" or "rejected," a binary signal that "does not accurately capture the quality of the steps."
2. **Data Discarding:** MCTS-DPO forms preference pairs using only the "best and worst steps," discarding other potentially valuable intermediate steps.

By providing *nuanced, relative correctness scores* to *all* sampled steps and integrating them into a weighted log-likelihood loss, the authors aim to overcome these limitations, providing a more effective and data-efficient process supervision signal without relying on human annotations or the complexities of a separate reward model and reinforcement learning. This positions the work as a significant refinement and advancement in the field of automated, self-supervised reasoning enhancement for LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **significantly enhance the multi-step reasoning abilities of Large Language Models (LLMs)**, particularly within the domain of mathematical problem-solving. The authors specifically aim to achieve this improvement through an iterative self-training paradigm that leverages automated process supervision.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations and challenges in the current landscape of LLM development:

- **LLMs' Reasoning Deficiencies:** Despite their impressive performance on a wide array of language understanding and generation tasks, current LLMs still exhibit limitations in robust, multi-step logical reasoning. This deficiency is particularly apparent in complex domains like mathematics, where problems require precise, sequential thought and error detection.
- **Limitations of Outcome-based Supervision:** Traditional fine-tuning methods that rely solely on supervising the final output (outcome supervision) are insufficient for cultivating genuine reasoning skills. An LLM might coincidentally arrive at a correct final answer even with flawed or incorrect intermediate reasoning steps, leading to a superficial understanding and brittle performance on novel or slightly varied problems. The lack of granular feedback prevents the model from learning *how* to reason correctly.
- **High Cost of Human Process Annotation:** While process supervision, which provides feedback on each intermediate reasoning step, has been shown to be superior to outcome supervision, it traditionally requires expensive and labor-intensive human annotation. This scalability bottleneck prevents widespread adoption of high-quality process supervision for diverse and challenging reasoning tasks.
- **Improving Upon Existing Automated Methods:** Previous attempts at automated process supervision, such as MCTS-DPO, have made strides but suffer from inherent limitations. Specifically, MCTS-DPO uses a binary (chosen/rejected) preference signal for steps and discards a significant portion of valuable data by only using the "best" and "worst" steps for preference pairs. This leads to a less informative and less efficient training signal.
- **Desire for Self-Improvement and Efficiency:** The authors are motivated to develop a method that allows LLMs to "self-supervise" their reasoning process. This iterative generate-then-train approach aims to enable models to learn from their own exploratory reasoning attempts, reducing external dependencies and potentially democratizing access to enhanced reasoning capabilities by making it feasible for smaller, more resource-constrained LLMs. They also aim to avoid the complexities and potential instability associated with reinforcement learning (RL) typically used with learned reward models.

In summary, the core motivation is to bridge the gap between LLMs' general language proficiency and their need for robust, trustworthy reasoning abilities, doing so through an innovative, automated, and self-improving process supervision mechanism that is more effective and efficient than prior art.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is centered around an **iterative self-training framework** that leverages Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to automatically generate high-quality process supervision data, which is then used to fine-tune the LLM. This "generate-and-fine-tune" cycle is repeated until convergence.

**Overall Framework:**
The process begins with an initial LLM (a pre-trained model). In each iteration:
1. **Data Generation:** The current LLM is used to generate a dataset of reasoning problems, partial solutions, candidate next steps, and "relative correctness" scores for these steps. MCTS is the core mechanism for this generation.
2. **Model Training:** The LLM is then fine-tuned using this newly generated, scored dataset.
This improved LLM becomes the base for data generation in the subsequent iteration, creating a self-reinforcing learning loop.

**Detailed Data Generation Process (The Core Innovation):**

For each problem `x_i` from a given dataset `P`, the authors treat each individual step in a problem-solving process as a "tree node." MCTS is applied *at each juncture* (or partial solution `p_i_j`) along a potential reasoning path to explore and evaluate possible "next steps."

The MCTS procedure for a given partial solution `p_i_j` involves four standard phases, adapted for reasoning:

1. **Selection:** Starting from the current partial solution node `p_i_j`, the algorithm selects child nodes by maximizing the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) value. This balance exploration (nodes less visited) and exploitation (nodes with higher average rewards) until an unexpanded node or a final solution step is reached.
2. **Expansion:** If the selected node is not a final step and has unexpanded children, the LLM is prompted to sample a *new next step* with a non-zero temperature (to encourage diversity). This new step becomes a child node in the search tree.
3. **Simulation (Rollout):** From the newly expanded node, the LLM continues to generate the remainder of the solution (subsequent steps) until a final answer is produced. This simulation acts as an estimate of the "quality" of the path starting from this new step.
4. **Backpropagation:** The final answer from the simulation is compared against the ground truth answer for the problem. A binary reward (1.0 for correct, 0.0 for incorrect) is propagated back up through all visited nodes in the MCTS tree, updating their visit counts and cumulative rewards.

**Scoring of Reasoning Steps (Key Differentiator):**

After repeating the MCTS procedure multiple times (performing many "playouts" or simulations), a search tree is constructed. For each child node `v_i,j,k` (representing a candidate next step `s_i,j,k` from the current partial solution `p_i_j`), a numerical score `r_i,j,k` is computed. This score is designed to capture the "relative correctness" of that specific step, distinguishing it from binary preferences (chosen/rejected) used in prior work:

`r_i,j,k = α * N(v_i,j,k) * (Q(v_i,j,k)/N(v_i,j,k) - (Σ_m Q(v_i,j,m) / Σ_m N(v_i,j,m)))`

Where:
- `Q(v)`: Cumulative reward of node `v` (sum of rewards from simulations passing through `v`).
- `N(v)`: Visit count of node `v`.
- `Q(v)/N(v)`: Average reward (win rate) of node `v`.
- `(Σ_m Q(v_i,j,m) / Σ_m N(v_i,j,m))`: Average reward of *all* sibling nodes (other candidate next steps from `p_i_j`).
- `α`: A manually set constant to control the scale of the scores.

The term in parentheses `(average_reward_of_node - average_reward_of_siblings)` quantifies how much better (or worse) a particular next step is compared to the average of all other explored next steps from that juncture. This difference, scaled by `α` and `N(v)` (emphasizing well-explored, high-performing steps), provides a nuanced "relative correctness" score. Only steps with scores greater than zero are included in the generated training dataset.

Finally, after scoring the next steps from a given `p_i_j`, the step with the highest UCB value is selected, appended to `p_i_j` to form a new, longer partial solution, and the MCTS process restarts for this new state. This continues until a final answer is reached or a maximum solution length is hit.

**Iterative Training (Fine-tuning):**

The generated dataset `D={(x_i, p_i_j, s_i_j, r_i_j)}` (problem, partial solution, next step, its score) is then used to fine-tune the LLM from the previous iteration. The training objective is to minimize a loss function:

`L(π_θ_i) = -E_{(x,p,s,r)∼D_i} [r * log π_θ_i(s|x,p)] + D_KL(π_θ_i(s|x,p) || π_θ_i-1(s|x,p))`

Where:
- `π_θ_i`: The LLM at the `i`-th iteration.
- **Weighted Negative Log-Likelihood:** The first term is a weighted negative log-likelihood. It trains the LLM to increase the probability of generating a step `s` given the problem `x` and partial solution `p`. Crucially, this term is weighted by the generated score `r`. This means steps that were deemed "more correct" or "relatively better" by MCTS receive a higher weight in the loss, guiding the model towards generating better reasoning steps.
- **KL Penalty:** The second term is a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty between the current model `π_θ_i` and the model from the previous iteration `π_θ_i-1`. Inspired by Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), this term acts as a regularizer, preventing the new model from drifting too far from its previous iteration, which helps mitigate distribution shift and stabilize training.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Models:** Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and deepseek-math-7b-instruct.
- **Datasets:** GSM8K (grade school math problems) and MATH (high school math competition problems).
- **Baselines:**
 - Zero-shot-CoT (Kojima et al.)
 - Rejective Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) (Yuan et al.)
 - Step-level DPO (a baseline mirroring MCTS-DPO, using DPO on best/worst steps for preference learning).
- **Efficiency:** All models are trained using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for efficient fine-tuning.

This comprehensive methodology allows for an automated, self-improving cycle that provides fine-grained, quantitative feedback on reasoning steps, aiming to instill robust reasoning abilities into LLMs without the prohibitive costs of human annotation or the inherent limitations of binary preference learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in enhancing the mathematical reasoning capabilities of LLMs on the chosen benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance on Reasoning Benchmarks

- **Consistent Outperformance:** The proposed method consistently and considerably outperforms all baselines (Zero-shot-CoT, RFT, and Step-level DPO) on both the MATH and GSM8K datasets.
- **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct Improvements:**
 - On MATH, the model improved from a Zero-shot-CoT accuracy of 47.07% to a peak of 51.92% with the proposed method (Iteration 4).
 - On GSM8K, the model improved from 80.77% (Zero-shot-CoT) to 85.80% (Iteration 2).
- **Deepseek-math-7b-instruct Improvements:**
 - On MATH, the model improved from 41.20% (Zero-shot-CoT) to 44.57% (Iteration 3).
 - On GSM8K, the model improved from 78.79% (Zero-shot-CoT) to 82.02% (Iteration 2).

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Iterative Training

- The accuracies generally improved with each successive iteration of the generate-then-train process, demonstrating the cumulative benefits of the self-supervision loop.
- **Convergence and Limitations:** The performance, however, converges relatively quickly. For instance, on GSM8K, significant improvements are seen up to iteration 2, with diminishing returns thereafter. On MATH, improvements extend to iteration 3 or 4. The authors note that using more problems for training (beyond approximately 2,000 for each dataset) or training for many more iterations did not lead to further improvements and, in some cases, even degraded performance. This highlights a critical limitation and an area for future research. The faster convergence on GSM8K is attributed to its lower difficulty.

#### 4.3. Superiority of the Data Generation Method

- A key finding is the significant superiority of the proposed MCTS-based scoring method over the "Step-level DPO" baseline. Step-level DPO, which uses binary preference pairs (best vs. worst steps), achieved only very marginal improvements on the MATH dataset. This strongly supports the authors' hypothesis that assigning *nuanced, relative correctness scores* to steps and leveraging *all* generated steps in a weighted loss function provides a much richer and more effective signal for training compared to simpler binary preference methods.

#### 4.4. Transferability of Enhanced Reasoning Ability

- The study also conducted transfer experiments, training models on one dataset (e.g., MATH) and testing them on the other (e.g., GSM8K) to ascertain if the learned reasoning ability was generalizable.
- **Positive Transfer:** Models trained with the proposed method consistently outperformed Zero-shot-CoT even on unseen datasets. For example, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct trained on GSM8K achieved 48.74% on MATH (vs. 47.07% Zero-shot), and when trained on MATH achieved 85.72% on GSM8K (vs. 80.77% Zero-shot).
- **Domain Specificity:** As expected, the improvements in transfer experiments were less substantial than those observed when training and testing on the same dataset. This is attributed to the inherent differences in mathematical skills and knowledge required by high school (MATH) vs. grade school (GSM8K) problems, indicating that while reasoning ability is enhanced, some domain-specific knowledge might still be beneficial. Nevertheless, the positive transfer confirms that the models indeed learned generalized mathematical reasoning capabilities, not just memorized solutions to specific problem types.

In conclusion, the main findings strongly validate the proposed methodology, demonstrating its ability to significantly enhance LLM reasoning through efficient, automated process supervision and iterative self-training, while also highlighting areas for future research regarding training stability and scalability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented by Li et al. offers several significant contributions and holds considerable potential impact across the field of Large Language Models and their applications.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Automated Process Supervision

The most significant contribution is the development of a novel and effective method for automatically generating process supervision data without human intervention. This addresses a critical bottleneck in training highly capable reasoning models. By using MCTS to assign nuanced "relative correctness" scores to intermediate reasoning steps, the method provides a much richer and more informative signal than binary outcome supervision or simple chosen/rejected preferences. This approach makes process supervision scalable and affordable, opening doors for its wider application beyond academic benchmarks.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Reasoning Abilities in LLMs

The demonstrated improvements on challenging mathematical reasoning datasets (MATH and GSM8K) are substantial, particularly for relatively smaller LLMs (7B and 8B parameters). This is crucial because smaller models are more computationally efficient for deployment in real-world applications. The method effectively instills deeper reasoning capabilities, moving beyond mere pattern matching to more robust, step-by-step problem-solving.

#### 5.3. Robust Self-Improvement Loop

The iterative "generate-then-train" framework highlights the power of self-supervised learning for complex tasks. LLMs can effectively generate their own training data, identify higher-quality reasoning paths, and then learn from these refined examples. This self-correction and self-improvement mechanism holds promise for reducing dependence on large, manually curated datasets, potentially accelerating AI development.

#### 5.4. Practical Implications and Accessibility

- **Reduced Annotation Costs:** By eliminating the need for expensive human annotation, the method significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing reasoning-capable LLMs. This can democratize access to advanced AI capabilities, allowing smaller research groups or companies (like Giant Network itself) to train powerful models.
- **Deployment Efficiency:** The focus on improving 7B/8B models, combined with the use of LoRA for efficient fine-tuning, makes the enhanced reasoning capabilities much more practical for real-world deployment where computational resources and latency are constraints.
- **Wider Applications:** The improved mathematical reasoning ability can transfer to other complex, multi-step problem-solving domains. Potential applications include:
 - **Education:** Development of intelligent tutoring systems that can not only provide answers but also guide students through correct reasoning steps.
 - **Science and Engineering:** Assisting researchers in areas requiring complex calculations, logical deductions (e.g., drug discovery, materials science).
 - **Software Development:** More robust code generation, debugging, and verification tools.
 - **General AI Assistants:** Creating more reliable and trustworthy AI assistants capable of handling complex queries and planning.

#### 5.5. Guiding Future Research

The paper also provides valuable insights for future research. The observation that performance converges quickly and can even degrade with too many iterations or too much data raises important questions about optimal training schedules, curriculum learning, and the potential for "over-supervision" or catastrophic forgetting in self-training loops. Understanding the underlying reasons for this phenomenon could lead to more stable and continuously improving self-training frameworks. Furthermore, the success of LoRA suggests that exploring full fine-tuning or alternative parameter-efficient methods might yield even greater improvements.

In essence, this work presents a pragmatic yet powerful advancement in the quest for truly intelligent LLMs, offering a pathway to more robust reasoning capabilities through an elegant, automated, and self-improving mechanism.
