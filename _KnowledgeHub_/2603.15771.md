# CorrectionPlanner: Self-Correction Planner with Reinforcement Learning in Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Johns Hopkins University and XPENG Motors developed CorrectionPlanner, an autonomous driving system that integrates an explicit self-correction mechanism inspired by LLM reasoning. This system reduced collision rates by over 20% in Waymax reactive simulations and achieved state-of-the-art planning scores on the nuPlan dataset by iteratively refining unsafe motion proposals.

## Problem

- Learning-based autonomous driving planners often lack an inherent mechanism to evaluate and correct potentially unsafe actions before they are executed.
- Once an unsafe action is proposed, existing systems typically lack a pathway for internal revision, which can lead to the execution of hazardous trajectories.
- Imitation learning alone, based on expert data, is insufficient to teach planners how to handle unseen collision scenarios or effectively revise unsafe actions.

## Method

- Introduced **CorrectionPlanner**, an ==autoregressive self-correction mechanism== for autonomous driving planning that operates at the ==motion token level==.
- Developed a =="propose, evaluate, and correct" loop== where a learned ==critic== identifies unsafe motion proposals, which are then added to a =="correction trace"== to inform subsequent revised actions.
- Employed a ==two-stage training scheme== combining initial ==imitation learning== with ==model-based reinforcement learning==, utilizing a ==reactive multi-agent world model== for realistic rollouts.

## Results

- Reduced collision rates by over **20%** compared to the best baseline (SMART) in Waymax reactive simulations (from **2.36%** to **1.68%**), a significant safety improvement.
- Achieved consistently better overall planning scores across most nuPlan splits, outperforming various baselines and demonstrating state-of-the-art performance while maintaining competitive progression rates.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the explicit self-correction loop, particularly the use of the correction trace, was responsible for the primary safety gains, surpassing the performance of rejection sampling and candidate selection methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An explicit self-correction mechanism, adapted from LLM reasoning to the motion-token space, can significantly enhance safety by allowing autonomous driving planners to revise unsafe actions pre-execution.
> - Leveraging a "correction trace" (a history of rejected unsafe tokens) enables the policy to learn from past failures and shift its subsequent proposals away from unsafe regions more effectively than simple rejection sampling.
> - Combining imitation learning with model-based reinforcement learning and a reactive world model is crucial for teaching proactive self-correction and exploring counterfactual unsafe scenarios not present in expert data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous driving systems require robust and safe decision-making capabilities, particularly in complex, long-horizon planning scenarios. Current learning-based planners often excel at learning rich scene context and predicting ego actions or trajectories. However, a common limitation of many such models is the absence of an inherent mechanism to evaluate and correct potentially unsafe actions *before* they are executed. Once an unsafe action is proposed, these systems typically lack a pathway for internal revision.

This work positions itself by drawing an analogy to advancements in large language models (LLMs). Recent research in LLMs has demonstrated that explicit reasoning, self-reflection, and iterative refinement within a language space can significantly enhance a model's reliability and mitigate the generation of unsafe or erroneous outputs. The paper identifies a gap: while LLMs perform this "reasoning" in language, autonomous driving systems require an equivalent mechanism in the physical domain.

Existing trajectory planning methods, including those based on continuous motion distributions, diffusion models, and autoregressive approaches, have developed techniques for representation learning and simulation performance. However, the authors argue that these methods generally do not incorporate pre-execution reflection on predicted actions. Reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied in autonomous driving for post-training or fine-tuning to improve performance beyond expert data, but typically without specifically endowing models with explicit self-correction capabilities in the context of unsafe action sequences.

The "CorrectionPlanner" aims to bridge this gap by introducing an autoregressive self-correction mechanism for autonomous driving planning. Instead of relying on post-hoc filtering, rule-based candidate selection, or natural language reasoning, this research proposes self-correction at the *motion token* level. This approach seeks to provide an explicit internal loop for evaluating and revising unsafe motion proposals, thereby enhancing safety and robustness, particularly in interactive multi-agent environments where the ego vehicle's actions can influence other agents' behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an autonomous driving planner capable of explicit self-correction prior to action execution, thereby improving safety in complex driving scenarios. The authors are motivated by the observation that most current learning-based planners, despite their ability to learn intricate scene contexts, operate without an internal mechanism to assess the safety of a proposed action and revise it if deemed unsafe. This can lead to the execution of hazardous trajectories.

Specifically, the key objectives and underlying motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Introduce an explicit self-correction mechanism:** The central motivation is to overcome the limitation of existing planners that propose actions without internal safety checks. Inspired by the success of self-reflection and reasoning in LLMs, the authors aim to adapt this concept to autonomous driving. Unlike LLMs that reason in language space, CorrectionPlanner is designed to perform self-correction at the motion token level, which represents discretized trajectory segments.
- **Enhance planning safety by reducing collision rates:** The ultimate goal is to directly address the critical safety requirements of autonomous driving. By enabling the planner to detect and correct unsafe motion proposals before execution, the work seeks to reduce collision rates in simulated environments. This is motivated by the understanding that a robust planning system must prioritize safety.
- **Develop a "propose, evaluate, and correct" loop:** The authors intend to formalize the self-correction process into an iterative loop. This involves the policy proposing an action (motion token), a learned critic evaluating its safety, and if unsafe, the policy revising the proposal based on a historical record of rejected actions. This structured approach aims to provide a systematic way for the planner to learn from its own unsafe proposals.
- **Leverage a "correction trace" for informed revision:** A key motivation is to make the self-correction process intelligent rather than merely random resampling. The proposed "correction trace," a sequence of historically rejected unsafe motion tokens, is designed to serve as the planner's internal "reasoning process" in motion-token space. This mechanism aims to allow the planner to update its subsequent proposals based on past failures, leading to more effective and context-aware corrections.
- **Design a two-stage training scheme combining imitation learning and reinforcement learning:** The authors recognize that imitation learning (IL) alone, based on expert data, might not adequately teach a planner how to handle unseen collision scenarios or how to effectively revise unsafe actions. Therefore, a two-stage training approach is motivated:
 - IL provides a foundation from expert trajectories.
 - Model-based reinforcement learning (RL) is used to explicitly train the self-correction ability, allowing the policy to explore and learn from counterfactual scenarios that are not present in expert demonstrations.
- **Utilize a reactive multi-agent world model for realistic RL rollouts:** A significant challenge in RL for autonomous driving is simulating realistic agent behaviors in response to ego vehicle actions. Traditional replay-based or pre-prediction methods are often non-reactive. The motivation here is to employ a pre-trained, reactive world model that can realistically simulate how other agents would respond to the ego's maneuvers, including its self-corrections. This ensures that the RL training occurs in a dynamic and interactive environment, which is crucial for learning robust safety behaviors.
- **Achieve competitive or state-of-the-art planning performance:** While safety is paramount, the planner also needs to maintain efficiency and overall driving competence. The objective is to demonstrate that the enhanced safety through self-correction does not come at the cost of significant degradation in progression or other planning metrics, ultimately aiming for state-of-the-art performance in standard benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CorrectionPlanner integrates an autoregressive motion planner with an explicit self-correction mechanism, employing a two-stage training methodology involving imitation learning and model-based reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries and System Modeling

Autonomous driving planning is framed as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state space includes ego vehicle information, surrounding agents' states, and map/navigation data. Actions are defined as next motion tokens. The environment dynamics are modeled by a transition kernel `P`, and a reward function `r` guides policy learning. A key aspect is the use of a learned world model, `Pˆ`, to reactively simulate multi-agent behaviors, addressing the challenge of realistic simulations for RL.

#### 3.2. Learned World Model for Multi-Agent Dynamics

The world model is built upon SMART (Scalable Multi-Agent Real-Time Motion Generation via Next-Token Prediction), an autoregressive multi-agent world model.

- **Tokenization:** Continuous trajectories are discretized into motion tokens. This involves segmenting trajectories into 0.5-second intervals and clustering these segments using a K-disk algorithm to create a discrete vocabulary of 1024 tokens. Each token represents a one-step change in position and heading, framing planning as a next-token generation problem.
- **Architecture:** The world model employs a Transformer decoder with spatio-temporal attention. It fuses motion tokens with vehicle information (speed, size). Its architecture includes:
 - Temporal self-attention for motion continuity.
 - Agent-map cross-attention for spatial constraints and adherence to road networks.
 - Agent-agent cross-attention for modeling interactions between agents.
 Relative spatio-temporal position embeddings ensure rotation and translation invariance. The ego and non-ego agents share the same encoder structure and token vocabulary.
- **Loss:** The world model is trained using a next-token prediction objective with cross-entropy loss across all agents. Once trained, this world model is frozen and serves as a reactive simulator for the subsequent RL training.

#### 3.3. Policy Network

The policy network, responsible for generating ego motions, shares a similar Transformer-based architecture with the world model, but specifically models the ego's behavior and incorporates navigation information.

- **Architecture:** It consists of stacked cross-attention modules:
 - Route/navigation cross-attention.
 - Temporal self-attention over the ego trajectory.
 - Ego-map cross-attention.
 - Ego-agent interaction layer.
 An MLP prediction head outputs a motion token distribution, from which an ego motion is sampled.
- **Correction Tokens:** Unsafe intermediate motions proposed during self-correction are not executed but are retained as "correction tokens" in an input sequence called the "correction trace." This trace is encoded by a self-attention layer to condition subsequent proposals.

#### 3.4. Two-Stage Training Scheme

**Stage 1: Imitation Learning (IL)**

- **Objective:** The policy is initially trained via next-token prediction on expert trajectories, minimizing cross-entropy loss.
- **Self-Correction in IL:** Even in the IL phase, an initial form of self-correction is introduced. If a policy proposal leads to a collision *at the current timestep*, corrected tokens are generated iteratively. The policy retains previous unsafe tokens in a correction trace, encoding them with self-attention, and generates new tokens conditioned on this trace. The objective for correction in IL is to minimize cross-entropy between these proposed actions (in the correction trace) and the expert trajectories, aiming to provide preliminary self-correction ability.
- **Limitations of IL Self-Correction:** IL only addresses current collisions and cannot simulate future counterfactuals. This motivates the need for RL.

**Stage 2: Self-Correction with Reinforcement Learning (RL)**

This stage focuses on further training the policy and a collision critic to enable proactive correction of unsafe motions.

- **Rule-based Reward:** A simplified reward function is used: `r = Progression Rate * I(Collision = 0) - I(Collision = 1)`. This incentivizes progress while heavily penalizing collisions.
- **Rollouts with World Model:** To enable learning beyond expert data, rollouts are generated using the frozen, pre-trained reactive world model.
 - At each planning step, the ego policy proposes a motion token.
 - The **collision critic** (described below) evaluates the collision risk within a future horizon `k`.
 - If predicted unsafe, the token is rejected and added to the **correction trace**.
 - The policy then autoregressively generates a revised motion token, conditioned on the scene history and the *entire* correction trace. This process repeats until a safe token is found or a maximum correction length is reached.
 - The final accepted token is executed in the world model, which then simulates reactive agent behaviors. These rollouts include both executed ego tokens and intermediate correction tokens.
- **Policy Optimization:** The policy is optimized to maximize the trajectory reward using a REINFORCE objective, regularized by a KL divergence penalty to prevent excessive deviation from the IL policy. Policy gradient updates are applied only to the *executed* token, as the trajectory-level reward is attributed to this final action. This trains the policy to generate a safe token given the cumulative context of the correction trace.

#### 3.5. Training the Collision Critic

A binary classifier (critic) is trained to predict future collisions for both RL training and inference.

- **Architecture:** The critic uses two attention blocks and an MLP prediction head. Each block includes temporal self-attention over ego and agent trajectories, and an ego-agent interaction layer.
- **Training Objective:** It is trained to minimize cross-entropy loss, predicting whether a collision will occur within `k` future planning steps based on rollout trajectory data generated by the policy and world model. During critic training, only executed tokens (not correction traces) are used.

#### 3.6. Comparison with Rejection Sampling

The self-correction mechanism is differentiated from simple rejection sampling. While rejection sampling rejects unsafe tokens and resamples from the *original* distribution, CorrectionPlanner leverages the correction trace to generate a *new* conditional distribution, allowing the policy to explicitly learn from past failures and shift its subsequent proposals away from unsafe regions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CorrectionPlanner was conducted using the Waymax simulator on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD) and on the nuPlan dataset, covering both reactive and non-reactive agent behaviors.

#### 4.1. Waymax Simulation Results

- **Collision Rate Reduction:** CorrectionPlanner achieved the lowest collision rate across both reactive and non-reactive settings. In reactive mode, it reduced collision rates by over 20% compared to the best baseline (SMART), lowering it from 2.36% to 1.68%. In non-reactive mode, it reduced collision rates from 3.02% (PlanT) to 2.43%.
- **Off-Road Performance:** The method demonstrated competitive off-road performance, closely matching the SMART baseline. This suggests the effectiveness of its spatio-attention mechanism in modeling map-ego relationships and ensuring adherence to drivable areas.
- **Progression Rate:** While LatentDrive achieved the highest progression, CorrectionPlanner maintained a comparable progression rate (e.g., 94.23% vs 98.70% for LatentDrive in reactive mode), suggesting a favorable safety-efficiency trade-off where improved safety is prioritized. The authors noted that progression might slightly decrease due to scenarios where the planner slows down or yields for safety, which they consider acceptable expert-like behavior.

#### 4.2. NuPlan Simulation Results

- **Overall Planning Score:** CorrectionPlanner demonstrated consistently better overall planning performance across most Val14, Test-Hard, and Test-Random splits in both non-reactive and reactive settings, outperforming baselines like PDM-Open, UrbanDriver, PlanTF, PLUTO, DiffusionPlanner, and SMART. For instance, on Val14 NR, it achieved a score of 91.22 compared to SMART's 90.03.
- **Reactive Mode Improvement:** The method showed more pronounced improvements in reactive mode scenarios compared to non-reactive, consistent with Waymax results. This suggests its advantage in handling interactive traffic dynamics.
- **Collision vs. Progression Trade-off:** Specifically comparing against SMART on Val14 NR, CorrectionPlanner achieved a lower collision rate (2.04% vs 2.70%) with a slightly higher TTC (92.25 vs 91.79) while maintaining similar performance on other metrics like drivable area, comfort, and speed. The progression rate was slightly lower (91.49% vs 91.68%), confirming the intentional safety-progression trade-off.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Results

Visualizations illustrated the self-correction mechanism in action:
- In scenarios involving left turns, the ego vehicle would adjust to a slightly wider turn or maintain speed to avoid collisions, particularly with right-turning vehicles.
- During lane changes, the self-correction mechanism would modify aggressive actions to prevent collisions.
- In situations where a slow left turn might lead to a collision, the ego would adjust speed to avoid braking and maintain a safer trajectory.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Comparison with Training Frameworks:**
 - IL (Imitation Learning) without RL or self-correction showed the highest collision rates.
 - IL with self-correction (IL w/ corr.) provided only marginal collision reduction, indicating IL's limited ability to teach effective self-correction.
 - RL without self-correction (RL w/o corr.) improved collision rates over IL, showing the benefit of RL training.
 - CorrectionPlanner (w/o corr.), which uses the proposed policy but disables self-correction during inference, showed comparable performance to pure RL, suggesting the policy itself is stronger with RL.
 - CorrectionPlanner (with self-correction enabled) achieved the lowest collision rates, confirming that the explicit self-correction loop is responsible for the primary safety gains, not solely a stronger underlying policy.
- **Comparison with Sampling Frameworks:**
 - **Rejection Sampling:** This baseline, which resamples from the original distribution when an unsafe token is detected, showed limited collision reduction. This is attributed to repeated actions from the same distribution tending to remain in similar, unsafe regions.
 - **Candidate Selection:** This method, which samples multiple trajectories and selects the safest/highest progression one, also underperformed CorrectionPlanner.
 - These results collectively support the hypothesis that the *correction trace*, by explicitly conditioning subsequent proposals on past failures, enables the policy to shift its distribution and escape collision-prone regions, which simple resampling or candidate selection cannot achieve.
- **Effect of Collision Classification Threshold and Correction Length:**
 - **Threshold:** A lower critic threshold (e.g., 0.7) led to higher recall (detecting more collisions) but lower precision, triggering self-correction too early and frequently. This resulted in significant progression loss despite very low collision rates. A higher threshold (e.g., 0.8) led to high precision but low recall, missing many potential collisions and providing insufficient time for correction. An intermediate threshold (e.g., 0.75) provided a balanced trade-off in precision/recall and trigger timing, yielding optimal safety-progression balance.
 - **Correction Length:** Generally, a longer maximum correction length (i.e., more iterative refinement steps) reduced collisions, demonstrating the increasing effectiveness of self-correction with a larger "budget." However, excessively long correction lengths did not yield further performance gains beyond a certain point (e.g., 5 steps), and could lead to the planner getting stuck or becoming overly conservative.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** A policy trained on the nuPlan dataset, when evaluated on the Waymax simulator, demonstrated improved collision performance with self-correction enabled ("Zero Shot (w/ corr.)" outperformed "Zero Shot (w/o corr.)"). This indicates the method's ability to generalize self-correction behaviors to unseen environments.
- **Latency:** The inference latency of CorrectionPlanner with its best-performing hyperparameters (0.75 threshold, 5 correction length) was 0.434s per trajectory, which is comparable to the base policy (0.357s) and baselines (SMART at 0.329s). This indicates that the safety improvements are achieved without a significant increase in computational overhead during inference.
- **Hyperparameter `k` for Critic Training:** Increasing `k` (the number of future planning steps for which the critic predicts collision) from 1 to 5 consistently reduced collisions. However, `k=7` showed only marginal further collision reduction while substantially decreasing progression, suggesting that predicting too far ahead can lead to overly conservative behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CorrectionPlanner research contributes a novel approach to enhancing the safety of autonomous driving systems by integrating an explicit self-correction mechanism within a learning-based planner. This work addresses a critical limitation of many existing data-driven planners, which often lack the ability to internally evaluate and revise unsafe action proposals before execution.

The primary significance lies in demonstrating that a "propose, evaluate, and correct" loop operating at the motion-token level can effectively reduce collision rates. By drawing inspiration from self-reflection and reasoning in large language models and adapting it to the physical world of motion planning, the paper introduces the concept of a "correction trace" as a form of internal reasoning for autonomous vehicles. This mechanism allows the planner to iteratively refine unsafe actions based on a history of rejected proposals, leading to more robust decision-making.

The empirical results, particularly the over 20% reduction in collision rates on the Waymax simulator and state-of-the-art planning scores on nuPlan, indicate a substantial improvement in safety performance while maintaining competitive progression rates. This suggests a favorable safety-efficiency trade-off, which is crucial for real-world autonomous driving deployment.

The two-stage training scheme, combining imitation learning with model-based reinforcement learning using a reactive multi-agent world model, is also a notable methodological contribution. This approach enables the policy to learn from realistic, interactive rollouts and develop effective corrective behaviors that might not be observable in expert demonstrations alone. The demonstration of zero-shot generalization further highlights the potential scalability and practical applicability of the method.

The low additional inference latency incurred by the self-correction mechanism, even with optimal safety-oriented hyperparameters, suggests that these safety improvements can be implemented without significantly impacting real-time operational requirements.

Beyond autonomous driving, the core concept of a motion-token-level self-correction with a correction trace could be extended to other safety-critical robotics applications where actions have direct physical consequences and iterative refinement is beneficial. The work thus presents a promising direction for bridging the gap between advanced reasoning capabilities seen in AI models and robust, safe decision-making in complex physical environments.
