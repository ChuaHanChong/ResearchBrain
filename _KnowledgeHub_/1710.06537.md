# Sim-to-Real Transfer of Robotic Control with Dynamics Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from OpenAI and UC Berkeley demonstrates a method for transferring robotic control policies trained entirely in simulation to physical robots. It combines extensive dynamics randomization during training with recurrent neural network policies, enabling a Fetch robot to successfully perform a puck-pushing task in the real world with a 0.89 success rate, closely matching simulated performance, and showing robustness to unmodeled changes.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DeepRL) policies trained in simulation struggle to transfer to physical robots due to the "reality gap" from discrepancies between simulated and real-world dynamics.
- Training DeepRL agents directly on physical robots is impractical due to high sample complexity, requiring millions of interactions, and significant safety concerns.
- Existing simulation-to-real transfer methods often require some real-world data for fine-tuning or adaptation, or rely on highly accurate, difficult-to-achieve simulator calibration.

## Method

- Train reinforcement learning policies across a wide distribution of randomized physical parameters in simulation (==dynamics randomization==), including masses, frictions, damping, controller gains, action latency, and observation noise.
- Employ a ==recurrent neural network (LSTM)== for the policy architecture, allowing it to maintain an internal memory and implicitly adapt to the inferred real-world dynamics during execution.
- Utilize an ==omniscient critic== (aware of current dynamics parameters) during off-policy training with ==Recurrent DDPG (RDPG)== and ==Hindsight Experience Replay (HER)== to stabilize learning despite high dynamics variability.

## Results

- Policies trained exclusively in simulation achieved a **0.89 ± 0.06** success rate on a real Fetch robot for a puck-pushing task, comparable to their **0.91 ± 0.03** success rate in simulation.
- Recurrent LSTM policies significantly outperformed feedforward networks in real-world transfer, with LSTM achieving **0.89** success rate versus **0.67** for a feedforward network with randomization.
- Randomizing action timestep and observation noise was critical for transfer, as fixing these parameters during training severely reduced real-world success rates to below **0.3**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Comprehensive randomization of multiple dynamics parameters (e.g., mass, friction, latency, observation noise) is crucial for robust sim-to-real transfer.
> - Recurrent neural networks (LSTMs) enable policies to implicitly infer and adapt to unknown real-world dynamics at runtime, outperforming feedforward networks.
> - The method demonstrates remarkable robustness to unmodeled dynamics, such as significant changes in object properties, without requiring explicit modeling or fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. The Reality Gap and Challenges

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DeepRL) has demonstrated remarkable success in complex simulated environments, achieving human-level performance in games and intricate control tasks. However, transferring these learned behaviors from simulation to the real world remains a significant hurdle, commonly referred to as the "reality gap." This gap arises primarily from the inherent mismatch between the simplified, idealized models used in simulation and the complex, noisy, and unmodeled dynamics of physical systems.

The challenges associated with training DeepRL agents directly on physical robots are substantial:
- **High Sample Complexity:** DeepRL algorithms often require millions, if not billions, of interactions with the environment to learn effective policies. Collecting such vast amounts of data in the real world is prohibitively time-consuming, expensive, and resource-intensive.
- **Safety Concerns:** During the exploratory phase of reinforcement learning, an agent may perform unpredictable or dangerous actions that could damage the robot, its surroundings, or injure human operators. This limits the types of tasks and the scale of exploration feasible in real-world setups.
- **Modeling Errors and Unmodeled Dynamics:** Even the most sophisticated physics simulators are approximations of reality. Factors like friction, elasticity, complex contact dynamics, sensor noise, latency in actuators, and varying material properties are difficult to model precisely and can differ significantly between simulation and reality.

These challenges have historically limited the widespread application of DeepRL to complex robotic tasks in the physical world, often relegating successful demonstrations to highly constrained or carefully calibrated environments.

#### 1.2. Existing Approaches to Sim-to-Real Transfer

Researchers have explored several strategies to bridge the reality gap:
- **High-Fidelity Simulation:** One approach is to invest heavily in building increasingly accurate and detailed simulators that closely mimic real-world physics. While this reduces the gap, it is computationally expensive, requires extensive calibration, and can never perfectly replicate all real-world complexities.
- **Direct Real-World Training (with enhancements):** Methods like Guided Policy Search (GPS) and parallelized training across multiple robots have enabled learning policies directly on physical systems, but often require specialized setups, careful reward shaping, or are limited to relatively simple tasks.
- **Domain Adaptation:** This field focuses on transferring models trained in a "source" domain (simulation) to a "target" domain (real world). Techniques include:
 - **Feature Invariance:** Learning representations that are consistent across domains, often using pairwise constraints or adversarial losses.
 - **Progressive Networks:** Reusing and augmenting features learned in simulation to reduce the data needed for real-world fine-tuning.
 - **Inverse Dynamics Models:** Training an inverse dynamics model from real-world data to help with policy transfer. A common limitation of these methods is the continued requirement for *some* real-world data during the training or adaptation phase.
- **System Identification:** Explicitly modeling or learning the unknown parameters of the physical system (e.g., mass, friction) and then either adapting the policy based on these identified parameters or incorporating them into the control loop. This approach often requires prior knowledge of the relevant parameters.

#### 1.3. The Role of Dynamics Randomization

Dynamics randomization is a complementary and increasingly popular approach for domain adaptation, particularly well-suited for simulation-to-real transfer. Instead of trying to precisely match simulation to reality, this method embraces the uncertainty. It involves randomizing various physical parameters of the simulator during training (e.g., mass, friction, damping, sensor noise, control latency) within plausible ranges. The core idea is that if a policy can learn to perform a task robustly across a *wide distribution* of simulated dynamics, it will implicitly learn to be robust to the unmodeled or mismatched dynamics of the real world, without ever needing to interact with the real system during training.

Previous work on domain randomization primarily focused on visual randomization for vision-based policies, or used dynamics randomization to develop robust *offline* controllers. Antonova et al. used dynamics randomization for manipulation policies, but their policies were memoryless, limiting adaptation. This paper builds upon this foundation by combining dynamics randomization with **recurrent neural network policies** and **on-policy learning**, allowing for explicit adaptation to unknown dynamics *at runtime* through the policy's internal memory. This distinguishes it from prior work by enabling a more adaptive and generalizable solution that can cope with significant calibration errors and unmodeled real-world variations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for effectively transferring robotic control policies trained exclusively in simulation to a physical robot, thereby bridging the "reality gap" without requiring any real-world training or fine-tuning.

The key motivations driving this objective are:
- **Overcoming DeepRL's Sample Complexity:** Modern DeepRL algorithms demand vast amounts of data, which is impractical and unsafe to collect on physical robots. Training in simulation provides an abundant and safe data source.
- **Addressing Safety Concerns:** By keeping the entire training process in simulation, the risks associated with an exploratory agent performing dangerous actions on a real physical system are eliminated.
- **Robustness to Modeling Errors:** Acknowledging that perfect simulation fidelity is elusive, the goal is to develop policies that are inherently robust to the inevitable discrepancies between simulated and real-world dynamics. Instead of meticulously calibrating the simulator, the authors aim for a policy that can adapt to *different* dynamics, including the real robot's.
- **Enabling Broader Deployment of DeepRL in Robotics:** If policies can be reliably transferred from sim-to-real, it unlocks the potential for applying powerful DeepRL techniques, which have shown great promise in simulated domains, to a much wider range of complex and dynamic real-world robotic tasks.

In essence, the paper aims to demonstrate that by purposefully varying the simulation's dynamics during training, an agent can learn adaptive behaviors that generalize robustly to the complexities of the real world, making deep reinforcement learning a more viable tool for practical robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core idea proposed by the authors is to train a policy that is robust to variations in environment dynamics by extensively randomizing physical parameters within the simulation during the training process. This approach is combined with advanced deep reinforcement learning techniques to enable implicit adaptation.

#### 3.1. Core Principle: Dynamics Randomization

Instead of attempting to create a perfect, high-fidelity simulation, the authors propose training the policy across a *distribution* of dynamics models. At the beginning of each training episode, a new set of dynamics parameters (e.g., mass, friction, damping, controller gains, action timestep, observation noise) is sampled from a predefined range. The policy is then trained to succeed under this randomly chosen set of dynamics for the entire episode. By doing this repeatedly across millions of training steps, the policy learns to be highly adaptive to a wide spectrum of physical properties, including those that might closely resemble the real world, even if the real-world parameters are unknown or poorly calibrated. The paper randomizes 95 parameters in total, including:
- Mass of each link in the robot's body
- Damping of each joint
- Mass, friction, and damping of the puck
- Height of the table
- Gains for the position controller
- Timestep between actions (as a model of latency)
- Observation noise (Gaussian noise applied to state features)

#### 3.2. Adaptive Policy Architecture

To enable runtime adaptation to unknown dynamics, the policy needs a mechanism to infer the environment's properties from its observations. The authors achieve this implicitly by using a **recurrent neural network (RNN)**, specifically a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) unit, for the policy.
- The LSTM maintains an internal memory (`z_t`) that summarizes the history of past states and actions (`h_t`).
- This internal memory acts as a learned "system identification module," allowing the policy to infer the underlying dynamics without needing explicit parameters as input.
- The network architecture consists of a feedforward branch (processing current state `s_t` and goal `g`) and a recurrent branch (processing `s_t` and previous action `a_t-1` to update memory `z_t`). Features from both branches are concatenated to produce the action. This ensures the policy has direct access to the current state while also learning a rich history-dependent representation of the dynamics.

#### 3.3. Learning Algorithm: RDPG with HER

The training process leverages established deep reinforcement learning algorithms suitable for continuous control and sparse rewards:
- **Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG):** A popular off-policy algorithm for continuous action spaces, which learns a deterministic policy and an action-value function (critic).
- **Recurrent Deterministic Policy Gradient (RDPG):** An extension of DDPG designed to train recurrent policies with off-policy data, essential for maintaining the LSTM's internal state across training samples.
- **Hindsight Experience Replay (HER):** A crucial technique for learning with sparse rewards. If an agent fails to achieve its initial goal in an episode, HER re-labels the episode with a new "achieved goal" (e.g., the final state achieved). This transforms failed trajectories into successful ones for a different goal, providing more positive training signals and making learning from sparse rewards feasible. The paper uses a binary reward (0 for success, -1 otherwise).
- **Omniscient Critic:** During training, the value function (critic) `Q` is provided with the *known* dynamics parameters (`μ`) of the current simulated environment, in addition to the state, action, memory, and goal. This "omniscient" critic helps reduce variance in policy gradients and provides more accurate value estimates, facilitating faster and more stable learning for the policy. Crucially, the policy itself (actor) *does not* receive `μ` as input, ensuring it learns to infer dynamics from observations alone, as would be necessary in the real world.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup: Fetch Robotics Pushing Task

The proposed methodology is demonstrated on an object pushing task using a 7-DOF Fetch Robotics arm.
- **Task:** The robot's goal is to push a puck to a randomly sampled target location on a table.
- **State Space:** A 52-dimensional state vector including joint positions/velocities, gripper position, and puck position/orientation/velocities.
- **Action Space:** 7-dimensional, representing relative joint angle offsets for a position controller.
- **Simulation Environment:** MuJoCo physics engine.
- **Training Scale:** Policies are trained for approximately 8000 update iterations, using about 100 million samples, requiring about 8 hours on a 100-core cluster.
- **Real Robot Evaluation:** Performance is evaluated on a physical Fetch arm, with the puck's location tracked by a PhaseSpace mocap system. The authors explicitly state that "little calibration was performed" between simulation and the real robot, highlighting the method's robustness to mismatch.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide compelling evidence for the effectiveness of dynamics randomization combined with recurrent policies for sim-to-real transfer.

#### 4.1. Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer

The most significant finding is that policies trained *exclusively* in simulation using dynamics randomization were able to directly and successfully perform the puck-pushing task on a real Fetch robot. The LSTM policy achieved a success rate of **0.89 ± 0.06** on the real robot, which is remarkably close to its performance in simulation (0.91 ± 0.03). This demonstrates a significant step towards bridging the reality gap without costly real-world data collection. The supplemental video visually confirms the policy's proficiency and robustness.

#### 4.2. Impact of Network Architecture

A comparison of different policy architectures revealed the superior performance of the recurrent LSTM model:
- **LSTM Policy:** Achieved the highest success rate in both randomized simulation (0.91 ± 0.03) and on the real robot (0.89 ± 0.06). It also learned faster in simulation. This confirms the benefit of an adaptive, memory-based policy for handling dynamic variability.
- **Feedforward Network (FF) with Randomization:** While training a memoryless feedforward network with randomization improved robustness compared to no randomization, its performance on the real robot (0.67 ± 0.14) was significantly lower than the LSTM. This suggests that simply being robust to varied dynamics is not enough; the ability to *adapt* at runtime using internal memory is key.
- **Feedforward Network with History (FF + Hist):** Providing explicit history (8 previous states and actions) to a feedforward network improved its simulated performance close to the LSTM (0.87 ± 0.03), but its real-world performance (0.70 ± 0.10) still lagged behind the LSTM. This indicates that the LSTM's learned, compact internal representation of history is more effective for implicit system identification and adaptation than raw input history.
- **Feedforward Network without Randomization (FF no Rand):** As expected, a policy trained without dynamics randomization performed very poorly, if at all, when faced with unseen dynamics in randomized simulation (0.51 ± 0.05) and completely failed on the real robot (0.0 ± 0.0). This highlights the critical role of randomization in enabling transfer.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study: Importance of Randomized Parameters

An ablation study, where subsets of parameters were held fixed during training, demonstrated the importance of randomizing specific dynamics parameters:
- Disabling randomization of **action timestep** (simulating latency) and **observation noise** led to significant performance drops on the real robot (0.29 ± 0.11 and 0.25 ± 0.12 success rates, respectively). This is a crucial insight, as these factors are often difficult to model accurately or measure in real systems.
- Fixing **link mass** (0.64 ± 0.10) and **puck friction** (0.48 ± 0.10) also reduced performance, though less drastically than action timestep and observation noise.
This indicates that a comprehensive randomization strategy across various dimensions is beneficial for achieving robust sim-to-real transfer.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Unmodeled Dynamics

The LSTM policy exhibited remarkable robustness to unmodeled changes in the real physical system. When a packet of chips was attached to the puck (significantly altering its mass, friction, and contact dynamics), the policy's success rate remained high at **0.91 ± 0.04**, comparable to its performance with the standard puck (0.89 ± 0.06). This showcases the policy's ability to generalize beyond the specific variations encountered during training, adapting to novel real-world conditions.

#### 4.5. Emergent Behaviors

The policies, trained using only a sparse binary reward, developed sophisticated and "clever" manipulation strategies. These included:
- Pressing on one side of the puck to partially upend it before sliding.
- Manipulating the puck from the top or sides for fine adjustments.
- Correcting for overshooting the target.
These emergent behaviors highlight the power of reinforcement learning to discover non-obvious and effective solutions when equipped with sufficient adaptation capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in the field of robotic reinforcement learning, offering a practical and scalable solution to the long-standing "reality gap" problem.

- **Practicality and Cost Reduction:** The most immediate impact is the significant reduction in the need for costly, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous real-world data collection and fine-tuning. By enabling training *exclusively* in simulation, this approach makes high-sample-complexity DeepRL algorithms much more accessible for real-world robotic applications. This democratizes access to powerful learning techniques for robotics researchers and practitioners.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The method results in policies that are not only capable of transferring from sim-to-real but are also inherently robust to unknown parameters, calibration errors, and even significant unmodeled changes in the physical environment. This robustness is critical for deploying robots in dynamic and uncertain real-world settings.
- **Enabling Complex Robotic Skills:** By decoupling training from the physical system, this work opens up possibilities for learning a wider and richer repertoire of complex manipulation and locomotion skills that might otherwise be too risky or expensive to train directly on hardware. The emergence of sophisticated pushing strategies without explicit programming underscores this potential.
- **Validation of Domain Randomization:** The paper provides strong empirical validation for the effectiveness of dynamics randomization, particularly when coupled with adaptive, recurrent policies. It emphasizes that embracing variability in simulation can be more effective than striving for perfect fidelity.
- **Future Directions:** The success of this approach encourages its extension to more complex tasks, multi-modal inputs (e.g., vision-based control), and potentially to systems with even higher degrees of freedom. This could pave the way for a new generation of highly capable and autonomous robots operating in unstructured environments.

In conclusion, "Sim-to-Real Transfer of Robotic Control with Dynamics Randomization" offers a transformative solution that leverages the scalability and safety of simulation to train powerful, adaptive robotic policies for real-world deployment. This work significantly lowers the barrier for applying cutting-edge deep reinforcement learning to practical robotics, promising to accelerate progress in fields ranging from industrial automation to service robotics.
