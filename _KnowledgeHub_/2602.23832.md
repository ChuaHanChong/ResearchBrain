# OmniTrack: General Motion Tracking via Physics-Consistent Reference

> [!summary] Summary
> OmniTrack introduces a two-stage learning framework to generate physics-consistent reference motions from human data, then trains a general policy to track them. This method enabled a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to perform diverse and complex motions for extended periods in real-world conditions and through online teleoperation, eliminating physical inconsistencies like penetration and floating from original motion data.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to robustly track human motion data due to physically infeasible artifacts (e.g., floating, ground penetration) in retargeted motions, arising from morphological and dynamic discrepancies.
- Existing general motion tracking policies face a fundamental trade-off between faithfully following inaccurate reference motions and maintaining physical stability, limiting long-term, generalized, and robust control.
- Partial observability in real-world deployment hinders control policies from implicitly discerning physical feasibility, often leading to dynamic failures and instability.

## Method

- **OmniTrack** proposes a ==two-stage learning framework== that explicitly decouples the generation of physically feasible motions from the general motion tracking task.
- The ==Physical Motion Generation (PMG)== stage trains a generalist policy with ==privileged information== in simulation to convert raw, inconsistent human motion data into strictly physics-consistent robot state trajectories.
- The ==General Motion Tracking (GMT)== stage then trains a single, general control policy with ==partial observability== and extensive ==domain randomization== to robustly track these pre-validated, physically consistent motions for real-world deployment.

## Results

- The PMG stage successfully eliminated all physical inconsistencies, achieving **0.0%** penetration and **0.0%** floating in generated motions from datasets initially showing up to **67.7%** penetration.
- In simulation, OmniTrack achieved a **96.88%** success rate and **34.83 mm** Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE) on unseen motions, outperforming state-of-the-art methods like OmniH2O (**48.32%** success rate).
- On a physical Unitree G1 robot, the system enabled hour-long stable tracking of diverse and complex motions (e.g., flips, cartwheels) in real-world conditions and supported robust, dynamic online teleoperation with noisy human inputs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decoupling physical feasibility from general motion control resolves a critical conflict, allowing the tracking policy to focus purely on adaptation and robust execution.
> - Utilizing privileged information during the physical motion generation stage enables the creation of high-quality, dynamics-consistent reference motions that strictly adhere to robot physics.
> - Providing clean, physically plausible reference motions dramatically improves the efficiency, scalability, and generalization capabilities of subsequent policy learning for real-world humanoid control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has made significant strides in learning complex behaviors by tracking human motion data. This paradigm, leveraging large-scale human demonstrations, has shown promise in enabling diverse and human-like actions in robots. However, achieving general, long-horizon, and robust control on physical humanoid hardware remains a formidable challenge.

Previous research efforts, such as those employing motion retargeting (e.g., PHUMA, OmniRetarget), attempt to adapt human motions to robotic morphologies. While these methods can align kinematic constraints, they often fall short in ensuring dynamic consistency, leading to physically infeasible artifacts like floating, ground penetration, and imbalance in the retargeted motions. These inconsistencies, compounded by data noise and the inherent "embodiment gap" between humans and robots, create a fundamental conflict for motion tracking policies: whether to faithfully follow an inaccurate reference motion or prioritize the robot's physical stability. Existing general motion tracking policies (e.g., ExBody2, OmniH2O, ASAP, GMT, BeyondMimic, Sonic) often struggle with this trade-off. They may exhibit limitations in handling long-term sequences, generalizing across heterogeneous motion types (e.g., balancing stable behaviors with highly dynamic actions or contact-rich interactions), or maintaining robustness against low-quality or noisy input references. Many approaches implicitly require the control policy to discern physical feasibility during execution, a task made difficult by partial observability in real-world settings.

OmniTrack directly addresses this core limitation by proposing a novel architectural solution: explicitly decoupling the concern of physical feasibility from the general motion tracking task. Rather than attempting to train a single controller to implicitly balance these conflicting objectives, OmniTrack enforces physical consistency *before* the control policy learns to track. This represents a significant advancement by providing a "cleaner" and more reliable foundation for policy learning, thereby enabling a single, generalizable policy to achieve unprecedented robustness, long-term stability, and dynamic capability across a wide range of behaviors on real humanoid robots. It shifts the paradigm from trying to "fix" imperfect references during control to ensuring perfect references from the outset, thus streamlining the subsequent control learning process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of OmniTrack is to enable general, stable, and diverse control for humanoid robots by learning from human motion data, while specifically overcoming the inherent challenges posed by physically infeasible artifacts in reference motions.

The core motivation stems from the recognition that discrepancies in morphology and dynamics between humans and robots, coupled with noise in motion capture data, consistently introduce physical inconsistencies (e.g., floating, penetration, unstable center-of-mass motions) into human reference motions when retargeted for robots. These artifacts lead to a critical conflict: a robot's control policy is faced with the dilemma of either precisely following an inaccurate reference motion or prioritizing its own physical stability. When operating under partial observability, typical in real-world deployments, the robot lacks the privileged information needed to resolve this conflict, often leading to dynamic failures, instability, and a severe hindrance to the development of truly generalizable motion tracking policies.

Therefore, the key objectives for OmniTrack are:
1. **Decouple Physical Feasibility:** To explicitly separate the process of ensuring physical plausibility from the task of general motion tracking.
2. **Generate Physics-Consistent References:** To create a robust mechanism for converting raw, noisy human motion data into physically feasible, dynamics-consistent robot trajectories that strictly adhere to the robot's physical constraints and capabilities.
3. **Learn General Motion Tracking:** To train a single, general control policy that can then robustly track these pre-validated, physically consistent motions, thereby ensuring stability, accuracy, and strong generalization to diverse behaviors.
4. **Achieve Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To enable seamless and effective deployment of the learned policies from simulation to real-world humanoid robots, demonstrating long-term stability and the capability to perform highly dynamic and complex maneuvers.
5. **Support Real-time Teleoperation:** To extend the framework to handle real-time, online inputs from human operators, enabling stable and dynamic human-style teleoperation even with noisy or unpredictable commands.

By achieving these objectives, OmniTrack aims to resolve the long-standing trade-off between tracking fidelity and physical stability, paving the way for more reliable and capable humanoid robot control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OmniTrack employs a novel two-stage learning framework that explicitly decouples physical feasibility from general motion tracking. Both stages are formulated as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The overall pipeline first converts raw, physically inconsistent reference motions into physics-consistent ones in simulation, and then trains a general policy to robustly track these refined motions under realistic conditions.

**Stage I: Physical Motion Generation (PMG)**
The first stage acts as a "motion filtering process" designed to generate physically feasible reference motions from raw, retargeted human motion data.
- **Goal:** To produce high-quality, dynamics-consistent robot state trajectories that strictly comply with the robot’s physics and actuation limits, while maintaining the style of the original human motion.
- **Policy Training:** A single generalist policy is trained in a physics simulator (e.g., IsaacLab).
- **Observation & Information:** This policy operates with *privileged information*, meaning it has access to the complete simulation state, including global poses, orientations, velocities, and accurate per-link contact states, which are unavailable on real hardware. This full observability allows the policy to accurately assess and enforce physical feasibility.
- **Reward Design:** Uses a common tracking reward formulation that penalizes deviations from the reference in root and link poses, orientations, and velocities, alongside regularization terms for action and joint-limit violations.
- **Domain Randomization:** Crucially, *no domain randomization* is applied to physical parameters in this stage. The focus is purely on maximizing reconstruction fidelity to the reference while enforcing physical consistency. However, noise is injected into motion commands (reference joint positions, velocities, root pose/orientation) to encourage robust rollouts across diverse sources.
- **Output:** The state trajectories (joint states, global poses, velocities, contact states) generated through rollouts in this stage are recorded as the "physically feasible reference trajectories." These trajectories also provide high-quality supervisory signals, such as accurate contact masks, for the subsequent stage.

**Stage II: General Motion Tracking (GMT)**
The second stage is dedicated to learning a robust and general control policy for real-world deployment, using the physically feasible trajectories generated in Stage I.
- **Goal:** To achieve robust tracking of diverse behaviors on physical hardware under realistic conditions, with the intrinsic conflict between tracking fidelity and physical stability largely alleviated.
- **Policy Training:** A single general control policy is trained.
- **Observation & Information:** This policy operates under *partial observability*, restricted to proprioceptive inputs (joint pose, joint velocity, root orientation, root angular velocity, previous action) that are available from onboard sensors in the real world.
- **Reward Design:** Builds upon the common tracking reward formulation. It incorporates *desired-contact rewards*, leveraging the accurate contact information from the Stage I generated references. The action smoothness penalty is adaptively scaled with reference joint velocities, allowing for more aggressive control changes during dynamic movements and smoother control during quasi-static phases.
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied to physical parameters (e.g., friction, restitution, joint properties, IMU offsets, center of mass) and external disturbances (random pushes) to improve sim-to-real transfer robustness. Observation noise is also injected to match real-world sensor characteristics.
- **Adaptive Sampling:** An adaptive sampling strategy is employed during training, segmenting motion sequences into bins and adjusting sampling probabilities based on failure counts to ensure sufficient exposure to challenging motion segments.

**Tasks and Applications:**
OmniTrack offers both offline and online applications:
- **Offline Motion Filtering and Tracking:** The PMG stage processes raw motions to create physically feasible references, which are then tracked by the GMT policy for stable, long-horizon execution of diverse behaviors.
- **Online Teleoperation:** The two stages combine into an end-to-end inference pipeline. Real-time motion commands (e.g., from MoCap or VR) are fed into the PMG module in simulation to generate valid trajectories, which are then immediately tracked by the GMT policy on the real robot, enabling dynamic human-style teleoperation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted both in simulation and on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot demonstrate OmniTrack's significant advancements in motion tracking capabilities.

#### 4.1. Quality of Generated Physically Plausible Motions (Stage I)

- **Artifact Elimination:** The Physical Motion Generation (PMG) stage successfully eliminates physically infeasible artifacts. Raw retargeted LAFAN1 data showed 20.3% penetration and 2.52% floating, while AMASS data had 67.7% penetration and 3.29% floating. After PMG, both datasets exhibited **0.0% penetration and 0.0% floating**.
- **Improved Smoothness and Fidelity:** Generated motions showed improved state smoothness (lower linear jerk). While enforcing dynamics-consistent corrections resulted in a moderate increase in Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE) (e.g., 21.0 mm for LAFAN1, 16.0 mm for AMASS), this was interpreted as necessary adjustments for physical realism rather than degradation, with stylistic characteristics of the original motions largely preserved.
- **Impact on Policy Learning:** Training with physically plausible motions dramatically improved learning efficiency and final performance. When scaling the LAFAN1 dataset, policies trained on raw motions showed significant degradation (e.g., success rate dropped from 95.1% to 88.2% from 1/8 to full dataset, MPJPE increased by 10.1 mm). In contrast, policies trained on PMG-generated motions maintained stable success rates (around 92.6% for the full dataset) and preserved motion fidelity (MPJPE increased by only 6.4 mm). This confirms the critical role of physical consistency for scalable humanoid motion learning.

#### 4.2. General Motion Tracking Performance in Simulation (Stage II)

- **Comparison of Training Pipelines:** OmniTrack consistently outperformed baseline training pipelines (Dagger, Dagger_hist, AAC) across all evaluation metrics (Success Rate (SR), MPJPE, ∆Vel, ∆Acc). On "seen motions," OmniTrack achieved 96.13% SR and 37.61 mm MPJPE, compared to ~93% SR for baselines. The advantage was even more pronounced on "seen (hard motions)," where OmniTrack maintained 84.81% SR while baselines dropped to ~70%. For "unseen motions," OmniTrack achieved the highest SR (96.88%) and lowest MPJPE (34.83 mm), demonstrating superior generalization. This highlights that structural decoupling frees the policy to focus solely on tracking.
- **Comparison with State-of-the-Art:** Against prominent SOTA methods (OmniH2O, ExBody2, BeyondMimic) on the challenging high-dynamic subset of LAFAN1, OmniTrack achieved the best performance. It attained an SR of 84.81%, significantly outperforming OmniH2O (48.32%), ExBody2 (58.93%), and BeyondMimic (70.04%). Consistent reductions were also observed in MPJPE, velocity error (∆Vel), and acceleration error (∆Acc). This indicates that physical inconsistency is a major bottleneck, especially for dynamic and contact-rich movements.

#### 4.3. Performance of General Motion Tracking in Real (Sim-to-Real)

- **Motion Tracking:**
 - **Diverse Skill Repertoire:** The policy enabled hour-long, continuous, and stable tracking of a wide range of human-like behaviors on the Unitree G1 robot, including locomotion, jumping, dancing, martial arts, fall recovery, balance control, and complex acrobatic motions such as flips and cartwheels.
 - **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Quantitative evaluation showed comparable tracking error levels in joint position, base orientation, and base angular velocity between MuJoCo simulation and real-robot execution for diverse motions, confirming strong sim-to-real alignment.
 - **Robustness in Challenging Scenarios:** The robot demonstrated robustness by continuously replaying motion sequences for approximately 30 minutes in outdoor environments (uneven terrain, soft soil, varying friction) under strong wind and external disturbances. It also performed 17 consecutive side flips without failure, showcasing sustained control over extended high-dynamic sequences.
- **Online Teleoperation:**
 - **Robustness to Noisy Inputs:** OmniTrack successfully supported real-time teleoperation using full-body motion capture systems and Pico VR headsets. The PMG module effectively processed noisy, discontinuous, and dynamically inconsistent raw teleoperation commands, generating physically feasible trajectories that the robot tracked smoothly and stably.
 - **Dynamic and Human-like Control:** The robot executed dynamic, static, and contact-rich behaviors with natural, human-like motion style, demonstrating responsive control and high-fidelity whole-body coordination, even with unpredictable user inputs. This confirms the system's strong generalization capabilities to unseen real-world commands.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

OmniTrack represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robot control, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of learning generalizable motion tracking from human data. Its core contribution—the explicit decoupling of physical feasibility from general motion control learning—is a fundamental architectural insight that effectively resolves a critical conflict inherent in prior approaches.

The potential impact of OmniTrack is multi-faceted:

1. **Enabling General Humanoid Control:** By creating a robust pipeline that guarantees physically consistent reference motions, OmniTrack allows control policies to focus purely on tracking and adaptation, leading to a single policy capable of continuously executing hour-long, diverse, and complex motions. This pushes the boundaries toward truly general and versatile humanoid robots.

2. **Advancing Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The framework's emphasis on generating physics-consistent references within the simulation environment, coupled with extensive domain randomization in the tracking stage, facilitates robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. This is crucial for practical robot deployment, reducing the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection and fine-tuning.

3. **Standardized Benchmarking:** The construction of a high-quality, physically consistent robot motion dataset (derived from LAFAN1 and AMASS) provides a standardized and unbiased benchmark for future research. With dynamic artifacts removed, performance differences among new policies will more accurately reflect their true tracking capabilities, fostering clearer evaluation and faster progress.

4. **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** The demonstrated capability for stable and dynamic online teleoperation, robust to noisy and unpredictable human inputs, significantly enhances the potential for intuitive human-robot interaction. This opens doors for humanoids to perform complex tasks in collaboration with humans in unstructured environments, from industrial settings to assistive robotics.

5. **Scalability and Robustness:** The method's ability to scale effectively to large and diverse motion datasets, while maintaining robustness in challenging real-world conditions (uneven terrain, external disturbances, prolonged dynamic maneuvers), positions it as a foundational technology for future generations of autonomous and interactive humanoid robots.

In conclusion, OmniTrack offers a principled and effective solution to a central problem in humanoid control, moving the field closer to realizing embodied artificial general intelligence. Its impact extends beyond motion tracking, influencing data generation, policy learning, and the practical deployment of humanoid robots in real-world scenarios.
