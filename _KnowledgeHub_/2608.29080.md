# GHOST in the Robots: Real-Time Exocentric Dual-Robot VR Teleoperation from Onboard Cameras

> [!summary] Summary
> GHOST is an open-source VR teleoperation system letting one operator control two Boston Dynamics Spot mobile manipulators with low-level 6-DoF commands using only onboard sensing. It fuses both robots' RGB-D streams into a real-time exocentric point cloud via GPU depth completion and ICP alignment, plus a mode-switching controller. Novices reached 1.6-4x the tablet's success rate; experts were 1.47x faster.

## Problem

- Existing multi-robot teleoperation needs one operator per robot, or leans on autonomy, restricting humans to occasional intervention rather than continuous direct control.
- Prior exocentric interfaces depend on external capture cameras or pre-built maps, and confine operators to high-level goal poses instead of precise low-level mobile manipulation.
- Egocentric single-robot VR views suffer arm occlusion and blind spots, forcing camera switching that raises cognitive load and blocks simultaneous dual-robot coordination.

## Method

- **SpotObserver**, a CUDA/ONNX Unity DLL, fetches RGB-D off ROS 2, prefills sparse depth by nearest-neighbor, runs ==PromptDA== depth completion, and shares GPU buffers via ==CUDA-DX12 interop==.
- Shared exocentric scene registers both point clouds using each robot's onboard localization refined by a LiDAR ==ICP== correction recomputed every 4 seconds, with virtual gripper targets masking latency.
- ==Mode-switching control== separates robot selection from Fly/Drive/Arm/Arm-Drive modes, plus a Dual-Robot mode driving a ==formation pivot== (centroid position, circular-mean yaw) while arms stay independent.

## Results

- Fifteen novices scored **1.6x** the tablet's success rate on plush-toy handoff and **4x** on box pick-and-place; SUS **64.8** vs **52.8**, not significant (**p=0.16**).
- Three experts completed **26/27** participant-task conditions versus **20/27** for the tablet, with dust-pan sweep and bedsheet folding infeasible on tablet, and **1.47x** average speedup.
- RGB-only ablation reached **22/27** conditions, isolating 6-DoF control as the speed driver; latency was **230 ms** sensor delivery, **131 ms** completion, **14 ms** render, **4.3 fps** scene updates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct 6-DoF pose mapping buys speed, whereas 3D reconstruction buys reliability; the two benefits are separable and worth measuring independently in interface studies.
> - Inaccurate reconstruction can be worse than none: poor depth completion on deformable or cluttered objects degraded performance below the RGB-only condition.
> - Locking relative base poses to a shared formation pivot lets two hand controllers drive five control channels without overwhelming operator attention.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot teleoperation systems are developed to enable remote control of robots, particularly in complex or unstructured environments where full autonomy may not be feasible or reliable. Virtual Reality (VR) interfaces are increasingly employed in teleoperation to provide operators with enhanced spatial awareness, stereo depth perception, and intuitive control, primarily for single-robot systems. However, a significant gap exists in the capability for a single operator to simultaneously control multiple robots at a low level of precision, using only onboard sensing, within an immersive VR environment.

Existing multi-robot teleoperation systems generally fall into several categories, each with limitations that GHOST aims to address:

- **Multi-operator systems:** Some approaches allocate one operator per robot (e.g., CollabTeleop), which increases human resource requirements and coordination complexity, while individual operators may have limited situational awareness from a single onboard camera.
- **Autonomous or semi-autonomous systems:** Many systems rely on high-level commands, task planning, or significant robot autonomy, with human intervention limited to occasional supervision or high-level goal setting (e.g., Spot-On, Roldan et al., Zick et al., Chen et al.). These often require pre-built maps or abstract 2D interfaces, constraining their use in novel or unknown environments and precluding direct low-level control. While Chen et al. integrate 3D reconstruction in a mixed reality interface for multiple robots, control is restricted to high-level drag-and-drop navigation goals, not low-level mobile manipulation.
- **Limited single-operator low-level control:** Other single-operator approaches implement coordinated control strategies such as shared pivot points (e.g., Laghi et al., Ozdamar et al.) or leader-follower architectures (e.g., Lewis et al.). These systems may support simultaneous control or attention-switching but often offer limited independent execution or provide only basic RGB video streams, which are insufficient for precise spatial reasoning. Body Extension is a notable effort that assigns one arm to each robot for a single operator but lacks integrated 3D vision and coordinated base control.

Visual representations in teleoperation are broadly categorized as egocentric (from the robot's perspective) or exocentric (from a global, third-person perspective). While egocentric VR is common for single robots, it can cause motion sickness and cognitive load when switching between multiple camera views in a multi-robot context. Exocentric interfaces, which GHOST employs, naturally support multi-robot coordination but traditionally face challenges with accurate, real-time 3D reconstruction from onboard sensors, as techniques like Gaussian splatting or neural radiance fields are computationally intensive for interactive rates. This motivates GHOST's integration of real-time depth completion.

This work specifically addresses the challenge of providing a single operator with both low-level independent and synchronized control over multiple mobile manipulators while maintaining a real-time, exocentric 3D understanding of a shared workspace, solely from onboard camera data. This combination of features distinguishes GHOST from prior research efforts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop and evaluate GHOST (GHOST Human Operator for Simultaneous Teleoperation), an open-source VR teleoperation system that enables a single human operator to control two mobile manipulators. This system was designed to provide direct, low-level commands, allowing for both individual and simultaneous robot control, using only data from the robots' onboard sensing.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and opportunities in the field of multi-robot teleoperation:

- **Addressing real-world task complexity:** Many real-world tasks, such as transporting large or elongated objects, assembling structures, or navigating complex environments with blind spots, inherently require multiple points of support, diverse viewpoints, or coordinated actions that a single robot cannot achieve efficiently or safely. Deploying multiple robots can overcome these limitations, but existing teleoperation systems struggle to facilitate this effectively for a single human operator.
- **Limitations of current multi-robot teleoperation:** Current systems often necessitate multiple operators, rely on a high degree of robot autonomy with minimal human oversight, or restrict operators to high-level commands. These approaches either incur increased human resource costs, limit the operator's ability to handle unexpected situations with precision, or fail to provide the fine-grained control necessary for complex manipulation tasks.
- **Perceptual and cognitive load challenges:** When teleoperating multiple robots, a single operator faces significant challenges in perceiving precise and coherent spatial information from disparate sensor feeds, dividing their limited attention across multiple robot bases, arms, and cameras, and efficiently mapping their intentions to the high-dimensional control space of multiple robots. Moreover, maintaining low latency across multiple video streams, 3D scene data, and synchronized commands over wireless connections is a critical system design challenge.
- **Enhancing operator spatial awareness:** Existing single-robot VR interfaces, often egocentric, can suffer from limited camera angles and arm occlusions, creating blind spots and forcing operators to frequently reposition the robot for a full view. An exocentric, unified 3D workspace from multiple robots' perspectives is hypothesized to improve spatial awareness and reduce reliance on frequent camera adjustments.

To address these motivations, GHOST pursued the following specific objectives:

1. **Develop an exocentric 3D workspace:** This involves reconstructing a real-time 3D point cloud by processing RGB-D streams from the robots' onboard cameras and aligning these into a coherent virtual scene.
2. **Implement learning-based depth completion:** To overcome the sparsity and noise inherent in onboard depth camera data, GHOST integrates real-time depth completion techniques to infer missing regions, thereby improving scene coverage and aiding operator spatial awareness.
3. **Design a mode-switching control architecture:** To manage the high-dimensional control space of two mobile manipulators with limited operator inputs (two hand controllers), the system needed an intuitive control scheme allowing individual robot control and synchronized dual-robot motion (e.g., locking relative base poses for cooperative carrying).
4. **Ensure low latency and responsive control:** The system architecture was designed to minimize latency for multiple video streams, 3D scene data, and synchronized commands, critical for effective real-time teleoperation.
5. **Evaluate new multi-robot capabilities:** The system's performance was evaluated on a set of challenging dual-robot tasks that are difficult or impractical for single robots or with existing teleoperation interfaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GHOST system is designed for single-operator teleoperation of two Boston Dynamics Spot mobile manipulators. Each Spot robot is equipped with a quadruped base, a 6-DoF arm with a gripper and gripper camera, a LiDAR unit, and five body-mounted RGB-D cameras (of which the two front-facing ones are utilized for scene reconstruction). Communication between the robots, a ROS 2 server, and a Unity-based VR client is established via an onboard 802.11n Wi-Fi network.

The system architecture is compartmentalized into three main components:

1. **Unity-based VR Client:** This client is responsible for rendering the visual feedback to the operator and processing operator inputs for mode-switching control, allowing individual or simultaneous command of the robots.
2. **ROS 2 Server:** This server manages low-bandwidth data streams, including robot state information, command execution, multi-robot localization, and synchronized navigation instructions.
3. **SpotObserver:** This is a custom, GPU-accelerated library implemented as a Unity DLL extension on the VR client. It is engineered to bypass standard ROS 2 messaging for high-bandwidth RGB-D data. Its function is to directly retrieve RGB-D data from each robot, perform real-time depth completion, and generate dense point clouds, which are then rendered by Unity with minimal latency.

#### 3.1. Visual Feedback

The operator's visual feedback within the VR environment is composed of four distinct elements:

1. **Robot Models:** Live URDF models of the Spot robots are rendered, with their joint states streamed from each robot via ROS 2. These models provide continuous, accurate kinematic representation.
2. **3D Point Clouds:** Real-time point clouds are generated by combining data from each robot's front-facing RGB-D cameras. These point clouds are then depth-completed and aligned into a shared virtual scene.
3. **2D Gripper-Camera Feeds:** Live feeds from the gripper cameras are displayed as floating panels within the virtual environment, providing close-up views for fine-grained manipulation and compensating for occlusions from the front-facing cameras.
4. **Virtual Gripper Targets:** A virtual gripper is rendered at the commanded end-effector pose, separate from the robot's physical model. This provides immediate visual feedback of the operator's intended action, mitigating the perception of latency caused by network delays and robot dynamics.

The **Vision Processing Pipeline** of SpotObserver is central to generating the 3D point clouds, addressing challenges of latency, spatial alignment, and depth data sparsity:

1. **Latency Reduction:** High-bandwidth RGB-D streams are processed by SpotObserver in dedicated threads, decoupled from Unity's main render loop. CUDA-DX12 interoperability is utilized to share GPU resources directly with Unity, eliminating costly CPU-GPU memory transfers. In experiments, the system achieved 71 fps headset rendering with a 4.3 fps scene update rate, with latency primarily attributed to sensor-frame network delivery (230 ms), followed by depth completion (131 ms) and rendering (14 ms).
2. **Relative 3D Transform:** The initial inter-robot transform, derived from each robot's built-in localization estimates, is refined. This refinement is achieved through an iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm, applied to LiDAR data from both robots and recomputed every 4 seconds, to achieve accurate point cloud alignment in a shared world frame.
3. **Metric Depth Completion:** SpotObserver employs the ViT-based PromptDA model for real-time depth completion. To improve the quality of completion, a nearest-neighbor interpolation step pre-fills sparse depth measurements before they are fed into the PromptDA model. Both pre-filling and model inference are executed on the GPU using CUDA and ONNX Runtime.

#### 3.2. Control

GHOST’s control scheme addresses the complexity of simultaneously managing two mobile manipulators with the limited inputs of two hand controllers (Meta Quest 3). A **mode-switching architecture** is implemented to provide task-relevant control combinations:

- **Robot Selection and Task-Level Modes:** Operators can assign each hand controller to a specific robot. Four task-level modes are available for single-robot operation:
 - **Fly:** Repositions the exocentric camera view.
 - **Drive:** Controls the robot's base movement.
 - **Arm:** Maps controller 6-DoF pose to the robot's end-effector pose and provides gripper control.
 - **Arm-Drive:** Combines base and arm control on a single controller.
- **Dual-Robot Mode:** For coordinated operations, a distinct Dual-Robot mode is available. In this mode, base commands are applied to a shared formation pivot, causing both robots to move synchronously while maintaining their relative configuration. Arm commands, however, remain independently controlled by the operator's two hands, allowing for bimanual coordination.

**Control Mappings:**

1. **Single-Robot Control:**
 - **Arm Control:** The 6-DoF motion of a hand controller is directly mapped to the robot's end-effector. A latch mechanism, activated by holding the controller trigger, records initial controller and end-effector poses, allowing subsequent controller motion to be interpreted as relative displacement. The virtual gripper target provides immediate visual feedback of the commanded pose, with the physical arm following at approximately 10 Hz via the Boston Dynamics API.
 - **Base Control:** Thumbstick input is translated into translational and rotational velocity commands for the selected robot.
 - **Camera Control:** The operator's head pose is tracked relative to a movable virtual base frame. Thumbstick input in Fly mode translates or rotates this base frame, enabling navigation of the exocentric scene.
2. **Dual-Robot Synchronous Control:** When activated, the system records the initial base poses of all robots and establishes a formation pivot (calculated as the centroid of robot positions and circular mean of their yaw angles). Each robot's offset from this pivot is maintained. Operator navigation commands (ΔT) update the pivot's pose, and each robot receives its new target waypoint (𝑇𝑤→piv(𝑡) · 𝑇piv→𝑖) at 10 Hz via the Boston Dynamics API, ensuring formation accuracy for tasks like cooperative carrying. Arm controls remain independent.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Design

GHOST was evaluated on nine challenging dual-robot tasks, categorized into:
- **Bimanual manipulation:** Tasks requiring coordinated simultaneous manipulation (e.g., plush toy handoff, box pick & place, bag filling, rope tying).
- **Perceptual blind spots:** Tasks benefiting from multi-viewpoint observations to reduce occlusion (e.g., cube stacking, cube collection).
- **Spatial context awareness:** Tasks requiring reasoning about relative spatial configuration (e.g., door passthrough, dust-pan sweep, bedsheet folding).

The system's performance was compared against the official Boston Dynamics tablet interface, which serves as an egocentric, velocity-control baseline, typically requiring two tablets for simultaneous dual-robot operation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GHOST involved both novice and expert user studies, comparing its performance against an off-the-shelf tablet interface and including an ablation study of the 3D scene component.

#### 4.1. Novice User Study

Fifteen novice participants (mean age 21.0 ± 2.4 years) with limited prior experience in VR or robotics were recruited for a within-subjects study. Participants attempted two tasks: plush toy handoff and box pick-and-place, chosen for their simplicity for first-time users while still requiring dual-robot coordination. Measures included task success rate, completion time, and System Usability Scale (SUS) scores.

- **Task Success Rate:** GHOST demonstrated a higher success rate compared to the tablet interface. Specifically, it achieved 1.6 times the tablet's success rate on the plush toy handoff task and 4 times the success rate on the box pick-and-place task.
- **Completion Time:** For trials that were successfully completed, GHOST showed comparable or faster completion times relative to the tablet interface.
- **System Usability Scale (SUS):** GHOST received an average SUS score of 64.8 ± 19.2, while the tablet scored 52.8 ± 22.4. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.16).
- **Participant Feedback:** Novice participants generally praised GHOST's intuitive control. Identified drawbacks included perceived point-cloud reconstruction quality and occasional motion-induced discomfort.

#### 4.2. Expert User Study

Three expert participants, who were also authors experienced with both interfaces, engaged in a comprehensive study attempting all nine dual-robot tasks. The study compared three interfaces: full GHOST, GHOST with RGB-only camera feeds (an ablation removing the 3D point cloud), and the official tablet interface. Metrics included task success (feasibility) and completion time.

- **Task Feasibility:**
 - **Full GHOST:** Achieved the highest task completion reliability, with 26 out of 27 feasible participant-task conditions. Eight of the nine tasks were feasible for all three experts, and bedsheet folding was feasible for two out of three experts.
 - **Tablet Interface:** Achieved 20 out of 27 feasible conditions. Two tasks, dust-pan sweeping and bedsheet folding, were deemed infeasible for all experts using the tablet. GHOST thus enabled the completion of tasks that were infeasible with the tablet.
 - **RGB-only GHOST:** Achieved 22 out of 27 feasible conditions, falling between the tablet and full GHOST in terms of reliability.
- **Completion Time:**
 - On the seven tasks where the tablet interface was successful, **full GHOST achieved a 1.47 times average per-task speedup** over the tablet.
 - Significant speed gains were observed in tasks such as box pick-and-place (2:48 with GHOST vs. 7:09 with tablet), plush toy handoff (1:39 vs. 3:00), cube stacking (4:01 vs. 5:50), and cube collection (2:39 vs. 3:49). GHOST also provided marginal speed advantages for bag filling and rope tying.
 - For the weighted-door passthrough task, GHOST was slower in successful trials but demonstrated higher feasibility (3/3 experts vs. 2/3 for tablet).
- **Ablation Study (RGB-only GHOST):**
 - The RGB-only GHOST ablation generally showed feasibility and completion times that fell between the tablet and full GHOST.
 - This version performed less reliably on tasks requiring coordinated spatial reasoning, including box pick-and-place, weighted-door passthrough, dust-pan sweeping, and bedsheet folding.
 - These results suggest that while the direct 6-DoF control provided by GHOST significantly contributes to speed benefits, the reconstructed 3D scene specifically enhances task completion reliability and spatial awareness, particularly in scenarios requiring complex spatial reasoning.
 - However, the benefits of the 3D scene were dependent on reconstruction quality; inaccurate reconstructions for deformable or cluttered objects could occasionally lead to reduced efficiency, with RGB-only GHOST sometimes matching or exceeding full GHOST in such cases.

#### 4.3. Discussion of Key Factors

The observed performance differences were attributed to several factors:

- **Coordinated Dual-Arm Control:** The VR system's bimanual mapping of robot arms to the operator's hands enabled simultaneous dual-arm control, crucial for tasks requiring synchronized manipulation (e.g., synchronized grasping, bedsheet folding). Tablet operators often had to alternate between arms due to the difficulty of simultaneous input.
- **Intuitive High-Precision and Rotation Control:** The direct hand-pose mapping in VR offered an advantage over joystick-based velocity control, particularly for tasks demanding precise alignment (e.g., cube stacking) and complex rotations (e.g., bag filling, dust-pan sweep).
- **Predictive Visual Feedback:** The virtual gripper target in GHOST provided immediate visual feedback of the commanded pose, reducing perceived latency and increasing operator confidence. Its benefit was less pronounced for tool-tip control during tool use.
- **3D Scene Representation Tradeoffs:** The 3D point cloud facilitated precise spatial alignment and provided a unified spatial reference, leading to improved task completion reliability (26/27 for full GHOST vs. 22/27 for RGB-only GHOST). While the 3D scene's impact on completion time was marginal, its contribution to overall reliability and spatial awareness, particularly for coordinated reasoning, was evident. Challenges with reconstruction quality in cluttered or deformable environments were noted as a factor that could mitigate its benefits.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GHOST system represents an advancement in single-operator multi-robot teleoperation by demonstrating a viable approach to provide precise, low-level control of two mobile manipulators using only onboard sensing within an immersive exocentric VR environment. This system combines several functionalities that have not been collectively demonstrated in prior work: real-time 3D scene reconstruction from onboard cameras, learning-based depth completion for improved scene coverage, an immersive exocentric view for enhanced spatial awareness, and a mode-switching control design that enables both individual and synchronized dual-robot low-level manipulation.

The significance and potential impact of this research include:

- **Enhanced Capabilities for Complex Tasks:** GHOST enables a single operator to perform tasks that are either difficult, inefficient, or entirely infeasible with traditional single-robot teleoperation or existing multi-robot interfaces. This is particularly relevant for tasks requiring bimanual manipulation, coordinated object transport, or navigation through environments with perceptual blind spots. The expert study results, showing the completion of tasks previously infeasible with tablet interfaces, support this.
- **Improved Efficiency and Reliability:** The study demonstrated that GHOST generally improved task success rates and reduced completion times for both novice and expert operators compared to a baseline tablet interface. This suggests that the system can make multi-robot operations more productive and less prone to errors.
- **Accessibility of Multi-Robot Control:** By providing an intuitive VR interface that leverages consumer-grade hardware (Meta Quest 3 controllers) and open-source components, GHOST could lower the barrier to entry for controlling complex multi-robot systems. This can facilitate wider adoption in various domains.
- **Foundational for Future Robotics Research:** The system's design, particularly the real-time 3D scene reconstruction and low-latency control pipeline, lays groundwork for several future research directions:
 - **Data Collection for Learning:** The collected teleoperation data could be used to train behavior cloning models, advancing autonomous and semi-autonomous robot capabilities.
 - **Scalability to Larger Robot Teams:** The architectural principles of GHOST could potentially be extended to control more than two robots, addressing challenges of operator attention and coordination in larger fleets.
 - **Integration of Autonomous Assistance:** The framework allows for future integration of autonomous assistance for shared control, where autonomy handles routine tasks while the operator focuses on critical decisions or fine manipulation.
 - **Advancements in 3D Reconstruction:** Ongoing efforts to improve the precision and consistency of the 3D reconstruction pipeline are expected to further enhance scene understanding and overall system performance, particularly for challenging objects like deformable or cluttered items.

While the study has limitations, such as the use of developers as expert participants and a limited number of novice participants, the preliminary results indicate a substantive improvement in the state-of-the-art for single-operator, low-level, multi-robot teleoperation.
