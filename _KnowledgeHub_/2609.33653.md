# Demonstration-Free Success-Probability Reward Learning for Generalist Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> eVTA0 is a demonstration-free reward model that learns dense success probabilities for robot manipulation directly from mixed-quality policy rollouts with only terminal success labels. A Qwen3-VL-4B backbone predicts eventual success from recent observations, trained via TD-style bootstrapping with an EMA target network; RLER periodically refits it on new rollouts. It lifts pi0.5 on LIBERO by 5.4-13.8% and real-world success by 20-26%.

## Problem

- Sparse terminal success rewards give RL fine-tuning of generalist robot policies little credit assignment, especially in long-horizon tasks spanning hundreds of actions.
- General-purpose reward models like VLAC and Robometer learn progress from expert demonstrations assuming monotonic completion, mismatching the suboptimal and failed rollouts seen during RL.
- Handcrafted, stage-based, and world-model-derived rewards need engineering, subtask annotations, or dynamics modeling on high-quality demonstrations, limiting scalability; training-free VLM rewards are noisy.

## Method

- ==Success-probability reward== theory: defines intermediate reward as P(success | trajectory prefix), proving it is a ==martingale== and that ideal ==one-step backward regression== recovers it exactly.
- **eVTA0** architecture: Qwen3-VL-4B with ==LoRA== reads the task instruction plus a 5-frame third-person and wrist window, then a ==reward-token head== with sigmoid outputs success probability.
- ==TD-style bootstrapping== with ==EMA target== trains on terminal labels only, soft targets for near-miss failures; reward p-1 avoids length bias, and **RLER** refits **eVTA0** on fresh rollouts each round.

## Results

- Reward quality on **400** LIBERO rollouts: Kendall tau **0.98** and MSE **0.06** versus fine-tuned Robometer's **0.85** and **0.10**; best VROC **0.77** on LIBERO, **0.44** on MetaWorld.
- GRPO on pi0.5 with **6,400** episodes: **82.9%** average LIBERO success versus **74.0%** SFT; LIBERO-Long rises **43.2%** to **54.0%** while Robometer drops it to **29.4%**.
- Real Franka, **two** RLER rounds with IQL: overall success **32/50** to **42/50** and **30/50** to **43/50**, beating fixed eVTA0 on matched data; TD beats Monte Carlo VOC **0.84** vs **0.37**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting eventual success instead of task progress makes rewards non-monotonic and behavior-responsive, so they penalize deviations and reward recoveries that progress models trained on demonstrations miss.
> - Reward models should be judged on the policy's own mixed-quality rollouts: Robometer's near-perfect demonstration VOC coexisted with weak failure discrimination and degraded RL.
> - A standalone success-probability model, TD-trained and refit as the policy shifts, beats both an implicit PPO critic and a frozen reward, suggesting rewards should co-evolve with learners.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In robotic manipulation, a robot may receive only a success-or-failure signal at the end of a task. That signal arrives too late to show which intermediate actions helped or hurt. In tasks lasting hundreds or thousands of actions, this makes it difficult for reinforcement learning (RL), which learns a policy through trial and error, to assign credit and improve.

Consider the paper's black-bowl task. In one run, the robot grasps and lifts the bowl but fails to place it stably and tips it over. In the other run, it completes the placement. A visible early success, the grasp, does not guarantee task success.

The authors' approach is to estimate, from the robot's recent experience, how likely it is to finish successfully. This differs from simply scoring how much apparent progress it has made. On the failed run, their model, eVTA₀, rises when the bowl is lifted. It then falls after placement becomes unstable and stays low as the bowl is tipped. The comparison model Robometer rises and remains high despite the failed placement. These are example predictions on two trajectories. They are not policy-success results.

Bowl task: a successful run (top), and a run where the bowl is grasped and lifted, then tipped over after unstable placement (bottom). Plots show predictions from 0 to 1 over timesteps for eVTA₀, Robometer, and GVL (GPT-5). eVTA₀ rises late on success and declines after the failed placement. Robometer scores the failed run high, and GVL is noisier.

### 2. From terminal outcome to useful feedback

The key insight is that a terminal success label can define an intermediate target: the probability of eventual success given the trajectory history so far. In the ideal population setting, this probability is temporally consistent. Ideal one-step regression from the next-step target also recovers the same probability. The theorem justifies propagating terminal supervision backward. It does not prove that a finite trained model is calibrated or accurate in all settings.

In practice, eVTA₀ takes the task instruction and a recent window of observations, five in the standard setting. A vision-language model then estimates the success probability. The training targets differ by transition type:

- For terminal transitions, the target is the observed outcome.
- For nonterminal transitions, the target is a slowly updated target model's prediction on the next observation history.

Fitting the current model to these targets propagates terminal supervision backward through the rollout. The authors also augment failed trajectories with soft terminal targets. These targets are estimated using a pretrained world model and successful-trajectory clusters.

For RL, the reward is the predicted success probability minus one. This avoids accumulating a positive per-step reward merely by taking longer, and it preserves the ordering and pairwise differences of the predictions.

### 3. Does better feedback change learning?

The authors trained the π₀.₅ policy with GRPO, giving each reward method the same 6,400 interaction episodes. On LIBERO-Long, suite success rose from 43.2% for the initial SFT policy to 54.0% with eVTA₀, a gain of 10.8 percentage points. SFT, or supervised fine-tuning, is the starting policy trained from demonstrations. Under this budget, eVTA₀ achieved the best average success rate across all four LIBERO suites. LIBERO-Long contains 10 tasks with 50 evaluation rollouts per task. The experiment measures final success after a matched interaction budget. It does not measure elapsed time or number of attempts.

### 4. Rewards that follow the policy

RLER repeats a loop: it collects policy rollouts, updates eVTA₀, and uses the adapted rewards for the next policy update. The authors tested it on two real-world tasks, each with 50 trials. Out-of-distribution (OOD) trials changed conditions such as object position, distance, rotation, or appearance.

- **Overall:** RLER's final success-rate improvements over the initial SFT policy were 20–26 percentage points.
- **OOD:** The improvements were 35–36 points.
- **Example:** In the shuttlecock task, OOD success rose from 9/20 trials with SFT to 16/20 after RLER round 2.

The authors also compared RLER with a fixed-eVTA₀ baseline. The fixed model was trained once on the same 100 accumulated rollouts, with matched reward-model and policy-training budgets. RLER beat this baseline by 8–12 points overall. This result is consistent with a benefit from updating rewards as the policy changes. It is evidence from two tasks, not proof of broad real-world generalization.

Real-world success rates for SFT, RLER rounds 1 and 2, and fixed eVTA₀, split into ID, OOD, and total. Carrot task, round 2 versus SFT: 68% versus 32% OOD, 84% versus 64% total. Shuttlecock task: 80% versus 45% OOD, 86% versus 60% total. Image sequences compare SFT failures with RLER successes in selected OOD trials.

The contribution is a reward model trained from policy rollouts and terminal outcomes, rather than from expert demonstrations or intermediate progress annotations. The demonstration-free claim applies only to the reward model. The policies still start from demonstration-trained SFT, which used 20 and 60 expert demonstrations for the two real-world tasks.
