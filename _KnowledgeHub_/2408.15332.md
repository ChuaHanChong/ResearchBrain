# What makes math problems hard for reinforcement learning: a case study

> [!summary] Summary
> This interdisciplinary work systematically investigates the challenges of applying reinforcement learning to hard mathematical problems, using the Andrews-Curtis conjecture as a case study. The research developed algorithmic enhancements for ultra-sparse reward environments, proposed a topological hardness measure, and achieved new mathematical results including the AC-trivialization of specific long-standing potential counterexamples and the full class of unknot presentations.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning struggles with vast search spaces and ultra-sparse reward signals inherent in abstract mathematical problem domains.
- Open mathematical conjectures, like the Andrews-Curtis conjecture, represent "needle in a haystack" search problems requiring the discovery of very long, rare sequences of operations.
- Current AI methods face difficulty in efficiently exploring these complex spaces and attributing credit over long action horizons.

## Method

- Leveraged the ==Andrews-Curtis== (AC) conjecture as a case study for "path-to-base search problems" using modified AC' moves, combining Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) RL, classical search (Greedy Search), and Transformer-based language modeling.
- Introduced algorithmic enhancements for RL such as "supermoves" and an adaptive action space to manage long horizons and sparse rewards.
- Developed a principled topological hardness measure and local analysis techniques to characterize problem difficulty.

## Results

- Classical Greedy Search (GS) significantly outperformed Breadth-First Search (BFS) on Miller-Schupp presentations (533/1190 vs 278/1190 solved), while an RL agent with variable horizon length trivialized two hard presentations requiring 195 and 381 AC' moves.
- The research proved new mathematical results for the Andrews-Curtis conjecture, including the AC-triviality of MS(1,w) for all w, length reducibility for the Akbulut-Kirby (AK) series, and the AC-triviality for all presentations derived from unknot diagrams.
- Language models trained without explicit labels learned embeddings that distinguish between solved and unsolved presentations, and predictive models achieved a **0.962** F1 score in classifying problem hardness based on topological features.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "hardness" of mathematical problems for reinforcement learning correlates with an intrinsic "linguistic structure" that can be learned by language models without explicit labels.
> - RL with variable horizon length discovers deeply hidden, long solution paths inaccessible to classical Greedy Search; dynamic action-space adaptation via "supermoves" is essential for efficiently exploring ultra-sparse reward environments.
> - Topological features derived from the problem's graph structure serve as highly predictive indicators of problem solvability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of artificial intelligence (particularly reinforcement learning) and pure mathematics, specifically combinatorial group theory. It addresses a critical frontier in AI research: the development of systems capable of complex reasoning and problem-solving in abstract domains, moving towards Artificial General Intelligence (AGI).

Recent successes of AI in complex games (Chess, Go) and scientific challenges (protein folding via AlphaFold) have demonstrated its capabilities in domains with well-defined rules and feedback. However, mathematical reasoning, especially tackling open conjectures, presents unique difficulties. The authors highlight two key challenges:
1. **Vast Search Spaces:** Mathematical problems often involve enormous search spaces for solutions or counterexamples.
2. **Ultra-Sparse Rewards:** Unlike typical games where rewards are frequent, in hard mathematical problems, the "solution" (e.g., a proof, a counterexample) is a rare "needle in a haystack," leading to an ultra-sparse reward signal, which notoriously hinders reinforcement learning.

The Andrews-Curtis (AC) conjecture, a long-standing open problem in combinatorial group theory concerning the trivialization of balanced presentations of the trivial group, serves as an ideal "case study." It embodies the "needle in a haystack" problem due to its potentially superexponential path lengths to trivialization, as evidenced by prior theoretical work. This makes it a formidable challenge for AI, especially traditional reinforcement learning algorithms that struggle with long horizons and sparse rewards.

The paper positions itself not just as an attempt to solve a specific math problem, but as a methodological contribution to AI. By tackling a complex, real-world mathematical conjecture, the authors aim to develop "new algorithms with adaptive capabilities that dynamically ‘learn how to learn’." This approach provides a "risk-free and cost-effective" environment to test and refine AI algorithms compared to direct applications to societal problems. The insights gained from understanding "what makes math problems hard for reinforcement learning" are expected to have broad implications for other sparse-reward search problems across scientific and engineering domains, thereby contributing to the fundamental capabilities required for AGI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations driving this research are multifaceted, combining both mathematical and AI-centric goals:

- **Understand Hardness in Mathematical Search Problems:** The primary objective is to systematically explore, from multiple perspectives, what makes mathematical problems hard for reinforcement learning agents. This involves analyzing the distribution of "hardness" within a family of problems and identifying key features that correlate with difficulty.
- **Develop Algorithmic Enhancements for RL:** Based on the insights into problem hardness, the authors aim to propose and implement algorithmic enhancements for reinforcement learning, particularly to address the challenges of long horizons and ultra-sparse rewards inherent in mathematical search problems. This includes developing concepts like "supermoves" and adaptive action spaces.
- **Propose a Principled Hardness Measure:** A key theoretical objective is to introduce a practical and principled topological hardness measure applicable to a broad class of "path-to-base search problems." This measure would provide a quantitative invariant to assess problem difficulty.
- **Address Open Mathematical Questions:** Beyond the methodological AI goals, a significant motivation is to make concrete contributions to the Andrews-Curtis conjecture by investigating its potential counterexamples. This involves:
 - Demonstrating length reducibility for previously open presentations (e.g., Akbulut-Kirby series).
 - Resolving various potential counterexamples in the Miller-Schupp series, including infinite subfamilies and specific hard instances.
 - Investigating the relationship between the standard and stable Andrews-Curtis conjectures and their connection to knot theory.
- **Advance Artificial General Intelligence (AGI):** The overarching motivation is to use mathematics as a rigorous and quantifiable domain for developing and testing self-improving AI systems that can reason as effectively as humans, thus paving the path toward AGI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-pronged, interdisciplinary approach combining classical search algorithms, reinforcement learning, language modeling, and topological data analysis, all centered around the Andrews-Curtis conjecture.

- **Andrews-Curtis (AC) Conjecture as a Case Study:**
 - The core problem is to transform a "balanced presentation of the trivial group" into the trivial presentation using a sequence of AC-moves.
 - The authors modify the standard AC moves (AC1-AC3) to "AC' moves" (AC'1-AC'2), primarily by combining inversion with concatenation to maintain a signal (length change) for algorithms, as standalone inversion leaves length invariant.
 - Two families of potential counterexamples are chosen: the Akbulut-Kirby (AK) series and the Miller-Schupp (MS) series. A dataset of 1190 MS presentations (n≤7, length(w)≤7) is created for systematic testing. A maximum relator length is enforced to keep the search space finite.

- **Classical Search Algorithms:**
 - **Breadth-First Search (BFS):** Implemented as a baseline, exploring states layer by layer up to 1 million nodes.
 - **Greedy Search (GS):** A variant of BFS that uses a priority queue ordered by presentation length (to minimize it), then path length (to prefer shorter paths among same-length presentations). Compared against BFS for performance.

- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - **Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation:**
 - **States (S):** Balanced presentations.
 - **Actions (A):** The 12 elementary AC' moves (composition of a relator with another, or conjugation by a generator/its inverse).
 - **Reward Function (R):** Sparse reward; -min(10, length(s_t+1)) for non-terminal states, and a large positive reward (1000) for reaching a trivial presentation. Clipping negative rewards helps stabilize training.
 - **Initial State Distribution (ρ):** A mixed distribution, preferentially sampling unsolved problems (3/4) over solved ones (1/4) to encourage exploration.
 - **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** Chosen as the RL algorithm due to its stability and performance. Details include using an actor-critic framework, clipped objective function ($L_{CLIP}$), Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), and entropy regularization.
 - **Horizon Length:** Explored both constant (200, 400, 2000) and variable horizon lengths (gradually increasing from 200 to 1200 during training) to manage the credit assignment problem in long-horizon tasks.

- **Algorithmic Enhancements ("The Cure"):**
 - **Supermoves:** The concept of composing elementary AC moves into larger "supermoves" is introduced to reduce effective path length for hard problems.
 - **Adaptive Action Space:** Proposes a meta-learning approach where an AI system dynamically selects and introduces/removes supermoves based on its encounter with "hard instances" (defined by increasing N, number of environment interactions, and path length). This aims for a "learn how to learn" paradigm.

- **Language Modeling:**
 - **Model:** A decoder-only Transformer model is used to analyze the "linguistic structure" of balanced presentations.
 - **Dataset Generation:** ~1.8 million AC-equivalent presentations are generated from the MS series, with a focus on creating an almost uniform distribution of presentation lengths (up to 128) to avoid bias.
 - **Analysis:** t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding) is applied to the learned embeddings of MS presentations to visualize clustering patterns and assess if the model can distinguish between "GS-solved" and "GS-unsolved" presentations without explicit labels.

- **Global Hardness Measure:**
 - **Concept:** A principled measure based on "l-increase" (minimum increase in presentation length during trivialization) is proposed.
 - **Formalization:** Connects this measure to the "barcode of the persistent reduced homology in degree 0 of the associated filtered based graph" from Topological Data Analysis (TDA). The absence of infinite bars in this barcode is noted as equivalent to the AC conjecture.
 - **Computation:** Approximated for AC and AC' trivialization problems by computing barcodes for induced subgraphs up to length L=16.

- **Local Analysis:**
 - **Neighborhood Sizes:** Investigates the distribution of the size of k-step neighborhoods (presentations reachable by k AC moves) for PPO-solved and PPO-unsolved presentations.
 - **Predictive Power of Local Features:** Uses a supervised learning approach (XGBoost Classifier) to predict whether a presentation is PPO-solved or PPO-unsolved based on various features: presentation length, neighborhood size, and topological features (e.g., barcode vector, persistence entropy) derived from the neighborhood graph.

- **Stable Andrews-Curtis Conjecture:**
 - Investigates the "stable" version of the conjecture (allowing addition/removal of trivial generators/relators).
 - Explores its connection to knot theory, specifically Wirtinger presentations of unknot diagrams, which are known to be stably AC-trivial.
 - Applies Lemma 11 ("Substitution and Removal") as a supermove to derive 2- and 3-generator presentations from higher-generator unknot presentations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields significant findings across all its methodological avenues:

- **Classical Search Performance:**
 - Greedy Search (GS) significantly outperforms Breadth-First Search (BFS) in AC-trivializing Miller-Schupp (MS) presentations. GS solved 533 presentations out of 1190, while BFS solved only 278.
 - GS successfully trivialized all MS(1, w) presentations in the dataset.

- **New Mathematical Results (Proved by Authors):**
 - **Theorem 2:** Proved that **MS(1, w) is AC-trivial for all w**. This generalizes an empirical observation from greedy search.
 - **Theorem 3:** Proved that **MS(n, w*) for w* = y^-1xyx^-1 is AC-trivial for all n**. This extends recent automated theorem proving results.
 - **Theorem 4:** Demonstrated that the **Akbulut-Kirby (AK) series AK(n) is AC-equivalent to a shorter presentation** (length n+11) for all n≥5, namely P(n, n-1) = ⟨x, y | x^-1yx = xyx^-1y, x = y^-(n-1)x^-1yxy⟩. This is a crucial finding, as AK(n) were long-standing potential counterexamples whose length reducibility was unknown. This also connects AK(n) to an infinite family of MS presentations (Theorem 6 and 7).
 - **Corollary 9:** As a consequence of Theorem 6, showed that **MS(2, w_k) and P(2, k) are AC-trivial**.
 - **Theorem 10:** An RL agent with a variable horizon length solved **two specific, hard Miller-Schupp presentations** that classical greedy search could not. These paths were very long (195 and 381 AC' moves), demonstrating RL's ability to find deeply hidden solutions.
 - **Theorem 20:** The presentation P(5, 2; 4/5), a potential counterexample from geometric origins, was trivialized in 49 steps by an RL agent.
 - **Theorem 16:** A major mathematical breakthrough showing that **all presentations constructed from Wirtinger presentations of unknot diagrams (as in Proposition 12 from MMS02) are not just stably AC-trivial, but are, in fact, AC-trivial**. This resolves a class of long-standing potential counterexamples to the standard AC conjecture.
 - **Misprint identified:** A subtle misprint in the classic [MMS02] paper regarding the Wirtinger presentation of a specific unknot diagram was identified, which affected the validity of one of their theorems.

- **Reinforcement Learning (PPO) Performance:**
 - PPO with constant horizon outperformed BFS but generally underperformed GS on the full dataset (e.g., T=200 solved 431 vs 533 for GS).
 - PPO was found to discover *shorter* trivializing sequences for many presentations already solved by GS.
 - The variable horizon length training strategy proved effective, enabling PPO to solve problems intractable for greedy search.

- **Algorithmic Enhancements (Supermoves):**
 - Empirical observations suggested that dynamic introduction/removal of supermoves is crucial. Optimal strategies require introducing diverse supermoves early, and later removing less useful ones. Performance improvements were modest in the current setup, highlighting the complexity of supermove selection.

- **Language Modeling Insights:**
 - A Transformer model, *trained without explicit hardness labels*, was able to learn embeddings that significantly distinguish between "GS-solved" and "GS-unsolved" presentations when visualized with t-SNE. This suggests an intrinsic "linguistic structure" or invariant within the balanced presentations that correlates with their hardness, and that this invariant is preserved under AC operations.

- **Global Hardness Measure:**
 - The proposed topological measure (barcode of persistent reduced homology in degree 0) provides a principled way to quantify problem hardness.
 - Empirical comparison of AC vs. AC' moves showed a consistent scaling factor for the number of edges and barcode properties (approximately 1.5), suggesting that the underlying problem complexity is maintained, albeit consistently scaled. The authors hypothesize that the absence of infinite bars in this barcode is equivalent to the AC conjecture itself.

- **Local Analysis and Predictive Power:**
 - **Neighborhood Sizes:** PPO-unsolved presentations are concentrated in a few specific (often maximal) 5-step neighborhood sizes, while PPO-solved presentations exhibit a broader, more uniform distribution. This indicates that local graph structure is a strong indicator of hardness.
 - **Predictive Modeling:** Predictive models built on neighborhood features achieved high F1 scores (0.962 for the optimal feature set). Topological features (barcode vector, persistence entropy) were particularly effective, outperforming basic length and even raw neighborhood size in predicting solvability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has profound implications across mathematics, artificial intelligence, and interdisciplinary scientific discovery:

- **Advancement in Mathematics:**
 - **Resolved Open Problems:** The paper provides concrete AC-trivializations for long-standing potential counterexamples (e.g., AK(n) length reduction, specific hard MS instances, and the entire MS(1,w) family). Crucially, it definitively proves that a large class of presentations derived from unknot diagrams, previously considered only stably AC-trivial, are in fact AC-trivial under the standard moves. This is a significant step towards understanding the AC conjecture.
 - **New Conjectures:** The paper formulates new mathematical conjectures (Conjecture 18 and 19) that further guide future research in group theory and the AC conjecture.
 - **Tool for Discovery:** It demonstrates the power of computational methods, particularly reinforcement learning, as a genuine tool for *mathematical discovery*, leading to explicit proofs and challenging long-held assumptions.

- **Advancement in Artificial Intelligence:**
 - **Tackling Ultra-Sparse Rewards:** The work provides a compelling case study and methodological advancements for RL agents operating in environments with extremely sparse rewards and long horizons, a critical challenge in broader AI applications.
 - **Adaptive Learning Systems:** The proposal of adaptive action spaces and the concept of "learning how to learn" based on problem hardness distribution is a novel contribution to meta-learning and self-improving AI systems, moving towards AGI.
 - **Hardness Quantification:** The introduction and empirical validation of a topological hardness measure offers a quantitative framework for characterizing complex search spaces, enabling more informed algorithm design and evaluation.
 - **Linguistic Structure in Math:** The finding that language models can implicitly learn distinctions between "hard" and "easy" mathematical objects based on their "syntactic" structure opens new avenues for leveraging LLMs in mathematical reasoning and automated theorem proving.

- **Interdisciplinary Impact:**
 - **Bridging Disciplines:** This research exemplifies how deep mathematical problems can serve as fertile ground for developing cutting-edge AI techniques, fostering a powerful synergy between pure mathematics, computer science, and theoretical physics.
 - **Generalizable Framework:** The insights and proposed methodologies for identifying and adapting to "hard instances" are not limited to the Andrews-Curtis conjecture. They could be generalized to other "needle in a haystack" problems in diverse scientific domains, such as materials discovery, drug design, or quantum computing, where optimal solutions are rare and costly to find.
 - **Efficient Resource Utilization:** The emphasis on achieving results with "modest computational resources" highlights the value of algorithmic sophistication over brute-force power, a crucial consideration for sustainable and accessible scientific research.

In summary, this paper pushes the boundaries of both mathematical understanding of the Andrews-Curtis conjecture and the capabilities of reinforcement learning in complex, abstract problem domains. It lays foundational groundwork for future self-improving AI systems capable of tackling open-ended scientific discovery.
