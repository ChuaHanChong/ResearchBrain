# Learning to Get Up Across Morphologies: Zero-Shot Recovery with a Unified Humanoid Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a unified deep reinforcement learning policy for humanoid robots to recover from falls. The policy is trained across multiple robot morphologies without explicit robot-specific data, demonstrating zero-shot transfer to unseen robot designs and enhancing fall recovery capabilities for morphologies that previously struggled.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots require robust fall recovery skills for sustained autonomy in dynamic and unpredictable environments.
- Traditional fall recovery methods, including specialized DRL, are morphology-specific, labor-intensive, and difficult to generalize across different robot designs.
- The high development cost and limited reusability of existing fall recovery solutions hinder the scalability and deployment of diverse humanoid robot platforms.

## Method

- A unified Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policy was developed, trained across a diverse suite of seven humanoid robot morphologies from the RoboCup community.
- A morphology-agnostic control interface, observation space, and reward structure were designed to allow the single policy to learn and adapt to various robot characteristics without explicit morphological identifiers.
- Extensive domain randomization of physical properties and varied episode initialization states were employed during training to ensure robustness and facilitate zero-shot transfer and sim-to-real capabilities.

## Results

- The unified policy demonstrated zero-shot transfer to an unseen robot, achieving a 72% success rate on Wolfgang, while exhibiting varying performance on other held-out morphologies (17-42% success).
- Compared to specialist policies, the shared policy achieved a significant +61% increase in success rate for the NUGUS robot, though it incurred moderate performance deficits (up to -20%) on some other morphologies.
- Morphological scaling analysis showed that zero-shot success improved with an increased number of well-chosen training morphologies (up to 86% on Wolfgang with four robots), but performance could be negatively impacted by including difficult or outlier morphologies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single DRL policy can learn to perform complex fall recovery across diverse humanoid morphologies and exhibit zero-shot transfer to unseen robots, extending generalization beyond simpler locomotion tasks.
> - Shared training can enable fall recovery capabilities for morphologies that are challenging or impossible to train in isolation (e.g., NUGUS), indicating benefits of collective experience and knowledge distillation.
> - Effective zero-shot generalization is critically dependent on the composition of the training set, requiring continuous and overlapping coverage of the morphological space rather than just a collection of disparate robot types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The ability of humanoid robots to recover from falls is a fundamental and mission-critical skill, especially in dynamic and unpredictable environments like RoboCup soccer, where collisions and balance loss are common. Traditionally, fall recovery policies were either meticulously hand-coded using key frame-based (KFB) sequences or developed using Model Predictive Control (MPC). While reliable in specific scenarios, these methods are notoriously labor-intensive, difficult to tune, and fragile when faced with novel initial conditions or variations in robot morphology.

Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) have offered a more adaptive paradigm for fall recovery. Works like Yang et al. and FRASA (Fall Recovery and Stand Up agent) have successfully demonstrated robust get-up behaviors for individual robots. However, a significant limitation of these existing DRL approaches is their robot-specific nature; each new robot morphology requires an entirely separate training process, which is time-consuming and resource-intensive.

This paper addresses a critical gap in the research landscape: the development of a *unified* DRL policy capable of generalizing fall recovery skills across multiple, diverse humanoid robot morphologies without requiring individual retraining. While the field has seen progress in "generalizing" robot behaviors across morphologies for *locomotion* (e.g., NerveNet, URMA, ModuMorph), these often rely on explicit structural information (e.g., joint graphs, morphology vectors) or focus on less dynamic tasks than fall recovery. This work differentiates itself by tackling the inherently more complex and dynamic challenge of fall recovery, and doing so with a "morphology-blind" approach that does not explicitly feed robot-specific parameters to the policy.

Furthermore, the research builds upon the established necessity of robust sim-to-real transfer, a common challenge in DRL. It incorporates extensive domain randomization techniques, similar to those employed in landmark works like Tan et al. and HoST, to ensure the learned policy's robustness to real-world variability. By extending generalization from locomotion to the complex skill of fall recovery using a unified, morphology-agnostic policy, this paper significantly advances the state-of-the-art towards truly generalist humanoid robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether a single Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policy, trained across a diverse suite of humanoid robot morphologies, can learn to perform fall recovery and then exhibit zero-shot transfer capabilities to unseen robots without any retraining. Underlying this main objective are two key hypotheses:
1. Can a unified DRL policy effectively learn to recover from falls across multiple humanoid robots with different physical characteristics?
2. Does increasing the morphological diversity of robots included during training lead to more generalizable strategies that enable the controller to adapt to novel robot geometries?

The motivation behind pursuing these objectives is multifaceted and highly practical:
- **Addressing a Critical Skill Gap:** Fall recovery is not merely a safety feature but a core competency for sustained autonomy and competitive success in dynamic environments. A robot unable to get up quickly is effectively out of commission, as frequently seen in RoboCup.
- **Improving Scalability and Reusability:** Current methods for fall recovery, whether handcrafted or DRL-based, demand significant engineering effort for each new robot morphology. This paper aims to reduce this substantial cost of deployment by demonstrating a single policy that can serve multiple platforms.
- **Paving the Way for Generalist Humanoid Control:** By proving the feasibility of morphology-agnostic control for a complex skill like fall recovery, the research lays a fundamental groundwork for developing more general-purpose humanoid skills that are not tied to specific robot designs. This vision of "generalist" policies could revolutionize how humanoid robots are developed and deployed.
- **Leveraging DRL for Adaptability:** DRL offers the promise of more adaptable and robust behaviors compared to traditional control methods, and this work seeks to unlock this potential for a broader range of robotic embodiments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core approach of this research involved modifying an existing single-robot DRL fall recovery framework (FRASA) to operate across multiple humanoid morphologies. This required careful design of a shared control interface and a robust training regimen.

**Humanoid Robot Suite:**
Seven distinct humanoid robot models from the RoboCup community were selected for training and evaluation. These models (Bez1, OP3-Rot, Bez2, Bez3, Sigmaban, Wolfgang, NUGUS) exhibited significant diversity in physical characteristics, including height (0.48–0.81 m), weight (2.8–7.9 kg), and degrees of freedom (18–20 DoF). To ensure compatibility with the MuJoCo simulator and a consistent control interface, these models were converted to MJCF format. Crucial standardization steps included rotating specific elbow joints, adjusting initial joint angles for stability, standardizing naming conventions for body links and actuators, and introducing consistent IMU and foot-frame reference points.

**Unified Action Space:**
A morphology-independent action space was defined, leveraging a reduced set of five common pitch joints across all robots: shoulder, elbow, hip, knee, and ankle. The policy outputs desired joint angle *changes* (˙q_desired) every 50 ms, which are then integrated to calculate the next target joint angles. This simplified, symmetric action space, combined with the simulator's enforcement of joint limits, removed the need for robot-specific scaling within the policy.

**Extended Observation Space:**
The observation space was designed to be morphology-agnostic, providing the policy with sufficient information to infer its state without explicit morphological identifiers. It included:
- Joint positions (q_t) and velocities (˙q_t) for the controlled joints.
- Desired joint positions (q_des_t).
- Trunk Euler angles (θ_rpy_t) and angular velocities (˙θ_rpy_t) across roll, pitch, and yaw axes to capture full body orientation.
- Head height (h_head_t) above the feet, serving as a morphology-independent metric to incentivize standing.
- The previous action (a_t-1) for temporal context.
A key aspect is the deliberate exclusion of any explicit morphological descriptors, forcing the policy to learn to distinguish robot dynamics solely from observed state changes.

**Morphology-Agnostic Reward Structure:**
The reward function was crafted using general physical criteria applicable to any humanoid, eschewing morphology-specific target poses. It comprised several components:
- **Upright Posture Reward (R_Up):** Exponentially rewards proximity of head height to a target value (1.0).
- **Pitch Alignment Reward (R_Pitch):** Rewards a vertically aligned torso (pitch close to zero), activated only after the robot has partially risen (h_head > 0.4) to avoid impeding early exploration.
- **Penalty Rewards (R_vel, R_var, R_collision):** Small exponential penalties for high joint velocities, abrupt action changes, and self-collisions, gently regularizing the policy towards smoother, safer motions.

**Robust Episode Initialization and Termination:**
To train the policy for versatile recovery, each episode began with the robot in a randomly fallen configuration. The torso orientation and joint angles were displaced up to ±90° from the initial position, simulating face-up, face-down, and side falls. Episodes lasted a maximum of 10 seconds. Early termination occurred if the robot entered an unrecoverable state (torso pitch exceeding 135° or angular velocity > 25°/s), matching safety cutoffs used in prior work. Success was defined by the robot standing upright and maintaining that posture for the remaining episode duration.

**Enhanced Domain Randomization:**
Extensive domain randomization was crucial for promoting robustness and facilitating sim-to-real transfer. At the start of each training episode, various physical properties were randomized within realistic ranges: mass and center of mass (±10%), ground and actuator friction (±15%), battery voltage/motor gains (±10%), and sensor orientation (IMU offset ±3°). This forces the policy to learn robust strategies applicable across a distribution of dynamics.

**DRL Algorithm and Training Enhancements:**
The policy was trained using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), augmented with the CrossQ algorithm for improved sample efficiency. The GPU-accelerated Stable Baselines X framework was employed to expedite training. Training involved 16 parallel MuJoCo environments, with each episode randomly sampling one of the seven robot models. The neural network architecture consisted of three hidden layers (512-512-256) to handle the increased complexity. Each policy type was trained on 10 random seeds for 600,000 timesteps (approximately 1.5 hours on the specified hardware), and evaluations were based on 100 episodes per seed to ensure statistical robustness, with 95% confidence intervals calculated via a 10k iteration bootstrap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented three primary experiments to validate the unified policy and analyze its generalization capabilities.

**Leave-One-Out (LOO) Zero-Shot Generalization:**
This experiment assessed the policy's ability to transfer to an unseen morphology without retraining. Shared policies were trained on six robots and evaluated on all seven. The diagonal entries of the LOO heatmap, representing zero-shot performance on the held-out robot, showed varying success. Wolfgang exhibited strong zero-shot transfer with a mean success rate of 72 ± 21%, while other unseen morphologies achieved lower rates (17–42%). This indicates that some morphological outliers are more challenging for zero-shot generalization. However, off-diagonal entries (trained on six, tested on one of the six) demonstrated high robustness, with 21 out of 42 entries exceeding 80% success. Notably, smaller robots' performance improved when taller robots were excluded from the training set, suggesting that certain morphologies provide more transferable experience. The NUGUS robot, which failed to learn get-up in isolation, greatly benefited from shared training (81 ± 6%), illustrating that difficult morphologies can acquire skills through knowledge distillation from shared experience. The findings confirm the possibility of zero-shot transfer for most kid-size humanoids but highlight the importance of including morphological outliers in the training set to improve worst-case generalization.

**Shared Policy vs. Specialist Policies:**
This experiment compared the performance of the best shared policy (trained on six robots) against individually trained "specialist" policies for each robot. The results showed:
- **Significant Gain on NUGUS:** The shared policy achieved a remarkable +61% increase in success rate on NUGUS compared to its specialist counterpart, with high statistical significance (p < 0.001). This is a pivotal finding, demonstrating that shared training can enable capabilities that single-robot training fails to achieve.
- **Moderate Deficits:** The shared policy experienced moderate performance deficits on Wolfgang (−20%, p < 0.05) and Bez2 (−18%, p < 0.05).
- **Minor Deficits:** For Bez1, OP3-Rot, Bez3, and Sigmaban, the deficits were minor, generally less than 15%.
- **Overall Robustness:** Despite some individual performance costs, the shared policy still maintained an overall high success rate, exceeding 58% on every robot and surpassing 80% on four out of seven morphologies.
These results underscore that the modest performance cost of generalization is often outweighed by the benefits of robustness, transferability, and the ability to unlock behaviors on challenging morphologies, making the shared policy a scalable and robust alternative.

**Morphological Scaling Analysis:**
This analysis investigated how the number and diversity of training morphologies influence zero-shot performance on two held-out robots: Sigmaban (midsize, moderate difficulty) and Wolfgang (high LOO zero-shot transfer rate).
- **Wolfgang:** Zero-shot success on Wolfgang steadily increased from 37% with one training robot to 86% with four well-chosen morphologies. However, performance slightly dipped when more difficult or outlier morphologies (NUGUS, Bez3) were added to the training set, suggesting that these might introduce conflicting strategies or dilute learning time.
- **Sigmaban:** Initial success rates were low (<15%) with one or two training robots. Introducing more "well-chosen" intermediate morphologies raised success to 40-46%. Similar to Wolfgang, adding difficult or poorly compatible morphs (NUGUS, Bez1) led to stagnation and wider confidence intervals, indicating inconsistent learning.
- **Importance of Diversity Type:** A training set composed solely of "extreme" morphologies (Bez3, Bez1, NUGUS) yielded very poor generalization (≤ 16%). This highlights that mere diversity is insufficient; effective generalization requires *continuous, overlapping coverage* of the morphology space, not just a collection of disparate robots.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in humanoid robot control, particularly in the domain of robust autonomy and generalizability.

- **Pioneering Unified Fall Recovery:** This paper introduces the first unified DRL policy capable of zero-shot fall recovery across a diverse set of humanoid robot morphologies. This extends the frontier of generalization from less dynamic tasks like locomotion to the highly complex and critical skill of fall recovery, which involves dynamic whole-body interactions and precise coordination.
- **Enhanced Practicality and Scalability:** By demonstrating that a single policy can effectively control multiple robots without individual retraining, this work drastically reduces the engineering effort and time required to deploy new humanoid platforms or manage heterogeneous robot teams. This is especially impactful for competitive environments like RoboCup, where teams often use different robot models or face hardware variations.
- **Unlocking New Capabilities:** The finding that a shared policy can not only match but sometimes surpass specialist policies, notably enabling fall recovery for morphologies like NUGUS where specialist training failed, is profound. It suggests that collective experience can lead to more robust and versatile skills that are difficult to learn in isolation, akin to knowledge transfer or multi-task learning benefits.
- **Foundation for Generalist Humanoid AI:** This research lays a crucial groundwork for developing "generalist" humanoid skills. If a complex, dynamic skill like fall recovery can be made morphology-agnostic, it opens the door for a broader range of general-purpose behaviors, moving beyond robot-specific controllers towards truly adaptive and versatile robotic intelligence.
- **Guidance for Training Set Design:** The morphological scaling analysis offers valuable insights into effective training strategies. It clearly demonstrates that generalization is not simply a function of the *number* of robots but crucially depends on *which* robots are included to provide continuous and overlapping coverage of the morphology space. This informs future work on curriculum design for multi-robot learning.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** Making the software open-source is a commendable practice that fosters reproducibility, collaboration, and accelerates further research in morphology-agnostic control.

Looking forward, the immediate impact lies in its potential for real-world deployment on physical humanoid robots, building on the extensive domain randomization employed to facilitate sim-to-real transfer. Future work could also explore incorporating explicit morphology conditioning to further enhance generalization across a wider, potentially more disparate, range of robot designs, or integrate techniques like Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to refine recovery motions for more human-like aesthetics. Overall, this work represents a significant step towards more autonomous, adaptable, and cost-effective humanoid robot systems.
