# Protection Levels for Vision-Based Pose Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> Extends residual-based RAIM to vision-based aircraft pose estimation, deriving per-axis protection levels for the nonlinear Perspective-n-Point problem. It linearizes and whitens the keypoint measurement model, enumerates keypoint-fault hypotheses, allocates integrity risk, and adds a worst-case failure-mode slope to a nominal Gaussian margin. On a runway case study, along-track bounds fall from over 400 m at four keypoints to below 26 m at thirty.

## Problem

- Prior vision-based pose pipelines added RAIM-style fault detection but never computed protection levels, leaving the quantitative error bound that closes the aviation integrity argument absent.
- Classical and uncertainty-aware PnP solvers give covariances or conformal sets, yet none allocate integrity risk across fault hypotheses or bound undetected-fault error against an alert limit.
- Integrity monitoring was extended to lidar, map-based and fused camera localization, but not to native learned-keypoint plus nonlinear PnP pipelines covering all six pose degrees of freedom.

## Method

- A ==canonical-form reduction== casts vision-based pose estimation into **Vision-Based RAIM**'s linear model: ==Taylor-linearize== the projection function about the pose estimate, inject a ==keypoint fault selector==, then ==whiten== by the measurement covariance factor.
- ==Keypoint fault hypotheses== indexed by faulted-keypoint subsets, with priors from an independent per-keypoint failure probability, and detectability constrained to subsets satisfying the ==redundancy condition== on residual degrees of freedom.
- Algorithm 1 computes per-axis ==protection levels==: worst-case failure-mode slope times the missed-detection non-centrality from inverting the ==non-central chi-square==, maximized over hypotheses, plus a Gaussian no-fault margin.

## Results

- On a **3 km** runway with **4** corner keypoints and **1 px** noise, full-pose along-track protection level exceeds **700 m** at **2 km**, versus **24-52 m** cross-track and elevation.
- Position-only estimation with known attitude shrinks bounds roughly an order of magnitude: along-track below **140 m**, cross-track about **2.5 m**, elevation about **6 m** at **2 km**.
- Adding keypoints drops the along-track protection level from over **400 m** at **4** keypoints to under **26 m** at **30**, matching the predicted inverse-square-root asymptote.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Protection levels scale linearly with keypoint noise, so tightening a predictor's calibrated pixel uncertainty buys operational availability directly, not merely better average accuracy.
> - Redundancy helps unevenly: the fault term decays faster than the nominal term with keypoint count, so nominal noise governs the asymptotic bound.
> - Estimating orientation jointly costs an order of magnitude in position bounds; fixing attitude externally or adding features constraining roll and yaw pays more than better solvers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-based navigation systems are being explored as a complementary technology to Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) for various aviation applications, particularly during aircraft approach and landing. These systems, which perform vision-based pose estimation (VBPE), offer an alternative in environments where GNSS signals may be denied or degraded. However, for integration into safety-critical avionics, VBPE systems must provide not only accurate pose estimates but also robust integrity guarantees.

In the context of civil aviation, integrity monitoring is a critical operational requirement, typically enforced at runtime using protection levels (PLs). For GNSS-based navigation, Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring (RAIM) is a standard framework that utilizes measurement redundancy to detect faults and compute PLs. These PLs provide a probabilistic upper bound on pose error, designed to remain valid even in the presence of undetected system faults. The integrity of a navigation system is then assessed by comparing its computed PL to a predefined alert limit, which specifies the maximum tolerable pose error for safe operation.

While previous research has focused on various aspects of VBPE, including classical PnP solvers for computational efficiency and robustness (e.g., RANSAC), and uncertainty-aware PnP methods (e.g., CEPPnP, conformal prediction sets), these approaches generally do not provide integrity guarantees in the specific aviation-style format of PLs. Existing methods for VBPE often provide calibrated uncertainty bounds or certified uncertainty sets but typically do not explicitly model monitored fault hypotheses, allocate integrity risk, or produce a runtime bound directly comparable to an operational alert limit.

Recent work has begun to extend integrity monitoring concepts beyond standalone GNSS to other perception-based systems, including lidar, multi-sensor Kalman filters, camera localization, and Graph-SLAM. Notably, Valentin et al. (2025) adapted residual-based RAIM for fault detection in a vision-based landing system that uses neural-network keypoint predictors. However, this prior work did not complete the integrity argument by computing PLs that explicitly account for undetected faults.

This paper addresses this specific gap by adapting the RAIM framework, particularly its PL computation methodology, to the domain of VBPE. It focuses on a pipeline that begins with 2D keypoint measurements from learned computer vision models, explicitly models keypoint faults, and produces a runtime pose-level bound suitable for aviation integrity assessment. This work aims to bridge the theoretical and practical gap between advanced computer vision techniques for pose estimation and the rigorous integrity requirements for safety-critical aviation applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and implement a methodology for computing Protection Levels (PLs) for vision-based pose estimation (VBPE) in an aviation context. Specifically, the work aims to derive PLs that cover all six degrees of freedom (6-DOF) of aircraft pose (position and orientation), providing probabilistic bounds on pose error that remain valid even when system faults are present but go undetected by the monitoring system.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Ensuring Safety in Critical Aviation Operations:** Vision-based navigation is a promising complement to GNSS for aircraft approach and landing, especially when GNSS signals are unavailable or degraded. However, the inherent susceptibility of computer vision models to subtle failures (e.g., misidentified features, illumination artifacts, or model misbehavior) means that their integration into safety-critical avionics necessitates stringent integrity guarantees. Without such guarantees, a system might provide "hazardous misleading information" (HMI), where an undetected error in the pose estimate exceeds safe operating limits.
- **Meeting Certification Requirements:** International and national aviation standards (e.g., ICAO, FAA) mandate the use of integrity monitoring, specifically through PLs, to ensure the trustworthiness of navigation systems. PLs provide a real-time, data-driven assessment of when a navigation system's output can be relied upon, by comparing the computed PL against a pre-defined alert limit. This is a critical step for the certification and operational deployment of any new navigation technology in aviation.
- **Addressing a Gap in Existing VBPE Integrity Frameworks:** While fault detection mechanisms have been adapted from RAIM to VBPE, the quantitative error bounds (PLs) that account for undetected faults were previously absent. Existing uncertainty-aware PnP or certification methods, while providing some form of uncertainty quantification, do not explicitly incorporate aviation-specific concepts such as integrity risk allocation across fault hypotheses or comparison against operational alert limits. This paper aims to "close the integrity argument" by providing these explicit, fault-tolerant bounds.
- **Optimizing System Availability:** Overly conservative PLs, while maintaining integrity, can lead to reduced system availability (the fraction of time the system can be safely used). Therefore, a key motivation is to derive PLs that are as tight as possible while still ensuring the specified integrity risk, thereby maximizing the operational utility of vision-based navigation systems.
- **Providing Design Insights:** The research aims to analyze the derived PL equations to characterize key properties specific to the VBPE setting. This includes understanding how factors such as distance to the runway, the number of keypoints used, and the magnitude of measurement noise affect the size and behavior of the PLs. Such insights are valuable for system designers to optimize sensor configurations, processing pipelines, and operational procedures.
- **Illustrative Validation:** The work is motivated to make these theoretical and analytical findings tangible by validating them on a representative runway case study, demonstrating the practical tradeoffs and implications for real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented adapts the Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring (RAIM) framework, originally developed for GNSS, to the domain of Vision-Based Pose Estimation (VBPE). This adaptation involves modeling keypoint measurements from a computer vision pipeline, defining fault hypotheses pertinent to visual features, and transforming the nonlinear pose estimation problem into a linear canonical form suitable for RAIM's probabilistic integrity assessment.

**A. Vision-Based Pose Estimation (VBPE) Formulation:**
The VBPE problem is formulated as a Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem.
1. **PnP Problem:** The system estimates the aircraft's pose (position and orientation, denoted as $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^3 \times \text{SO}(3)$) relative to a set of known 3D reference points ($\xi_k$, e.g., runway corners) by matching their 2D pixel projections in an image. The projection function, $\text{proj}_C(\xi_k | \beta)$, maps 3D points to 2D pixel coordinates given camera intrinsics $C$.
2. **Keypoint Predictions and Noise Model:** A computer vision model (e.g., a neural network) provides noisy 2D pixel predictions ($\hat{\mu}_k$) for each keypoint, along with an associated predictive uncertainty ($\hat{\Sigma}_k$). These are interpreted as a nominal Gaussian measurement model, $\mathbf{y}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_k, \hat{\Sigma}_k)$, with departures from this model considered as faults.
3. **Least-Squares Pose Estimation:** The aircraft's pose estimate ($\hat{\beta}$) is obtained by minimizing the sum of squared reprojection residuals, weighted by the measurement uncertainties ($\sum_{k=1}^{n_{kp}} \| \hat{\mathbf{y}}_k - \text{proj}_C(\xi_k | \beta) \|^2_{\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}_k}$).
4. **Pose Uncertainty:** The uncertainty of the pose estimate ($\hat{\Sigma}_\beta$) is derived by linearizing the projection function around $\hat{\beta}$ and propagating the per-keypoint uncertainties. This results in $\hat{\Sigma}_\beta = (\mathbf{H}_{\text{raw}}^\top \Sigma_{\text{meas}}^{-1} \mathbf{H}_{\text{raw}})^{-1}$, where $\mathbf{H}_{\text{raw}}$ is the Jacobian matrix of the projection function with respect to the pose parameters.

**B. Adaptation to Canonical RAIM Form:**
To apply RAIM, the VBPE measurement model is linearized and whitened.
1. **Linearization:** The projection function $\text{proj}_C(\xi_k | \beta)$ is approximated using a first-order Taylor expansion around the current pose estimate $\hat{\beta}$. Stacking these expansions for all keypoints yields $\Delta \mathbf{y} \approx \mathbf{H}_{\text{raw}} \Delta \beta + \mathbf{v}_{\text{raw}}$, where $\Delta \mathbf{y}$ is the measurement deviation, $\Delta \beta$ is the pose perturbation from $\hat{\beta}$ to the true pose $\beta^*$, and $\mathbf{v}_{\text{raw}}$ is the raw measurement noise.
2. **Fault Injection:** Under a fault hypothesis $H_S$ (where $S$ is the set of faulted keypoints), a fault vector $\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{A}_{S,\text{raw}} \delta_S$ is added to the measurement model, representing structural errors not captured by the nominal noise model. $\mathbf{A}_{S,\text{raw}}$ is a selector matrix that maps the fault magnitude $\delta_S$ to the corresponding pixel coordinates. The model becomes $\Delta \mathbf{y} \approx \mathbf{H}_{\text{raw}} \Delta \beta + \mathbf{v}_{\text{raw}} + \mathbf{A}_{S,\text{raw}} \delta_S$.
3. **Whitening:** The entire equation is transformed by multiplying with $\mathbf{L}^{-1}$, where $\Sigma_{\text{meas}} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top$. This produces the canonical linear RAIM form: $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{H} \beta + \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{A}_S \delta_S$, where $\mathbf{z}$ is the whitened reprojection error, $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{\text{raw}}$ is the whitened observation matrix, $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{\text{raw}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ is whitened noise, and $\mathbf{A}_S = \mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{S,\text{raw}}$ is the whitened fault selector.

**C. Fault Hypotheses:**
Fault hypotheses $H_S$ enumerate structural errors in keypoint predictions, where $S$ denotes the subset of faulted keypoints. A fault hypothesis is detectable only if the remaining unfaulted measurements are sufficient to estimate the pose ($2(n_{kp} - |S|) \geq m$). The system monitors up to $s_{\text{max}}$ simultaneous keypoint faults. For a four-corner runway with 6-DOF pose estimation ($n_{kp}=4, m=6$), only single-keypoint faults ($s_{\text{max}}=1$) can be monitored. Prior probabilities for these hypotheses, $P(H_S)$, are assigned assuming independent per-keypoint failure probability $P_{kp}$.

**D. Protection Level (PL) Computation (Algorithm 1):**
The PL computation for each pose axis $j$ combines contributions from nominal noise and potential undetected faults.
1. **Risk Allocation:** The total integrity risk budget $I_{\text{REQ}}$ is split into $I_{H_0}$ (for the no-fault hypothesis) and $I_{H_S}$ (for all fault hypotheses). A baseline equal split ($I_{H_0} = I_{H_S} = I_{\text{REQ}}/2$) is used in the absence of calibrated fault statistics. $I_{H_S}$ is further allocated among specific fault hypotheses proportional to their prior probabilities $P(H_S)$.
2. **Detection Threshold ($\tau$):** The threshold for the residual norm test is determined from the continuity allocation $C_{\text{REQ}}$ (assumed equal to the probability of false alarm, $P_{\text{FA}}$) and the no-fault prior $P(H_0)$. If the squared residual exceeds $\tau^2$, a fault is detected.
3. **Worst-Case Fault Direction:** For each monitored fault hypothesis $H_S$, the worst-case fault direction $\bar{\mathbf{f}}_S$ is identified. This direction maximizes the fault-induced pose error along axis $j$ while minimizing its detectability, quantified by the failure-mode slope $\bar{g}_{S,j} = \frac{|e_j^\top \mathbf{H}^\dagger \mathbf{f}|}{\|(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^\dagger)\mathbf{f}\|_2}$. A closed-form solution for $\bar{g}^2_{S,j}$ is derived.
4. **Maximum Undetected Fault Magnitude ($\bar{\lambda}$):** The non-centrality scale $\bar{\lambda}$ represents the largest fault magnitude that could be missed by the detection test with a specified missed detection probability ($P_{\text{MD}}$). $P_{\text{MD}}$ is computed from the allocated integrity risk $I_{H_S}$ and $P(H_S)$, and $\bar{\lambda}$ is found by numerically inverting the non-central $\chi^2$ CDF.
5. **Final Protection Level ($PL_j$):**
 - **No-fault contribution:** $PL_{0,j} = k_{H_0} \sigma_{\beta,j}$, where $\sigma_{\beta,j}$ is the standard deviation of the pose error along axis $j$ under nominal noise, and $k_{H_0}$ is a Gaussian quantile derived from $I_{H_0}$.
 - **Fault-induced contribution:** For each $H_S$, this is $\bar{g}_{S,j} \cdot \bar{\lambda} + k_{H_0} \sigma_{\beta,j}$.
 - The overall $PL_j$ is the maximum of these contributions across all monitored hypotheses $S \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{mon}}$ (including $H_0$, where $\bar{g}_{\emptyset,j} = 0$).

**E. Simulation Setup:**
Experiments are conducted using a simulated runway scenario (50m wide, 3km long) with four keypoints at its corners. A camera (4000x3000 px, f=25mm, 3.4μm pixel size) is positioned 1km from the runway threshold at 150m elevation. Gaussian noise with $\sigma=1$ px is added to pixel coordinates. The PnP problem is solved using Levenberg-Marquardt. Default integrity parameters include a continuity-risk budget $C_{\text{REQ}} = 10^{-5}$, an integrity-risk budget $I_{\text{REQ}} = 10^{-5}$, and a per-keypoint failure prior $P_{kp} = 10^{-4}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents analytical characterizations of Protection Level (PL) behavior and validates these findings through simulations on a runway scenario.

**A. Protection Level Characteristics (Analytical)**
1. **Noise Magnitude:** The PL for any pose axis scales linearly with the standard deviation of the keypoint measurement noise ($\sigma_{\text{scale}}$). This relationship applies to both the nominal-noise component and the fault-induced component of the PL. Thus, $PL \propto \sigma_{\text{scale}}$. This highlights the importance of precise keypoint detection.
2. **Number of Keypoints ($n_{kp}$):**
 - The nominal-noise term of the PL, which depends on the pose covariance $\sigma_{\beta,j}$, decreases asymptotically with the inverse square root of the number of keypoints, i.e., $PL_{NF} \propto n_{kp}^{-1/2}$.
 - The fault-induced term of the PL, which combines the failure-mode slope and non-centrality parameter, decays faster, specifically as $PL_S \propto n_{kp}^{-3/4}$. This is attributed to the slope term scaling as $n_{kp}^{-1}$ and the non-centrality parameter scaling as $n_{kp}^{1/4}$.
 - As a result, for a large number of keypoints, the nominal-noise contribution becomes the dominant factor governing the asymptotic behavior of the PL, leading to an overall decay of $PL_j \propto n_{kp}^{-1/2}$.
3. **Position-only vs. Full-pose Estimation:** When the aircraft's attitude is known externally and only its 3-DOF position is estimated, the resulting PLs for position components are significantly tighter compared to those for full 6-DOF pose estimation. This is because fixing the orientation parameters reduces the covariance of the remaining position parameters and increases the residual degrees of freedom for fault detection. This effect is particularly pronounced in head-on viewing geometries where along-track translation and pitch can induce similar pixel motions, leading to high correlations in the 6-DOF estimation problem.

**B. Experimental Validation (Runway Case Study)**
The simulation experiments used a representative runway scenario to demonstrate these characteristics.
1. **Geometric Sensitivity - Distance to Runway:**
 - **Full 6-DOF Pose:** As the aircraft approaches the runway along a 3° glide slope, PLs generally decrease. However, the along-track (depth) PL remains notably large, exceeding 700 m at a distance of 2 km. This is due to the near-degenerate geometry for depth estimation from a head-on viewpoint, making along-track position highly unobservable. Cross-track and elevation PLs are substantially tighter (24–52 m). For orientation, pitch is well-constrained (< 0.5°), but roll and yaw PLs remain relatively high (> 4°) due to the limited baseline provided by only four coplanar keypoints.
 - **Position-only (3-DOF) Pose:** For position-only estimation, all PLs are reduced by approximately an order of magnitude. The along-track PL at 2 km distance drops below 140 m, and cross-track/elevation PLs are approximately 2.5 m and 6 m, respectively. This substantial reduction highlights the challenge that unobservable orientation (especially roll and yaw) poses for position accuracy in 6-DOF estimation from this geometry.
2. **Effect of Measurement Redundancy (Number of Keypoints):**
 - Beyond the minimum of $n_{kp}=4$ keypoints (which provides only 2 residual degrees of freedom for 6-DOF pose estimation), increasing the number of keypoints leads to a decrease in PLs. The empirical results demonstrate a decay consistent with the predicted $n_{kp}^{-1/2}$ asymptotic behavior on a log-log plot. For single-keypoint fault monitoring ($s_{\text{max}}=1$), the along-track PL decreases from over 400 m (at $n_{kp}=4$) to below 26 m (at $n_{kp}=30$) when 1 km from the runway. Cross-track and elevation PLs follow similar decay trends but at smaller absolute values.
 - **Monitoring Multi-keypoint Faults ($s_{\text{max}}=2$):** When the framework is extended to monitor for simultaneous faults in two keypoints, the PLs are generally higher compared to monitoring only single-keypoint faults. This is because considering a larger fault subspace increases the worst-case failure-mode slope. However, this penalty decreases as $n_{kp}$ increases, as additional measurements improve overall detectability and dilute the prior probability allocated to unmonitored fault combinations. Monitoring for two-keypoint faults is geometrically feasible for $n_{kp} \geq 5$.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous navigation by providing a comprehensive framework for integrity monitoring in vision-based pose estimation, which is a critical step towards integrating such systems into safety-critical aviation applications.

The **primary significance** lies in bridging the gap between advanced computer vision techniques for pose estimation and the rigorous integrity requirements mandated for aviation. By adapting the well-established RAIM methodology from GNSS to the nonlinear PnP problem in VBPE, the authors have developed a method to compute analytical protection levels that account for the worst-case undetected pose errors across all considered fault hypotheses. This capability is essential for system certification, as it provides quantitative, probabilistic bounds on navigation error, crucial for real-time safety assurance.

The **potential impact** of this work is multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Certification and Operational Deployment:** The derivation of aviation-style PLs for 6-DOF aircraft pose estimation is a prerequisite for certifying vision-based navigation systems for applications such as aircraft approach and landing. This framework allows for a direct comparison of the computed PLs with predefined alert limits, providing a clear operational decision criterion for system availability and trustworthiness.
- **Enhancing Safety and Reliability:** By explicitly accounting for undetected faults, the framework provides a more robust and conservative estimate of navigation uncertainty compared to standard uncertainty propagation methods. This reduces the risk of hazardous misleading information (HMI) in safety-critical operations, thereby increasing the overall safety and reliability of autonomous flight.
- **Guiding System Design and Optimization:** The analytical characterization of PL behavior offers valuable insights for designing and optimizing VBPE systems.
 - The linear scaling of PLs with keypoint noise standard deviation underscores the importance of improving the precision and calibration of computer vision models for keypoint prediction.
 - The inverse square root dependence on the number of keypoints ($n_{kp}^{-1/2}$ asymptotically) provides a quantitative basis for determining the required measurement redundancy.
 - The empirical finding that position-only estimation yields significantly tighter PLs compared to full 6-DOF estimation highlights the challenges of estimating orientation from certain geometries and suggests that leveraging external attitude sources (e.g., IMUs) could substantially improve position integrity for VBPE.
- **Foundation for Future Research and Development:** This work establishes a robust foundation for subsequent research. The authors identify key areas for future work, including:
 - Validation on real-world runway imagery (e.g., using datasets like LARD).
 - Empirical calibration of per-keypoint fault probabilities, which is crucial for refining integrity risk allocation.
 - Integration of the integrity monitor into a complete approach and landing navigation architecture, including fault detection and exclusion (FDE) strategies.
 - The methodologies developed could potentially be extended to other perception-based navigation systems beyond runway environments, broadening their applicability for autonomous systems in general.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in vision-based navigation integrity, moving it closer to practical implementation in safety-critical aerospace applications by addressing a fundamental requirement for certification and operational safety.
