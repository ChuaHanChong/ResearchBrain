# Embedding Physics Priors in Robot Learning: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey reviews how physics priors are embedded into robot learning, covering models from perceptrons to diffusion models, VLAs and video world models. It proposes a three-way taxonomy: physics-guided inputs, data and representations; physics-encoded architectures; and physics-informed losses. It maps these onto dynamics learning, planning, control and estimation, and argues physics priors complement data-driven learning.

## Problem

- Physics-embedded robot learning is fragmented across terminology, methods, software and application domains, so the growing body of work is hard to compare or assess.
- Earlier surveys cover physics priors in fluid and solid mechanics or scientific computing, not robotics, or cover only narrow slices such as rigid-body dynamics.
- Robotics data is costly and scarce and its dynamics are high-dimensional and contact-rich, so purely data-driven methods struggle with safe generalization, yet no guidance exists on how to inject priors.

## Method

- A unified taxonomy adapted from prior scientific-ML work classifies each method by where physics enters: ==physics-guided== inputs, data and representations, ==physics-encoded architectures==, or ==physics-informed training losses==.
- A five-family scope of physics priors: ==governing equations==, ==conservation laws and symmetries==, geometric and kinematic structure, ==constitutive and contact models==, and ==physical admissibility constraints==.
- Reviewed papers are categorized into dynamics learning, trajectory planning and prediction, control, and estimation, with an open-source software survey and a continuously updated public repository with a classification decision flow.

## Results

- Physics-encoded architectures dominate the reviewed literature at **71%** of methods, while physics-guided approaches account for **16%** and physics-informed losses for **13%**, with the last two growing quickly since 2022.
- More than half of the reviewed methods were published between 2023 and 2025, and **62%** target manipulators or vehicles, while only **7%** address legged robots.
- Only **4%** of methods combine multiple physics-embedding routes, and those combinations remain ad hoc. The survey covers **329** papers, **232** of them robotics-specific, far above the **16 to 21** of earlier surveys.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treat physics priors as robotics-specific inductive biases that complement data, the way convolution and attention do, rather than as a replacement for learning.
> - No universally optimal embedding exists. Choice of prior, injection point and enforcement strength depends on prior fidelity, data available and needed closed-loop properties, and stronger priors can limit expressiveness.
> - Combining guided, encoded and informed routes systematically, together with physics-aware foundation models, standardized evaluation, safety guarantees and cross-robot transfer, is the main open opportunity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Collecting robot data is costly and constrained by hardware, supervision, and safety. Robots can also have complex dynamics, contact, and many degrees of freedom. One response is to give robot-learning systems some knowledge of how the physical world works, rather than asking them to infer everything from robot data. This survey maps a fragmented literature by identifying three complementary places to put that knowledge: the model's inputs or representations, its architecture, or its training loss. The authors argue that physics can complement data-driven learning without replacing it. Stronger priors can also constrain what a model can represent if the assumed physics is wrong or incomplete.

Physics may enter through data or representations, through the model architecture, or through the loss.

### 2. Three routes for adding physics

The survey gives each route a name:

- **Physics-guided** approaches use physical knowledge to transform, select, enrich, or curate inputs, data, or representations. This may happen before training or as pre- or post-processing at inference.
- **Physics-encoded** approaches build the prior into the model's structure, such as its layers, equations, constraints, or symmetries. The prior remains active in training and inference.
- **Physics-informed** approaches add a physics-derived residual or regularization to the training objective. The loss acts during training, and it influences later predictions only through the learned parameters.

A single method can receive more than one label.

Deep Lagrangian Networks (DeLaN) are an example of the physics-encoded route. A DeLaN learns a robot's inertia and potential-energy terms. It parameterizes the inertia using a Cholesky factor with a positive diagonal, so the inertia matrix remains positive definite.

The survey classifies 232 physics-embedded robot-learning methods among 329 cited references. It covers dynamics learning, planning and prediction, control, and estimation. The catalog is available in the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/TUM-AVS/survey-physics-embedded-robot-learning).

The closest conceptual predecessor is [Faroughi et al. (2024)](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/computingengineering/article/24/4/040802/1193884/Physics-Guided-Physics-Informed-and-Physics-Encoded-Neural-Networks-and-Operators-in-Scientific-Computing-Fluid-and-Solid-Mechanics). That work also distinguishes physics-guided, physics-informed, and physics-encoded approaches, but it focuses on fluid and solid mechanics rather than robotics.

### 3. What the map reveals, and what it cannot prove

This survey is a review of reported research. It is not a benchmark in which approaches are tested against one another.

Across the reviewed corpus, physics-encoded architectures make up the largest share of methods: 71%, compared with 16% physics-guided and 13% physics-informed. These percentages assign each method to its **primary** embedding route. They describe the reviewed corpus and say nothing about comparative performance.

The methods spread across tasks and platforms. Control is the largest application category, with 84 papers, followed by dynamics learning with 78. These are category counts, not mutually exclusive portions of a single total, because papers may appear in multiple task or robot classes.

Number of papers by application (left) and robot class (right), split into physics-guided, physics-encoded, and physics-informed. Category totals are printed at the ends of bars. Categories can overlap because papers may address multiple applications or robot platforms.

Adoption is also uneven across robot types. According to the survey, 62% of methods target manipulators or vehicles, and 7% address legged robots. The paper identifies validation on high-degree-of-freedom systems and contact-rich dynamics as common gaps. These are gaps in the reviewed literature, not proof that the methods cannot scale.

An obvious next question is whether combining routes beats using one route alone. Only 4% of reviewed methods combine routes, and those combinations are largely ad hoc. Systematic comparisons remain limited. The survey therefore does not establish that combining routes improves performance.

Individual routes also have their own qualifications. Physics-informed losses are soft constraints, so a small training residual does not guarantee exact equation satisfaction at inference.

### 4. A map, not a winner

In the authors' interpretation, the usefulness of physics embedding depends on three things: which prior is available, where and how strongly it is applied, and whether it fits the system. No "universally optimal" embedding strategy has emerged. The survey's contribution is a robotics-focused organization of where physics can enter learning. Its main boundary is that it maps and reviews the field rather than testing these strategies head to head.
