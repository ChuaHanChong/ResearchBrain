# Online Experiential Learning for Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Microsoft Research developed Online Experiential Learning (OEL), a reward-free framework allowing large language models to continuously improve by extracting transferable knowledge from unstructured real-world textual feedback and consolidating it into model weights. This process consistently enhanced task performance and inference efficiency, while also mitigating catastrophic forgetting across various model scales.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) developed through offline training remain static post-deployment, unable to adapt or learn from real-world interactions.
- Real-world deployment scenarios often lack direct server-side environment access and typically yield unstructured textual feedback instead of explicit scalar reward signals required by traditional RL.
- The impracticality of engineering verifiable reward functions or training custom reward models for every new deployment scenario limits continuous online learning.

## Method

- A two-stage Online Experiential Learning (OEL) framework is proposed: first, extract transferable experiential knowledge from collected user-side interaction trajectories.
- This knowledge extraction is reward-free, relying solely on raw textual environment feedback and accumulating knowledge sequentially.
- Second, consolidate the accumulated experiential knowledge into the model's parameters using on-policy context distillation, performed server-side without direct environment access during training.

## Results

- OEL consistently achieved progressive improvements in task performance (e.g., pass rate) across successive iterations in text-based game environments like Frozen Lake and Sokoban.
- Models trained with OEL demonstrated improved token efficiency, reducing average per-turn response length by approximately 30% while simultaneously increasing task accuracy.
- The framework mitigated catastrophic forgetting across different Qwen3 model scales (1.7B, 4B, 8B), maintaining out-of-distribution performance (IF-Eval accuracy) close to the initial model's baseline, unlike off-policy alternatives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Extracted and processed experiential knowledge from raw interactions is substantially more effective for model improvement than using raw trajectories directly.
> - On-policy context distillation is crucial for internalizing knowledge into model weights while effectively preserving out-of-distribution capabilities and mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
> - The iterative OEL loop establishes a virtuous cycle where improved models collect higher-quality trajectories, leading to richer experiential knowledge and further advancements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The current dominant paradigm for developing and improving large language models (LLMs) involves offline training using either human-annotated datasets for supervised fine-tuning or simulated environments with verifiable rewards for reinforcement learning. This approach trains and deploys models as static artifacts. While effective within their specific training distributions, these models are limited by the data and environments curated prior to deployment. Once deployed, they encounter an expansive and dynamic landscape of real-world tasks and user needs but are unable to learn or adapt from these new interactions. The valuable experience accumulated during real-world deployment is typically not utilized.

This research envisions an alternative paradigm: online learning, where models continuously improve by learning from their interactions in real-world environments post-deployment. However, realizing this vision presents significant challenges. Server-side training environments often lack direct access to the user-side environments where models operate. Furthermore, real-world interactions seldom provide explicit scalar reward signals, instead yielding unstructured textual feedback such such as natural language descriptions of outcomes or state changes. Conventional reinforcement learning algorithms are not designed to directly process such unstructured signals, and the task of constructing verifiable reward functions or training reward models for every new deployment scenario is often impractical.

Against this backdrop, the proposed Online Experiential Learning (OEL) framework directly addresses these limitations. It seeks to bridge the gap between static offline training and a desired state of continuous, adaptive online learning from unstructured, real-world interaction data. OEL differentiates itself by developing a reward-free mechanism to extract and consolidate valuable training signals from raw textual experience without requiring direct environment access or reward supervision on the server side, thereby pushing towards a more scalable and open-world learning paradigm for LLMs. This work builds on and contributes to the fields of on-policy distillation, context distillation, and general learning from experience in AI, particularly for language models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables large language models to continuously learn and improve from their own deployment experiences in real-world environments. This aims to overcome the limitations of the prevalent offline training paradigm, which results in static models that cease to learn after deployment.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations and challenges:
- **Exploiting Real-World Experience:** The vast amount of interaction data generated during a language model's real-world deployment is currently unexploited for model improvement. This represents a significant missed opportunity for continuous refinement and adaptation.
- **Bottleneck of Offline Training:** Current offline training methods, reliant on pre-curated datasets and simulated environments, create an inherent bottleneck. Model performance is bounded by the quality and coverage of the pre-deployment data, and further scaling requires increasingly costly human annotations or highly faithful, yet complex, simulations.
- **Lack of Server-Side Environment Access:** In real-world deployment scenarios, the server-side infrastructure used for model training typically cannot directly access or interact with the user-side environments where the model is operating. This architectural separation makes traditional online reinforcement learning impractical.
- **Absence of Scalar Reward Signals:** Real-world interactions rarely provide explicit, verifiable scalar reward signals, which are typically required by standard reinforcement learning algorithms. Instead, environments often return only unstructured textual feedback, such as descriptions of outcomes, errors, or state changes.
- **Impracticality of Reward Engineering:** Developing and maintaining verifiable reward functions or training custom reward models for every new or evolving real-world deployment scenario is resource-intensive and often unfeasible.

To address these motivations and challenges, the research aims to propose a novel online learning paradigm that is "reward-free," meaning it requires no human annotation, no verifiable reward functions, and no simulated environments on the server side. The core objective is to devise a method to convert raw textual environment feedback into transferable experiential knowledge that can be extracted, accumulated, and ultimately internalized into the model's parameters, thereby fostering a continuous improvement cycle in a practical and scalable manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Online Experiential Learning (OEL) is proposed as a reward-free framework designed to facilitate continuous model improvement from deployment experience. It operates through an iterative, two-stage process:

**Stage 1: Extract Experiential Knowledge from User Trajectories**
1. **Trajectory Collection:** The deployed language model (π_θ) interacts with a real user-side environment (E), collecting multi-turn interaction trajectories. Each trajectory (τ) comprises an alternating sequence of model actions and textual environment feedback.
2. **Knowledge Extraction:** A designated language model (π_extract), typically the current deployed model π_θ, sequentially processes these collected trajectories. It extracts transferable experiential knowledge. This extraction is accumulative; when processing the i-th trajectory, the model conditions on previously accumulated experiential knowledge (e_i-1) to generate new knowledge (e'_i), which is then concatenated to form the updated accumulated knowledge (e_i).
3. **Reward-Free Process:** This extraction process relies solely on the interaction trajectories with the user-side environment and does not require ground-truth labels or scalar reward signals.
4. **Knowledge Formats:** The extracted knowledge can be in a structured format (e.g., a list of items prefixed with "– EXPERIENCE ITEM:") or an unstructured format, depending on prompting. If π_extract is a thinking model, the reasoning part is removed, and only the answer is retained as experiential knowledge.
5. **Accumulation Settings:** Experiments used `n = 15, 25, or 50` trajectories for accumulation and a maximum generation length (`L_max`) of `2048` or `8192` tokens. The accumulation process is repeated `K=10` times with different random seeds to generate a set of accumulated experiential knowledge (C).

**Stage 2: Consolidate Experiential Knowledge into Model Weights**
1. **Data Preparation:** A separate set of interaction trajectories (T') is collected from the environment using the current model. From these, partial rollout prefixes (`x`) are extracted. Each prefix captures the interaction history up to, but not including, a specific model response.
2. **On-Policy Context Distillation:** The accumulated experiential knowledge from Stage 1 (C) is consolidated into the model's parameters (θ) using on-policy context distillation, as detailed in previous work [YDW+26].
 - **Student and Teacher:** The student model is the current policy (π_θ), which aims to internalize the knowledge. The teacher model (π_teacher) is the frozen initial π_θ, conditioned on both the partial rollout prefix (`x`) and a randomly sampled piece of experiential knowledge (`e`) from C.
 - **Training Objective:** For each training step, a prefix `x` from the prepared dataset and experiential knowledge `e` from C are sampled. The student model `π_θ` generates a response `y` conditioned only on `x`. The model is then optimized to match the knowledge-conditioned output of the teacher `π_teacher` through token-level reverse KL divergence, minimizing `L(θ) = E[1/|y| * Σ D_KL(π_θ(·|x, y_<t) || π_teacher(·|e, x, y_<t))]`.
 - **Server-Side Training:** This entire training procedure is carried out on the server side using the pre-collected trajectories, without requiring direct access to the user-side environment during training.
 - **Dense Signal:** The experiential-knowledge-conditioned teacher provides dense, token-level training signals derived solely from textual feedback, removing the need for reward models or explicit rewards.

**Online Learning Process (Iteration)**
The two stages form a continuous online learning loop. After each round of consolidation, the updated model (π_θ) is redeployed to the user-side environment. This improved model collects higher-quality trajectories, which, in turn, yield richer experiential knowledge during the subsequent extraction stage. This creates a virtuous cycle where better models produce better experiences, leading to further model improvements.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Environments:** Text-based games Frozen Lake (3x3 grid) and Sokoban (6x6 grid) from TextArena [GCY+25], with no explicit rules provided initially.
- **Models:** Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B (thinking variants), and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (non-thinking variant).
- **Training Details:** 20 or 100 gradient steps per OEL round, 64 game samples per step (1280 or 6400 trajectory samples per round). Maximum 5 turns per interaction, 1024 tokens max response length. Reverse KL divergence uses top-256 tokens.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Pass rate on a held-out test split (128 maps, averaged over 10 seeds) for in-distribution performance. Prompt-level strict accuracy on IF-Eval [ZLM+23] for out-of-distribution (OOD) performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings demonstrating the effectiveness and characteristics of the Online Experiential Learning (OEL) framework:

- **Enabled Online Learning and Performance Improvement:** OEL consistently achieves progressive improvements in task performance over successive iterations. Figures 1 and 4 illustrate that on environments like Frozen Lake (Qwen3-1.7B, thinking model) and Sokoban (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, non-thinking model), the model's pass rate steadily increases. While experiential knowledge accumulation via in-context learning initially boosts performance, it eventually saturates. The subsequent consolidation stage, which internalizes this knowledge into model weights, not only retains but often surpasses the in-context performance, indicating effective learning and generalization beyond the teacher's in-context capabilities. Each new OEL iteration starts from a stronger baseline, collecting higher-quality trajectories that yield richer experiential knowledge, thereby reinforcing the learning loop.

- **Improved Token Efficiency:** Beyond enhancing task accuracy, OEL also leads to improved inference efficiency. As experiential knowledge is internalized across OEL rounds, the average per-turn response length of the model (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B on Frozen Lake) decreases. Figure 5 shows response lengths reducing to approximately 70% of the initial length by the third iteration. This indicates that the model learns to solve problems more concisely and with less reasoning effort, while simultaneously increasing its pass rate.

- **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting:** The on-policy context distillation method employed in OEL's consolidation stage demonstrates superior performance in preserving out-of-distribution (OOD) capabilities compared to off-policy alternatives. Figure 6 shows that OEL achieves higher in-distribution (game pass rate) performance and largely maintains OOD performance (IF-Eval accuracy) close to the initial model's baseline. In contrast, off-policy context distillation exhibits a notable degradation in OOD performance over training steps, which is consistent with prior findings that on-policy training helps mitigate catastrophic forgetting.

- **Effect of Model Size:** OEL yields substantial performance improvements across various Qwen3 model scales (1.7B, 4B, and 8B) on Frozen Lake (Figure 7). While the initial performance difference across model sizes is relatively flat, OEL consistently improves all models. Larger models generally achieve higher absolute pass rates, and the performance gain from Round 1 to Round 2 remains consistent across scales. This suggests a synergistic effect where increased model capacity allows for the generation of higher-quality trajectories and the extraction of more effective experiential knowledge, leading to amplified performance gains.

- **Analysis: Efficacy of Experiential Knowledge vs. Raw Experience:** The research validates the importance of the experiential knowledge extraction stage. Table 1 illustrates that extracted experiential knowledge significantly outperforms raw interaction trajectories both when used in-context and after consolidation. Raw trajectories offer only modest improvements and can introduce noise, whereas processed experiential knowledge leads to substantial gains (e.g., 18.2% vs. 10.9% in-context, and 21.4% vs. 7.8% after consolidation for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on Sokoban), confirming that the extraction process is essential for OEL's effectiveness.

- **Analysis: Importance of On-Policy Consistency:** The study highlights the critical role of on-policy consistency between the experiential knowledge source and the policy model. Table 2 shows that Qwen3-1.7B benefits more from experiential knowledge derived from its *own* trajectories compared to knowledge extracted from a larger, potentially stronger model (Qwen3-4B). On-policy knowledge yields higher pass rates (e.g., 23.8% vs. 18.0% in-context, and 31.1% vs. 22.7% after consolidation). This suggests that knowledge from a stronger, off-policy model may not transfer effectively if it encodes strategies beyond the capabilities of the smaller, student model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Online Experiential Learning (OEL) represents a significant development in the field of large language model (LLM) advancement, particularly for continuous improvement in dynamic, real-world scenarios.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Novel Online Learning Paradigm:** OEL proposes a reward-free framework that allows LLMs to learn continuously from their own deployment experience. This directly addresses the fundamental limitation of current offline training approaches, where models cease to improve after initial deployment.
- **Overcoming Real-World Constraints:** The framework successfully navigates practical challenges associated with real-world LLM deployment. It bypasses the need for server-side access to user environments and operates entirely without explicit scalar reward signals or human annotations, relying instead on raw textual feedback. This makes it a more scalable and adaptable approach for diverse and open-ended deployment scenarios.
- **Effective Knowledge Extraction and Consolidation:** The two-stage process of extracting transferable experiential knowledge from interaction trajectories and consolidating it into model parameters via on-policy context distillation is central to OEL's effectiveness. The finding that extracted knowledge is substantially more effective than raw trajectories validates the extraction stage, while on-policy distillation is shown to be crucial for preserving general capabilities and preventing catastrophic forgetting.
- **Demonstrated Performance Gains:** OEL achieves consistent improvements in task accuracy and response efficiency across multiple model scales and variants in text-based game environments. The reduction in token length alongside increased accuracy indicates that models not only learn to solve problems better but also with more concise reasoning.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Continuous LLM Improvement:** OEL paves the way for truly adaptive LLMs that can continuously refine their capabilities throughout their operational lifespan, rather than being static after deployment. This could lead to more robust, capable, and user-responsive AI systems.
- **Scalability for Real-World Learning:** By leveraging the "virtually unlimited and ever-evolving source of learning signal" from real-world user interactions, OEL offers a fundamentally scalable paradigm. It reduces reliance on costly and limited human-curated datasets or complex simulated environments for ongoing improvement.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The mitigation of catastrophic forgetting through on-policy context distillation is crucial, ensuring that models can specialize on new tasks without sacrificing their broader out-of-distribution performance.
- **Foundation for Experience-Driven AI:** This work supports the broader vision of artificial intelligence agents that learn primarily from their own interactions with the world, moving beyond pre-programmed or purely data-driven learning. It encourages further research into how models can autonomously acquire, refine, and internalize knowledge from diverse experiences.
- **Practical Deployment:** The server-side training with only user-side trajectory collection simplifies practical deployment, offering a feasible pathway for organizations to implement continuous learning for their deployed LLMs.

In conclusion, OEL represents a promising step towards the next generation of LLMs, where deployment marks not the end of training but the beginning of a continuous, self-improving learning journey.
