# Frozen Policy Iteration: Computationally Efficient RL under Linear $Q^π$ Realizability for Deterministic Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Frozen Policy Iteration (FPI) is an online reinforcement learning algorithm that achieves statistical and computational efficiency under the linear Q
P
realizability assumption for MDPs with stochastic initial states and deterministic transitions. It eliminates the need for simulator access, providing a regret bound of e~O(sqrt(d^2 H^6 T) + sqrt(dH^2 T κ)).

## Problem

- Bridging the computational-statistical gap in reinforcement learning with function approximation, where statistically efficient algorithms often suffer from computational inefficiency.
- The reliance of existing statistically and computationally efficient algorithms under linear Q
P
realizability on simulator access or computationally intractable optimization problems.
- The challenge of developing an online RL algorithm for stochastic initial states and deterministic dynamics that is efficient without requiring repeated sampling from specific states.

## Method

- Introduces **Frozen Policy Iteration** (FPI), an online RL algorithm presented in both ==PAC== and regret minimization versions, designed to handle stochastic initial states and rewards with deterministic transitions.
- Employs a 'policy freezing' mechanism where Q-functions for well-explored states are fixed, preventing updates for these states to maintain data integrity and on-policy relevance.
- Utilizes selective data inclusion, adding only the data from the last state-action pair in a trajectory that was not yet 'covered' (i.e., sufficiently explored) at a given accuracy level, thereby avoiding off-policy data and eliminating the need for simulator-based resampling.

## Results

- Achieves a regret bound of e~O(sqrt(d^2 H^6 T) + sqrt(dH^2 T κ)) and a Uniform-PAC guarantee of e~O(d^2 H^6 / ε^2) for the FPI algorithm.
- Presents the first algorithm that is both statistically and computationally efficient for online RL under linear Q
P
realizability with stochastic initial states and deterministic transitions, without relying on simulator access.
- The methodology extends to function classes with bounded eluder dimension, broadening its theoretical applicability, and empirical tests on control tasks like CartPole-v1 and InvertedPendulum-v4 demonstrate its practical feasibility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is possible to achieve both computational and statistical efficiency in online RL under linear Q
P
realizability without simulator access by strategically managing data collection and Q-function estimation.
> - The 'freezing' mechanism allows Q-function estimates for explored states to remain stable and on-policy, enabling reliable learning even as the overall policy evolves.
> - By selectively incorporating data points corresponding to under-explored parts of the state-action space, the algorithm avoids accumulating off-policy data, circumventing a major challenge in online policy iteration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) with function approximation has seen substantial theoretical progress in understanding statistical efficiency under various structural assumptions. However, a persistent challenge is the computational inefficiency of many statistically efficient algorithms, leading to a "computational-statistical gap." This gap has attracted significant attention, prompting the development of computationally efficient algorithms and the identification of computational hardness results.

Within RL with linear function approximation, two primary structural assumptions are often considered:
1. **Linear Bellman Completeness**: This assumption posits that Bellman backups of linear state-action value functions remain linear with respect to a fixed feature representation. While statistically tractable, algorithms under this assumption often require solving computationally intractable optimization problems. Recent efforts have aimed to bridge this gap for specific MDP settings, such as those with an explorability assumption, deterministic dynamics, stochastic rewards, and stochastic initial states, or with a limited number of actions. A noted drawback of this assumption is its non-monotonicity; adding features might invalidate it.

2. **Linear Qπ Realizability**: This assumption, central to the current work, states that the Q-function of *any* policy is linear in a given state-action feature representation. This property is desirable due to its monotonicity (adding features does not break realizability). However, under this assumption, the computational-statistical gap has been less explored. Existing methods with polynomial sample complexity often rely on computationally intractable optimization oracles. Other algorithms, while having polynomial running time, typically require (local) access to a simulator, enabling them to restart from any visited state and recollect samples for updated policies. This simulator reliance is a significant limitation in the standard online RL setting, where state spaces can be large and initial states stochastic, making revisiting specific states improbable or impossible.

This paper addresses the specific challenge of achieving both statistical and computational efficiency in the *online RL setting* under the linear Qπ realizability assumption, particularly for Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with stochastic initial states, stochastic rewards, and deterministic transitions. It aims to bridge the gap left by prior work that either sacrifices computational efficiency or relies on simulator access.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a computationally efficient online RL algorithm under the linear Qπ realizability assumption. This algorithm is specifically designed for MDPs characterized by stochastic initial states, stochastic rewards, and deterministic transitions.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in prior work:

- **Computational Intractability**: Many existing statistically efficient algorithms under linear Qπ realizability require solving complex, often NP-hard, optimization problems or rely on intractable oracles. This renders them impractical for real-world application.
- **Reliance on Simulator Access**: Other algorithms, which exhibit polynomial running time, critically depend on access to a "generative model" or "local simulator." Such simulators allow the algorithm to restart learning from any previously visited state and execute the updated policy multiple times to collect fresh, on-policy data. This mechanism is fundamental for maintaining accurate Q-value estimates as policies evolve.
- **Inapplicability to Online Settings with Stochastic Initial States**: The simulator-reliant approach becomes unfeasible in typical online RL scenarios where:
 - The state space is large, meaning a specific state might be encountered only once, if at all, during the learning process.
 - Initial states are stochastic (or even adversarially chosen), preventing the learner from actively choosing to revisit a state or starting point.
 - Even with deterministic transitions, the inability to revisit states poses a challenge for policy iteration methods that assume resampling capabilities to ensure data remains "on-policy" with respect to the current policy.

The authors explicitly identify the gap: "In contrast, in the standard online RL setting, there is no known RL algorithm that is both statistically and computationally efficient [under linear Qπ realizability]." They further highlight that "even when the transition dynamics are deterministic, it is unclear if computationally efficient RL is possible."

Thus, the core motivation is to develop an algorithm that operates effectively within the constraints of a standard online RL environment, eliminating the need for simulator access while maintaining computational and statistical efficiency under the linear Qπ realizability assumption.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel online RL algorithm called **Frozen Policy Iteration (FPI)**. The methodology is presented in two phases: first, a PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) version for conceptual clarity, and then a regret minimization version. The approach centers on strategically managing data collection and Q-function estimation to avoid the need for simulator-based resampling, primarily by "freezing" policy updates for well-explored states.

#### 3.1. Frozen Policy Iteration - PAC (FPI-PAC)

FPI-PAC serves as a foundational algorithm:

- **Datasets (D_h)**: For each stage `h` of the horizon, a dataset `D_h` is maintained, storing `(s_h, a_h, q_h)` tuples, where `q_h` is the total reward from `(s_h, a_h)` onwards when a specific policy `π_t` was executed.
- **Exploration Mechanism (Cover Function)**: At each round `t`, a policy `π_t` is defined. For any state `s` at stage `h`, the algorithm checks if all actions `a` are "covered" by existing data in `D_t,h`. The `Cover(D, ε)` function identifies state-action pairs `(s, a)` where the least squares estimate error for `Q(s, a)` is bounded by `ε`, based on the feature covariance matrix `Σ`. If all actions are covered, `π_t(s)` is a greedy policy with respect to an estimated `Q_t`. Otherwise, `π_t(s)` selects an uncovered action `a` to encourage exploration.
- **Avoiding Off-Policy Data and Resampling (Selective Data Inclusion)**: This is a core novelty. Instead of resampling, FPI-PAC strategically includes only a specific part of the trajectory data. After executing `π_t` and collecting a trajectory, the algorithm identifies the *last* stage `h_t` in the trajectory where `(s^(t)_h, a^(t)_h)` was *not* covered by `D_t,h_t` at the current `ε` level. Only the data `(s^(t)_h_t, a^(t)_h_t, ˆq_t)` (where `ˆq_t` is the sum of rewards from `h_t` onwards) is added to `D_t,h_t`. Data from later stages (`h > h_t`) in the trajectory are discarded because those states were already "well-covered," implying `π_t` was near-optimal there. This choice ensures that the collected `ˆq_t` remains a valid Q-value for the policy that was executed at `(s^(t)_h_t, a^(t)_h_t)`.
- **Freezing High-Confidence States (Policy Freezing)**: The algorithm defines `Q_t(s,a)` not based on the entire `D_t,h`, but only on the *initial segment* of data points in `D_t,h` (up to `k_t(s)`) that was sufficient to cover all actions at state `s` when they were added. This "freezing" prevents `Q_t(s,a)` from changing for a state `s` once `s` is deemed "well-explored," ensuring that data associated with `s` remains relevant and effectively on-policy even if the overall policy `π_t` evolves in later rounds.

#### 3.2. Frozen Policy Iteration - Regret Minimization (FPI-Regret)

To achieve a `√T`-type regret bound, FPI-Regret extends the PAC version by incorporating multiple accuracy levels:

- **Multiple Accuracy Levels (l)**: Instead of a fixed `ε`, FPI-Regret uses a range of accuracy levels, `l`, corresponding to `ε = 2^-l`. For each level `l` and stage `h`, a separate dataset `D^(l)_h` is maintained.
- **Adjusting Accuracy Level Dynamically**: At the start of each episode, the accuracy level `l` is initialized to the highest accuracy `L`. As the agent traverses the trajectory, for each state `s^(t)_h`, it checks if `s^(t)_h` is "covered" at the current accuracy level `l`. If `s^(t)_h` is not covered at level `l` (i.e., `I^(l)_t(s)` is false), the algorithm finds the *lowest* `l'` for which `s^(t)_h` is *not* covered. It then switches to this `l'` for the current state `s^(t)_h` and takes an exploratory action. If `s^(t)_h` is covered at all levels up to `l`, a greedy action (exploitation) is taken using the `Q^(l)_t` function.
- **Exploration under Accuracy Constraints**: A crucial refinement is how exploratory actions are chosen. Instead of exploring any uncovered action, FPI-Regret defines `A^(l)_t(s)`, a subset of actions whose estimated suboptimality (based on `Q^(l-1)_t`) is bounded by `2^-l`. Policy freezing and cover checks are then performed only for actions within `A^(l)_t(s)`. This ensures that exploration remains focused on promising actions while still gaining coverage.
- **Dataset Updates**: Similar to FPI-PAC, data `(s^(t)_h_t, a^(t)_h_t, ˆq_t)` is added to `D^(l)_h_t` where `h_t` is the stage where an exploratory action was taken at accuracy level `l`.

**Mathematical Tools**: The analysis relies on standard techniques such as the elliptical potential lemma (to bound dataset sizes), concentration inequalities for self-normalized processes (for Q-value estimation confidence), and the performance difference lemma (for policy evaluation). Lemma 2 and 10, demonstrating that Q-functions remain effectively on-policy due to freezing and selective data inclusion, are critical for the overall analysis.

**Extensions**: The methodology is extended to the Uniform-PAC setting and to function classes with bounded eluder dimension, demonstrating its versatility beyond linear function approximation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

This research presents an online RL algorithm, Frozen Policy Iteration (FPI), that achieves computational and statistical efficiency under the linear Qπ realizability assumption for MDPs with stochastic initial states, stochastic rewards, and deterministic transitions. This marks a notable step in addressing a long-standing computational-statistical gap in this setting, specifically by eliminating the reliance on simulators.

The main findings and results are:

- **First Computationally Efficient Online Algorithm**: The paper introduces the first algorithm that is both statistically and computationally efficient for online reinforcement learning under the linear Qπ realizability assumption, operating with stochastic initial states, stochastic rewards, and deterministic dynamics. Prior methods either lacked computational efficiency or required simulator access for resampling.
- **Regret Bound for FPI-Regret**: The proposed FPI-Regret algorithm achieves a regret bound of `e~O(sqrt(d^2 H^6 T) + sqrt(dH^2 T κ))`. Here, `d` is the dimensionality of the feature space, `H` is the horizon length, `T` is the total number of episodes, and `κ` is the approximation error from linear Qπ realizability. This bound is indicated to be optimal for linear (contextual) bandits, which is a special case of the setting with `H=1`.
- **Uniform-PAC Guarantee**: The FPI algorithm also provides a Uniform-PAC guarantee of `e~O(d^2 H^6 / ε^2)`. This type of guarantee bridges the gap between PAC and regret minimization, offering assurance on learning near-optimal policies with specified error probabilities.
- **Extension to Eluder Dimension**: The methodology is successfully extended to function classes characterized by bounded eluder dimension. For such classes, the algorithm achieves a regret bound of `O(sqrt(H^6 T dim_E(F, T^-1) (log(HT/δ) + log N(F, T^-2, ||·||∞))))`. This demonstrates the broader applicability of the FPI framework beyond strictly linear function approximation.
- **Empirical Demonstration**: Proof-of-concept experiments on standard control tasks (CartPole-v1 and InvertedPendulum-v4) are conducted. These experiments, utilizing tile coding for feature maps, illustrate the practical applicability of Algorithm 1 (FPI-PAC).
- **Ablation Study on Freezing**: An ablation study comparing FPI with a version lacking the "freezing" operation demonstrates that the freezing mechanism contributes to improved performance, supporting its role in maintaining data integrity and learning stability.

The computational complexity of FPI-Regret is `e~O(HT^2|A| poly(d))`, and its space complexity is `O(T poly(d))`. For FPI-PAC, the time complexity is `e~O(HT|A| poly(d)/ε^2)` and space complexity is `e~O(H poly(d)/ε^2)`.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding and practical applicability of reinforcement learning with function approximation, particularly under the linear Qπ realizability assumption.

The key aspects of its significance and potential impact include:

- **Bridging a Computational-Statistical Gap**: The paper successfully addresses a long-standing challenge by proposing the first algorithm that is both statistically and computationally efficient for online RL in the linear Qπ realizability setting, without relying on simulator access. This advances the theoretical frontier of RL by demonstrating that efficient learning is possible under these practical constraints.
- **Enabling Online Learning without Simulators**: By circumventing the need for repeated sampling from specific states or access to a generative model, the FPI algorithm makes the linear Qπ realizability framework more applicable to real-world online RL scenarios. In many practical applications, restarting from arbitrary states or simulating specific state transitions is not feasible due to large state spaces, stochastic initial conditions, or the cost of interaction.
- **Robustness to Off-Policy Data**: The "freezing" mechanism and strategic data inclusion are significant methodological innovations. They offer a novel way to manage data collection and maintain on-policy relevance in policy iteration algorithms, even when the underlying policy is continuously updated and historical data might otherwise become off-policy. This approach contributes to developing more robust and stable learning algorithms.
- **Theoretical Generalizability**: The extension of the FPI framework to function classes with bounded eluder dimension demonstrates its potential for broader applicability beyond linear function approximation, suggesting a flexible foundation for future research in more complex settings.
- **Implications for Practical Applications**: While the deterministic transition assumption is a current limitation, the ability to handle stochastic initial states and rewards, combined with computational efficiency, makes FPI relevant to a class of real-world control tasks and environments where randomness primarily arises from resets rather than internal process noise (e.g., some robotics simulations, classical control problems).
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The paper explicitly highlights open problems, such as reducing the polynomial dependence on the horizon `H` in the regret bounds and extending the algorithm to MDPs with stochastic transitions. These open questions provide clear directions for future research to further enhance the generality and performance of online RL algorithms. The current reliance on deterministic transitions is a notable limitation, as stochastic dynamics are common in many real-world environments.

In summary, this work provides a foundational algorithm that resolves a critical bottleneck in the theoretical development of online RL with linear function approximation, paving the way for more practical and theoretically sound applications in complex environments.
