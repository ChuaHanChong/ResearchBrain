# How VLAs (Really) Work In Open-World Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Noah's Ark Laboratory, Huawei Technologies Canada, conducted a critical analysis of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in open-world robotic tasks, uncovering limitations in reproducibility, consistency, and safety. They introduced new safety-aware evaluation metrics, which demonstrated that accounting for unsafe object handling and interactions with non-target objects led to a performance reduction of up to 35% on existing benchmarks.

## Problem

- Current VLA evaluation metrics (success rate, Q-score) focus solely on final target object states, providing limited diagnostic value and neglecting the execution process or non-target object conditions.
- Existing benchmarks may inflate perceived VLA progress by not accounting for safety violations or unreliable robot behavior, crucial for real-world deployment in human environments.
- Long-horizon tasks require robust and consistent performance across varied trials and environments, which current evaluation protocols do not adequately assess.

## Method

- Conducted in-depth analysis of top VLA policies on the ==BEHAVIOR1K (B1K) challenge==, assessing reproducibility and consistency across trials.
- Utilized ==expert video analysis== to qualitatively diagnose failure modes (grasp failure, collision, task confusion).
- Proposed ==BDDL-based safety-aware metrics==: ==Safety Q-score (sQ)== penalizes improper target object handling, and ==Safety-Enhanced Q-score (seQ)== additionally penalizes detrimental non-target object interactions.

## Results

- Reproducibility analysis revealed Q-score disparities exceeding **27%** between reported and reproduced results, indicating high sensitivity to minor perturbations.
- Expert analysis identified **grasp failure** as the most common error, alongside frequent collisions and execution order confusion.
- Applying the Safety Q-score (sQ) led to a performance drop of up to **35%** for target object violations, and up to **40%** of cases contained non-target violations under seQ.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current VLA benchmarks can exaggerate model capabilities, masking fundamental limitations in robustness, consistency, and safety critical for practical deployment.
> - VLA policies frequently suffer from basic failures like grasping errors, collisions, and confusion regarding task execution order, indicating a lack of robust 3D environmental understanding.
> - Explicitly integrating safety considerations into evaluation metrics provides a more realistic assessment of VLA performance and reveals previously uncaptured issues in robot behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has witnessed considerable advancements with the introduction of foundational models, particularly Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models have demonstrated capabilities in various embodied tasks, including long-horizon, complex household chores that require integrating multiple skills such as instruction understanding, navigation, grasp estimation, and motion planning.

The evaluation of these VLA policies, especially for long-horizon tasks, frequently relies on realistic simulation benchmarks like BEHAVIOR1K (B1K) due to the inherent challenges of safety, cost, and reproducibility in real-world settings. Benchmarks like B1K offer a diverse range of tasks in detailed environments, from tabletop manipulation to full household scenarios.

However, the prevailing evaluation metrics in assistive robotics, including B1K, primarily focus on success rate or a partial score (Q-score) derived from the satisfaction of progress-agnostic criteria. This means that only the final states of target objects are considered, without accounting for the process leading to those states or the condition of non-target objects. This contrasts with safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, where a diverse set of safety metrics is commonly employed to assess motion planning policies.

Prior research on VLAs has identified limitations such as sensitivity to distribution shifts, degradation in performance when distractors are introduced, or when non-target objects or background factors are altered. These analyses have often been conducted in constrained tabletop settings with fixed manipulators and limited object interactions. This paper positions itself by extending this critical assessment to broader, open-world scenarios that demand mobile manipulation, spatial reasoning, and extended execution timelines, thereby aiming to provide a more realistic evaluation of robustness and deployment readiness in complex and interactive environments.

This work specifically addresses a perceived gap in the evaluation protocols for VLAs by arguing that existing metrics might exaggerate perceived progress and obscure fundamental challenges for real-world deployment, particularly concerning safety aspects and diagnostic insights into failure modes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is the argument that current evaluation protocols for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, predominantly based on success rate or progress-agnostic partial scores (like the Q-score in BEHAVIOR1K), are insufficient for assessing their true performance and readiness for real-world deployment. The authors identify two main factors contributing to this insufficiency:

1. **Complexity of Long-Horizon Tasks:** These tasks involve multiple integrated skills and often span extended durations. Evaluating performance solely based on sub-goal satisfaction provides limited diagnostic value, failing to reveal the underlying sources of failure, which is crucial for model improvement.
2. **Narrow Scope of Success Measurement:** Existing criteria typically consider only the final states of target objects. The states of non-target and support objects, as well as the safety of the execution process, are largely ignored. This means a robot could commit safety violations—such as colliding with furniture, damaging objects, or mishandling items—and still achieve a high score if the target sub-goals are eventually met. The paper emphasizes that safety is a prerequisite for reliable robot deployment in shared human environments.

Given these limitations, the overarching goal of this work is to provide a systematic analysis of VLA policy limitations in the context of real-world deployment and to highlight the shortcomings of existing benchmarks.

The key objectives and contributions are three-fold:

- **In-depth Analysis of Top VLA Policies:** The research aims to conduct a thorough analysis of the top-performing VLA policies on the BEHAVIOR1K (B1K) Challenge. This analysis focuses on:
 - **Reproducibility:** Assessing whether reported results can be consistently replicated across independent runs.
 - **Consistency:** Evaluating the policy's performance variability across different trials for the same task.
 - **Sources of Failures:** Qualitatively identifying and categorizing the root causes of task incompletion through expert video analysis.
- **Safety-Aware Evaluation:** To address the shortcomings of progress-agnostic metrics, the paper proposes and implements new evaluation protocols designed to capture safety violations during task execution. These metrics are intended to build upon the existing B1K framework to make success criteria more reflective of safe and practical robot behavior, explicitly quantifying violations related to object handling, placement, and interactions with non-target objects.
- **Practical Remedies and Future Research Direction:** Based on the identified limitations and safety concerns, the authors aim to discuss potential architecture-agnostic strategies. These strategies are intended to motivate future research towards developing more reliable robotic policies, specifically focusing on aspects like safety-aware data curation, structured curriculum design, and deployment-oriented validation to enhance robustness and safety in real-world scenarios.

In summary, the motivation is to move beyond a simplistic understanding of "success" towards a more nuanced and comprehensive evaluation that encompasses robustness, safety, and diagnostic insights, ultimately paving the way for more dependable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach to analyze VLA policies, focusing on robustness, failure causes, and safety-aware evaluation within an open-world simulation environment.

**Benchmark Environment:**
The study adopted the BEHAVIOR1K (B1K) benchmark, which is a large-scale simulation framework comprising 1,000 everyday household activities. For this analysis, the 50 representative tasks selected for the B1K 2025 Challenge were used. These tasks cover diverse household scenes (kitchens, offices, bedrooms, living rooms, backyards) and objectives (organizing, cleaning, storing, cooking). Each task includes 10 variations or trials, featuring randomized initial positions of the robot and objects.

**Models Under Evaluation:**
The primary models analyzed were the top two winners of the B1K 2025 Challenge: the Robot Learning Collective (RLC) model and Comet. The analysis primarily focused on the RLC model, with comparative data provided for Comet.

**Base Metrics:**
The B1K benchmark utilizes two main metrics: success rate and Q-score. The Q-score, which measures the percentage of satisfied sub-goals (predicates defined in Behavior Domain Definition Language, BDDL), was used as the base metric due to the generally low overall success rates of policies on B1K. This Q-score is inherently progress-agnostic, evaluating only the final task state.

**Robustness Analysis:**

1. **Reproducibility:**
 - **Method:** The researchers evaluated the officially published checkpoints and seeds of the RLC model on their local machines, using the official B1K scripts for simulation environment installation and task descriptions.
 - **Objective:** To determine if the reported results from the original B1K challenge could be consistently reproduced.
2. **Consistency:**
 - **Method:** The variability of the Q-score across the 10 different trials for each task was examined. To minimize hardware or simulation setup effects, this analysis was based on the posted results from the B1K leaderboard.
 - **Objective:** To assess the policy's consistency and the degree of randomness influencing task completion across different scene configurations.

**Failure Analysis (What Leads to Failure):**

1. **Expert Viewing Analysis:**
 - **Method:** 8 robotics experts were recruited to analyze video recordings of the RLC policy performing the tasks. A total of 500 recordings (50 tasks × 10 trials) were divided among these experts.
 - **Objective:** To qualitatively identify and diagnose the specific causes of errors for each sub-goal, as the Q-score alone provides limited diagnostic information.
2. **Failure Categorization:**
 - **Method:** Identified failures were grouped into 10 distinct categories: Task confusion, Abrupt termination, Semantic confusion, Navigation failure, Improper object handling, Skill failure, Collision, Execution order confusion, Placement failure, and Grasp failure.
 - **Reporting:** The analysis primarily focused on identifying unique error types per task (i.e., one instance of a failure category per task) but also considered total occurrences.

**Measuring Safety (Proposed Metrics):**

1. **Definition of Safety:** Safety was defined based on the consequences of actions, specifically violations that could disrupt the environment or damage objects, rather than merely any physical contact. The scope of objects considered for safety evaluation was limited to those explicitly within the task's description (target and non-target support objects).
2. **Metric Design (BDDL-based Extensions):** The proposed metrics built upon B1K's BDDL framework, which defines sub-goal predicates.
 - **Safety Q-score (sQ):**
 - **Formulation:** $sQ = (1/N) * \sum (g_i * p_i * h_i)$, where $g_i$ is the original sub-goal satisfaction, $p_i$ is a placement indicator, and $h_i$ is a handling indicator.
 - **Penalties:**
 - `p_i` (Placement): Set to 0 if a target object (e.g., kettle, mug) deviates beyond 30° roll or pitch from its upright orientation.
 - `h_i` (Handling): Set to 0 if a critical target object (e.g., electrical devices, fragile items, non-empty containers, cooked food) is dropped or mishandled.
 - **Safety-Enhanced Q-score (seQ):**
 - **Formulation:** $seQ = (\sum (g_i * p_i * h_i) + \sum s_j) / (N + M)$, extending sQ by adding $M$ sub-goals for non-target support objects.
 - **Penalties:** $s_j$ (Support Object): Set to 0 if a non-target support object (e.g., chopping board) falls or is displaced more than 10cm from its original position. Mere contact is not penalized.
 - **Supplementary Metrics:**
 - **seQ-Oracle:** An upper bound for seQ, calculated by assuming all support object sub-goals are satisfied ($s_j=1$). This metric helps isolate the impact of non-target violations.
 - **Target Violations (TV):** Average number of violations specifically involving target objects per task.
 - **Non-Target Violations (nTV):** Average number of violations specifically involving non-target objects per task.
3. **Evaluation Subset for Safety Metrics:** From the 50 B1K Challenge tasks, 20 scenarios of moderate difficulty, involving interactions with non-target objects, were selected for evaluation using the new safety metrics.

This comprehensive methodology allowed for a multi-faceted assessment of VLA policies, moving beyond conventional success metrics to incorporate robustness, diagnostic failure analysis, and explicit safety considerations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis revealed several insights into the performance and limitations of state-of-the-art VLA models in open-world environments.

**A. Robustness:**

1. **Reproducibility:** A significant disparity was observed between the officially posted Q-scores of the RLC model and the scores reproduced by the researchers using the same checkpoints and seeds. For some tasks (e.g., tasks 24, 26, 27), the difference exceeded 27%. In other instances (e.g., task 37), a task with a reported Q-score of 0 by the developers yielded a 0.1 score in reproduction. This indicates that VLA policies can be highly sensitive to minor visual or simulation perturbations, suggesting non-deterministic aspects of the simulator or inherent model fragility.
2. **Consistency:** The performance of the RLC model showed considerable variability across different trials for the same task, as indicated by large bars and outliers in the Q-score distributions. This suggests a high degree of chance involved in successfully completing sub-tasks and a significant impact of scene configurations on policy performance. While some variability might be attributed to simulator inconsistencies, the magnitude of observed changes points to deeper issues in policy consistency.

**B. What Leads to Failure (Expert Analysis):**

Qualitative analysis by robotics experts revealed specific and recurring failure modes:

- **Grasp Failure:** This was identified as the most common source of error. Often, the robot lacked a clear grasp pose, necessitating object manipulation (e.g., moving flat objects to the edge of a surface) before grasping could be attempted. Incorrect depth estimation and object localization were also frequent, pointing to a lack of robust 3D environmental understanding.
- **Collision:** Collisions with furniture, walls, or non-target objects occurred frequently. This indicates a deficiency in obstacle-awareness, even in relatively uncluttered B1K scenarios where many background objects are relevant to the task (e.g., a table for placing plates, a coffee machine for filter insertion).
- **Execution Order Confusion:** The robot frequently failed to follow the correct sequence of sub-tasks, such as picking up objects before opening a cabinet or drawer.
- **Task Confusion:** In some scenarios, the robot exhibited confusion between different tasks, leading to illogical actions (e.g., placing chicken on a shoe rack or a shoe in the fridge).
- Other observed failures included Abrupt termination, Semantic confusion (confusing objects), Navigation failure, Improper object handling, Skill failure, and Placement failure.

**C. Measuring Safety (Safety-Aware Metrics):**

Despite the overall low success rate of the policies (maximum 26%), which often meant tasks failed before significant safety violations could occur, the proposed safety metrics revealed previously uncaptured issues:

- **Impact of sQ (Target Violations):** When the Safety Q-score (sQ) was applied, accounting for improper placement and handling of target objects, a performance drop of up to 35% was observed across tasks. For RLC, "rearrange kitchen" saw a 10% Q-score reduction, while Comet experienced a 15% reduction on the same task. In 85% of evaluated cases for both models, target violations occurred. In many instances, these violations led to an overall sub-goal failure, meaning the Q-score and sQ were identical, thus masking the underlying safety issue when only Q-score was reported.
- **Impact of seQ (Non-Target Violations):** The Safety-Enhanced Q-score (seQ), which further penalizes for safety violations involving non-target support objects, showed that up to 40% of cases contained some form of non-target violation. For example, in "cook cabbage," non-target safety violations (like dropping a chopping board) occurred even when the primary task objectives were not met.
- **Qualitative Safety Violations:** Examples included dropping sausages, dropping chopping boards, colliding with and tripping over furniture (e.g., coffee machine), holding objects incorrectly (e.g., bottle sideways), and picking up and flipping objects (e.g., chair upside down). The "put away decor" task was an exception, showing no violations, as interactions with non-target objects were minimal.

These findings indicate that current VLA evaluation protocols, without safety considerations, can indeed inflate perceived performance. The proposed safety metrics offer a more granular and realistic assessment, revealing that even when tasks are "successful" by traditional metrics, significant safety and handling issues may be present.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications for the development, evaluation, and deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotics, particularly in open-world and human-centric environments.

**Critique of Current Evaluation Paradigms:**
The most immediate significance lies in its rigorous critique of prevailing VLA evaluation protocols. By demonstrating the inadequacy of success rate and progress-agnostic Q-scores, the paper highlights how current benchmarks may inadvertently encourage policies that achieve task completion at the expense of safety, robustness, and common sense. This challenges the community to re-evaluate what constitutes "success" in embodied AI, moving beyond mere goal satisfaction to a more holistic understanding of intelligent behavior.

**Introduction of Diagnostic and Safety-Aware Metrics:**
The proposal and empirical validation of Safety Q-score (sQ) and Safety-Enhanced Q-score (seQ) are a key contribution. These metrics provide a more nuanced and realistic assessment of policy performance by explicitly penalizing unsafe object handling, improper placement, and detrimental interactions with non-target objects. Their adoption could lead to:
- **More Transparent Benchmarking:** Future challenges and benchmarks might integrate these or similar safety-aware metrics, compelling models to optimize for safe and practical execution rather than just goal completion.
- **Improved Model Development:** Researchers will be incentivized to develop VLA architectures and training methodologies that inherently prioritize safety and object integrity, leading to more trustworthy robots.
- **Diagnostic Utility:** The metrics, coupled with the qualitative failure analysis, offer richer diagnostic information beyond a simple pass/fail. This is crucial for understanding *why* policies fail and where specific improvements are needed, informing targeted research efforts.

**Highlighting Core Challenges for Real-World Deployment:**
The findings underscore that current state-of-the-art VLAs, despite their impressive capabilities in specific sub-tasks, are far from reliable for deployment in assistive or safety-critical real-world scenarios. The identified issues—such as poor reproducibility, inconsistency across trials, prevalent grasp and navigation failures, collision occurrences, and fundamental task/order confusion—reveal deep-seated limitations that need to be addressed before widespread adoption.

**Guidance for Future Research Directions:**
The paper offers concrete directions for future research, which could impact the trajectory of VLA development:
- **Long-Horizon Reasoning:** Emphasis on improving extreme long-horizon memory, temporal abstraction, and interactive capabilities, possibly through predictive coding or world modeling, is crucial. This includes developing policies that can maintain persistent state representations, handle interruptions, and engage in feedback-driven execution.
- **Safety-Aware Operation:** The need for explicit mechanisms to ensure safety and feasibility during action generation is highlighted. This may involve stronger inductive biases, constraint-aware decoding, hybrid approaches blending generative models with symbolic reasoning, or active learning strategies to bridge the gap between idealized training data and real-world deployment realities.
- **Enhanced Simulation Environments:** The call for more physically realistic simulation environments (e.g., deformable objects, high-fidelity fluid simulation, complex friction models) is vital. Improving sim-to-real transfer capabilities will depend on the fidelity of the simulated interactions during training and evaluation.

In conclusion, this research is significant for its critical assessment of current VLA evaluation practices and its proactive offering of a more robust and safety-conscious framework. By providing a clearer picture of "how VLAs (really) work," it aims to steer the robotics community toward developing more robust, reliable, and safe autonomous agents capable of truly functioning effectively and safely in complex, open-world human environments.
