# ARMADA: Augmented Reality for Robot Manipulation and Robot-Free Data Acquisition

> [!summary] Summary
> The ARMADA system, developed by researchers from Apple and the University of Colorado Boulder, uses Apple Vision Pro to enable robot-free data acquisition for imitation learning. It provides real-time augmented reality feedback of a virtual robot's execution, leading to a substantial increase in the replay success rate of human demonstrations on physical robots, with improvements of up to 85.4% observed.

## Problem

- Robot imitation learning faces a scarcity of large-scale, high-quality human demonstration datasets due to limitations in traditional data collection methods.
- Teleoperation requires constant access to physical robot hardware, creating a bottleneck that limits the quantity and diversity of collectible data.
- Learning directly from passive human videos is challenging due to the 'embodiment gap,' as human kinematics and dynamics differ significantly from robot capabilities, making actions difficult to directly translate.

## Method

- The **ARMADA** system leverages ==Apple Vision Pro== to provide real-time augmented reality feedback of a virtual robot overlaid on a user's bare hands, enabling robot-free data acquisition.
- Human hand and finger movements are tracked by ARKit and translated into virtual robot control commands via inverse kinematics for the robot arm and a threshold for gripper control.
- Real-time visual cues (e.g., color changes for singularity, red walls for workspace limits, 'Slow Down' text for velocity) are provided to guide human demonstrators towards robot-compatible actions.

## Results

- Demonstrations collected with real-time AR feedback achieved substantially higher replay success rates on physical robots, with increases of **76.0%** for 'Pick Tissue', **48.0%** for 'Declutter', and **85.4%** for 'Bimanual Wipe' compared to conditions without feedback.
- Even without live AR feedback, prior exposure to the system improved replay success rates (e.g., 'Pick Tissue' rose from **2.7%** to **37.3%**), indicating implicit learning by human demonstrators.
- Participants rated the robot visualization as highly intuitive (mean **6.4** ± **0.6**) and useful (mean **6.0** ± **1.2**) for understanding robot motion and predicting behavior across varying levels of prior robotics and VR/AR experience.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Real-time augmented reality feedback significantly guides human demonstrators to produce motions that are directly compatible with robot kinematics and operational constraints.
> - The system effectively bridges the 'embodiment gap' by allowing humans to intuitively understand and adapt to robot limitations without needing a physical robot during demonstration.
> - Consumer-grade AR devices enable a scalable and accessible method for collecting high-quality robot demonstration data, democratizing data collection beyond specialized labs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of robot imitation learning (IL), which aims to train robots to perform complex manipulation tasks by mimicking human demonstrations. The authors acknowledge that IL has shown promise, particularly with expert data collected using specialized hardware. However, a significant challenge highlighted is the scarcity of large-scale datasets for robot control, unlike the internet-scale datasets available for natural language processing and 2D computer vision that have propelled recent advancements in large language and vision models.

Current data collection methods for robot imitation learning face two primary limitations:
1. **Scalability of Teleoperation:** Traditional teleoperation, while effective, requires constant access to physical robot hardware and specialized setups. This one-to-one hardware-to-demonstrator requirement creates a bottleneck, limiting the quantity and diversity of data that can be collected.
2. **Embodiment Gap in Human Video:** Learning directly from passive human videos is challenged by the fundamental differences in kinematics, dynamics, compliance, and actuation between human hands and robot end-effectors. This "embodiment gap" means human actions often cannot be directly replicated by robots, frequently necessitating additional teleoperation data to bridge the discrepancy.

The paper identifies augmented reality (AR) as a promising technology to address these issues. Existing AR/VR research in robotics primarily focuses on teleoperation interfaces (e.g., virtual teach pendants) or previewing robot motions. While these applications demonstrate the utility of AR/VR for robot control, they do not directly tackle the problem of scalable, robot-compatible data acquisition without a physical robot.

The ARMADA system distinguishes itself from related work by offering real-time augmented reality feedback of a virtual robot's execution directly overlaid on a user's bare hands via a consumer-grade AR device (Apple Vision Pro). Previous efforts such as AR2-D2 provided robot-free demonstrations but lacked real-time feedback. DART explored AR for data collection but focused on simulation, introducing a sim-to-real gap. ARCap, a concurrently developed system, also offers real-time AR feedback but relies on more cumbersome hardware, including external cameras, motion capture gloves, and VR controllers, potentially hindering natural barehanded interaction. ARMADA's approach, using only a Vision Pro for barehanded input, aims to provide a more accessible and intuitive method for collecting robot-compatible human demonstrations, thereby facilitating the creation of large-scale datasets essential for advancing general-purpose robot manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the scalability challenges in collecting high-quality human demonstration data for robot imitation learning. Current methods are limited by:
- **Hardware Bottleneck:** The reliance on physical robot hardware for teleoperation significantly constrains data collection efforts. Access to robots is often limited, expensive, and geographically concentrated, making it difficult to generate the vast datasets needed for modern machine learning approaches.
- **Embodiment Gap:** Human dexterity, compliance, and kinematics are fundamentally different from those of robotic end-effectors and arms. This disparity creates a significant challenge when attempting to directly translate human hand movements into robot control commands, making passive human video data less effective for robot learning without extensive translation or bridging data.

In response to these challenges, the key objectives of the ARMADA project are:
- **Enable Robot-Free Data Acquisition:** To develop a system that allows for the collection of high-quality robot demonstration data without requiring direct access to a physical robot during the data collection process. This addresses the hardware availability bottleneck, potentially democratizing data collection.
- **Provide Real-Time Virtual Robot Feedback:** To integrate a digital twin of a robot into an augmented reality environment (specifically, using Apple Vision Pro) that provides real-time visual feedback to the human demonstrator. This feedback is intended to intuitively convey how the demonstrator's barehanded actions translate into robot motions, including critical information about robot kinematics, dynamics, and speed.
- **Generate Robot-Compatible Human Data:** To ensure that the human data collected, despite being barehanded and robot-free, is compatible with the limitations and capabilities of physical robot hardware. The real-time feedback loop is hypothesized to guide human demonstrators to produce actions that are directly executable by a robot.
- **Demonstrate Improved Data Quality:** To empirically evaluate whether the provision of real-time AR robot feedback significantly improves the quality of collected human demonstrations, making them more directly replayable on physical robot hardware.
- **Facilitate Scalable Dataset Creation:** By enabling "in-the-wild" data collection using a personal device like the Apple Vision Pro, the project aims to pave the way for creating significantly larger datasets (potentially tens of thousands of hours) for robot manipulation, an order of magnitude greater than existing teleoperation-based datasets. Such scale is considered crucial for enhancing generalization across tasks, environments, and robot platforms in imitation learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ARMADA system comprises a software application running on Apple Vision Pro, integrated with an external robot control node, to enable augmented reality (AR) enhanced robot-free data collection.

**A. Software System Architecture**
The system establishes communication between the ARMADA app on the Apple Vision Pro and a robot control node on an external compute device using ROS and websockets. This architecture is designed for modularity, allowing different robot hardware platforms to be integrated by swapping 3D models and formatting robot information appropriately.
- **ARMADA App (Apple Vision Pro):** This component serves three main functions:
 - **Visualization:** Receives robot link transformations and constraint information from the external compute. The virtual robot's base is anchored relative to a QR code in the physical environment, with subsequent links added relative to the base frame. Constraints (singularity, workspace, speed) are visualized to the user.
 - **Data Storage:** Captures image frames from the Vision Pro's front-facing cameras and human skeleton data, which can be exported for post-processing.
 - **User Interface:** Provides controls for managing visualization settings and the data collection pipeline.
- **Robot Control Node (External Compute):** This component also has three primary responsibilities:
 - **Robot Control:** Transforms human skeleton data, received from the Vision Pro, into robot control commands.
 - **Data Storage:** Stores proprioceptive data (joint angles, velocity, torque) and any external camera frames locally.
 - **Constraint Calculation:** Computes real-time information regarding singularity, speed, and workspace violations during robot execution in simulation, and transmits this data back to the ARMADA app for AR visualization.
The entire feedback loop, from human motion capture to robot visualization, operates at a rate of 30 Hz.

**B. Robot Arm Control**
The virtual robot's movements are directly controlled by the human demonstrator's bare hands.
- **Human Pose Tracking:** ARKit in visionOS 2.0 tracks the human's finger and wrist positions. While the wrist position provides accurate location, the orientation is refined by using the average of the four knuckle positions. This combined pose is then slightly adjusted to place the virtual gripper's center between the human's thumb and index finger.
- **Inverse Kinematics (IK):** The desired end-effector pose (derived from the human hand pose) is fed into the Drake robotics toolbox, which computes the corresponding joint positions for the robot arm via inverse kinematics. In cases of IK failure, the last successfully commanded joint position is maintained.
- **Gripper Control:** The opening and closing of the virtual robot's pinch gripper are controlled by the distance between the human's index finger and thumb, regulated by a predefined threshold.
This control system effectively simulates a two-finger pincher robot, and its execution loop runs at 30 Hz.

**C. Augmented Reality Feedback Methods**
The system incorporates visual cues to inform the user about the robot's state and limitations:
- **Singularity:** As the virtual robot approaches a singular configuration (where it loses degrees of freedom), its appearance gradually shifts to yellow, indicating proximity to this critical state. This is determined by monitoring the determinant of the manipulator Jacobian.
- **Workspace Constraints:** If the demonstrator's hands move beyond the defined Cartesian workspace of the robot, a red "wall" appears in the AR environment at the respective boundary, indicating a violation.
- **Speed:** To encourage smooth, consistent motion necessary for accurate camera tracking, a "Slow Down" text overlay appears at the user's eye level if the robot's simulated velocity exceeds a set limit.
For the user study, a subset of these feedback modalities was utilized to manage participant fatigue.

**D. User Study**
A user study was conducted with 15 participants (11 male, 4 female, aged 25-64) with varying levels of experience in virtual reality and robotics.
- **Feedback Conditions:** Participants performed tasks under three sequential feedback conditions:
 1. **No Feedback:** No AR visualization of the robot was provided. Participants were instructed to use natural human motion.
 2. **Feedback:** Real-time AR digital twin of the robot was displayed, guiding participants to control the virtual robot. A 1-minute trial period was given to acclimatize.
 3. **Post Feedback:** AR visualization was again removed, but participants were instructed to leverage their experience from the "Feedback" condition to mentally predict robot responses.
- **Tasks:** Three manipulation tasks were designed, each with 5 distinct starting states:
 1. **Pick Tissue:** Grasping and removing a tissue from a box using one arm.
 2. **Declutter:** Picking up a soft toy and placing it into a cardboard box using one arm.
 3. **Bimanual Wipe:** Wiping a cloth across a table surface using both arms.
- **Protocol:** Each participant completed 5 demonstrations for each of the 3 tasks under each of the 3 feedback conditions, totaling 45 demonstrations. Task order within each condition was randomized. Demonstrations began by placing hands within AR spheres and ended by holding palms face up. Joint angles from each trajectory were recorded. Post-study, a survey assessed user experience.
- **Robot System for Replay:** All 45 collected trajectories were replayed on a physical system consisting of a pair of 6 DOF Interbotix ViperX 300 S robot arms, each with a 1 DOF pinch gripper. Success was determined by specific task completion criteria (e.g., tissue removed, toy in box, cloth pushed).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's results demonstrated a substantial impact of real-time augmented reality feedback on the quality and robot compatibility of human demonstrations.

**A. Replay Success Rate (Table I)**
- **Impact of Live Feedback:** Demonstrations collected under the "Feedback" condition (with real-time AR robot visualization) achieved significantly higher replay success rates on physical robot hardware compared to the "No Feedback" condition:
 - Pick Tissue: 78.7% (Feedback) vs. 2.7% (No Feedback), an increase of 76.0%.
 - Declutter: 48.0% (Feedback) vs. 0.0% (No Feedback), an increase of 48.0%.
 - Bimanual Wipe: 86.7% (Feedback) vs. 1.3% (No Feedback), an increase of 85.4%.
 These findings indicate that visual robot feedback is critical for producing human demonstrations that are directly executable by physical robots without prior teleoperation.
- **Learning Transfer without Live Feedback:** The "Post Feedback" condition, where live AR feedback was removed after initial exposure, also showed an improvement in replay success rates compared to "No Feedback":
 - Pick Tissue: 37.3% (Post Feedback) vs. 2.7% (No Feedback), an increase of 34.6%.
 - Declutter: 16.0% (Post Feedback) vs. 0.0% (No Feedback), an increase of 16.0%.
 - Bimanual Wipe: 46.7% (Post Feedback) vs. 1.3% (No Feedback), an increase of 45.4%.
 This suggests that human demonstrators can learn and adapt their movements based on prior experience with robot feedback, retaining some level of improved performance even when the visual cues are no longer present.
- **Superiority of Live Feedback:** Despite the improvements in the "Post Feedback" condition, the "Feedback" condition consistently outperformed it:
 - Pick Tissue: 78.7% (Feedback) vs. 37.3% (Post Feedback).
 - Declutter: 48.0% (Feedback) vs. 16.0% (Post Feedback).
 - Bimanual Wipe: 86.7% (Feedback) vs. 46.7% (Post Feedback).
 This indicates that while learning occurs, continuous real-time visual feedback remains essential for achieving the highest levels of replayability and precision.

**B. Survey Results**
- **Intuitiveness and Usefulness:** Participants generally rated the robot visualization as highly intuitive and easy to understand (mean 6.4 ± 0.6 on a 7-point Likert scale). They also found it useful for comprehending robot motion and for predicting robot behavior (mean 6.0 ± 1.2), suggesting a strong reliance on the visual cues.
- **User Experience:** The study population included individuals with a broad range of experience levels in both robotics (from none to expert) and VR/AR devices (from none to over 20 hours of prior use). The system's ability to accommodate this diverse group suggests accessibility across different user proficiencies.

**C. Analysis of Failure Modes and Qualitative Observations**
- **Failure Modes:** In the "No Feedback" and "Post Feedback" conditions, replay failures were attributed to factors such as excessive human hand speed, imprecise robot poses, undetected gripper opening/closing (possibly due to hand occlusion), and unpredictable inverse kinematics behavior. For "Feedback" demonstrations, failures were mainly due to subtle imprecision in robot positions or orientations, potentially resulting from depth perception inaccuracies in the AR display. The "Declutter" task, which required higher precision, exhibited consistently lower success rates across conditions.
- **Qualitative Results:** Participant feedback indicated that with real-time robot feedback, they consciously adjusted their behavior. Reported changes included slowing down movements, modifying hand positions and orientations, actively considering the robot's gripper operation, altering approach angles, and focusing their attention on the virtual robot's end-effectors rather than their own hands. In the "Post Feedback" phase, participants reported being able to mentally visualize the previously experienced AR feedback, which informed their actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ARMADA system presents a notable advancement in the methodologies for collecting robot manipulation data, with several significant implications for the field of robot learning.

**Scalable and Accessible Data Collection:** The primary significance of this work lies in its potential to address the long-standing bottleneck of data collection for imitation learning. By demonstrating that high-quality, robot-compatible demonstrations can be acquired using a consumer AR device (Apple Vision Pro) without requiring access to physical robot hardware, ARMADA offers a pathway to scalable data generation. This "robot-free" approach means that data can be collected "in-the-wild" by a broader population of users, potentially leading to the creation of datasets orders of magnitude larger (tens of thousands of hours) than current teleoperation-based datasets. Such large-scale data is considered a crucial component for achieving generalization in robot learning across diverse tasks and environments.

**Bridging the Embodiment Gap:** The real-time augmented reality feedback provided by ARMADA plays a critical role in implicitly guiding human demonstrators to produce actions that are compatible with a robot's kinematics, dynamics, and control limitations. This mechanism effectively mitigates the "embodiment gap" that hinders direct learning from passive human videos. By allowing users to intuitively understand how their barehanded motions translate to robot behavior, the system enables the collection of data that is inherently suitable for robot execution, thereby improving the direct replay success rate on physical hardware.

**Enhanced User Experience and Learning:** The user study results indicate that the robot visualization is intuitive and useful, even for individuals with limited prior experience in robotics or virtual reality. This ease of use lowers the barrier to entry for potential data collectors, further contributing to the scalability and diversity of collected datasets. The observation that demonstrators can improve their performance even after the live feedback is removed suggests a form of implicit learning about robot capabilities and constraints, which could potentially reduce the continuous need for live AR feedback after an initial training phase.

**Future Research Directions:** The work opens several avenues for future research. One immediate direction is to train robot control policies using the data collected via ARMADA. The ability to transform egocentric visual observations from the Vision Pro into robot observations through techniques like masking and inpainting could enable effective policy learning. Furthermore, enhancing the system's retargeting and inverse kinematics capabilities could allow for tackling more complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks and working with non-compliant objects, expanding the utility of this data collection paradigm.

In summary, ARMADA represents a significant step towards democratizing robot data collection, accelerating the development of general-purpose, dexterous robot manipulation capabilities by making large-scale, high-quality, robot-compatible human demonstration data more accessible.
