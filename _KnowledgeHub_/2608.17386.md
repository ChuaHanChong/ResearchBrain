# MANIGUARD: A Benchmark and Data Suite for Specification-Grounded Safety Evaluation and Improvement of Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ManiGuard is a specification-grounded benchmark and paired data pipeline for evaluating and improving safety in foundation-model robot manipulation. Six contact-rich task families use LTLf specs checked by DFA runtime monitors over physics-grounded predicates, orthogonal to task success. Fine-tuning on 8,000 safety-annotated demonstrations raises safe task completion from near zero to 7.5-29.8%, though 21-42% of engaged rollouts still violate specs.

## Problem

- Manipulation benchmarks measure task success and generalization but leave safety implicit, never defining or monitoring it during execution.
- Embodied-agent safety benchmarks judge hazard awareness via text or vision-language judges rather than execution-grounded, contact-rich trajectory-level monitoring.
- Existing formal-spec benchmarks operate in stylized simulators without contact physics or retrofit monitors onto tasks not designed for them, and pair no safety-annotated training data.

## Method

- **ManiGuard-Bench** organizes 200 locked tasks across six contact-rich household families along a skill by constraint taxonomy, with ==LTLf== safety specifications independent of task success.
- Specifications compile into ==deterministic finite automata== that serve as runtime monitors over physics-grounded ==BDDL== predicates, emitting per-step categorical violation labels with no learned classifier or LLM judge.
- A paired ==trajectory-generation pipeline== combines cuRobo automated motion planning over 1,547 grasps and human teleoperation, both annotated by the same per-step LTLf monitor for safety-aware fine-tuning.

## Results

- Across **23,000-plus** evaluation rollouts, **6-21%** of successful fine-tuned rollouts still violate the safety specification, showing success does not imply safety.
- Fine-tuning raises safe task completion from near zero to **7.5-29.8%** and engaged-and-safe behavior from **16-40%** to **51-72%** across four VLA policies.
- A persistent gap remains: **21-42%** of engaged rollouts still violate constraints, two of six task families stay below **2%** safe success, and failures carry over to a physical Franka.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Raw safety rate is misleading when policies rarely engage the task; apparent safety can be bought entirely by inaction rather than safe behavior.
> - Two policies can reach nearly identical task success yet differ by six points in safety violation rate, so safety must be scored as an independent objective.
> - Scaling the same demonstration distribution does not close the residual safety gap, implying safety failures need distinct data or objectives beyond more behavior cloning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of foundation models for robotic manipulation has led to advancements in task success and generalization across various scenarios and robot embodiments. However, the rigorous evaluation of whether these systems operate safely in contact-rich, open-world environments remains an area with unmet needs. Existing manipulation benchmarks primarily focus on task completion and generalization (e.g., Liu et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024; Nasiriany et al., 2024) but do not explicitly define or monitor safety during execution. Similarly, embodied-agent safety benchmarks (e.g., Lu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2024; Sermanet et al., 2025) assess hazard recognition or safe planning, but often stop short of evaluating contact-rich, trajectory-level execution.

A gap exists in benchmarks that combine explicit safety specifications, trajectory-level checking, and manipulation task designs where safety is treated as an objective orthogonal to task success. While some recent efforts have incorporated explicit safety checks (e.g., Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; 2025a) or formal temporal logic specifications (e.g., Zhan et al., 2025a; Huang etal., 2026), these often operate in stylized simulators, retrofit monitors onto existing tasks not designed for safety constraints, or lack paired safety-annotated data suites for policy improvement. Furthermore, simulation-based approaches for safety evaluation need to handle contact-rich physics and ideally extend to real-world platforms, with some works exploring damage-aware simulation (Balaji et al., 2026).

This work addresses these identified gaps by introducing ManiGuard, a framework designed to bridge the divide between advancing manipulation capabilities and the need for robust, formally specified safety evaluation. It integrates physics-based simulation with formal safety specifications and provides a paired dataset for safety-aware learning, an aspect often absent in existing formal safety benchmarks. The research positions itself as foundational for developing safer robotic manipulation policies, particularly with the increasing deployment of generalist foundation models in diverse, unstructured environments where physical damage and safety violations can have significant consequences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the rapid progress of foundation-model policies in robotic manipulation, particularly in achieving high task success rates and generalization capabilities. Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in the rigorous evaluation of whether these policies perform tasks safely. As robotic systems transition towards deployment in open-world, unstructured, and human-proximate environments, ensuring physical safety becomes a primary concern.

The paper identifies several challenges that necessitate a dedicated safety evaluation framework:
- **Cost of Failure:** Physical damage in real-world robotic operations is costly, difficult to reverse, and can have safety implications for humans or the environment.
- **Vast Space of Safety-Critical Scenarios:** Learning safe manipulation primarily through large-scale real-world failures is impractical due to the immense number of potential unsafe scenarios. This underscores the need for efficient, physics-based simulation environments that can expose safety-critical consequences without incurring actual damage.
- **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** Current manipulation benchmarks largely focus on task success and generalization, often leaving safety as an implicit or secondary consideration. Embodied-agent safety benchmarks tend to evaluate hazard recognition or plan safety at a high level, rather than at the trajectory-level in contact-rich manipulation.

Against this background, the key objectives of ManiGuard are:
1. **Introduce a Specification-Grounded Framework:** To develop a formal framework for evaluating and improving the safety of foundation-model-based robotic manipulation, where safety requirements are explicitly defined and monitored.
2. **Develop ManiGuard-Bench:** To create a task suite comprising 200 contact-rich household manipulation tasks, organized by a skill × constraint taxonomy. A core objective is to design these tasks such that safety is specified independently of task success, meaning a policy can succeed unsafely or fail safely.
3. **Implement Formal Safety Evaluation:** To utilize Linear Temporal Logic on finite traces (LTLf) for specifying safety requirements. These specifications are compiled into deterministic finite automata (DFAs) for runtime monitoring, enabling per-step categorical safety labels grounded in physics-based predicates extracted directly from simulator state, rather than relying on learned classifiers or language model judgments. This aims for reproducible and objective safety diagnostics.
4. **Incorporate Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Evaluation:** To rigorously test policy robustness by evaluating each task under one in-distribution and four single-axis out-of-distribution perturbations (target object appearance, instruction, object location, and background), while keeping the safety specification fixed. This objective aims to isolate the impact of visual, linguistic, or geometric shifts on safety.
5. **Create a Paired Safety-Annotated Trajectory-Generation Pipeline and Data Suite:** To provide a mechanism for generating high-quality, safety-annotated demonstrations. This pipeline, combining automated motion planning and human teleoperation, produces data that is filtered and annotated by the same LTLf runtime monitor used for evaluation. This objective supports safety-aware fine-tuning of policies towards safer behavior.
6. **Benchmark State-of-the-Art Policies:** To systematically evaluate leading vision-language-action (VLA) policies, both zero-shot and fine-tuned, on ManiGuard-Bench to identify prevalent safety failure modes and assess the effectiveness of fine-tuning on safety-annotated data. This includes examining the effect of data scaling and specification representation, and conducting initial sim-to-real transfer studies.

These objectives collectively aim to establish a foundation for understanding, measuring, and improving safety in robotic manipulation systems, moving beyond task completion as the sole performance metric.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ManiGuard is a framework for evaluating and improving robotic manipulation safety, structured around the ManiGuard-Bench task suite and a paired safety-annotated trajectory-generation pipeline.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The framework formalizes the problem by defining policies interacting with a physics-based simulator, generating trajectories $\tau = (s_0, a_0, \ldots, s_T)$. A finite set of atomic propositions (AP) are Boolean predicates over simulator states, which map to labels $L(s_t)$. Safety requirements are expressed as conjunctive LTLf formulas $\varphi = \bigvee_i \psi_i$, where each $\psi_i$ is an LTLf formula over AP. A trajectory $\tau$ satisfies $\varphi$ under standard LTLf semantics on the finite label word.

Safety evaluation is performed via runtime monitoring. Each $\psi_i$ is compiled into a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) $A_{\psi_i}$ (a bad-prefix monitor). This monitor processes the trajectory step-by-step, emitting a categorical per-step safety label $c_\varphi^{(i)}(s_t, q_t)$ indicating a violation if the automaton enters a rejecting state $Q_{\text{reject}}^{(i)}$. The episode-level safety verdict $\nu(\tau)$ is defined as 1 (safe) unless a violation occurs *after* the policy first engages a task-relevant object ($t_{\text{viol}} \geq t_{\text{eng}}$). This ensures that policy-induced violations are charged to the policy, not to initially unsafe scene configurations.

For improvement, a demonstration dataset $D_\varphi$ is created, where each trajectory is safety-annotated with per-step labels and retained only if it completes the task and receives a safe monitor verdict. This allows for safety-aware learning, such as supervised fine-tuning, with policies being trained and evaluated against identical safety semantics.

#### 3.2. Benchmark Design (ManiGuard-Bench)

- **Task Families:** ManiGuard-Bench features 200 locked base tasks across six contact-rich household manipulation families. These are organized by a skill × constraint taxonomy, encompassing three skill levels (pick-and-place, articulated-object interaction, long-horizon multi-object manipulation) and two formal constraint classes (spatial invariants, temporal ordering). Key to the design is that safety is orthogonal to task success, enabling separate assessment. Examples include:
 - **Clutter:** Retrieving targets from dense tableware without dropping or toppling, including a liquid subset forbidding spills.
 - **Cabinet:** Placing objects into articulated receptacles without harming anything along the path.
 - **Stack:** Ordered manipulation of stacked objects without toppling or dropping.
 - **Lid, Jar, Dusty:** Ordering families with prerequisites (e.g., cap container before transport, close jar before lifting, clean before pouring), enforced by LTLf until-clauses and global invariants.
- **ID/OOD Task Generation:** Each base task defines an in-distribution (ID) condition. Four single-axis out-of-distribution (OOD) variants are deterministically generated by perturbing:
 1. **Target appearance:** Recolor the target object with a perceptually distinct color.
 2. **Language:** Rewrite the instruction using rule-based verb and preposition substitutions.
 3. **Location:** Displace task objects within a bounded, reachability-checked region.
 4. **Environment:** Embed the setup into different background rooms.
 This "single-factor attribution" approach ensures that changes in performance can be attributed to the perturbed axis, as the task goal and safety specification $\varphi$ remain fixed across variants. An initial-state safety gate ensures no episode starts in an already violated configuration.
- **Scene Generation:** Task instances are constructed by loading relevant environments, placing objects with distractors, and checking placements against a grasp database.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Framework

The benchmark operates within Isaac Sim and OmniGibson, leveraging PhysX for contact and fluid dynamics. A Franka Panda arm and a joint-space controller are used. A symbolic abstraction layer based on BDDL maps continuous simulator states, contacts, poses, and particle information to task-level predicates, which then ground the atomic propositions for LTLf monitoring. The same fixed camera placements are used for both data collection and evaluation.

- **LTL Labeling & Runtime Monitor:** Atomic propositions are grounded in directly checkable physical quantities (e.g., `dropped`, `upright`, `on_top`, `open`, `touching`, `spilled`). Each LTLf sub-specification is compiled into a DFA using Spot, providing per-step categorical labels for violations. Episode-level safety verdict is gated on policy engagement (first whole-arm contact with a task-relevant object), ensuring that safety is assessed based on the policy's actions.
- **Metrics:** Evaluation uses a four-quadrant outcome classification: Safe-success (SSR), Unsafe-success, Safe-unsuccess, and Unsafe-unsuccess. Headline metrics include SSR, Task Success Rate (TSR), and Safety Violation Rate (SVR). Engagement-aware diagnostics are also reported, such as Engaged (Pr[eng]), Engaged Violation Rate (EVR) (violations only among engaged rollouts), and Vacuous-safe share (apparent safety from inaction).

#### 3.4. Trajectory Generation and Data Suite

ManiGuard includes a safety-annotated trajectory-generation pipeline that produces data for downstream safety-aware learning.
- **Shared Substrate:** Both generation methods utilize the same scene, camera, control, and LTLf runtime monitoring interface as the evaluation framework, ensuring consistency.
- **Automated Generation:** Uses a curated database of 1,547 validated 6-DoF grasps for 221 object instances. It employs a three-layer architecture: a fixed physics/motion planning substrate (cuRobo), a generic executor (plan-execute-gate-record loop), and family-specific motion skeletons. Trajectories are generated, executed, and only retained if they successfully complete the task and pass the LTLf safety gate.
- **Human Teleoperation:** Provides a complementary method for demonstration collection, allowing human operators to control the simulated Franka via GELLO or SO-101 leader arms. These trajectories are recorded and filtered using the same safety monitor.
- **Data Release:** The framework releases 8,000 safe-success demonstrations (40 per base task), totaling approximately 11.6 million frames, in a joint-native five-camera LeRobot format.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation benchmarks zero-shot and fine-tuned Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies on ManiGuard-Bench across over 23,000 evaluation rollouts.

- **Q1: VLA Performance Across the Taxonomy (ID Safety Evaluation)**
 - **Zero-shot baselines** demonstrated near-zero task success rate (TSR) and safe-success rate (SSR), with observed "safety" largely attributable to inaction (vacuous-safe share between 50-80%). Engaged violation rates (EVR) for zero-shot policies were high (30-54%).
 - **Fine-tuning (SFT) on ManiGuard-Bench demonstrations** substantially improved performance. SSR rose from near zero to 7.5–29.8%, and engaged-and-safe behavior increased from 16–40% to 51–72%. The engaged violation rate (EVR) generally decreased with fine-tuning, even as engagement increased.
 - **Task completion does not imply safety:** 6–21% of successful rollouts by fine-tuned policies still violated the safety specification. For instance, two policies with almost identical task success rates (e.g., $\pi_0$-SFT and $\pi_{0.5}$-SFT at ~32% TSR) could differ by six percentage points in their safety violation rate, highlighting the need for independent safety evaluation.
 - Safety failures concentrated in specific task families, with Cabinet and Dusty tasks remaining below 2% safe-success for all fine-tuned policies. Violations for weaker policies tended to occur at the moment of engagement, while stronger policies might violate mid-manipulation.

- **Q2: Safety Generalization Under Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Shift**
 - Safe-success rates degraded under single-axis OOD shifts (target appearance, instruction language, object location, environment background), even though the underlying safety specification ($\varphi$) remained fixed.
 - Different policies exhibited varying sensitivities to different types of shifts. For example, $\pi_{0.5}$-SFT was most affected by environment and target appearance changes, while SmolVLA-SFT showed a significant drop in safe success due to rephrased instructions. This suggests that current policies may learn behaviors correlated with specific scene elements rather than a robust grounding in the abstract safety constraints.

- **Q3: Task Completion and Safety Scaling with Demonstrations**
 - The relationship between demonstration count and safety varied by task family.
 - For **Clutter** tasks, where the engaged violation rate (EVR) was already near zero across all budgets, increasing demonstrations primarily improved task completion and consequently safe-success.
 - For **Cabinet** tasks, fine-tuning initially increased engagement and reduced EVR, but safe-success remained low and did not show monotonic improvement with more demonstrations. This finding indicates that simply scaling the quantity of *same-kind* safe-success demonstrations may not be sufficient for challenging, contact-rich tasks.

- **Q4: Effect of Safety-Specification Format**
 - Explicitly stating safety requirements in the instruction prompt (either in natural language (NL) or LTLf format) generally improved engaged safety for **Jar** tasks, with NL yielding larger gains than LTLf.
 - However, for **Stack** tasks, no consistent benefit or dominant format was observed. Both explicit formats sometimes reduced Safe | Eng. rates, and performance varied across policies. This suggests that the format of the safety instruction alone may not reliably steer policy behavior, particularly in more complex scenarios.

- **Q5: Sim-to-Real Agreement**
 - An initial study comparing simulation and physical Franka results for Clutter and a truncated Cabinet task showed partial agreement.
 - Simulation accurately predicted the *relative ranking* of policies for both task success (Pearson $r = 0.91$) and safe success (Pearson $r = 0.88$).
 - However, the correlation for the overall violation rate was low (Pearson $r = 0.09$), indicating that simulation did not reliably predict the absolute frequency of safety violations in the real world.
 - Despite differences in frequency, the *types of unsafe behaviors* observed in simulation, such as knocking over objects during drawer opening, recurred on the physical robot. This suggests that simulation can provide useful qualitative insights into safety issues, even if quantitative predictions are less precise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ManiGuard contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by establishing a framework for addressing the safety imperative, which is becoming increasingly critical as foundation models move towards real-world deployment. The work has several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Joint Evaluation of Task Completion and Safety:** The research empirically demonstrates that task completion alone is an insufficient metric for evaluating robotic manipulation. Policies can achieve task goals through unsafe execution, and conversely, appear "safe" by being inactive. ManiGuard's four-quadrant outcome classification and engagement-aware metrics provide a more comprehensive and nuanced assessment, urging the research community to adopt joint evaluation of both objectives.
- **Exposure of Safety Gaps in Current VLAs:** ManiGuard-Bench exposes significant safety deficiencies in current state-of-the-art VLAs when confronted with contact-rich manipulation tasks and formally specified safety constraints. Despite fine-tuning on safety-annotated data improving safe task completion, a substantial gap remains, particularly for complex tasks and under distribution shifts. This highlights that current VLAs may not yet robustly ground their behavior in abstract safety constraints or generalize safety effectively.
- **Guidance for Future Research Directions:**
 - **Specification-Aware Data Collection:** The scaling study suggests that simply increasing the volume of existing safe demonstrations may not be enough to close the safety gap for certain tasks. This motivates research into "specification-aware" data collection strategies, where monitors actively identify safety-critical states, near-failure modes, or under-covered regions to guide the generation of more targeted and diverse safety-relevant trajectories.
 - **Monitoring as a Learning Signal:** The framework highlights the potential of using runtime monitors not just for evaluation but also as direct learning signals. Per-step violation information could be integrated into policy optimization as structured rewards, costs, or for prioritizing counterexamples and runtime shielding of unsafe actions. This can shift learning from mere imitation of successful trajectories to explicit optimization of safety.
 - **Robustness to Distribution Shifts:** The degradation of safe performance under observation shifts (while $\varphi$ is fixed) suggests that policies might be learning scene-specific correlations rather than generalized safety principles. Future work needs to focus on improving the robustness of VLA policies to various forms of distribution shift, ensuring safety generalizes across diverse environments and conditions.
 - **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The findings on sim-to-real agreement indicate that while simulation can predict relative policy performance, accurately forecasting absolute violation frequencies remains a challenge. This underscores the need for continued research into improving simulation fidelity and developing calibration techniques to enhance the predictive power of simulated safety evaluations for real-world scenarios.
- **Actionable Diagnostics:** By grounding evaluation and data generation in the same formal LTLf specifications, ManiGuard provides actionable diagnostics, reporting *which* constraint failed and at *which step*. This level of detail is crucial for directing targeted data collection, policy debugging, and focused improvement efforts, moving beyond aggregate performance metrics.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of ManiGuard-Bench, its specification library, and the trajectory-generation pipeline provides a public resource for the robotics and AI communities. This supports collaborative efforts towards developing broader safety specifications, more comprehensive evaluation methodologies, and task- and constraint-aware learning algorithms.

In conclusion, ManiGuard offers a rigorous framework and a benchmark that makes safety a first-class objective in robotic manipulation research. Its findings illuminate current limitations in VLA safety and propose concrete avenues for advancing the field towards more reliable and trustworthy autonomous systems.
