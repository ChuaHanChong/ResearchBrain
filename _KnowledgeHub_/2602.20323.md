# PhysMem: Scaling Test-Time Memory for Embodied Physical Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysMem lets a frozen VLM robot planner learn physical principles at test time via a scientific memory loop: it records interaction experiences, drafts candidate hypotheses, and verifies them through targeted interaction before committing them as principles. On brick insertion, principled abstraction reaches 76% success versus 23% for direct experience retrieval.

## Problem

- VLMs describe physics concepts abstractly but misjudge instance-specific parameters, like rolling distance or stone stability, that visual pretraining alone cannot reveal.
- Retrieval-augmented and reflection methods apply retrieved past experience directly without checking it still holds, letting stale rules harden into repeated errors.
- Parameter-updating adaptation methods (online RL, memory-augmented finetuning) produce implicit, uninterpretable knowledge that cannot be inspected, edited, or transferred.

## Method

- **Scientific Memory Loop**: ==resonance-checked== experience collection flags surprising outcomes, triggers clustering into candidate hypotheses, then action-level attribution updates confidence for promotion.
- ==Three-tier memory architecture== separates raw episodic experiences, working-memory typed hypotheses (AVOID/PREFER/SEQUENCE), and long-term verified principles with importance-based decay.
- ==Memory folding== compresses supporting episodes into promoted principles to keep context tractable, and verified principles/hypotheses are injected into the frozen VLM's prompt at inference.

## Results

- On medium-difficulty brick insertion, PhysMem reaches **76%** success versus **53%** no-memory, **23%** direct retrieval, **53%** MemER-style, and **61%** Reflexion-style baselines.
- Real-world 30-minute deployments show Parts Organization score rising from **-1** to **9.7** and Ball Navigation from **0.7** to **14.7** with memory versus flat without.
- Gains scale with base VLM capability, from **+5%** (Gemini-ER-1.5) to **+23%** (Gemini-3-Flash) on medium tasks; removing verification drops success from **76%** to **64%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Verification-before-commitment matters more than memory access itself: unverified retrieval can underperform no memory once situations stop repeating exactly.
> - More retrieved context is not inherently better; token-heavy memory trailed leaner verified-principle retrieval while costing far more tokens.
> - Test-time memory mainly amplifies a model's existing capability rather than installing new physics knowledge, so gains scale with base-model strength.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "PhysMem" addresses a recognized challenge in the field of artificial intelligence and robotics: the gap between the declarative knowledge held by Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and their ability to apply this knowledge to specific, often novel, physical situations in an embodied context. VLMs have demonstrated proficiency in understanding abstract physical concepts such as friction, balance, and momentum through extensive pre-training on web-scale data. When deployed as robot planners, however, they frequently encounter difficulties in predicting the precise application of these principles to unique objects and environments without direct interaction experience. This limitation can result in planning failures, as a single misjudgment in contact or dynamics can invalidate an entire action sequence.

The paper positions itself within the broader context of adaptation paradigms for deployed VLM/VLA (Vision-Language-Action) agents. Existing approaches to enable deployed agents to improve through interaction include:
- **Online Reinforcement Learning (RL)**: Updates the model parameters through gradient descent, leading to implicit weight adjustments.
- **Trained Memory VLAs**: Incorporates memory mechanisms directly into the model architecture, often requiring imitation finetuning or end-to-end VLA training.
- **Reflection (in-episode)**: Uses natural language feedback to enable LLMs to learn from failures within an episode, typically without parameter updates.
- **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**: Retrieves raw past experiences or relevant information and feeds it as context to the VLM, also without parameter updates.

"PhysMem" introduces a distinct paradigm: *verified abstraction*. This approach also operates with a frozen base VLM and structured memory, but critically incorporates a verification step before committing learned knowledge. This contrasts with retrieval-augmented methods that apply past experiences without explicit verification, which the authors argue can lead to "stale rules" when environmental conditions change. By emphasizing the formation and revision of compact, verifiable principles rather than direct memorization of episodic events, PhysMem aligns with cognitive models of human learning and the scientific method, where hypotheses are tested and refined. The work aims to bridge the domain gap between VLM general capabilities and specific physical grounding in real-world manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable Vision-Language Model (VLM) robot planners to acquire and utilize useful physical understanding during deployment through direct interaction, without modifying the VLM's core parameters. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing the Gap Between Declarative Knowledge and Physical Grounding**: VLMs, despite their ability to describe physical concepts in general terms, often struggle to predict the behavior of specific objects in particular environments. For instance, a VLM may comprehend the concept of friction but fail to accurately predict how a specific ball will roll on a given surface, or it may understand stability but misidentify a stable foundation among irregular stones. This limitation leads to frequent planning failures in embodied tasks where precise physical intuition derived from interaction is critical.
2. **Learning at Test Time Without Model Updates**: The authors aim to explore whether robots can learn new physical principles on the fly, as they interact with the world, rather than relying solely on pre-trained knowledge or requiring extensive retraining. This "test-time learning" approach is particularly relevant for scenarios where physical properties are not visually apparent, cannot be easily inferred from pre-training data, or change over time. The constraint of not updating VLM parameters isolates the learning to the memory framework, ensuring that any performance improvements are attributable to enhanced reasoning rather than changes in the base model's capabilities.
3. **Overcoming Limitations of Direct Experience Retrieval**: A common approach to enhance VLM performance is to provide memory in the form of stored experiences, which are then retrieved based on scene similarity. However, the authors argue that embodied situations rarely repeat exactly. Direct retrieval or reflection-based methods often apply past experiences without verifying their continued relevance or validity in the current context. This can lead to past experience hardening into a "stale rule," turning a previously useful heuristic into a source of repeated errors when physical conditions undergo minor changes. The research seeks to avoid this "dogmatism problem."
4. **Developing Verified Abstraction**: Motivated by human cognitive processes and the scientific method, the paper advocates for forming compact, abstract principles rather than merely memorizing individual events. A central motivation is to establish a mechanism that not only stores experiences but also clusters them into testable hypotheses, verifies these hypotheses through targeted interaction, and revises them when evidence contradicts expectations. This "scientific memory loop" is intended to produce robust, generalizable physical understanding.
5. **Creating Interpretable and Transferable Knowledge**: A further objective is to ensure that the learned physical principles are human-readable. This interpretability allows for inspection and editing by human operators, and potentially facilitates knowledge transfer to new, related tasks or environments. This contrasts with implicit policy changes that result from model parameter updates, which are often difficult to interpret.

To achieve these motivations, the research focuses on three distinct manipulation tasks:
- **Parts Organization**: Requires learning spatial relationships for efficient packing of irregular shapes.
- **Ball Navigation**: Involves discovering contact dynamics and surface friction for controlling a ball through an obstacle course.
- **Balanced Stacking**: Demands stability judgments for irregular stones, where mass distribution and surface friction are not visually apparent.

The overarching goal is to demonstrate that a structured test-time learning framework can significantly improve VLM planners' performance on physical tasks by generating principles that are effective for decision-making and interpretable for human understanding.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PhysMem is presented as a test-time memory framework designed to enable Vision-Language Model (VLM) robot planners to learn physical principles through interaction. Its core mechanism is a "scientific memory loop" that iteratively collects experiences, generates and tests hypotheses, and promotes validated knowledge. The system does not modify the VLM's parameters.

**System Architecture**:
The PhysMem architecture comprises three main components:
1. **VLM-based Planner**: This component receives observations, task context, and retrieved principles/hypotheses from memory. It uses this information to generate high-level decisions or options (e.g., which part to place, which direction to push, which stone to stack). The paper utilized Gemini-3.0-Flash as the VLM planner in real-world experiments.
2. **Memory System**: Organized into three tiers—episodic, working, and long-term—this system is responsible for storing, processing, and consolidating knowledge.
3. **Executor**: This component translates the VLM's high-level decisions into low-level robot actions using pre-defined policies or motion planners. This separation ensures that performance improvements are attributed to better physical understanding rather than improved motor control.

**Scientific Memory Loop (SML)**:
The SML is the central operational mechanism, consisting of four phases:
1. **Experience Collection with Resonance Checking**:
 - **Experience Definition**: Each interaction generates an experience `e = (o, ω, r, c, s)`, recording the observation (`o`), selected option (`ω`), outcome (`r` ∈ {0, 1}), context (`c` – task description, subtask), and symbolic state (`s` – discrete features like action type, object properties).
 - **Episodic Memory**: Raw experiences are stored in an episodic memory, bounded by a maximum capacity (e.g., `N_max` = 3000). Older or folded experiences are subject to garbage collection.
 - **Resonance Checking**: A resonance score `ρ(e, P_active)` is calculated to measure how well an experience aligns with the currently active principles (`P_active`). If `ρ < 1` (a "surprise"), the experience is prioritized for consolidation, indicating a discrepancy between expectation and outcome. If `ρ = 1`, existing principles are reinforced. This mechanism focuses learning on novel situations.

2. **Hypothesis Generation**:
 - **Clustering**: Experiences in episodic memory are periodically clustered based on symbolic similarity (e.g., using hierarchical agglomerative clustering on text embeddings of symbolic states). Clusters with a sufficient number of experiences (`|C_k| ≥ n_min`) are considered for hypothesis generation.
 - **Reflection Model**: A reflection model (a VLM in real-world experiments, or an LLM in simulation, e.g., Qwen3-VL) analyzes these clusters, along with existing principles and hypotheses, to generate candidate hypotheses (`H_k`). This avoids redundant knowledge generation.
 - **Typed Hypotheses**: Hypotheses are generated in a structured, typed format, such as:
 - `AVOID`: "Don't do X when Y" (derived from failures).
 - `PREFER`: "Do X when Y" (derived from successes).
 - `SEQUENCE`: "Do X before Y" (temporal constraints).
 - `COMPARE`: "X works better than Y when Z".

3. **Action-Level Attribution**:
 - **Confidence Update**: Instead of evaluating hypotheses solely on episode-level success, confidence (`conf(h)`) is updated based on the outcomes of *specific actions* related to the hypothesis. This isolates the effect of individual actions from confounding factors within an episode. The confidence is adjusted proportionally to the success rate of relevant actions.

4. **Verification and Principle Promotion**:
 - **Promotion**: Hypotheses that achieve a high confidence score (`conf(h) ≥ τ_p`, typically 0.8) and are supported by sufficient evidence (e.g., `|E_support| ≥ 3` experiences) are promoted to "principles" and moved to long-term memory.
 - **Memory Folding**: Upon promotion, the supporting source experiences for the principle are "folded" into it. This compresses episodic memory, reducing storage and retrieval overhead while retaining the learned knowledge.
 - **Refutation**: Hypotheses with low confidence (`conf(h) ≤ τ_r`) and contradicting evidence are refuted and removed from working memory, preventing misleading information from guiding future decisions.
 - **Principle Decay**: Principles in long-term memory have importance scores that decay over time (`score_t+1 = score_t * γ`, with `γ = 0.995`), allowing for the gradual forgetting of outdated or less relevant knowledge.

**Memory Architecture Details**:
- **Episodic Memory**: Stores all raw experiences `e = (o, ω, r, c, s)`. Bounded by `N_max`, with a garbage collection strategy to remove old or folded experiences.
- **Working Memory**: Holds candidate hypotheses (`h`) currently under testing. Each hypothesis has a confidence score, updated through action-level attribution.
- **Long-term Memory**: Contains verified principles (`p`) that have passed the verification stage. These principles are used to guide VLM decisions and decay over time.

**Retrieval and Application**:
At inference time, PhysMem retrieves the most relevant principles and active hypotheses by symbolic filtering (matching action types and object properties in the current state) followed by semantic ranking. The top-k (`k=5` in experiments) retrieved items are then injected into the VLM's prompt as structured text, influencing its high-level decision-making. If the resonance score is low, the system prioritizes learning new information over strictly applying potentially outdated prior knowledge.

**Experimental Setup**:
The methodology included evaluation in two distinct environments:
- **Real-World Manipulation**: An xArm6 robot with soft grippers and multi-view Intel RealSense D435 cameras was used. The VLM planner was Gemini-3.0-Flash, and the reflection model for hypothesis generation was Qwen3-VL. Three tasks were designed: Parts Organization, Ball Navigation, and Balanced Stacking, each requiring inference of unobservable physical properties.
- **Simulation Benchmark**: The Reflect-VLM brick insertion benchmark, based on MuJoCo physics with a Franka Panda robot, was used for large-scale, controlled experiments. This setting allowed for evaluation across multiple VLM backbones (Gemini-3-Flash, Gemini-ER-1.5, GPT-5.1, Qwen3-VL-235B) and varying difficulty levels (2-8 bricks).

The experimental design intentionally separated high-level VLM planning from low-level motion control to ensure that observed performance improvements were due to enhanced reasoning and physical understanding, rather than improved execution capabilities. Both task success rates and plan quality metrics (like resonance score) were used for evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across real-world manipulation tasks and a simulation benchmark demonstrate that PhysMem enables Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to acquire and apply physical principles through interaction, leading to improved embodied reasoning.

1. **Comparison Against Memory Paradigms**:
 - On the medium-difficulty brick insertion task, PhysMem achieved a 76% success rate. This substantially outperformed a "No Memory" baseline (53%), direct retrieval of raw experiences (23%), MemER-style retrieval (53%), MemoryVLA-style token memory (35%), and Reflexion-style free-form reflection (61%).
 - These results suggest that merely providing memory or context (as in direct retrieval or token memory) is insufficient; the *abstraction and verification* of principles are crucial. Unverified memory approaches often performed worse than the "No Memory" baseline on harder tasks, indicating that stale or inappropriate past experiences can degrade performance.

2. **Learned Physical Principles and Resonance**:
 - The resonance score (ρ), which measures the alignment between the VLM's predictions and observed outcomes, consistently increased over deployment sessions in real-world tasks. Across Parts Organization, Ball Navigation, and Balanced Stacking, ρ typically climbed from approximately 0.2 in early episodes to around 0.9 by episode 10.
 - This trend indicates that PhysMem accumulates verified physical understanding, allowing the planner to anticipate task dynamics more accurately, rather than merely achieving task success through conservative or suboptimal strategies. Episodes in the high-resonance regime (ρ > 0.7) showed significantly higher scores (2.3×) than early, low-resonance episodes.
 - The principles learned were human-readable, describing specific spatial constraints, dynamics rules, and stability heuristics relevant to the tasks.

3. **Test-Time Evolution**:
 - PhysMem enabled clear learning curves in real-world tasks. For Parts Organization, the score improved from -1 to 9.7 with full memory, compared to remaining near 0 without memory. In Ball Navigation, the score increased from 0.7 to 14.7.
 - The rate and extent of improvement varied with task complexity. Complex dynamics, such as in Ball Navigation, benefited most from using full experience, while simpler tasks like Balanced Stacking showed diminishing returns with increased experience beyond 50%.

4. **Memory Transfer to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Scenarios**:
 - In transfer experiments with OOD task variants, prior knowledge alone was beneficial when the underlying physics were similar (e.g., Balanced Stacking, where stability principles generalized well, and Parts Organization, where packing principles reduced common errors).
 - However, for tasks with significantly altered dynamics (e.g., Ball Navigation with new ball types), prior knowledge alone provided no benefit over zero-shot performance. In these cases, test-time adaptation via PhysMem's scientific memory loop was essential, improving success from 10% to 40% by learning novel friction and elasticity properties.
 - The full PhysMem system, combining prior knowledge and test-time adaptation, achieved the best performance across all OOD tasks, demonstrating the complementary nature of these two components.

5. **Scaling Across VLMs**:
 - The performance gain provided by PhysMem scaled with the baseline capability of the VLM. Gemini-3-Flash, the most capable VLM tested, showed the largest improvement (+23% on medium difficulty, from 53% to 76%). Less capable models like Gemini-ER-1.5 exhibited smaller gains (+5%, from 29% to 34%).
 - This indicates that PhysMem amplifies the VLM's existing reasoning capabilities by providing grounded context, rather than installing entirely new physical knowledge.

6. **Principle Scaling and Ablation Studies**:
 - Performance on medium-difficulty tasks showed rapid improvement between 2 and 8 principles learned, then stabilized with further principle accumulation (e.g., 76% at 64+ principles). Hard tasks also improved, often by accumulating "AVOID" constraints from failures.
 - **Ablation studies** confirmed the criticality of PhysMem's components:
 - **Principle Abstraction** was identified as the load-bearing component; direct retrieval of raw experiences consistently failed, dropping to 8% success on hard tasks.
 - **Resonance filtering**, **verification**, and **working memory** became increasingly important with task complexity, preventing the degradation of decisions from conflicting or unverified hypotheses.
 - **Forgetting** (Ebbinghaus-style decay) provided a small accuracy boost on harder tasks while significantly reducing token consumption (up to 4.8× reduction), by preventing stale principles from interfering.
 - **Memory folding** compressed redundant experiences into principles, providing efficiency gains (reducing token cost by 1.4× to 2.8×) with minimal impact on accuracy.

Overall, the findings indicate that PhysMem's scientific memory loop enables VLMs to develop a more grounded and robust understanding of physical interactions during deployment, leading to consistent performance improvements and the generation of interpretable physical principles.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "PhysMem" offers several significant contributions and has the potential for considerable impact on the field of embodied AI and robotics:

1. **Enabling Test-Time Physical Reasoning**: The most significant contribution is the demonstration that VLM robot planners can acquire useful physical understanding from interaction at test time, without necessitating updates to the VLM's parameters. This addresses a critical limitation where VLMs struggle to ground abstract knowledge in specific, real-world physical dynamics. This capability allows robots to adapt to novel objects and environments autonomously, which is crucial for deployment in unstructured and dynamic settings.

2. **Introduction of Verified Abstraction**: PhysMem introduces a novel paradigm of "verified abstraction" for memory-augmented VLM agents. By actively testing hypotheses against new observations before committing them as principles, the framework mitigates the "dogmatism problem" associated with unverified retrieval, where stale or incorrect past experiences can lead to persistent errors. This principled approach to knowledge acquisition, inspired by the scientific method, enhances the robustness and adaptability of robotic systems.

3. **Generation of Interpretable and Transferable Knowledge**: The framework produces human-readable principles that are effective for decision-making. This interpretability is a key advantage, as it allows human operators to inspect, understand, and even edit the learned knowledge. Such explicit, structured knowledge is also more amenable to transfer across different tasks or similar environments, providing a strong starting point for new deployments and reducing the need for extensive re-learning. This contrasts with the opaque, implicit knowledge embedded in neural network weights resulting from traditional online learning methods.

4. **Demonstrated Performance Gains**: The consistent and substantial performance improvements observed across three real-world manipulation tasks and a simulation benchmark, and across various VLM backbones, validate the effectiveness of PhysMem. The gains over no-memory baselines and other memory paradigms highlight the practical utility of the scientific memory loop for complex physical reasoning tasks.

5. **Scaling with VLM Capability**: The finding that PhysMem's benefits scale with the underlying capability of the VLM suggests that the framework acts as an amplifier for existing VLM intelligence. This implies that as VLMs become more capable, the effectiveness of test-time learning frameworks like PhysMem will likely increase, driving further advancements in embodied reasoning.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy**: By enabling robots to learn and adapt to physical conditions on the fly, PhysMem can contribute to the development of more autonomous and intelligent robotic systems capable of operating in less controlled, real-world environments. This could impact applications in manufacturing, logistics, service robotics, and even hazardous environments where human intervention is limited.
- **More Robust and Adaptable Systems**: The verification mechanism fundamentally enhances the robustness of VLM-driven robots. The ability to revise beliefs based on new evidence makes these systems less prone to errors when faced with variations in physical properties or environmental shifts, which are common in real-world deployment.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration**: The generation of interpretable principles could facilitate more effective human-robot collaboration. Humans can better understand the robot's reasoning, provide targeted corrections, or transfer insights gained from one robot to another, leading to more efficient and safer interactions.
- **Foundation for Lifelong Learning**: PhysMem's architecture, with its tiered memory and forgetting mechanism, provides a foundation for truly lifelong learning in robots. The system can continuously accumulate and refine knowledge over extended periods without suffering from catastrophic forgetting or being overwhelmed by redundant information.

**Future Directions**:
The paper also outlines several avenues for future research, including the integration of richer observation modalities (e.g., tactile, force, audio), the exploration of continuous reasoning representations beyond text (e.g., visual chain-of-thought, world models for predicting future states), and bridging the gap between high-level planning and end-to-end VLA execution. The idea of running the verification step against world-model rollouts, where text principles and learned dynamics can share a single loop, represents a promising direction for more efficient and comprehensive hypothesis testing. Such advancements could further unlock the potential of embodied physical reasoning.
