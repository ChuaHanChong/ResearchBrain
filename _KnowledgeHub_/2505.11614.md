# Using Reinforcement Learning to Train Large Language Models to Explain Human Decisions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Princeton University and Georgia Institute of Technology trained Large Language Models using reinforcement learning to generate natural language explanations for human risky choices, while maintaining predictive accuracy comparable to supervised methods. The resulting Chains-of-Thought reveal dominant cognitive mechanisms, such as expected value computation and risk aversion, that the model leverages to explain human decisions.

## Problem

- Computational cognitive models, especially complex neural networks, often lack interpretability, functioning as "black boxes" despite high predictive accuracy.
- Large Language Models (LLMs) can predict human behavior but typically do not provide transparent, natural language reasoning for their decisions.
- Cognitive science aims for deep theoretical understanding of human behavior, which opaque models hinder, leading to an "interpretability dilemma."

## Method

- Applied Reinforcement Learning, specifically the ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== algorithm, to fine-tune a Large Language Model (Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct) to generate step-by-step reasoning (Chains-of-Thought) alongside predictions.
- Designed an ==outcome-based reward function== that incentivized both accurate prediction of human choices and structured, well-formatted reasoning explanations, also incorporating a format reward to guide output structure.
- Compared the efficacy of RL training against standard ==Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)== and a Centaur-style SFT variant on a large-scale human risky-choice dataset (choices13k).

## Results

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) trained LLMs achieved predictive accuracy on human risky choices comparable to supervised fine-tuning methods (e.g., MSE around **0.0148**).
- RL models generated more elaborate and psychologically grounded explanations, with human evaluators perceiving RL-generated CoTs as more reasonable in **61.5%** of cases compared to backbone models.
- Analysis of the CoTs revealed that "expected value computation" and "risk aversion" were the two most frequently used cognitive mechanisms (**29-36%** of thoughts), alongside other psychological factors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement Learning can effectively guide LLMs to produce psychologically grounded and interpretable natural language explanations for human decisions.
> - The quality of the generated Chain-of-Thought (CoT) is causally linked to prediction accuracy, suggesting that the reasoning process itself contributes to better behavioral fit.
> - The success of RL in eliciting meaningful CoT and accurate predictions is dependent on the underlying backbone LLM's inherent capabilities and pre-trained knowledge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the intersection of cognitive modeling and artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to understand human decision-making. Cognitive modeling traditionally aims to develop computational models that not only predict human behavior but also offer insights into the underlying cognitive mechanisms. Recent advancements in neural networks have led to models with high predictive accuracy for human behavior, yet these models frequently lack interpretability, presenting a challenge in extracting meaningful explanations of cognitive processes. For instance, models like the Centaur model, which fine-tunes LLMs for behavioral prediction, demonstrate impressive performance but provide limited explanatory power regarding human cognition.

A related area of research explores using LLMs for the automated discovery of cognitive models. These approaches typically involve prompting LLMs to generate symbolic programs or heuristics that can be fitted to behavioral data, yielding inherently interpretable theories. However, these methods often rely on the LLM's in-context learning capabilities without further fine-tuning for model discovery during the search process.

This work addresses the existing gap between the predictive power and explanatory limitations of current cognitive models. It positions LLMs as potential dual-purpose cognitive models capable of both accurate prediction and interpretable, natural language explanations. The paper proposes that the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning generated by LLMs can serve as a verbalized account of cognitive mechanisms. By integrating reinforcement learning (RL) with outcome-based rewards, the research aims to guide LLMs not only to predict human choices but also to articulate the reasoning processes behind these predictions, thereby contributing to the development of more transparent and theoretically informative computational models of cognition. This approach seeks to move beyond mere behavioral matching towards a deeper theoretical understanding of human cognition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop interpretable cognitive models using pretrained Large Language Models (LLMs) that can both accurately predict human behavior and provide explicit, natural language explanations of the underlying cognitive mechanisms.

The motivation for this work stems from a recognized dilemma in cognitive modeling:
- **Predictive Power vs. Interpretability:** Contemporary computational models, particularly those based on neural networks and fine-tuned LLMs, have achieved significant success in predicting human behavior across various tasks. However, these models often function as "black boxes," offering limited insight into the cognitive processes they purportedly capture. This lack of interpretability hinders their utility for advancing theoretical understanding in cognitive science, as a direct match in behavior does not inherently equate to a deep theoretical explanation.
- **The Need for Explanation:** Cognitive science seeks not just to predict what humans will do, but *why* they do it. Current high-performing models struggle to articulate the "why," limiting their contribution to developing new cognitive theories.
- **Leveraging LLM Capabilities:** The authors identify the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generation capabilities of LLMs as a promising avenue to address this interpretability gap. They hypothesize that the sequential reasoning steps generated by LLMs, typically prior to their final responses, can be treated as verbalized accounts of cognitive mechanisms. This approach would allow cognitive scientists to examine these CoTs to assess their meaningfulness and interpretability as explanations for observed human data.

Specifically, the paper sets out to:
1. **Evaluate Reinforcement Learning for CoT Generation:** Test whether reinforcement learning (RL) with outcome-based rewards can effectively post-train a backbone LLM to generate explicit reasoning traces (CoTs) that explain human decisions in the domain of risky choice.
2. **Benchmark Against Existing Methods:** Compare the performance of this RL-based approach against established supervised fine-tuning (SFT) methods, including a variant used in the Centaur model, which is known for its strong predictive capabilities in cognitive tasks.
3. **Assess CoT Quality and Adaptability:** Analyze the quality, nature, and causal influence of the generated CoTs on model predictions. Furthermore, investigate whether the CoTs adapt to the underlying structure of different training data (e.g., human choices vs. synthetic data from a rational model).
4. **Identify Cognitive Mechanisms:** Characterize the types of cognitive mechanisms and psychological theories that emerge in the LLM's CoT reasoning following RL training.
5. **Examine Model Capacity Dependence:** Investigate how the capabilities of the backbone LLM influence the success of RL in generating high-quality CoT explanations.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of training LLMs to serve as comprehensive cognitive models that simultaneously achieve high predictive accuracy and provide interpretable, theory-rich explanations in natural language.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a comparative approach, evaluating three distinct post-training strategies for Large Language Models (LLMs) applied to the task of modeling human risky choice.

#### 3.1. Backbone LLM and Adaptation

- **Backbone LLM:** Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) served as the base model for all experiments.
- **Adaptation:** Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) modules were used for fine-tuning. The LoRA configuration involved setting both rank and alpha parameters to 32, applying LoRA modules to all linear layers, and including a dropout probability of 0.05. This resulted in approximately 80.74 million trainable parameters, constituting 1.05% of the 7B model's total parameters.

#### 3.2. Dataset

- The `choices13k` dataset (Peterson et al., 2021), a large-scale collection of human risky choice problems, was utilized.
- The dataset was partitioned into a 90/10 split for training (13,102 problems) and testing (1,462 problems) at the unique problem identifier level, ensuring no overlap of specific problems between sets.

#### 3.3. Post-training Strategies

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
 - **Objective:** To adapt the LLM to predict aggregated human choice proportions for risky options.
 - **Input Format:** Risky choice problems described in natural language (e.g., "Option A offers a 100.0% chance to win $27.0.").
 - **Output Format:** JSON object containing rounded choice percentages for Option A and Option B (e.g., `{"option_A": 42, "option_B": 58}`).
 - **Training:** 6 epochs, fixed learning rate of $10^{-5}$, AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017), and gradient accumulation steps of 8. This followed a standard autoregressive next-token prediction paradigm.

2. **Centaur-style SFT (Binz et al., 2025):**
 - **Objective:** A variant of SFT designed to adapt LLMs to cognitive tasks by focusing the learning on human data points.
 - **Input/Output Format:** Similar to SFT, but human choice proportions in the JSON output were enclosed within special brackets (`<<` and `>>`).
 - **Training:** Only tokens within these special brackets contributed to the loss function during training, masking out other tokens. The training parameters were otherwise identical to standard SFT.

3. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):**
 - **Algorithm:** GRPO (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) was used to fine-tune the LLM.
 - **Generation Process:** For each training step and risky-choice problem (q), the model generated 12 candidate completions (`o_1..., o_G`). Each completion could be up to 1,024 tokens long, designed to accommodate intermediate Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning followed by a final prediction in JSON format.
 - **Reward Function:** The reward `R(q, o)` for a completion `o` was composed of two parts:
 - **Outcome-based reward:** `1 - |o_B - p_B|` if the model's predicted probability for option B (`o_B`) and option A (`o_A`) were coherent (0 <= `o_A`, `o_B` <= 1, and `o_A` + `o_B` = 1). If not coherent, the reward was 0. `p_B` represents the empirical proportion of human participants choosing option B. The maximum correctness reward was 1.
 - **Format reward:** +0.25 if exactly one JSON output was generated, and an additional +0.25 if the JSON output appeared after reasoning tokens. The maximum format reward was 0.5.
 - **Advantage Function:** The advantage for each candidate `A_i` was defined as `R(q, o_i) - mean({R(q, o_1)..., R(q, o_G)})`, notably *omitting* reward normalization by standard deviation as recommended by Liu et al. (2025) to prevent mode collapse.
 - **Objective Function:** A PPO-like (Schulman et al., 2017) clipped surrogate loss, operating at the token level, was used. It included asymmetric clipping values (`ϵ_low` = 0.2, `ϵ_high` = 0.28) for enhanced exploration, and a KL divergence penalty term (`βD_KL(π_θ || π_reference)`, with `β = 10^-4`) to prevent policy deviation.
 - **Training:** 3 epochs, learning rate of $3 \times 10^{-6}$, and a cosine learning rate scheduler.
 - **Key Distinction from Centaur-style SFT:** RL assigned rewards based on the numerical predicted values rather than their tokenized forms, and used these rewards to weight policy updates, which has been associated with improved generalization.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics and Parameters

- **Predictive Performance:** Mean Squared Error (MSE) was the primary metric, calculated on both training and test sets.
- **Inference Parameters:** All models were evaluated using `vLLM` (Kwon et al., 2023) with temperature = 0.7, top-p = 0.95, and top-k = 50.
- **Token Generation Limits:** RL models were allowed up to 1,024 tokens to accommodate CoT, while SFT and Centaur-style models were restricted to 30 tokens, consistent with their training objectives.

#### 3.5. CoT Analysis

- **Segmentation:** RL-generated CoTs were segmented into "thoughts" using regular expressions.
- **Embedding and Clustering:** Each thought was embedded using `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` from SBERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), then projected to 2D using t-SNE (Van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008), and clustered using k-means. Cluster centroids were summarized to provide interpretable reasoning patterns.
- **Cognitive Mechanism Identification:** GPT-4.1 was prompted (with temperature=0) to identify standard psychological effects or cognitive biases from the CoT outputs, acting as an expert judge.
- **Supplementary Analyses:** Included small-scale human evaluations, large-scale LLM-as-a-judge assessments, CoT swapping between backbone and RL models, and testing of SFT-generated CoTs.

#### 3.6. Control Experiments

- **Data Control:** RL was trained on synthetic choice rates generated by an expected-value maximization model, instead of human data, to observe CoT adaptability.
- **Model Control:** The experiment was replicated using a weaker backbone LLM, Gemma-2-2B-Instruct (Team et al., 2024), to assess the dependence of RL success on backbone capacity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of reinforcement learning in training LLMs to explain human decisions in the context of risky choice.

#### 4.1. Predictive Performance and Learning Dynamics

- **Improved Prediction:** All three post-training methods—Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Centaur-style SFT, and Reinforcement Learning (RL)—resulted in gradual improvements in model predictions, as indicated by decreasing Mean Squared Error (MSE) on both training and test sets.
- **Comparable Final Accuracy:** At their respective final checkpoints, the fine-tuned models achieved comparable predictive accuracy on the test set. SFT yielded an MSE of 0.0144 (SE = 0.0006), Centaur-style SFT an MSE of 0.0155 (SE = 0.0006), and RL an MSE of 0.0148 (SE = 0.0006). Statistical tests revealed no significant differences in MSE among these methods. These results were also found to be comparable to or better than some existing neural network models for risky choice.
- **RL Training Efficiency:** RL demonstrated a faster reduction in prediction error when measured by the number of training examples processed. However, this came with substantially higher computational costs due to the generation of multiple completions per training example.
- **Reward Stability:** The RL model's correctness reward and format reward curves showed stable improvements throughout training, with the format reward reaching its maximum early in the process. The completion length for RL models stabilized between 500 and 650 tokens after an initial increase.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Chains-of-Thought (CoT)

- **CoT Alteration:** RL training led to observable changes in both the CoT reasoning and the final predictions generated by the LLM for risky choice problems. The training appeared to amplify the LLM's references to psychological factors and cognitive biases within the CoT, while preserving core reasoning steps such as expected value calculation.
- **Reasoning Patterns:** The CoTs generated by the RL model were found to be itemized and segmented into distinct "thoughts." Clustering these thoughts in an embedding space identified nine common reasoning patterns, broadly categorized into five types:
 1. Accurate computation of expected values for both options.
 2. Coarse comparison based on these expected values.
 3. Consideration of psychological influences and behavioral biases.
 4. Incorporation of human risk preferences and outcome variability.
 5. Formulation of a final prediction based on the preceding factors, often conditional on the magnitude of the expected value difference.
- **Impact of CoT Quality:** Supplementary analyses confirmed that RL post-training improved the quality of CoT reasoning, which in turn contributed to enhanced prediction accuracy.
 - Human evaluations indicated that CoTs generated by the RL model were deemed more reasonable than those from the backbone model in 61.5% of trials.
 - LLM-as-a-judge assessments by GPT-4.1 showed modest increases in CoT quality ratings for the RL model, plateauing after the first epoch.
 - CoT swapping experiments demonstrated a causal link: using the RL model's CoT significantly reduced the backbone model's prediction error, while using the backbone's CoT increased the RL model's error.
 - SFT-generated and Centaur-generated CoTs, when used to prompt the backbone LLM, did not improve predictive performance, suggesting that RL is specifically effective in eliciting informative CoTs.

#### 4.3. Identified Cognitive Mechanisms

- **Dominant Mechanisms:** Using GPT-4.1 to summarize the CoT outputs, the study identified "expected value computation" and "risk aversion" as the two most frequently used cognitive mechanisms, each accounting for 29% to 36% of thoughts across risky-choice problems.
- **Other Mechanisms:** The RL model also frequently referenced "loss aversion," "certainty effect," "risk seeking," "probability weighting," "framing effects," and "ambiguity aversion."
- **Theoretical Implications:** The prominence of expected value and risk aversion in the LLM's explanations suggests that LLM-based cognitive models might emphasize rational principles and their deviations, potentially informing new theories of human behavior grounded in rational resource use.

#### 4.4. Control Experiment Results

- **Data Control (Synthetic Expected-Value Maximization Data):** When trained on synthetic data generated by an expected-value maximizer, the RL model successfully adapted its CoT reasoning to focus predominantly on computing and comparing expected values. It correctly identified mechanisms like "rational choice theory" and "all-or-nothing thinking" as important. This demonstrated the adaptability of the RL method to the structure of the training data.
- **Model Control (Weaker LLM - Gemma-2-2B-Instruct):**
 - RL applied to the weaker Gemma model performed significantly worse in predictive accuracy (higher MSE) compared to SFT and Centaur-style SFT on the same model, and worse than RL on the stronger Qwen model.
 - Analysis of the Gemma-RL model's CoT revealed a failure to consistently compute and compare expected values, indicating that the absence of these crucial cognitive mechanisms in its reasoning contributed to its weaker performance.
 - Conversely, SFT and Centaur-style SFT on Gemma still achieved predictive performance comparable to the stronger Qwen model, suggesting different generalization patterns between RL and SFT, and highlighting the dependence of RL's success on the backbone LLM's inherent capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method for developing interpretable cognitive models using Large Language Models (LLMs) by explicitly eliciting Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning through reinforcement learning (RL) post-training. The study, using human risky choice as a case study, demonstrates the feasibility and implications of this approach.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Cognitive Modeling

- **Dual-Purpose Models:** The work addresses a long-standing challenge in cognitive modeling: the trade-off between predictive accuracy and interpretability. By training LLMs to generate explicit reasoning alongside predictions, the research provides a pathway towards developing "dual-purpose" cognitive models that are both quantitatively strong and qualitatively explanatory. This can lead to a deeper theoretical understanding of human cognition beyond merely matching observed behavior.
- **Automated Theory Generation:** The RL-elicited CoTs represent verbal theories of human choices. This method offers a mechanism for automated discovery and verbalization of cognitive mechanisms, potentially accelerating the generation and testing of psychological theories. The identified cognitive mechanisms, such as the dominance of expected value and risk aversion, provide specific insights into how LLMs "explain" human behavior, potentially guiding future human-driven theoretical development.
- **Adaptability to Data Structure:** The control experiments demonstrated that the RL-trained LLMs can adapt their reasoning strategies to match the underlying structure of the training data. This adaptability suggests that the method is robust and generalizable across different behavioral phenomena, provided appropriate training data and a capable backbone LLM.

#### 5.2. Methodological Contributions

- **Reinforcement Learning for Explainability:** The successful application of RL with outcome-based rewards (RLVR) to elicit coherent and informative CoTs marks a methodological advancement. It distinguishes itself from approaches like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which would require extensive expert annotation, or Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF), which can lack transparency. RLVR directly links model predictions to verifiable outcomes, offering a potentially more objective way to shape explanatory outputs.
- **Beyond Predict-Then-Explain:** The approach simultaneously optimizes for both prediction and explanation, avoiding the potential brittleness or limitations of post-hoc explainability techniques applied to black-box models. This integrated optimization could lead to more inherently interpretable models.

#### 5.3. Implications for Psychological Theory

- **Emphasis on Rational Principles:** The findings, particularly the prominence of expected value and risk aversion in the LLM's explanations of human risky choice, suggest a potential shift in focus for cognitive theorists. While much research on risky choice emphasizes systematic deviations from rationality, the LLM highlights fundamental rational calculations and basic risk preferences as dominant explanatory factors. This could motivate the development of new theories grounded in rational principles, such as the rational use of cognitive resources.

#### 5.4. Limitations and Future Research Directions

- **Elicitation Hypothesis:** A primary limitation is the "elicitation hypothesis," which suggests that RL post-training primarily elicits pre-existing knowledge within the pretrained LLM rather than generating entirely novel theories. This implies that truly groundbreaking cognitive theories (e.g., a theory akin to prospect theory before its discovery) might not be discoverable by RL alone. Future work could explore how RL might facilitate cross-disciplinary knowledge integration to foster novel explanatory frameworks.
- **Backbone LLM Dependency:** The model control experiment highlighted that the effectiveness of the RL approach is strongly dependent on the capabilities of the backbone LLM. Weaker LLMs may not possess the latent knowledge or reasoning capacity necessary for generating high-quality CoT explanations.
- **Generalization Patterns and Combination Strategies:** The study suggests differing generalization patterns between RL and SFT. Further research is needed to systematically investigate these differences, identify conditions favoring each method, and explore strategies for effectively combining RL and SFT to build more robust and interpretable cognitive models.
- **Computational Cost:** The higher computational demands of RL training, compared to SFT, present a practical consideration for scaling this approach.

In conclusion, this work offers a promising direction for creating cognitive models that not only accurately forecast human behavior but also offer interpretable, natural language explanations, thereby contributing to both predictive accuracy and theoretical advancement in cognitive science.
