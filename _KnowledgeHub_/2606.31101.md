# Efficient Sim-to-Real Transfer of World-Action Models from Synthetic Priors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Purdue University and the Robotics and AI Institute achieved the first zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a world-action model for robotic manipulation, demonstrating an average 35% success rate on real-world tasks using only synthetic data. The policy, trained on 3,200 automatically generated demonstrations, outperformed baselines trained with up to 50 real-world demonstrations and generalized to novel objects.

## Problem

- Training robotic manipulation policies directly in the real world is costly and difficult to scale due to reliance on human teleoperation for data collection.
- A significant 'sim-to-real gap' exists, causing policies trained in simulation to often fail when transferred to physical robots due to visual and physical discrepancies.
- The capacity of world-action models, which predict future visual observations and robot actions, to bridge the sim-to-real gap for robotic manipulation had not been empirically established.

## Method

- A ==world-action model==, based on the ==Cosmos Policy== framework, was trained exclusively in a GPU-accelerated simulation environment.
- Extensive ==domain randomization== was applied across textures, camera poses, lighting, and object placements to increase the training distribution's diversity.
- Approximately 3,200 expert demonstrations were automatically generated using the ==AnyTask== framework and ==ViPR motion planning==, without any human teleoperation or real-world data.

## Results

- Achieved the first reported zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a world-action model, with an average **35%** success rate across **four** diverse manipulation tasks on a real Franka robot.
- The synthetic-data-trained model outperformed Diffusion Policy baselines trained with **10** real demonstrations (**5%** success) and **50** real demonstrations (**25%** success).
- Demonstrated successful out-of-distribution generalization by grasping and lifting a novel object (a bottle) that was not part of the synthetic training set.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World-action models, when trained with comprehensive domain randomization and pretrained video priors, can achieve effective zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for robotic manipulation.
> - The joint prediction of both future visual states and robot actions in a unified generative model enables learning of robust visuomotor primitives from synthetic data.
> - The ability to generalize to novel objects suggests that the model acquires fundamental manipulation skills rather than memorizing task-specific training examples.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic manipulation policies often relies on extensive data collection, which is a resource-intensive process in real-world settings. Simulation offers a scalable alternative by enabling parallelized data generation; however, a significant challenge known as the "sim-to-real gap" frequently impedes the direct transfer of policies trained in simulation to real robots. This gap arises from inherent visual and physical discrepancies between simulated and real environments, leading to policy failures upon deployment. Bridging this gap is a central problem in deploying learned manipulation policies.

This work specifically addresses the application of "world-action models" within the sim-to-real paradigm. World-action models are a class of unified generative models designed to predict future visual observations in conjunction with robot actions. Prior research has demonstrated the potential of such models in controlled settings, such as "sim-to-sim" (training and testing within simulation) or "real-to-real" (training and testing on real robots). However, the capability of world-action models to transfer from simulation to real-world robotic manipulation has not been previously established.

This paper positions itself as an exploration into whether the benefits of sim-to-real learning—namely, the use of inexpensive, scalable, and automatically generated synthetic data—can be extended to this specific class of policy models. If successful, it would signify a new avenue for developing robust robot policies without the prohibitive costs associated with real-world data acquisition and human teleoperation. The work aims to empirically answer whether a world-action model, trained exclusively on synthetic data, can achieve zero-shot deployment on a physical robot for manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the high cost and impracticality of training manipulation policies directly in the real world. Real-world data collection typically involves human teleoperation, which is expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale. While simulation environments offer a more scalable and cost-effective alternative for generating training data, the visual and physical inconsistencies between simulation and reality—the sim-to-real gap—frequently result in policies trained in simulation failing when deployed on real robots. The overarching objective of sim-to-real transfer methods is to reduce or eliminate the reliance on costly real-world data.

This paper specifically focuses on world-action models, which are generative models capable of predicting both future visual states and robot actions. While these models have shown promise in other contexts (e.g., sim-to-sim or real-to-real transfer), their capacity for bridging the sim-to-real gap for robotic manipulation had not been empirically demonstrated. This knowledge gap represents a significant limitation, as a positive result would enable world-action models to leverage the advantages of sim-to-real learning, such as the use of readily available, automatically generated synthetic data.

The central research question posed by the authors is whether a world-action model, trained exclusively on synthetic demonstrations, can effectively perform zero-shot real-world manipulation. The research does not aim to provide an ablation study to prove the necessity of joint video-action prediction for sim-to-real transfer. Instead, it seeks to establish a foundational empirical result: demonstrating the feasibility of such a transfer. By achieving this, the work aims to validate that world-action models can inherit the core benefits of sim-to-real learning, thereby opening a new data-efficient direction for transferring generative robot policies from synthetic priors to the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involved training a world-action model exclusively in simulation using a combination of a video diffusion model, extensive domain randomization, and automated demonstration generation, followed by zero-shot deployment on a real robot.

**Policy Architecture:** The core of the policy is built upon Cosmos Policy, a framework that adapts a pretrained video diffusion model (Cosmos-Predict2) for visuomotor control. This adaptation is achieved through a single-stage post-training process, where robot actions, future observations, and value estimates are encoded as latent frames within a unified diffusion process. This unified representation allows the model to predict both visual outcomes and corresponding actions.

**Simulation Environment and Domain Randomization:** Training environments were constructed using GPU-accelerated simulation, likely leveraging platforms such as Isaac Gym, which facilitates high-performance physics simulations. To mitigate the sim-to-real gap, comprehensive domain randomization was applied across multiple parameters:
- **Textures:** Object surfaces, table materials, and backgrounds were varied using both procedural and photographic libraries. This aims to expose the policy to a wide range of visual appearances.
- **Camera Poses:** Both wrist and third-person camera positions and orientations were perturbed with varying translations and rotations.
- **Lighting:** The number, position, intensity, and color temperature of scene lights were randomized.
- **Object Placements:** Objects were uniformly sampled for placement within the robot's reachable workspace.
The purpose of these extensive variations was to ensure that the randomized training distribution encompassed a broad spectrum of visual and physical conditions, increasing the likelihood that the real-world test scene would fall within this learned distribution, thereby reducing the need for manual matching of simulation to reality.

**Demonstration Generation:** Expert demonstrations, referred to as synthetic priors, were generated automatically without human teleoperation. This was achieved using the AnyTask framework, which employs foundation-model-guided motion planning (ViPR). Approximately 800 synthetic demonstrations were generated for each of four manipulation tasks: lifting a banana, lifting a brick, opening a drawer, and putting a strawberry into a bowl, totaling around 3,200 demonstrations. Each demonstration consisted of paired RGB observations and corresponding end-effector action trajectories.

**World-Action Model Training:** The Cosmos Policy model was trained for 32 epochs using a computational infrastructure comprising 40 H100 GPUs, a process that took approximately 72 hours. During training, the model learned to predict future visual frames and actions based on the synthetic demonstrations. A qualitative forward-prediction check was performed on real robot executions, comparing the model-predicted frames with synchronized live camera observations during approach, grasp, and lift stages. This check aimed to assess whether the model had learned dynamics relevant to the real scene, despite being trained solely on simulated data.

**Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer and Evaluation:** The trained policy was deployed directly on a real Franka Research 3 robot without any real-world demonstration collection or fine-tuning on real data. The only real-world setup involved standard fixed RGB views from wrist and third-person cameras streaming to the policy at inference time. The policy's performance was evaluated by conducting 10 trials per task, and success rates were recorded. For contextual comparison, rather than direct architectural ablation, Diffusion Policy (DP) baselines were provided. These baselines were trained with 10 or 50 real demonstrations per task, serving as a reference point for the performance achievable with varying amounts of real-world data, which sim-to-real methods aim to circumvent.

**Out-of-Distribution Generalization:** To assess the model's ability to generalize beyond its training set, an additional evaluation was performed by deploying the policy to lift a novel object (a bottle) that was not part of the initial training tasks. This test aimed to determine if the model had acquired generalizable visuomotor primitives rather than merely memorizing task-specific objects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capabilities of world-action models.

**Primary Transfer Success:** The central finding is the demonstration of the first reported zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a world-action model for robotic manipulation. The policy, trained exclusively on synthetic data, achieved an average success rate of 35% when deployed directly on a real Franka Research 3 robot across four distinct manipulation tasks. This success was achieved using approximately 800 automatically generated synthetic demonstrations per task and zero real-world demonstrations.

**Task-Specific Performance:**
- Lift banana: 5/10 success rate
- Lift brick: 5/10 success rate
- Open drawer: 2/10 success rate
- Put strawberry into bowl: 2/10 success rate
The average success rate across these four tasks was 35%.

**Comparison with Real-Data Baselines:** While not serving as a controlled architectural ablation, Diffusion Policy (DP) baselines provided a contextual reference for performance when real-world data is used.
- DP trained with 10 real demonstrations per task achieved an average success rate of 5% (0/10 for banana, 0/10 for brick, 2/10 for drawer, 0/10 for strawberry).
- DP trained with 50 real demonstrations per task achieved an average success rate of 25% (4/10 for banana, 3/10 for brick, 3/10 for drawer, 0/10 for strawberry).
The world-action model, trained entirely on synthetic data, achieved a higher average success rate (35%) than both DP baselines utilizing real demonstrations in the described setup. This result suggests that the synthetic-data-trained world-action model can outperform policies trained with a limited number of real demonstrations.

**Qualitative Alignment of Predictions:** Qualitative analysis (Figure 2) indicated that the model's predicted future frames exhibited visual consistency with live camera observations during real robot execution. The predicted frames maintained alignment regarding object location, robot pose, and the overall task stage (e.g., approach, grasp, lift). This observation suggests that the model, despite being trained solely in simulation and without manual reproduction of the real test scene, developed an understanding of real-scene dynamics relevant for task execution. The authors attribute this transfer ability to a combination of pretrained video priors, extensive domain randomization, and the joint action-video prediction objective.

**Out-of-Distribution Generalization:** The policy demonstrated an ability to generalize to novel objects unseen during training. When tasked with lifting a bottle, an object not included in the synthetic training set, the policy successfully approached, grasped, and lifted it (Figure 3). This suggests that the model acquired generalizable visuomotor primitives rather than merely memorizing the specific objects present in its training data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a step in the field of robotic manipulation by demonstrating the first successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a world-action model. This achievement indicates that world-action models, which generate both future visual states and robot actions, can effectively bridge the simulation-to-real gap without requiring any real-world training data or fine-tuning.

The primary significance lies in showing that world-action models can inherit the core benefit of sim-to-real learning: reducing dependence on expensive and time-consuming real-world data collection. By achieving a non-zero, and in this case, a comparatively higher average success rate than baselines trained with limited real demonstrations, the research provides empirical evidence that policies capable of understanding and predicting both visual outcomes and actions can be effectively trained using only automatically generated synthetic priors.

This capability has several potential impacts:
- **Cost Reduction:** It offers a pathway to significantly reduce the financial and logistical burden associated with deploying learned manipulation policies, as it bypasses the need for costly real robot demonstrations and human teleoperation.
- **Scalability:** The reliance on automatically generated synthetic data makes the data collection process highly scalable, allowing for the generation of vast datasets without human intervention. This can accelerate the development and iteration cycle for robotic manipulation policies.
- **Enhanced Policy Robustness:** The use of extensive domain randomization in simulation, combined with pretrained video priors and joint action-video prediction, contributes to the model's ability to generalize from diverse simulated environments to the potentially varied conditions of the real world. The demonstrated out-of-distribution generalization to novel objects further supports the idea that the model learns fundamental visuomotor skills rather than task-specific memorization.
- **New Research Direction:** This work establishes a data-efficient direction for transferring generative robot policies. It opens avenues for future research into further enhancing the robustness and capabilities of world-action models in sim-to-real settings, potentially through controlled ablation studies to isolate the contributions of different methodological components (e.g., video priors, domain randomization, joint prediction objective).

In summary, this research validates the potential of world-action models as a viable and data-efficient approach for developing and deploying robot manipulation policies, moving towards a future where complex robotic behaviors can be learned predominantly from synthetic data.
