# Sampling Strategies for Robust Universal Quadrupedal Locomotion Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Oxford developed and evaluated adaptive sampling strategies for morphology and joint PD gains during reinforcement learning, enabling a single policy to achieve robust, zero-shot locomotion on diverse simulated quadrupeds and a physical ANYmal robot. This approach demonstrated comparable or improved velocity command tracking on hardware (e.g., linear RMSEs 0.09-0.11 m/s) and allowed deployment without needing unsafe PD gains, unlike several baseline methods.

## Problem

- Most existing quadruped control designs are tailored to specific robots, demanding extensive retraining and tuning for each new morphology, which hinders scalability.
- Significant morphological and dynamical variations among quadrupeds, such as mass distribution and actuator properties, complicate the design of a single effective control policy.
- A suitable robot parameter generation approach, especially for crucial joint Proportional-Derivative (PD) gains, is needed to ensure efficient and stable training across a wide range of configurations for universal controllers.

## Method

- A universal locomotion controller was developed using an ==asymmetric actor-critic== reinforcement learning architecture, incorporating ==robot-specific morphology parameters== into an estimator network.
- Multiple strategies for generating diverse robot configurations and randomizing morphology parameters were investigated, including uniform randomization, a ==performance-based curriculum==, and a novel ==adaptive curriculum using particle filtering==.
- Different methods for sampling ==joint PD gains== were evaluated, ranging from mass-dependent mappings and uniform randomization to interpolation with nominal sets and a performance-based adaptive particle filtering approach.

## Results

- The proposed ==Particle Filter adaptive sampling== and Uniform SR=1 approaches successfully enabled zero-shot deployment on the ANYmal hardware robot, achieving stable locomotion and comparable velocity command tracking (e.g., linear RMSEs **0.09-0.11 m/s**) across various PD gain settings.
- In contrast to the proposed methods, several state-of-the-art baseline controllers either failed to achieve stable walking on hardware or required dangerously high PD gains, preventing their safe deployment.
- Sim-to-sim robustness tests showed that performance-adaptive sampling strategies generally resulted in more robust locomotion policies (higher success rates), particularly for larger quadrupeds, providing better coverage and stability across wider parameter ranges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adaptive sampling strategies, particularly the proposed particle filtering method, generally yield more robust universal locomotion policies in simulation and on hardware compared to uniform or simple curriculum-based approaches.
> - Careful randomization and adaptive tuning of joint PD gains are critical for enabling zero-shot deployment of learned policies on diverse physical quadrupeds and effectively bridging the simulation-to-real gap.
> - Training with broader sampling ranges for PD gains is essential for developing locomotion policies that can generalize across varied hardware platforms without requiring platform-specific fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadrupedal robotics has advanced significantly, leading to diverse applications such as industrial inspection, surveillance, research, and search and rescue. Much of this progress has been facilitated by developments in numerical optimization, reinforcement learning (RL), and hybrid control approaches. However, a prevailing limitation in current quadrupedal robot control is the platform-specific nature of most designs. Controllers are typically optimized for a single robot morphology and require substantial retraining and parameter tuning when applied to different platforms or when robot dynamics change. This individualized approach presents a scalability challenge, demanding significant computational resources, labor-intensive manual tuning, and specialized hardware expertise for each new quadruped robot. As the variety of commercial quadruped robots expands, from lightweight models like the A1 (12 kg) to heavier platforms such as the ANYmal (50 kg), the need for more generalized control solutions becomes increasingly evident.

Addressing this challenge, a growing area of research focuses on universal locomotion controllers capable of generalizing across varied robot morphologies, actuation systems, and sensing modalities. Prior work in this domain has explored several strategies. One approach integrates morphology directly into the policy architecture, often using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) or transformers, which have shown promising simulation transfer capabilities but limited real-world validation, especially on heavier robots. Another avenue involves Meta-RL techniques, such as Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML), which enable rapid adaptation to new morphologies, though these typically aim for few-shot adaptation rather than zero-shot deployment.

A critical element in developing universal locomotion policies is the strategy employed to sample diverse morphologies during training. Existing hardware-deployed controllers exhibit variations in their sampling methods: GenLoco uses imitation learning with uniform randomization of morphology parameters, mapping total robot mass to joint PD gains linearly. ManyQuadrupeds adopts a similar morphology diversity approach but relies on Central Pattern Generators and robot-specific inverse kinematics without domain randomization. MorAL utilizes a morphology encoder and extensive domain randomization. URMA employs a transformer-based policy with domain randomization around nominal robot-specific parameters, which limits robustness due to narrow sampling ranges. PAL extended MorAL by uniformly sampling robot parameters, including PD gains, from wide predefined ranges, demonstrating zero-shot deployment on heavier platforms.

This paper builds upon these efforts by specifically investigating improved sampling strategies for robot configuration variations and joint proportional-derivative (PD) gains. It aims to develop a single reinforcement learning policy that can generalize robustly across multiple parameter configurations and enable zero-shot hardware deployment, addressing the identified gaps in existing universal locomotion control research, particularly concerning robustness and scalability to diverse and heavier quadrupeds.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop robust universal locomotion policies for quadrupedal robots through the investigation of configuration sampling strategies. The motivation stems from three central challenges identified in achieving such universal locomotion:

1. **Morphological and Dynamic Variability**: Quadrupedal robots exhibit substantial variations in mass distribution, joint configurations, actuator properties, and kinematic structures. This inherent diversity hinders the design of control policies that can effectively generalize across different platforms without specific tuning.
2. **Impact on Locomotion Dynamics**: These morphological differences directly influence the robot's locomotion dynamics, necessitating distinct gait patterns and control strategies. This complexity complicates controller design, the transfer of policies from simulation to hardware, and the consistent evaluation of performance across various robots.
3. **Efficient Parameter Generation for Training**: A suitable approach for generating diverse robot parameters is required to support efficient and stable training across a wide spectrum of configurations. A specific difficulty lies in determining optimal proportional-derivative (PD) gain schedules for previously unencountered robots, as such schedules are often unknown.

To address these challenges, the authors aim to:
- Investigate and compare different approaches to sampling robot configurations, including kinematic and dynamic properties.
- Evaluate and improve strategies for joint PD gain tuning to enhance locomotion performance and robustness.
- Develop a single reinforcement learning policy that can generalize effectively across multiple parameter configurations.
- Achieve successful zero-shot transfer of the trained policy to a range of diverse quadrupeds in simulation and, critically, to physical hardware, specifically the ANYmal robot, using models not encountered during the training phase.

The overarching motivation is to overcome the scalability issues associated with platform-specific control designs, thereby reducing the computational resources, manual tuning effort, and hardware expertise required to deploy locomotion controllers on new or modified quadrupedal systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on an asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning framework, augmented with novel robot configuration and PD gain sampling strategies. All training is conducted within the RaiSim simulation environment.

**A. Universal Locomotion Controller Architecture**
The control framework is based on an asymmetric actor-critic architecture, similar to MorAL. It consists of three main modules:

1. **Estimator**: This network is responsible for estimating the base linear velocity ($v^*_{x,y,z}$) and robot-specific morphology parameters ($o^*_m$). Morphology parameters include base body center of mass, individual link masses (base, hip, thigh, shank), joint link offsets, and foot offset. The input to the estimator is a history buffer ($o^H_t$) containing past robot states, including base orientation and angular velocity, joint position offset and velocity, previous joint action, and desired velocity commands.
2. **Actor**: The actor generates desired joint positions ($q_{des}$) as its action. Its input ($o^a_t$) comprises the estimated base linear velocity and morphology parameters from the estimator, the current robot state, and nominal joint positions. Actions are sampled from a Gaussian distribution, added to nominal joint configurations, and then passed to a joint PD controller.
3. **Critic**: The critic evaluates the state-action value. It receives privileged information that is not available to the actor during hardware deployment. This includes actual base linear velocity, actual morphology parameters, foot contact states, feet contact friction, feet height, and joint PD gains. Compared to prior work, this architecture incorporates the base robot body's center of mass and omits height map information, aiming to reduce training time and complexity.

**B. Reward Structure**
The reward function, adapted from PAL, incentivizes the robot to follow desired velocity commands (linear and angular) while maintaining stable posture and smooth movements. It comprises various terms for base linear and angular velocity, base orientation, base height, undesired base motion, joint position, velocity, acceleration, torque, and action smoothness. A penalty is applied for early termination due due to collisions.

**C. Joint Controller**
Each joint utilizes a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller to convert the actor's desired joint positions ($q_{des}$) into commanded torques ($\tau_{cmd}$). The formula is $\tau_{cmd_j} = K_p(q_{des_j} - q_j) - K_d \dot{q}_j$, where $K_p$ and $K_d$ are the proportional and derivative gains, respectively. During training, the system randomizes between this custom PD controller and RaiSim's built-in PD controller, with torques clipped to actuator limits.

**D. Robot Configuration Generation and Randomization**
The paper investigates different strategies for generating diverse robot configurations and PD gains.

1. **Quadruped Reference Models**: Four simplified reference models (Unitree A1, Aliengo, ANYmal B, ANYmal C) are used. Their nominal parameters serve as a basis for resampling to generate 40 robot configurations per model, randomizing kinematic and dynamic properties. A generic quadruped is modeled with rigid bodies, masses, joint connections, and foot properties. Leg geometries are sampled as either type A (knees backward) or type X (knees toward base).
2. **Robot Model Parameter Generation**: Morphology parameters, including base center of mass, masses, joint link offsets, and foot offsets, are uniformly sampled within specified ranges, extending prior methods. Configurations are validated to ensure they are collision-free in a nominal pose.
3. **Sampling Strategies for Robot Configurations**: The paper compares two main approaches:
 - **Uniform Randomization (Baseline)**: A fixed number of configurations ($n_u$) are sampled from fixed uniform distributions. The sampling range (SR) is typically fixed (e.g., SR=1), representing state-of-the-art methods like PAL.
 - **Adaptive Curriculum Sampling (Proposed)**: This method combines two strategies applied after every $N_{traj}$ rollouts:
 - **Performance-based Scaling of Sampling Ranges (SR)**: A portion of configurations ($n_{SR}$) are generated by uniformly sampling around nominal parameters, but the SR (clamped between 0.1 and 1.0) is adapted online. SR is initialized at 0.1 and updated based on aggregate linear and zero-velocity command tracking performance (Tr$_{lin_{mean}}$, Tr$_{zero_{mean}}$). If performance is good, SR increases; if poor, SR decreases.
 - **Particle Filtering Weighted Resampling**: A Sequential Importance Resampling (SIR) particle filter updates another portion of configurations ($n_w$). Importance weights are assigned based on configurations yielding mid-to-upper-range performance (Tr$_{lin_i}$, Tr$_{zero_i}$ between 0.4 and 0.9), ensuring focus on challenging but solvable configurations. These configurations are then updated via a random nearest neighbor walk.
 - Additionally, $n_r$ configurations are sampled from a replay buffer of previously sampled configurations to prevent degradation of the parameter set.
 - Joint nominal position and torque limits are uniformly sampled with SR=1 in all cases.

4. **Joint PD Gains Randomization**: Four strategies for sampling PD gains ($c_{PD_i}$) are compared:
 - **Mass-dependent mapping**: Gains are parameterized as a linear or polynomial function of the robot's total mass, with optional uniform noise, inspired by GenLoco and MorAL.
 - **Uniform randomization**: All PD gains are uniformly sampled within SR=1, following PAL.
 - **Interpolation with nominal sets**: Gains are interpolated between predefined nominal values for different robots and subjected to limited uniform noise (small SR), inspired by URMA.
 - **Performance-based adaptive particle filtering**: The proposed adaptive curriculum strategy (SR scaling and particle filter) is applied to the joint PD gain parameters.

**E. Training Setup - RL Algorithm**
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to train the locomotion and estimator policies. Both are parameterized as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with leaky-relu activation. A penalty for early termination due to collisions is applied. Hyperparameters are detailed in the paper, and training occurs in RaiSim using 8 CPU cores and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a comparative analysis of various sampling strategies for universal quadrupedal locomotion, focusing on sim-to-sim robustness and sim-to-real transfer.

**A. Performance Benchmark**
The study compares the proposed "Particle Filter, adaptive sampling range" method with several state-of-the-art reference-free locomotion controllers, including PAL, GenLoco, ManyQuadrupeds, MorAL, and URMA. A key finding was that only the proposed adaptive particle filter approach and PAL achieved stable locomotion on hardware. GenLoco, ManyQuadrupeds, MorAL, and URMA (in their original, non-adaptive full sampling range formulation) either showed reduced robustness in simulation or required PD gains that were deemed unsafe for hardware deployment. Specifically, mass-dependent gain mappings in GenLoco and ManyQuadrupeds led to derivative gains exceeding hardware-safe limits, causing aggressive controller dynamics and mechanical impacts in preliminary trials, thus excluding them from further hardware evaluation.

**B. Sim-to-Sim Robustness**
Robustness was evaluated using a success rate (SR*) metric, defined as 1 minus the ratio of early terminations due to collisions to the total number of rollouts. Policies were tested on three quadrupeds (A1, Laikago - an unseen morphology, and ANYmal) under various perturbations: horizontal force, ground friction, and base mass changes.

- **Adaptive Sampling Advantage**: Across almost all evaluated quadrupeds, adaptive sampling of the configuration space improved robustness compared to fixed uniform sampling (SR=1), with the exception of MorAL on the A1.
- **Scalability Limitations**: GenLoco performed well on the A1 but failed to transfer effectively to the larger ANYmal platform. This suggests that gain mappings derived from imitation learning or CPGs may not reliably scale across significantly different robot morphologies.
- **Out-of-Distribution Generalization**: Zero-shot transfer to out-of-distribution morphologies, exemplified by the Laikago results, remained challenging. None of the approaches achieved consistently high SR* across all tested perturbations, indicating a degradation in performance with substantial morphological shifts outside the training distribution.
- **Comparison with Baselines**: URMA demonstrated strong robustness among the state-of-the-art approaches, supporting its curriculum-based design. However, PAL with SR=1 and the proposed adaptive particle filter consistently achieved equal or slightly higher robustness across both A1 and ANYmal.
- **PD Gain Influence**: Visualizations of SR* over a range of PD gain combinations (Figure 4) highlighted that the particle-filter-based adaptive strategy yielded the largest contiguous regions of high SR* (yellow areas). This indicates stable locomotion across a wider range of PD gain combinations, particularly for the ANYmal platform. In contrast, GenLoco showed an almost absent region of stable performance for ANYmal within hardware-safe PD gain ranges. PAL with SR=1 and the adaptive particle filter both produced large stable regions. The particle filter approach demonstrated the most extensive coverage of high-performance PD gains.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer**
Hardware experiments were conducted on the ANYmal quadruped, transferring the trained dynamics encoding and locomotion policy networks zero-shot.

- **Hardware Velocity Command Tracking**: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between commanded and estimated base velocities (X, Y, Yaw) was used to evaluate tracking performance (Table III).
 - The proposed Particle Filter sampling strategy achieved tracking performance comparable to the PAL baseline, with similar RMSE values. Both methods enabled reliable and stable hardware deployment (indicated by '✓').
 - Adaptive variants of URMA and MorAL exhibited degraded but executable behavior (indicated by '⋄'), differing from their original formulations with full sampling ranges (SR=1), which were deemed unsafe for hardware ('×').
 - All other configurations were unsafe for hardware deployment based on simulation evaluations.
 - Both Particle Filter and PAL also demonstrated successful locomotion with an additional 13 kg payload (25% increase over nominal mass).
- **Hardware Base Velocity Estimator**: The RMSE of the trained MLP estimator network's base velocity estimate (X, Y, Z) was compared against the ANYmal's onboard manufacturer state estimator (Table IV).
 - Both PAL and the Particle Filter approaches proved robust enough for zero-shot hardware deployment.
 - PAL's sampling strategy generally yielded slightly better velocity estimation, which the authors attribute to the estimator network's earlier and broader exposure to the full range of possible morphology parameters from the beginning of training.

Overall, the results indicate that larger PD gain sampling ranges during training, particularly those informed by adaptive strategies, significantly enable robust zero-shot universal locomotion and sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the advancement of universal locomotion control for quadrupedal robots by addressing critical challenges in scalability and robustness. The work establishes that traditional fixed uniform sampling and mass-dependent PD gain mappings can lead to limitations in robustness and even unsafe hardware deployment, especially when scaling policies across diverse morphologies and controller gains, or to larger platforms like the ANYmal.

The introduction of a performance-based adaptive curriculum for reshaping morphology and PD gain distributions during training represents a significant methodological contribution. By dynamically adjusting sampling ranges and employing particle-filter-based resampling, the policy is exposed to a more effective and challenging distribution of robot configurations. This adaptive approach has been demonstrated to expand stable operating regions under various disturbances and gain variations, as evidenced by extensive simulation experiments across A1, ANYmal, and an unseen Laikago morphology.

The successful zero-shot deployment onto the ANYmal hardware, including reliable locomotion under an additional 25% payload mass exceeding nominal manufacturer specifications, is a key practical impact. This indicates that the developed policies can be directly applied to physical robots without requiring extensive platform-specific tuning or additional training, thus reducing the time, cost, and expertise traditionally associated with deploying new controllers.

While the paper acknowledges that zero-shot transfer to strongly out-of-distribution morphologies remains an ongoing challenge, the substantial improvement in robustness within a broad and practically relevant morphology range offers significant potential. The findings pave the way for more generalizable and resilient quadrupedal robot control systems. Future work, integrating perceptive components for terrain-aware locomotion and validating the approach across an even wider array of robot types and scales, could further amplify this impact, enabling autonomous quadrupeds to operate reliably in highly varied and unstructured environments with minimal prior adaptation. This research ultimately moves the field closer to the vision of truly universal robot controllers.
