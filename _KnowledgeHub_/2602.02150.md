# ECHO: Entropy-Confidence Hybrid Optimization for Test-Time Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> ECHO introduces an Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Optimization framework to enhance Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) by mitigating rollout collapse and self-reinforcing overfitting in tree-search methods. The framework consistently improves reasoning performance across various mathematical and multimodal benchmarks, achieving up to 12.36% relative gains on text-based tasks and 12.8% on multimodal tasks.

## Problem

- High-entropy branching in existing tree-search TTRL methods can trigger 'rollout collapse,' inefficiently consuming the branching budget and limiting exploration coverage.
- Early pseudo-label bias in TTRL training leads to 'self-reinforcing overfitting,' causing premature convergence to locally optimal but potentially unreliable solutions.
- Traditional TTRL approaches often require a large number of samples for stable pseudo-label generation, incurring significant computational costs.

## Method

- ECHO employs an ==entropy–confidence guided tree-structured rollout mechanism== that adaptively controls branch width and prunes unpromising branches based on local entropy and group-level confidence.
- The policy update strategy incorporates ==confidence-adaptive clipping== for the importance ratio, which adjusts the trust region based on trajectory reliability.
- An ==entropy–confidence hybrid advantage shaping mechanism== is introduced to reallocate learning weight towards effective but low-confidence (uncertain yet informative) tokens, promoting robust exploration.

## Results

- ECHO achieved consistent performance gains, improving average accuracy by **0.63%** to **12.36%** on natural-language mathematical reasoning tasks and by approximately **2.6%** to **4.1%** on multimodal reasoning tasks, relative to baselines.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that the Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Tree Search (EC-Tree) component contributed most significantly to performance improvements by enhancing trajectory quality during search.
- Quantitative analysis confirmed that ECHO successfully mitigated rollout collapse by increasing rollout diversity and reduced self-reinforcing overfitting by slowing the rapid decay of policy entropy in early training stages.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly considering local entropy and group-level confidence during branching decisions effectively allocates computational budget, preventing rollout collapse and maintaining exploration diversity.
> - Adapting the clipping radius for policy updates based on trajectory confidence can stabilize learning, preventing aggressive updates driven by noisy or biased early-stage pseudo-labels.
> - Shaping the advantage function with both entropy and inverse confidence biases policy updates towards ambiguous yet informative decisions, mitigating premature convergence and fostering robust learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has increasingly focused on improving reasoning capabilities, often through preference alignment using high-quality human-labeled data. However, the acquisition of such data is frequently costly or impractical. This challenge has led to the emergence of Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL), a paradigm that enables models to enhance their reasoning by optimizing based on self-generated feedback signals during inference, thereby circumventing the need for external supervision.

Traditional TTRL approaches typically involve generating multiple candidate solutions for a given query, constructing pseudo-labels through self-consistency or majority voting, and then performing online policy updates. A significant limitation of these methods, particularly those employing chain-based rollouts, is their high sensitivity to the rollout budget. Achieving stable pseudo-labels often necessitates a large number of samples, leading to substantial parallel sampling costs and computational overhead.

To address the inefficiency of chain-based rollouts, recent advancements in TTRL have introduced tree-search-based rollouts. This approach allows models to share reasoning prefixes and branch at key decision points, aiming to achieve broader and more efficient sampling coverage under a fixed token budget. Such tree-structured rollouts are designed to generate more reliable learning signals at a reduced computational expense by improving exploration and diversity. This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by seeking to enhance the robustness and efficiency of tree-structured TTRL, specifically by mitigating identified challenges related to exploration strategies and the reliability of pseudo-labels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address two identified challenges within existing tree-search-based Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) methods, with the goal of improving reasoning capability, training robustness, and generalization, particularly under limited rollout budgets.

The first challenge concerns **high-entropy branching triggering rollout collapse**. Current tree-search TTRL methods, such as ETMR, often branch at nodes exhibiting high entropy, aiming to increase exploration diversity. However, under a majority-vote pseudo-labeling scheme, the allocated branching budget can become concentrated on a limited number of trajectories that exhibit consecutive high-entropy steps. This leads to a rapid reduction in the number of effectively explored branches, causing the search tree to degenerate into an almost chain-like rollout. Consequently, pseudo-labels become dominated by homogenized trajectories, which diminishes exploration coverage and degrades the quality of learning signals. The motivation here is to prevent this budget concentration and maintain broad, diverse exploration.

The second challenge relates to **early pseudo-label bias leading to self-reinforcing overfitting**. In the initial stages of TTRL training, the pseudo-rewards generated from self-feedback are inherently noisy and biased. This early estimation bias can prematurely guide the policy to converge to solutions that appear "high-scoring" within the current sampling distribution, even if they are not truly optimal or robust. This creates a detrimental feedback loop where the output distribution rapidly sharpens (entropy drops), exploration is suppressed, and the model's capacity for later-stage performance and generalization is constrained. The motivation for addressing this is to enhance training robustness by making updates less susceptible to early-stage noise and bias, thereby maintaining a healthy balance between exploration and exploitation throughout the learning process.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose a novel framework named Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Group Relative Policy Optimization (ECHO). The motivation is to achieve more stable and effective test-time self-improvement by jointly leveraging both uncertainty (entropy) and reliability (confidence) signals in a more sophisticated manner during both the rollout generation and policy update phases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Group Relative Policy Optimization (ECHO) framework integrates novel mechanisms into both the tree-structured rollout phase and the policy update phase to address the identified challenges. The framework is composed of three main parts: an entropy–confidence guided tree-structured rollout, confidence-adaptive clipping, and entropy–confidence hybrid advantage shaping.

#### 3.1. Tree-Structured Rollout with Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Optimization

This component aims to improve the quality and diversity of candidate trajectories while mitigating rollout collapse.

- **Entropy Increment and Window Smoothing:** To obtain more stable signals for branching decisions and pruning, ECHO applies temporal smoothing to both token-level confidence and entropy. Grouped confidence ($C^G_t$) and tail confidence ($C^{tail}_t$) are calculated as moving averages over recent steps, with window sizes $W_G$ and $W_T$ respectively. Historical mean entropy ($\bar{H}_{t-1}$) is computed over a window $W_H$, and the entropy increment ($\Delta H_t$) is used to track changes in uncertainty.
- **Branch Width: Entropy–Confidence Joint Scheduling:** When a branching point is triggered, the number of new branches ($B_t$) is determined by jointly considering the local token-distribution entropy ($H_t$) and the grouped confidence ($C^G_t$). A warm-up phase calibrates entropy bounds ($H_{low}$, $H_{high}$). The branch width is computed using a formula that increases branching for high entropy and low grouped confidence, and conversely, shrinks it for high confidence to suppress unproductive expansions. This mechanism is designed to adaptively allocate resources based on the perceived uncertainty and reliability of the current reasoning path.
- **Branch Selection, Expansion, and Online Pruning:** At a branching step, the top-$B_t$ tokens by log-probability are selected as branching candidates. Each child branch is expanded autoregressively. To prevent rollout collapse and efficiently use the budget, ECHO employs online pruning based on three criteria:
 - **Low-confidence pruning:** A branch is terminated if its running minimum grouped confidence ($m_t$) falls below a threshold ($\tau_{prune}$).
 - **Tail-decline pruning:** A branch is pruned if its tail confidence ($C^{tail}_{i,t}$) consistently decreases for a specified patience length ($S_{tail}$) and falls below a threshold ($\tau_{tail}$).
 - **Entropy-spike pruning:** Branches exhibiting a sequence of large entropy increments ($\Delta H_t > \delta_{upper}$) for a consecutive length ($S_\Delta$) are terminated, indicating persistently unstable uncertainty.
These pruning mechanisms reallocate the rollout budget towards more promising trajectories and avoid "high-entropy traps."
- **Majority Voting Reward Function:** Consistent with existing TTRL methods, ECHO uses majority voting to generate pseudo-labels. For a given prompt, $G$ candidate responses are sampled. The final answer with the highest vote count ($\hat{y}$) is chosen as the pseudo-label, and a binary reward ($R_i \in \{0, 1\}$) is assigned to each trajectory based on agreement with $\hat{y}$.

#### 3.2. Confidence-Adaptive Clipping

This mechanism addresses the issue of noisy pseudo-rewards leading to self-reinforcing overfitting during policy updates. ECHO modifies the trust region in policy optimization to be adaptive to trajectory confidence.
- The per-token importance ratio ($r_{i,t}(\theta)$) is clipped within an interval $[1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon]$.
- The clipping radius $\epsilon(o_i)$ is dynamically adjusted based on the trajectory-level tail confidence ($C^{tail}(o_i)$), which is the average tail confidence over the last $W_{tail}$ tokens.
- A sigmoid function scales the confidence into a range between $\epsilon_{min}$ and $\epsilon_{max}$. Higher trajectory confidence leads to a tighter trust region (smaller $\epsilon(o_i)$), preventing overly aggressive updates from potentially spurious high-reward signals. Conversely, lower confidence allows for a wider trust region, facilitating more exploratory updates and counteracting premature entropy collapse.

#### 3.3. Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Advantage Shaping

This component guides where within a trajectory the model should learn more aggressively, emphasizing locally uncertain yet informative decisions.
- A group-normalized trajectory-level advantage ($A^{grp}_{g,i}$) is computed for each trajectory, reflecting its relative reward within its rollout group.
- An entropy–confidence hybrid shaping signal ($S_{i,t}$) is introduced, which is a weighted sum of token entropy ($H_{i,t}$) and inverse token confidence ($1 - C_{i,t}$). This signal is designed to be high for tokens in uncertain and low-confidence regions.
- This shaping signal is incorporated into the token-level advantage: $A^{hyb}_{i,t} = A^{grp}_{g,i} (1 + aS_{i,t})$, where $a$ is a scaling factor. This design biases policy updates towards tokens involved in ambiguous reasoning steps, promoting robust exploration.

#### 3.4. Overall Objective

The complete ECHO objective combines the confidence-adaptive clipping and entropy–confidence hybrid advantages within a PPO-style policy gradient framework. It includes a clipped policy-gradient term and a KL regularization term to constrain policy drift for stability. The gradient derivation shows that the confidence-adaptive clipping bounds the impact of spurious high-reward samples, while the hybrid advantage term reallocates gradient mass towards informative yet low-certainty decisions, suppressing rollout collapse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of ECHO focused on demonstrating its consistent performance gains, robustness, and generalizability across various reasoning benchmarks, as well as quantitatively analyzing its impact on the identified challenges.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

ECHO consistently demonstrated advantages on both natural-language mathematical reasoning and multimodal mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
- **Natural-language reasoning:** Evaluated on AIME2024, AMC, MATH-500, GPQA-Diamond, and AIME2025 using pass@16 accuracy. ECHO consistently outperformed a range of test-time optimization and confidence baselines (TTRL, ETMR, EVOL-RL, INTUITOR) across different backbone models (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B) and training data setups. Average gains ranged from 0.63% to 12.36%, with up to 12.36% improvements on challenging tasks like AIME2025. This suggests that ECHO effectively filters low-quality trajectories and mitigates search degeneration.
- **Multimodal reasoning:** Evaluated on MathVision, MathVerse, MathVista, and LogicVista using pass@1 accuracy. ECHO showed consistent effectiveness on multimodal benchmarks (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-8B backbones), improving average pass@1 by approximately 2.6% to 4.1%, and achieving up to 12.8% relative gains on LogicVista. This indicates that ECHO's entropy-confidence scheduling is beneficial for cross-modal reasoning by enhancing trajectory quality.
- **Robustness under IID settings:** Even when training and testing on the same datasets (strict IID evaluation), ECHO delivered consistent improvements, increasing average accuracy by 4.01%–8.88% and up to 16.50% on high-difficulty tasks. This implies that the method's benefits are inherent to its search and update dynamics, not solely dependent on distribution shifts.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted to assess the contribution of each of ECHO's key components:
- **EC-Tree (Entropy–Confidence Tree-structured rollout):** Removing this component resulted in the largest overall performance degradation, indicating its crucial role in generating high-quality candidate trajectories.
- **CA-Clip (Confidence-Adaptive Clipping):** Removing CA-Clip caused a substantial performance drop on challenging benchmarks (e.g., AIME2025 accuracy dropped from 54.3 to 30.1), highlighting its importance in stabilizing policy updates by suppressing early bias.
- **E-SC Adv (Entropy–Confidence Hybrid Advantage Shaping):** Removing E-SC Adv led to consistent but milder performance degradations across benchmarks, confirming its contribution to promoting exploratory learning from uncertain samples.

#### 4.3. Quantitative Analysis of Challenges

The paper provided quantitative evidence that ECHO mitigates the two main challenges:
- **High-entropy collapse:** Visualizations and budget allocation statistics (Figure 2, Figure 5) demonstrated that purely entropy-driven tree search (e.g., ETMR) leads to budget concentration on a few trajectories and limited coverage. ECHO, by incorporating confidence constraints and online pruning, achieved a more balanced allocation of branches and prevented over-expansion into noisy high-entropy regions, resulting in greater rollout diversity.
- **Pseudo-label overfitting:** Training dynamics (Figure 4) indicated that entropy-driven methods often reduce entropy too rapidly in early stages, amplifying unreliable pseudo-labels and leading to premature convergence. ECHO, through confidence-adaptive clipping and entropy–confidence advantage shaping, slowed entropy decay and reduced the dominance of early noisy supervision in policy updates, thus mitigating self-reinforcing overfitting.

#### 4.4. Additional Analyses

- **Failure modes (Appendix B.1):** The study identified that inappropriate pruning hyperparameters (e.g., overly aggressive thresholds or short tail-window sizes) could prematurely terminate potentially correct trajectories, especially for problems requiring long derivations. Similarly, a lack of prior knowledge in the backbone model on the target task could lead to unreliable pseudo-labels and diminished gains or even negative transfer, particularly when the training data distribution significantly mismatched the target benchmarks.
- **Comparison with supervised GRPO (Appendix B.2):** ECHO, a label-free method, showed competitive performance against supervised GRPO (RLVR). On text-only benchmarks, ECHO was close to or even outperformed supervised GRPO on several tasks. For vision-based reasoning, a larger gap remained, suggesting greater challenges for label-free optimization in tasks requiring stronger visual grounding.
- **Performance under varying Pass@K (Appendix B.3):** Both text-only and vision-based reasoning showed that performance monotonically increased with higher K (more rollouts), with gains saturating at larger K. ECHO maintained strong performance across K values and effectively utilized increased sampling budgets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, with the introduction of ECHO, contributes to the field of Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) by addressing critical limitations of existing tree-structured rollout methods. Its significance lies in improving the robustness, efficiency, and generalization capabilities of models that learn from self-generated feedback.

One primary impact is the mitigation of **rollout collapse**. By proposing an entropy–confidence hybrid tree search, ECHO prevents the search process from becoming trapped in persistently uncertain, low-quality trajectories. The adaptive branch width and online confidence-based pruning mechanisms allow for a more efficient allocation of the rollout budget, leading to increased exploration diversity and higher-quality candidate trajectories. This directly enhances the effectiveness of self-supervision in TTRL, where the quality and diversity of sampled solutions are crucial for generating reliable pseudo-labels.

Another significant contribution is the alleviation of **self-reinforcing overfitting** caused by noisy early pseudo-labels. The introduction of confidence-adaptive clipping and entropy–confidence hybrid advantage shaping makes the policy update process more robust. Confidence-adaptive clipping prevents overly aggressive updates from potentially unreliable signals, while hybrid advantage shaping ensures that the model learns more effectively from informative, uncertain reasoning steps rather than prematurely converging to locally optimal but fragile solutions. This fosters a more balanced exploration-exploitation trade-off during training, promoting sustained learning and better generalization.

The experimental results demonstrate that ECHO achieves consistent performance gains across diverse mathematical and visual reasoning benchmarks, for various model scales and under different training data setups. This indicates the broad applicability and transferability of the proposed methods. The ability to enhance reasoning in a label-free manner, particularly for complex multimodal tasks, has practical implications for scenarios where human annotations are scarce or expensive. ECHO's performance approaching or even surpassing supervised methods in some cases suggests a path toward more autonomous and scalable self-improvement for large language models.

In summary, ECHO offers a method for making TTRL more reliable and efficient. By dynamically managing exploration and enhancing the robustness of learning from self-generated signals, it contributes to advancing the field of autonomous model improvement, potentially broadening the applicability of LLMs to more complex and data-constrained reasoning tasks.
