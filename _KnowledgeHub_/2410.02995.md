# Task-agnostic Lifelong Robot Learning with Retrieval-based Weighted Local Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> RWLA (Retrieval-based Weighted Local Adaptation) is a task-agnostic lifelong robot learning framework: a compact demonstration memory serves both experience replay during sequential training and a pre-deployment 'review' phase that retrieves scenario-similar demos by image plus language embedding distance and fine-tunes on them, up-weighting failure-divergent frames. Overall LIBERO success rises from 39.65% (ER) to 52.42%.

## Problem

- Prior lifelong robot learning methods depend on explicit task boundaries or task IDs, which are unavailable in open-ended, scalable real-world deployment where tasks blur together.
- Architecture-based methods like PackNet consume network capacity per task, so plasticity collapses on later tasks once the pruning budget is exhausted.
- Meta-learning adaptation assumes access to a full task distribution during meta-training, an assumption violated by sequential lifelong settings suffering catastrophic forgetting.
- Replay alone cannot prevent partial forgetting, since training emphasis on the current task inevitably erodes earlier skills.

## Method

- **RWLA**'s ==data retrieval== scores stored demonstrations by a weighted sum of L2 distances between workspace-image embeddings (R3M) and language embeddings (Sentence-Similarity model), needing no task ID.
- ==Selective weighting== builds an ==Embedding Distance Matrix== between retrieved demo frames and failed rollout frames, extracts a smoothed distance curve, and marks the divergent ==Separation Segment==.
- ==Weighted local adaptation== fine-tunes a copy of the frozen final policy for ~20 epochs on retrieved demos with per-frame weights (+0.3 on segments, clipped at 2, L1-normalized).
- Policy backbone concatenates 64-dim vision, language, and proprioception embeddings into a 4-layer ==temporal Transformer== with a 5-mode ==GMM action head==.

## Results

- ER-RWLA reaches **52.42%** overall average success versus **39.65%** for ER, **16.44%** for AGEM, and **1.10%** for EWC; AGEM-RWLA lifts AGEM to **46.85%**.
- PackNet, given task IDs, scores **56.52%** overall but collapses to **0.00%** on the last several of **20** tasks in libero_different_scenes, where ER-RWLA wins **45.17%** vs **32.92%**.
- Selective weighting beats uniform adaptation (RULA) on all three benchmarks: **60.61** vs **58.83** on object, **64.22** vs **63.17** on goal, **37.94** vs **37.22** on spatial.
- Swapping Sentence-Similarity for BERT degrades task separation and retrieval accuracy, yet adaptation still helps at retrieval accuracy around **0.375**, and ER-RWLA also beats LoRA-FT given full target-task data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Retrieval by embedding similarity is a workable substitute for task identity, so forgetting can be countered without task labels or boundaries.
> - A short pre-deployment 'review' on a handful of stored demos recovers forgotten skills cheaply, echoing human retrieval practice and re-learning savings.
> - Importance weighting yields consistent but modest gains on converged expressive models; expect low-cost reliability improvements, not step changes in success rate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of lifelong learning aims to enable artificial intelligence systems to continually acquire, refine, and transfer knowledge across a sequence of tasks without forgetting previously learned information. Significant progress has been observed in domains such as computer vision and natural language processing. However, extending lifelong learning to robotics presents distinct challenges due to the sequential decision-making nature, the high cost and complexity of physical interactions, and inherent data scarcity. These factors make it crucial to develop strategies that sustain a robot's long-term performance and adaptability.

A primary challenge in lifelong learning is "catastrophic forgetting," where the acquisition of new skills degrades the performance on previously learned tasks. Existing approaches to mitigate this include regularization-based methods (e.g., Elastic Weight Consolidation, EWC, or Synaptic Intelligence), architecture-based methods (e.g., PackNet that prunes network weights), and memory-based methods (e.g., Experience Replay, ER, or Average Gradient Episodic Memory, AGEM) that store and replay past data.

A further complication, particularly relevant for real-world robotic applications, is the requirement for "task-agnostic" operation. Many existing lifelong learning algorithms rely on explicit task boundaries or identifiers (IDs) to manage knowledge retention or select relevant parameters. However, in open-ended, scalable real-world scenarios, such clear task distinctions are often unavailable, requiring robots to learn continuously without explicit segmentation of tasks. This work specifically addresses this gap by proposing a method that operates without relying on task boundaries or IDs.

The paper draws inspiration from human cognitive processes, where forgotten knowledge can be rapidly recovered through targeted review. This concept positions the work within a growing area of research exploring efficient knowledge retrieval and adaptation mechanisms in lifelong learning, distinct from approaches that attempt to retain every detail throughout continuous training or rely on extensive meta-learning pre-training that might not align with sequential, real-world data acquisition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The fundamental objective of this research is to advance the development of lifelong learning robots capable of manipulating objects and interacting with environments in novel and unseen scenarios over extended periods.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges inherent in current lifelong robot learning paradigms:

1. **Catastrophic Forgetting:** As robots continually learn new manipulation tasks and skills from demonstrations, the sequential nature of learning often leads to shifts in data distribution. This can cause the robot to forget previously acquired skills, diminishing its overall competence. The paper aims to mitigate this problem, allowing for more stable and robust skill retention.

2. **Scalability in Real-World Settings (Task-Agnosticism):** Conventional lifelong learning methods often depend on the availability of distinct task boundaries or identifiers. In practical, open-ended real-world scenarios, providing such explicit labels for every new task is often infeasible and unscalable. The researchers are motivated to develop a framework that enables a robot to learn effectively and continuously without external cues regarding task distinctions. This means the robot should implicitly handle multiple encountered tasks and integrate new knowledge without relying on predefined task IDs.

3. **Inefficient Knowledge Recovery:** While experience replay helps retain learned skills, partial forgetting remains inevitable due to the multi-tasking nature of lifelong learning and the focus on acquiring new skills. The researchers are motivated by human learning observations, where forgotten knowledge can be efficiently and rapidly recovered through targeted review, often faster than initial learning. They aim to translate this human cognitive mechanism into a robotic learning paradigm, enabling robots to actively "review" and recover forgotten manipulation skills on demand.

4. **Optimizing Limited Resources:** In practical robot learning, data collection is expensive and time-consuming. Therefore, efficiently utilizing limited stored demonstrations is crucial. The paper seeks to introduce mechanisms, such as selective weighting, to maximize the utility of stored experiences by focusing adaptation on the most relevant or "forgotten" segments of a skill, without requiring human intervention for identification of these segments.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Develop a task-agnostic framework for lifelong robot learning that addresses catastrophic forgetting.
- Implement a retrieval-based local adaptation strategy that does not require task boundaries or IDs.
- Incorporate a selective weighting mechanism to focus adaptation on critical, failure-prone action segments.
- Provide a generalizable, plug-and-play solution that can enhance existing memory-based lifelong learning algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Retrieval-based Weighted Local Adaptation (RWLA), is structured into three phases: Lifelong Learning, Reviewing, and Testing. The core idea is to equip the robot with a memory of past experiences and a mechanism to retrieve and adapt to relevant knowledge before policy deployment, particularly in task-agnostic settings.

**Policy Backbone Architecture:**
The robot utilizes a visuomotor policy learned through behavior cloning. The network integrates multiple input modalities:
- **Visual Inputs:** Images from a workspace camera and an eye-in-hand camera are processed by a pre-trained R3M (Robotic and Real-world Representation for Manipulation) visual encoder, followed by an MLP to reduce dimensionality to 64.
- **Language Descriptions:** Natural language instructions are embedded using a pre-trained Sentence Similarity (SS) model, then passed through an MLP to a 64-dimensional embedding.
- **Proprioceptive Inputs:** Joint and gripper states are processed by an MLP to yield a 64-dimensional embedding.
These 64-dimensional embeddings from ten consecutive time steps are concatenated and fed into a Transformer-based temporal encoder. The Transformer's output is then passed to a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) serving as the policy head, which samples robot actions. The policy is trained by minimizing the discrepancy between predicted actions and expert actions from demonstrations using a standard loss function for behavior cloning.

**Lifelong Learning Phase:**
In this phase, the robot is exposed to a sequence of tasks (T_1, T_2..., T_K).
1. **Sequential Training:** The model is trained on the demonstrations of the current task (D_K).
2. **Experience Replay (ER):** To mitigate catastrophic forgetting and preserve previously acquired skills, a subset of demonstrations from prior tasks is stored in a compact storage memory M. These stored demonstrations are replayed during the training of new tasks, combined with the current task's data. This process balances the acquisition of new skills with the retention of old ones.
Notably, tasks are presented sequentially, and task boundaries or IDs are not provided to the learning algorithm, reflecting the task-agnostic objective. The language descriptions for tasks are paraphrased using a large language model (Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct) to further obscure distinct task boundaries and create a more realistic, ambiguous learning environment.

**Reviewing Phase:**
This phase occurs *before* policy deployment when the robot encounters a new testing scenario (S_deploy). It is designed to emulate human "review" processes for skill recovery.
1. **Initial Rollouts:** The robot first performs a small number of rollouts (e.g., 10 episodes) on S_deploy using its current policy (trained during the Lifelong Learning phase). This step assesses the robot's current performance and identifies potential forgetting or difficulties in the new scenario.
2. **Data Retrieval:** The method then retrieves the most relevant demonstrations from the storage memory M. This retrieval is task-agnostic, meaning it does not rely on pre-defined task IDs. Relevance is determined by a weighted L2 distance (D_R) between the current deployment scenario's context (visual observation from the workspace camera and language description) and the stored demonstrations' contexts. This weighted distance combines visual (D_v) and language (D_l) embedding distances (D_R = α_v * D_v + α_l * D_l). The parameters α_v and α_l control the relative importance of visual and language cues, with specific weights tuned for different benchmark categories (e.g., higher language weight for `libero_goal` where tasks are visually similar).
3. **Selective Weighting Mechanism:** To optimize the use of retrieved demonstrations, a selective weighting mechanism is applied:
 - **Failure Detection:** If an initial rollout fails, the system compares images from the retrieved demonstrations with images from the failed rollout trajectories using L2 distances of their embeddings, generating an Embedding Distance Matrix (EDM).
 - **Separation Segment Identification:** From the EDM, an Embedding Distance Curve (EDC) is derived, tracking the minimum visual discrepancy over time. "Separation Segments"—critical points where the failed rollout's behavior diverged significantly from the demonstration's expected path—are identified. These segments indicate where mistakes are likely to occur.
 - **Weight Assignment:** Frames within these identified Separation Segments in the retrieved demonstrations are assigned higher weights (an increment of 0.3, clipped to 2) during the subsequent local adaptation. This directs the learning process to focus on repairing specific, vulnerable parts of the skill.
4. **Local Adaptation with Fast Fine-tuning:** Using the `˜N` retrieved, selectively weighted demonstrations, the model's parameters are fine-tuned for a limited number of epochs (e.g., 20). This short fine-tuning process aims to rapidly recover forgotten knowledge and adapt the policy to the specifics of S_deploy. **Testing Phase:**
After local adaptation, the fine-tuned model (θ') is deployed for a new set of episodes (e.g., 20) in S_deploy to assess its improved performance.

**Experimental Setup:**
The method is evaluated using LIBERO benchmarks (libero_spatial, libero_object, libero_goal, libero_different_scenes), which feature diverse task goals, objects, and layouts. Baselines include EWC, ER, AGEM, an AGEM-RWLA variant, and PackNet (as a reference for task ID-dependent methods). The primary metric is Average Success Rate (ASR) across multiple random seeds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides several key findings regarding the effectiveness, generalizability, and robustness of the RWLA framework:

1. **Enhanced Robot Performance:** ER-RWLA, the proposed method combining Experience Replay with Retrieval-based Weighted Local Adaptation, consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to other task-agnostic baselines such as vanilla Experience Replay (ER), Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), and Average Gradient Episodic Memory (AGEM) (Table 1). ER-RWLA achieved an overall Average Success Rate (ASR) of 52.42%, outperforming vanilla ER (39.65%), AGEM (16.44%), and EWC (1.10%). This indicates that the "review" mechanism effectively recovers forgotten skills and enhances performance in open-ended, task-agnostic scenarios.

2. **Generalizability as a Paradigm Solution:** The effectiveness of RWLA was further demonstrated by applying it to the AGEM baseline, resulting in AGEM-RWLA. AGEM-RWLA significantly improved upon vanilla AGEM (from 16.44% to 46.85% overall ASR), suggesting that RWLA is a generalizable, "plug-and-play" paradigm that can complement various memory-based lifelong learning approaches and boost their performance.

3. **Comparison with Task ID-Dependent Methods:** PackNet, an architecture-based method that relies on explicit task IDs, performed well initially. However, its performance significantly deteriorated in benchmarks with a larger number of tasks (e.g., `libero_different_scenes` with 20 tasks, see Appendix A.6). This is attributed to its diminishing trainable capacity as more tasks are added, highlighting a limitation in plasticity when task IDs are available but finite capacity is a concern. In contrast, ER-RWLA maintained more consistent performance across tasks.

4. **Impact of Selective Weighting:** Ablation studies comparing RWLA (with selective weighting) to RULA (uniform local adaptation) indicated that the selective weighting mechanism systematically improved performance, albeit modestly (Table 2). This suggests that focusing adaptation on "Separation Segments" (critical, failure-prone parts of a skill) contributes to more efficient and effective skill recovery, without incurring substantial computational overhead.

5. **Effect of Blurry Task Boundaries and Language Encoding:** The paraphrasing of task descriptions successfully blurred task boundaries, creating a more realistic task-agnostic learning environment. The choice of language encoding model played a crucial role in data retrieval (Figure 5). The Sentence Similarity (SS) Model effectively differentiated semantically similar tasks, leading to higher ASR and Retrieval Accuracy (RA) compared to BERT, especially for visually similar tasks like those in `libero_goal`. This indicates that robust semantic understanding is critical for effective retrieval in task-agnostic settings.

6. **Robustness to Imperfect Retrieval:** Despite BERT's limitations in task distinction and lower Retrieval Accuracy, RWLA-based methods using BERT embeddings could still improve model performance when RA was moderately acceptable (e.g., 0.375). This indicates a degree of robustness and fault tolerance in the RWLA approach, where even partially relevant retrieved demonstrations can contribute to skill recovery.

7. **Comparison with Test-Time Adaptation:** When compared against LoRA-FT, a method employing LoRA adapters for adaptation, ER-RWLA consistently surpassed its performance across benchmarks (Figure 10). This was particularly notable as LoRA-FT was pre-trained on a larger dataset but then adapted to unseen tasks, suggesting potential issues with distributional mismatch that ER-RWLA, with its memory-based lifelong learning foundation, navigated more effectively.

8. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The selective weighting mechanism was found to be robust to variations in its hyperparameters, such as lower/higher thresholds for identifying separation segments and padding steps (Figure 9).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a framework that addresses several challenges in the field of lifelong robot learning, moving towards more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems.

The primary significance lies in its **task-agnostic nature**. By developing a method that operates effectively without relying on explicit task boundaries or identifiers, the work brings lifelong robot learning closer to realistic, open-ended deployment scenarios. This capability is crucial for scaling robotic applications beyond controlled environments where tasks are neatly segmented and labeled.

The proposed **Retrieval-based Weighted Local Adaptation (RWLA)** mechanism is a key contribution. Inspired by human cognitive processes of reviewing forgotten knowledge, this on-demand "review" phase provides an efficient means for robots to rapidly recover degraded skills. This contrasts with traditional approaches that primarily focus on preventing forgetting during continuous training, offering a complementary strategy for knowledge management. The ability to retrieve relevant past demonstrations and then selectively emphasize critical segments through weighting optimizes the limited data available for adaptation, making the process both efficient and targeted.

The demonstration of RWLA as a **"plug-and-play" paradigm** by enhancing the performance of existing memory-based lifelong learning algorithms (like AGEM) highlights its broad applicability and potential to improve current state-of-the-art methods. This suggests that the proposed adaptation strategy could be broadly integrated into various lifelong learning architectures.

The impact of this work is potentially significant for several reasons:
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** Robots equipped with RWLA could operate more robustly in dynamic, real-world environments, continuously learning new tasks and adapting to unforeseen circumstances without requiring human intervention for task labeling or extensive retraining.
- **Improved Efficiency:** The selective weighting mechanism ensures that adaptation resources are concentrated on the most critical parts of a skill, leading to more efficient recovery and reduced computational overhead compared to uniform adaptation.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By mitigating catastrophic forgetting and enabling efficient skill recovery, the framework could reduce the need for costly and time-consuming retraining from scratch, allowing for continuous refinement of robot capabilities over its operational lifetime.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the scalability of storage memory (though storing embeddings is a noted solution) and the potential for identifying equivariant features among similar tasks, provide clear directions for future research. This could lead to more compact memory representations and further generalization across variations, enhancing the system's plasticity and mitigating redundant learning.

In conclusion, this research presents a practical and robust framework for lifelong robot learning that is task-agnostic, efficient in its use of stored experience, and capable of enhancing the adaptability and long-term performance of robotic systems in complex, open-ended environments.
