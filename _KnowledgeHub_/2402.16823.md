# Language Agents as Optimizable Graphs

> [!summary] Summary
> GPTSwarm models LLM agents as computational graphs. Nodes are operations such as LLM queries or tool calls, edges carry information flow, and graphs compose into swarms. Two automatic optimizers improve them: node optimization rewrites prompts, and edge optimization learns inter-agent connectivity with REINFORCE. It filters adversarial agents on MMLU, raises Mini Crosswords and HumanEval accuracy, and roughly doubles GAIA performance over baselines.

## Problem

- Prompting schemes such as chain of thought, tree of thought and Reflexion, and multi-agent frameworks, are each hand-designed and live in separate, disparate code bases that need heavy human engineering.
- No unified representation exists for single agents and multi-agent collaboration, so prompting techniques cannot be systematically compared, recombined or hierarchically composed into larger systems.
- Agent orchestration topology and node prompts are fixed by hand and not automatically improved, and the discrete, non-differentiable search over agent connectivity is combinatorially hard.

## Method

- Agents are ==directed acyclic computational graphs== of nodes with routines, executed in topological order. A ==swarm== is a composite graph whose extra cross-agent edges act as communication channels.
- ==Edge optimization== parameterizes a distribution over DAGs with one learnable probability per potential edge, and trains it with ==REINFORCE== using the task utility as reward.
- ==Node optimization== has an LLM rewrite each node's prompt from its recent inputs, outputs and task feedback, iterating over time. It is applied here to code generation and hyperparameter-style prompt refinement.

## Results

- On MMLU with equal numbers of honest and adversarial agents, **200** REINFORCE iterations of edge optimization recover the single-agent baseline score. With seven role-diverse agents, the swarm beats the baseline by **2.1% ± 1.1%**.
- Mini Crosswords: an optimized three-agent swarm reaches **0.800 ± 0.0616** accuracy, beating tree of thought at 0.675 and a GPT-4-Turbo variant at 0.668. HumanEval node optimization lifts accuracy from 0.76 to **0.88**.
- On GAIA, a self-consistency swarm of seven tree-of-thought agents scores **18.45** average versus 9.70 for GPT-4-Turbo, with Level 2 up **260%**, at the cost of much longer runtime, and edge/node optimization was not applied.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating an agent system as an optimizable graph turns prompt engineering into learning problems. Connectivity can be trained by policy gradient even though the utility is non-differentiable.
> - Learning distributions over edges lets the system automatically prune harmful or useless agents and recombine known prompting techniques, instead of relying on human-designed topologies.
> - Gains from swarms come with growing compute and latency. Diversity among agents matters, since identically prompted agents gave no improvement while role-diverse agents did.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of LLM-powered autonomous problem solvers, often referred to as "agents," is a rapidly expanding area of AI research. These agents aim to leverage LLMs' reasoning capabilities for complex tasks, extending beyond simple query-response interactions.

**Evolution of LLM Interaction:** The field has progressed from basic zero-shot or few-shot prompting (e.g., Kojima et al., 2022; Brown et al., 2020) to more structured prompting techniques. Methods like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022), ReAct (Yao et al., 2022), Tree-of-Thought (ToT) (Yao et al., 2023), Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), and Graph-of-Thought (GoT) (Besta et al., 2023) have significantly improved LLMs' reasoning abilities for text-based tasks.

**Agent Frameworks:** Concurrently, frameworks for building single LLM agents (e.g., AutoGPT, BabyAGI, LangChain, Llama-index) have emerged, providing functionalities like tool usage, function calling, and embodied actions. More recently, multi-agent frameworks (e.g., NL-SOMs, CAMEL, Generalist Agents, ChatDev, AutoGen, MetaGPT) have gained prominence. These systems involve multiple LLMs collaborating, often by taking on specialized roles, which can lead to superior performance due to task decomposition and specialization.

**The Unification Challenge:** A critical challenge identified by the authors is the proliferation of "increasingly different and disparate code bases" arising from these various prompt engineering techniques and multi-agent communication paradigms. This fragmentation demands substantial human engineering to define prompting schemes, agent workflows, and inter-agent communication. GPTSwarm directly addresses this by proposing a unified, graph-based representation for LLM agents, abstracting away the low-level implementation details and allowing for a more systematic, automatable approach.

**Distinction from Related Graph-based LLM Systems:**
- **Graph of Thoughts (GoT):** While GoT also uses graph representations, it primarily focuses on LLM prompting schemes for reasoning and does not comprehensively model other fundamental agent capabilities like external tool usage, nor does it propose methods for *optimizing* the graph structure itself.
- **LangGraph:** A concurrent open-source framework, LangGraph focuses on building multi-actor stateful LLM applications with potentially cyclic operations. However, the authors note its practical applicability has not been systematically studied, and it does not emphasize automatic optimization of the graph as GPTSwarm does.
- **DSPy:** Like GPTSwarm, DSPy also conceptualizes LLM pipelines as computational graphs with modular LLM queries as nodes, and aims to optimize their parameters (prompts and weights). However, DSPy typically employs a two-stage optimization process involving generating candidate solutions and then optimizing across their Cartesian product. GPTSwarm, in contrast, proposes an *iterative* optimization process, which may be more scalable for complex graphs by improving each node individually per iteration.
- **Prompt Optimization Methods (OPRO, PromptBreeder):** These methods focus on optimizing a single, global prompt. GPTSwarm extends this by optimizing multiple node-level prompts *within a larger computational graph structure*, where prompts interact.
- **Structure Optimization (DyLAN, STOP):** DyLAN uses fixed heuristics to improve agent collaboration. STOP optimizes both prompts and inference structure. GPTSwarm contributes to this area by specifically applying reinforcement learning (REINFORCE) to optimize the *edge connectivity* of the graph, a distinct mechanism for structural self-improvement.

By representing agents and swarms as optimizable computational graphs, GPTSwarm introduces a meta-learning approach to agent design, allowing the system to learn not just *what* to say (prompt optimization) but also *how* to interact and orchestrate (edge optimization). This positions GPTSwarm as a significant advancement towards more adaptable, autonomous, and efficient LLM-based AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the GPTSwarm project are to:

1. **Unify Language Agent Systems:** Consolidate the disparate and often ad-hoc approaches to designing LLM-powered agents and their prompt engineering techniques. The goal is to provide a cohesive, universal description of these systems using a computational graph metaphor, thereby simplifying their development and integration.
2. **Enable Hierarchical Intelligence:** Model LLM-based agents and their collaborative societies in a modular and hierarchical manner. This involves defining basic operations as "nodes," individual agents as "graphs" of these nodes, and multi-agent collaborations ("swarms") as "composite graphs" formed by connecting agent graphs. This structure aims to foster the emergence of higher-level intelligence from simpler components, inspired by Minsky's "Society of Mind."
3. **Automate Agent Improvement:** Develop novel, automatic optimization methods that can refine both the internal logic of individual agents and the orchestration patterns among multiple agents. Specifically, this includes:
 - **Node Optimization:** Automatically improving the prompts and computational routines associated with individual nodes within an agent's graph.
 - **Edge Optimization:** Automatically learning and adjusting the connectivity (information flow and communication channels) between agents in a composite graph to maximize task utility.
4. **Reduce Human Engineering Effort:** Mitigate the substantial manual effort currently required for designing, fine-tuning, and integrating LLM-based agents. By automating prompt design and inter-agent communication patterns, GPTSwarm aims to accelerate the development of robust and high-performing LLM applications.
5. **Enhance Performance and Robustness:** Demonstrate that automatic graph optimization leads to significant performance improvements on various benchmarks, enabling agents to self-improve and adapt to diverse tasks, including in adversarial or complex real-world scenarios.

The motivation stems from the recognition that while LLM agents are powerful, their current development is often bespoke, labor-intensive, and lacks a unifying framework for systematic improvement. GPTSwarm seeks to provide this framework, pushing the boundaries towards more autonomous, self-organizing, and efficient AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GPTSwarm proposes a highly modular and hierarchical framework based on computational graphs to represent and optimize LLM-based agents.

#### 3.1. Language Agents as Graphs

The core of the methodology is to define LLM agents as computational graphs, inspired by the "Society of Mind" concept.
- **Nodes:** These are the fundamental operational units. Each node implements a specific computational routine, such as querying an LLM, using an external tool (e.g., web search, file analysis), making a function call, or performing an embodied action. Inputs and outputs are typically strings (natural language), but can be generalized to other data types.
- **Agents (Graphs):** A single language agent is modeled as a directed computational graph `G = (N, E, F, o)`. `N` is a set of nodes, `E` is a set of directed edges representing information flow between nodes, `F` maps nodes to their computational routines, and `o` is the designated output node. The framework primarily focuses on Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), ensuring a clear topological execution order.
- **Swarms (Composite Graphs):** A swarm is a collection of `K` individual agents, represented as a composite graph `G_E = (N', E_E, F', o')`. `N'`, `F'`, and `o'` are unions of the constituent agents' elements. Crucially, `E_E` includes both the internal edges of each agent and *new edges* that connect nodes from *different* agents. These inter-agent edges define communication channels and collaboration patterns within the swarm.

#### 3.2. Optimization Mechanisms

The graph representation naturally lends itself to two levels of automatic optimization:

##### 3.2.1. Edge Optimization

This addresses the challenge of optimizing the communication and orchestration between agents within a swarm.
- **Problem Formulation:** Given a task `τ` and a utility function `u_τ` (e.g., accuracy, success rate), the goal is to find the optimal set of inter-agent edges `E` that maximize `u_τ(G_E)`.
- **Challenges:** The search space for `d` potential edges is `2^d`, which is combinatorially complex. Additionally, the utility function is typically non-differentiable due to LLM tokenization.
- **Continuous Reformulation:** To overcome these challenges, the problem is reformulated as a continuous optimization problem. Instead of optimizing discrete edge choices, the system optimizes a set of real-valued parameters `θ = [θ_1, ..., θ_d]` where each `θ_i` represents the probability of including a potential edge `e_i`. This defines a probabilistic distribution `D_θ` over feasible DAGs.
- **Optimization Algorithm:** The **REINFORCE algorithm** (Williams, 1992), a classic policy gradient method from reinforcement learning, is applied for gradient ascent. In each iteration, `M` graphs are sampled from `D_θ`, their utility is estimated, and `θ` is updated based on the gradient of the expected utility, `∇_θ E_{G_E~D_θ}[u_τ(G_E)]`. This allows the system to learn which inter-agent connections are most beneficial for a given task. Adam optimizer is used for practical implementation.

##### 3.2.2. Node Optimization

This focuses on improving the internal prompts of individual nodes within an agent's graph.
- **Problem:** Each node's routine `f_n` is parameterized by a prompt `p_n`. Optimizing `p_n` is complex because it affects downstream nodes.
- **Approach:** The methodology hypothesizes that, due to the separation of concerns in the graph, it is sufficient to update each node's prompt individually, assuming other prompts are fixed, in an iterative process.
- **Algorithm:** An "improver function" `I` is used. This function, potentially an LLM itself or an existing prompt optimization method (like OPRO), takes a node's accumulated input-output history `h_n`, its current prompt `p_n`, and a natural language description `d_n` of its intended function, and returns an improved prompt. The process is iterative:
 1. Sample an input `x`.
 2. Execute the graph `G_P(x)`.
 3. Record the input-output pairs for each node and add them to their respective history sets `h_n`.
 4. Update each node's prompt `p_n` by applying `I(h_n, p_n, d_n)`. This continuous feedback loop allows nodes to self-improve their operational capabilities.

#### 3.3. Framework Implementation

GPTSwarm is an open-source framework developed in Python and PyTorch. Its architecture includes classes for `Node`, `Graph`, and `CompositeGraph`, with edges implicitly stored as adjacency lists. It abstracts `Agents` (e.g., `IO`, `TOT`) and `Operations` (e.g., `DirectAnswer`, `WebSearch`). It integrates LLM backends (e.g., OpenAI API) and provides interfaces for `Dataset` and `PromptSet` to manage benchmarks and customize node behavior. An `Evaluator` class orchestrates the optimization processes. The framework leverages asynchronous computations for parallelism.

This comprehensive methodology allows GPTSwarm to automatically discover effective agent internal logic and inter-agent collaboration patterns, moving beyond manual design and fostering true self-improvement in LLM systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper validates GPTSwarm's framework and optimization capabilities across several challenging benchmarks, demonstrating its ability to unify existing methods and achieve significant performance improvements through automation.

#### 4.1. MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) Benchmark

- **Adversarial Setting:** Experiments were conducted with swarms containing both truthful and deliberately adversarial agents. The key finding was that **edge optimization successfully filtered out the influence of adversarial agents**, recovering the swarm's performance to match that of a single truthful agent baseline. This demonstrates the framework's ability to prune detrimental connections and select for beneficial information flow. A notable practical outcome was the **significantly lower computational cost** of GPTSwarm's optimization (USD 5.32) compared to competitive baselines like DyLAN (USD 105.93) for similar tasks.
- **Collaborative Setting:** When using a swarm of seven *diverse* truthful agents (each assigned a different role, e.g., "Botanist," "Journalist"), the optimized swarm **improved the score over the single agent baseline by 2.1%**. This shows that beyond filtering bad agents, edge optimization can genuinely enhance collaboration among specialized LLM agents.

#### 4.2. Mini Crosswords Dataset

This benchmark tests the framework's ability to improve the performance of standard agents from the literature (Tree of Thought, Reflexion, Chain of Thought) by optimizing their interconnections.
- **Edge Optimization Performance:** The optimization process, applied to a swarm of three distinct agents, led to a substantial increase in accuracy from an initial distribution score of 0.465 to **0.575**. This also outperformed a "best-of-three" individual agent baseline (0.320), indicating that the synergistic effect of the optimized connections was key.
- **Efficiency of Optimization:** The improvements were not merely due to increased computational budget or denser graphs; a randomly connected distribution with a similar average number of edges performed worse (0.510), confirming the effectiveness of the learned connectivity.
- **State-of-the-Art Results:** Most impressively, when one of the final optimized edge distributions was evaluated with GPT-4-Turbo, it achieved an accuracy of **0.800**, significantly outperforming the previous state-of-the-art method, Tree of Thought, evaluated with GPT-4 (0.675) and even its own GPT-4-Turbo implementation (0.668). This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that GPTSwarm's edge optimization can automatically discover and implement high-performance algorithmic structures through agent orchestration.

#### 4.3. HumanEval Dataset

This dataset focuses on code generation and is highly sensitive to prompt design, making it ideal for testing node optimization.
- **Node Optimization Effectiveness:** Using a ReAct-style agent, **node-based prompt optimization significantly improved accuracy from 0.76 (no optimization) to 0.88** within about five iterations. This demonstrates that the iterative, local prompt improvement strategy is effective, reducing the need for laborious manual prompt crafting.

#### 4.4. GAIA (General AI Assistants) Benchmark

GAIA tests the generality of AI assistants on complex, real-world questions requiring reasoning, multi-modality processing, and various tool uses (e.g., web browsing, file analysis).
- **Framework Applicability and Swarm Performance:** While these specific experiments did not include edge or node optimization (left for future work in this context), they showcased GPTSwarm's **general applicability and modularity** in constructing complex swarms.
- **Outperformance of Baselines:** A swarm of seven Tree of Thought agents combined with a self-consistency strategy for final decision-making achieved an average accuracy of **18.45% across all difficulty levels**. This significantly **outperformed GPT-Series models (GPT-3.5: 4.85%, GPT-4: 6.06%, GPT-4-Turbo: 9.70%) and AutoGPT (4.85%)**.
- **Impact of Agent Numbers and Tool Variety:** Ablation studies showed that incorporating more agents and a greater variety of node operations (tools) generally led to better performance, albeit with increased computational time. The results highlight the potential for further performance gains with enhanced web capabilities and future optimization.

In summary, the findings robustly validate GPTSwarm's core propositions: its graph-based representation unifies disparate agent designs, and its automatic optimization methods for both nodes and edges yield substantial performance benefits and efficiency gains across diverse tasks, moving LLM agent development towards a more automated and self-improving paradigm.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GPTSwarm represents a significant step forward in the design, development, and deployment of Large Language Model (LLM)-based autonomous agents and multi-agent systems. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

1. **A Unified Paradigm for Agent Design:** By framing LLM agents and their collaborations as optimizable computational graphs, GPTSwarm introduces a cohesive, principled framework that can unify the myriad of disparate prompt engineering techniques and agent architectures. This moves beyond ad-hoc, human-engineered solutions towards a more systematic and scalable approach.
2. **Automated Self-Improvement of AI Systems:** The most profound impact lies in its dual-level automatic optimization.
 - **Node Optimization:** Automating prompt refinement within individual operational units (nodes) drastically reduces the labor-intensive and often heuristic process of manual prompt engineering. This makes agents more adaptable and efficient.
 - **Edge Optimization:** The ability to automatically learn and adjust the *orchestration* and *communication patterns* between agents is a crucial step towards truly self-organizing and intelligent multi-agent systems. This represents a form of meta-learning, where the system learns *how to learn* or *how to organize itself* for optimal performance, a long-standing goal in AI research, particularly aligning with Jürgen Schmidhuber's work on meta-learning and self-referential systems.
3. **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** The experimental results clearly demonstrate that automatic graph optimization leads to tangible performance gains, even achieving state-of-the-art results on challenging benchmarks like Mini Crosswords. The ability to filter out adversarial agents also highlights increased robustness. This means more effective and reliable LLM applications.
4. **Accelerated Development and Reduced Human Effort:** By automating critical aspects of agent design and integration, GPTSwarm can significantly accelerate the development lifecycle of complex LLM applications. It reduces the need for constant human intervention to fine-tune prompts or manually design communication protocols, freeing up developers to focus on higher-level problem definitions.
5. **Scalability to Complex Systems:** The modular, hierarchical graph representation is inherently designed for scalability. As the ambition for LLM systems grows, moving towards "societies of millions or billions of agents" (as suggested in Appendix A.1), the ability to automatically optimize their interconnections becomes indispensable. GPTSwarm lays a foundational piece for such future large-scale AI ecosystems.
6. **Open-Source Contribution:** Releasing the framework as open-source is a vital contribution, enabling broader adoption, further research, and collaborative development in the field of intelligent agents. This fosters innovation and allows the research community to build upon GPTSwarm's foundations.
7. **Ethical Considerations and Responsible AI:** The authors include an explicit "Impact Statement" acknowledging the multifaceted societal consequences, including potential job displacement and the critical need for safeguards against biased or unethical AI behaviors. This demonstrates a responsible approach to developing powerful AI technologies, emphasizing the importance of ethical guidelines and oversight as AI systems become more autonomous.

In essence, GPTSwarm pushes the frontier of LLM agent research from a human-driven, craft-based approach to a more autonomous, machine-optimized paradigm. This could pave the way for more sophisticated, adaptable, and efficient AI systems capable of tackling increasingly complex real-world problems.
