# Trajectory-based actuator identification via differentiable simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TrajID identifies robot actuator models by trajectory matching through a differentiable simulator (MJX), fitting actuator and physics-engine parameters from commanded positions and encoder angles/velocities alone, with no torque sensors or test stand. It supports parametric, neural, and free-torque-oracle parameterizations, cutting held-out position error from 14.20 to 7.54 mrad versus a stand-trained supervised baseline, and improving real locomotion.

## Problem

- Ideal torque-source/PD abstractions collapse inner control loops, delays, saturation, and friction into one mapping, leaving an actuation gap that dominates sim-to-real mismatch.
- Test-stand characterization needs torque transducers and dynamometers, and its steady-state operating points miss transients, inertial effects, and high-load low-speed braking regimes.
- Domain randomization gives no guarantee the real system lies in the sampled range, while gradient-free trajectory fitting is sample-inefficient and sensitive to excitation design.

## Method

- ==Trajectory-matching== objective: weighted position/velocity residual over short overlapping segments (N=3 at 500 Hz), rolled out from measured initial states and minimized with ==Adam== by ==backpropagating through MJX==.
- Unified parameterization spans **TrajID-Param** (three parameters: PD stiffness, damping, armature), **TrajID-NN** (1,217-parameter ==MLP== torque map), and MuJoCo internals like armature, frictionloss, damping.
- **Torque-Oracle** mode optimizes a free per-timestep torque sequence as an expressiveness upper bound, and emits ==pseudo-labels== for supervised actuator training without any torque sensor.

## Results

- On held-out **10 s** rollouts, stand-trained Bench-Sup reached **14.20±11.50 mrad** MAE, versus **7.54±4.57** for TrajID-NN and **8.73±5.31** for TrajID-Param; Torque-Oracle bounded at **2.86 mrad**.
- TrajID-NN matched the ASAP-style residual-action baseline (**7.17 mrad**) while training **23.4×** faster: **1.98** minutes versus **46.34** minutes of PPO wall-clock time.
- Over **25** seeds, identified parameters converged tightly (stiffness **3.684±0.013 N m/rad**); the refined miniPi model raised real walking distance **1.12→1.64 m** and cut yaw drift **109°→27°**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fitting closed-loop trajectories captures effective actuation behavior that steady-state bench characterization misses, so encoder motion can replace torque instrumentation for simulator calibration.
> - Compact parametric models are insensitive to loss weighting and prediction horizon, while high-capacity models overfit noisy velocity signals and spike at particular horizons.
> - Gradient-free search suffices for three-parameter fits, but differentiability is what makes thousand-parameter actuator models and physics-engine internals jointly identifiable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in robotics known as the "reality gap" or "actuation gap," which refers to the discrepancy between simulated robot behavior and real-world robot behavior. Accurate modeling of a robot's digital twin is crucial for effective control, planning, and analysis, particularly for controllers developed and tuned in simulation before deployment on hardware. A primary source of this mismatch lies in the actuation interface, where simplified models often fail to capture complex electrical, control, and mechanical effects such as delays, bandwidth limits, nonlinearities, and internal low-level control loops.

Existing approaches to actuator modeling and compensation can be broadly categorized:
- **Simplified Abstractions:** Many robotics simulators and control frameworks use idealized torque-source or proportional-derivative (PD) abstractions. While convenient, these neglect detailed electrical and mechanical dynamics, leading to inaccuracies in dynamic scenarios.
- **Hardware Characterization:** Traditional methods involve using dedicated test stands with torque sensors and dynamometers to experimentally characterize actuators under controlled excitation. These provide accurate steady-state data for metrics like torque-speed curves and friction parameters. However, they are costly, require specialized hardware and setup, and often do not capture transient dynamics or high-frequency effects relevant to rapid accelerations or impacts.
- **Robustness and Learning Strategies:**
 - **Domain Randomization:** This technique perturbs simulation parameters during training to encourage policy robustness, but its effectiveness depends on the chosen parameter ranges and can lead to conservative behaviors if too broad.
 - **Offline Trajectory-Matching Identification:** More recent learning-based methods aim to identify actuator dynamics from trajectory data by fitting model parameters to match real-world observations. These typically rely on observable kinematics (joint positions/velocities) without torque sensing but often use gradient-free optimization, which can be sample-inefficient and sensitive to excitation design.
 - **Neural Residual Actuation Compensation:** Approaches like Unsupervised Actuator Nets (UAN) or ASAP learn neural modules to compensate for dynamics mismatch by predicting residual actions or state corrections. These models are highly expressive but can overfit, may lack physical interpretability, and can be challenging to validate outside their training distribution.

This research integrates and extends aspects of trajectory-matching identification, specifically leveraging differentiable simulation. Differentiable simulators, such as MJX (the JAX-based MuJoCo), enable efficient gradient-based optimization for system identification by backpropagating losses through dynamics. This allows for fitting actuator and simulator parameters directly from real-world robot rollouts. The present work aims to bridge the gap by providing a scalable, torque-sensor-free method for transient, system-level actuator identification using differentiable trajectory matching, targeting physically meaningful models while also supporting more expressive neural architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a trajectory-based actuator identification method that enhances the fidelity of simulated robot behavior relative to real robots, thereby improving sim-to-real transfer for control and learning applications. This objective is motivated by several factors:

- **Addressing the Actuation Gap:** Accurate actuation models are identified as critical for closing the "actuation gap" between simulation and real-robot performance. Errors in actuator modeling can significantly degrade the effectiveness of control policies developed in simulation when deployed on physical hardware.
- **Limitations of Traditional Characterization:** Traditional methods for obtaining high-fidelity actuator dynamics typically involve dedicated test stands and torque sensing. These setups are often expensive, time-consuming, and primarily capture steady-state characteristics, failing to adequately model the transient dynamics, inertial effects, and high-frequency variations that occur during dynamic robot operation.
- **Need for Torque-Sensor-Free Identification:** The difficulty and cost associated with integrating torque sensors and other electrical measurement equipment on every robot joint or test stand motivate the development of methods that can identify actuator models directly from more readily available, observable motion data, such as joint angles and velocities obtained from encoders.
- **Leveraging Differentiable Simulation:** The emergence of differentiable physics simulators provides an opportunity for efficient, gradient-based optimization of complex system parameters. This can potentially overcome the sample inefficiency and convergence challenges associated with gradient-free optimization methods used in prior trajectory-matching approaches.
- **System-Level Actuator Behavior:** The goal is to capture the overall effective behavior of the entire actuation stack, including embedded low-level controllers, delays, bandwidth limits, and nonlinearities, rather than modeling each internal component explicitly. This system-level approach aims to produce models that are more relevant for high-level robot control.
- **Model Agnostic Framework:** The research seeks to establish a unified optimization pipeline that supports various model classes, from compact structured parameterizations (e.g., PD gains, armature) to more expressive neural actuator mappings, allowing for flexibility in model complexity and interpretability.
- **Downstream Impact:** Beyond improved simulation accuracy, a key motivation is to demonstrate the practical impact of refined actuator models on real-robot control tasks, specifically in the context of reinforcement learning for locomotion.

In summary, the core motivation is to provide a practical, data-driven, and cost-effective method for accurately modeling robotic actuators by directly matching observed robot trajectories, thereby improving the predictive power of simulations and the transferability of simulation-trained controllers to real robots, without requiring specialized torque-sensing hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method frames actuator identification as a trajectory-matching problem, solved using gradient-based optimization enabled by a differentiable simulator, specifically MJX.

**Formulation:**
The system state $s = [q, \dot{q}]$ consists of joint angles and velocities, typically obtained from encoders. The control input $a = q_{des}$ is the commanded joint position from a higher-level controller. The objective is to identify an unknown parameter vector $z^*$ that allows the simulator to reproduce the real system dynamics, such that $s_{i+1} = s'_{i+1} = \Phi_{z^*}(s_i, a_i, \delta)$, where $\Phi_z$ represents the discrete-time integration of system dynamics parameterized by $z$, and $\delta$ is the simulation timestep.

**Objective Function:**
To mitigate the accumulation of numerical integration errors over long horizons, trajectories are divided into short segments of length $N$. The optimization minimizes a weighted output mismatch over $M$ sampled segments:
$L_{batch}(z) = \frac{1}{MN} \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} W \|s'_{i,j} - s_{i,j}\|^2_2$
subject to $s'_{0,j} = s_{0,j}$ and $s'_{i+1,j} = \Phi_z(s'_{i,j}, a_{i,j}, \delta)$.
Here, $W = \text{diag}(w_q, w_{\dot{q}})$ is a diagonal weight matrix penalizing angle and velocity residuals. For the main results, $W = \text{diag}(1, 0)$, meaning only position error is penalized, as measured velocity was observed to be noisier. Each segment is rolled out from its measured initial state.

**System and Parameterization:**
The parameter vector $z$ can represent different types of actuator/simulator parameters. The study evaluates three main model classes:
1. **Identified Parametric TrajID (TrajID-Param):** Optimizes a small, interpretable set of MuJoCo joint parameters: PD gains ($k_p$ for position stiffness, $k_v$ for velocity damping) and armature. This model has three parameters.
2. **Identified Neural TrajID (TrajID-NN):** Utilizes a feed-forward Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) mapping concatenated inputs $[q_{des}, q, \dot{q}]$ to torque $\tau$. The MLP architecture is, comprising two hidden layers of 32 neurons each, resulting in 1,217 trainable parameters. It does not use recurrence or state history to maintain alignment with other instantaneous models.
3. **Torque Oracle (Torque-Oracle):** An upper-bound reference model that directly optimizes a free, per-timestep torque sequence $\{\tau_i\}^N_{i=1}$ for each evaluated trajectory segment. This represents the best achievable trajectory fit within the simulator's capabilities, without parametric constraints on the torque. It also serves to generate pseudo-labels for potential supervised training.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
- **Bench-Sup:** A supervised MLP trained on ground-truth torque data from a dedicated motor test stand. The MLP maps $(q_{des}, q, \dot{q}) \rightarrow \tau$. This represents a traditional, hardware-characterized approach.
- **Param-ES:** The same parametric model family as TrajID-Param, but optimized using sMAES, a gradient-free evolution strategy, to assess the benefits of gradient-based optimization.
- **Residual-RL:** An ASAP-inspired residual-action baseline that learns a correction $\delta a_i$ to the commanded position $a_i$, resulting in a corrected command $\tilde{a}_i = a_i + \delta a_i$. This model uses an MLP with architecture, similar to TrajID-NN, and is trained using PPO to maximize a reward corresponding to the negative rollout loss.

**Data Collection and Experimental Setup:**
- **Actuator:** Experiments were conducted on a high-gear-ratio actuator (210:1) with an embedded, hand-tuned PD controller, derived from the ROKI-2 robot.
- **Rig:** The actuator was mounted on a table-top rig with a rigid bar (mass 0.24 kg) as an external load to induce dynamic behavior.
- **Excitation Trajectories:** Desired velocity profiles ($\dot{q}_{des}(t)$) were synthesized as a sum of randomly sampled Fourier modes to ensure diverse and dynamic excitation, covering transient segments, near-limit operating points, and quasi-steady regions. These were then integrated to obtain position commands ($q_{des}(t)$).
- **Data Logging:** Training trajectories of 40 seconds and one test trajectory of 10 seconds were collected at a sampling rate of 500 Hz ($\delta = 0.002$ s).
- **Training Details:** For identified models, training used the Adam optimizer (Optax) with a learning rate of $10^{-2}$. Optimization steps used $M=2,000$ sampled trajectory segments, divided into short windows of length $N=3$ (6 ms). Early stopping was implemented with patience of 200 epochs.

**Limitations and Assumptions:**
The method assumes that the primary discrepancy between simulation and reality can be captured by the parameter vector $z$. It may be less effective if unmodeled effects (e.g., external disturbances, time-varying properties, sensor noise, hysteresis, contact mismatch) are significant and not included in the parameterization. While exact dynamic matching is challenging, the approach is considered effective if the optimized simulator provides an accurate approximation within acceptable error margins.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents validation and comparative analysis across various actuator models, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed trajectory-based differentiable simulation identification method.

**A. Trajectory-Matching Performance:**
The models were evaluated on their ability to reproduce measured joint trajectories under identical command sequences using the Mean Absolute Position Error (MAE).
- **Baseline Performance:** The **Bench-Sup** model, trained on steady-state torque data from a test stand, yielded the highest MAE (14.20 ± 11.50 mrad) on held-out trajectories. This indicates its limitation in capturing dynamic actuator behavior.
- **Trajectory-Identified Models:** All trajectory-identified models substantially outperformed the Bench-Sup baseline.
 - **TrajID-Param** (parametric model) achieved an MAE of 8.73 ± 5.31 mrad.
 - **Param-ES** (gradient-free parametric) achieved an identical MAE of 8.73 ± 5.31 mrad, suggesting that for this low-dimensional (3-parameter) problem, both gradient-based and gradient-free optimizers converged to similar solutions.
 - **TrajID-NN** (neural model) achieved an MAE of 7.54 ± 4.57 mrad.
 - **Residual-RL** (ASAP-inspired action residual) achieved an MAE of 7.17 ± 4.56 mrad, demonstrating performance comparable to TrajID-NN, as both utilized neural models with similar capacities.
- **Upper Bound:** The **Torque-Oracle** model, which directly optimized a free per-timestep torque sequence, provided an upper bound with an MAE of 2.86 ± 2.12 mrad, representing the best possible fit within the simulator's capabilities.
- **Training Efficiency:** TrajID-NN was significantly faster to train (1.98 minutes) compared to Residual-RL (46.34 minutes), highlighting the efficiency benefits of direct gradient-based parameter optimization.

In summary, the trajectory-based, torque-sensor-free identification methods reduced mean absolute position error by approximately 1.88x (from 14.20 mrad to 7.54-8.73 mrad) compared to the stand-trained baseline.

**B. Stability and Sensitivity Analysis:**
- **Parameter Consistency:** For TrajID-Param, 25 independent trials with varying random seeds showed high consistency in identified parameters and loss convergence. For example, $k_p$ was 3.684 ± 0.013 N m/rad, $k_v$ was 0.552 ± 0.002 N m s/rad, and armature was 0.00321 ± 2.5 × $10^{-5}$ kg m$^2$. This indicates that the objective function is well-conditioned for this parametric model.
- **Objective Weight Sensitivity (W Sweep):** Varying the weighting between position and velocity errors ($w_q = \alpha, w_{\dot{q}} = 1-\alpha$):
 - TrajID-Param showed negligible sensitivity, maintaining an MAE near 8.73 mrad across all $\alpha$.
 - TrajID-NN exhibited mild sensitivity, with optimal MAE near 7.21 mrad at $\alpha \approx 0.57$ and slightly higher errors at extremes, suggesting a trade-off in fitting noisy velocity data.
 - Torque-Oracle was largely flat for $\alpha < 1$ (MAE $\approx 9.95$ mrad) but significantly dropped to 2.86 mrad at $\alpha = 1$ (position-only weighting), indicating overfitting on noisy velocity when it was included in the loss.
- **Prediction-Horizon Analysis:** Evaluating MAE across different prediction horizons (N=1 to 4):
 - TrajID-Param remained stable, with MAE decreasing slightly from 9.49 mrad at horizon 1 to 8.74 mrad at horizon 4, indicating robust long-horizon consistency.
 - TrajID-NN showed a spike at horizon 2 (25.5 mrad) but recovered to 7.00 mrad at horizon 4, suggesting sensitivity to specific horizon lengths.
 - Torque-Oracle was highly horizon-dependent, with poor performance at horizon 1 (612 mrad), dropping to 2.86 mrad at horizon 3, then rising to 6.14 mrad at horizon 4. This behavior indicates that very short horizons can lead to overfitting on local noise for highly flexible models.

**C. Downstream Impact on Real-Robot Locomotion:**
The identified actuator model's practical utility was evaluated in a real-robot locomotion task using a miniPi robot. A structured parameterization (armature, frictionloss, damping) was identified for the miniPi's MuJoCo model.
- **Comparison:** A policy trained in simulation with the trajectory-identified model was compared against a policy trained with the default (unadjusted) actuator model.
- **Results:** Over 10 runs on the real robot:
 - The baseline policy (default model) achieved a mean travel distance of 1.12 ± 0.13 m with 109 ± 14.3 degrees of rotational deviation.
 - The policy trained with the refined (trajectory-identified) model achieved a mean travel distance of 1.64 ± 0.08 m with only 27 ± 6.4 degrees of rotational deviation.
- **Quantitative Improvement:** This represents a 46% increase in travel distance and a 75% reduction in rotational deviation, providing evidence of the practical advantage of using refined actuator models. The identified parameters were interpreted as physically plausible effective joint-space parameters for the simulator.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces a method for actuator identification that offers several advantages and has implications for robot control and sim-to-real transfer.

**Significance:**
- **System-Level Actuator Identification:** The method directly identifies effective actuator behavior from closed-loop motion trajectories, capturing the combined effects of electrical, mechanical, and internal control dynamics. This system-level approach is relevant for how actuators are perceived and controlled by high-level robot controllers.
- **Torque-Sensor-Free Operation:** A notable contribution is the ability to identify actuator models using only commanded inputs and encoder-based joint position and velocity measurements. This eliminates the need for expensive, specialized torque sensors or access to internal electrical states, making the identification process more accessible and scalable.
- **Quantitative Accuracy Improvement:** The study demonstrates a quantitative improvement in trajectory alignment, reducing mean absolute position error by approximately 1.88 times compared to a supervised baseline trained on steady-state test-stand data. This indicates that dynamic trajectory matching better captures the nuances of actuator behavior during active motion.
- **Unified Differentiable Optimization Framework:** The proposed framework supports diverse model classes, from compact parametric models to neural networks and even direct torque sequence optimization (Torque-Oracle). This flexibility allows researchers to select models based on needs for interpretability or expressiveness within a consistent gradient-based optimization pipeline.
- **Demonstrated Downstream Impact:** The real-robot locomotion study provides empirical evidence that improved actuator models translate to tangible performance gains in complex tasks. Policies trained with refined actuator models exhibited a 46% increase in travel distance and a 75% reduction in rotational deviation compared to policies trained with default models. This underscores the practical utility of accurate actuator modeling for reinforcement learning in robotics.
- **Reproducibility:** The provision of publicly accessible code, datasets, and scripts facilitates reproducibility and further research.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** By providing more accurate simulated digital twins, the method can significantly reduce the "reality gap," enabling more robust and effective transfer of controllers and policies developed in simulation to physical robots. This is particularly relevant for learning-based control methods (e.g., reinforcement learning) where simulation fidelity is paramount.
- **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** Eliminating the need for dedicated test stands and torque sensors for dynamic characterization can lower the cost and accelerate the development cycle of new robotic systems and control strategies.
- **Improved Control Performance:** Better actuator models can lead to more precise and dynamic control, allowing robots to perform more complex and agile maneuvers.
- **Applicability Across Robotic Platforms:** While validated on a specific high-gear-ratio actuator, the framework's model-agnostic nature suggests potential applicability across various actuator technologies and robot platforms, contributing to a generalized approach to robot system identification.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The method opens avenues for further exploration into incorporating other unmodeled effects (e.g., hysteresis, temperature drift), scaling to multi-joint and contact-rich environments, and developing more sophisticated neural architectures for actuator modeling.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
- The expressiveness of the model is bounded by the chosen parameterization; unmodeled effects (e.g., temperature drift, battery-voltage sag, hysteresis, contact mismatch) may persist as residual errors.
- Performance depends on the richness of excitation data and the quality of sensor measurements; long-horizon rollouts can accumulate integration errors.
- Experimental validation is limited to a single actuator class and a small number of hardware instances, indicating a need for broader validation across diverse actuator technologies and platforms.
