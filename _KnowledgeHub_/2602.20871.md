# GeCo-SRT: Geometry-aware Continual Adaptation for Robotic Cross-Task Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> GeCo-SRT reframes sim-to-real transfer as continual, cross-task knowledge accumulation. A frozen simulation diffusion policy is corrected by a shared point-cloud perception residual: a Geometry-aware Mixture-of-Experts routing local planarity, linearity, and saliency cues to specialized experts, plus a replay scheme prioritizing samples that activate under-used experts. Across four real manipulation tasks it lifts average success 52% over baseline using one-sixth the correction data.

## Problem

- System identification needs hand-specified model structures and heavy real data, and fails on complex nonlinear dynamics, making per-task physical calibration labor-intensive.
- Domain randomization depends on expert-tuned randomization ranges that may never cover true real-world visual and dynamic properties, so coverage is unverifiable.
- Human-correction and data-driven transfer methods adapt one task at a time, discarding prior transfer experience and forcing costly retuning from scratch for every new task.

## Method

- ==Human-in-the-loop== pipeline: a frozen ==point-cloud diffusion== base policy trained in simulation is deployed under shared autonomy; teleoperated corrections merge with sim data into a growing replay buffer.
- **Geo-MoE** (==Geometry-aware Mixture-of-Experts==) serves as a shared perception residual: ==k-NN== point groups yield ==PCA== planarity/linearity/saliency features that gate experts, whose weighted output concatenates with frozen encoder features.
- **Geo-PER** (==Geometry-expert-guided Prioritized Experience Replay==) reprioritizes historical samples by inverse current-task expert utilization rather than task loss, refreshing idle experts to block expert-level ==catastrophic forgetting==.

## Results

- Single-task transfer: Geo-MoE reaches **50.0%** average success across **four** tasks versus **38.3%** for the human-correction baseline, **9.2%** action-residual, and **3.1%** direct deployment.
- Continual four-task sequence: GeCo-SRT attains **63.3%** average success and **26.5%** normalized negative backward transfer, beating Geo-MoE+PER (**55.7%**/**29.6%**), Geo-MoE+EWC (**38.3%**), and correction-baseline+PER (**40.0%**/**55.0%**).
- Ablation isolates the observation residual as the main driver (**45.8%** average, MoE adds **10** points to **55.8%**); continual Pick Cube hits **76.6%** with **20** trajectories, matching from-scratch at **60**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local geometry is doubly invariant: stable across sim-real rendering gaps and shared across manipulation tasks, making it a strong medium for accumulating reusable adaptation knowledge.
> - Correcting the observation representation matters far more than correcting actions; residual action heads alone barely dent a reality gap rooted in perception.
> - Replay priority should track expert utilization, not task loss, since loss-based sampling starves idle modules; benefit also requires geometric task similarity, else transfer goes negative.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The application of machine learning in real-world robotic systems often leverages simulated environments for training due to their scalability and cost-effectiveness. However, a persistent challenge, known as the "sim-to-real gap," arises from discrepancies between simulated models and physical reality. These discrepancies, which can include unrealistic visual rendering or simplified physical dynamics, lead to significant performance degradation when policies trained in simulation are deployed in the real world.

Existing strategies to address the sim-to-real gap typically fall into several categories, each with limitations:
- **System Identification** aims to model real-world physics, but this approach is labor-intensive, requires pre-defined model structures, extensive data collection for parameter tuning, and struggles with complex, non-linear dynamics.
- **Domain Randomization** trains policies across diverse simulated variations, intending for the real world to be implicitly covered. Its effectiveness, however, is contingent on the manual configuration of randomization ranges, which may not adequately capture real-world properties.
- **Data-driven transfer methods**, while mitigating reliance on manual design, have largely been limited to single-task adaptation.

A common limitation across these approaches is the treatment of each sim-to-real transfer as an isolated endeavor. This paradigm necessitates repeated, costly efforts for new task adaptation, negating the potential to accumulate and leverage past experiences. Concurrently, the field of continual learning in robotics focuses on enabling agents to acquire new skills sequentially without forgetting previously learned ones. While advances have been made in mitigating catastrophic forgetting using replay-based or regularization-based methods, their direct application to robotic manipulation is challenging due to high-dimensional continuous state-action spaces and long-horizon temporal dependencies. Furthermore, much of the existing work on continual learning in robotics has concentrated on imitation learning, rather than the specific challenges of cross-task sim-to-real transfer.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing a novel "continual cross-task sim-to-real transfer paradigm." This paradigm aims to move beyond isolated sim-to-real efforts by explicitly building a mechanism for knowledge accumulation across iterative transfers. The work integrates principles of continual learning into the sim-to-real domain, specifically focusing on how to efficiently and effectively adapt to novel tasks by leveraging accumulated experience, thereby reducing the need for repeated, task-specific tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and effective method for continually adapting robotic policies from simulation to reality across a sequence of diverse tasks, accumulating knowledge over time rather than treating each transfer in isolation. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing the Inefficiency of Isolated Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Current sim-to-real methodologies are largely task-specific, demanding significant, repetitive, and costly tuning efforts for each new task. This approach wastes valuable experience gained from prior transfers. The researchers aim to establish a paradigm where knowledge can be accumulated and reused, thereby accelerating the deployment of robotic systems in dynamic real-world scenarios.

2. **Facilitating Effective Adaptation to Novel Tasks**: The goal is to enable a robotic system to adapt to new tasks rapidly and effectively, even with limited new data, by leveraging knowledge acquired from previous sim-to-real transfers. This requires a robust mechanism for knowledge accumulation and transferability across different tasks and domains.

3. **Identifying Transferable Knowledge**: A core motivation is to identify and leverage a robust form of knowledge that is invariant across both simulation/reality domains and different manipulation tasks. The authors hypothesize that local geometric features possess this "dual invariance," making them an ideal candidate for transferable knowledge:
 - **Domain Invariance**: Unlike visual properties (e.g., texture, lighting) or complex physical dynamics, fundamental local geometric features (e.g., surface normals, edges, corners derived from 3D data) tend to remain structurally consistent between simulated and real environments. This consistency provides a stable foundation for bridging the observation gap.
 - **Task Invariance**: Many robotic manipulation tasks rely on similar geometric primitives. For instance, the flat surface of a cube is relevant for both picking and stacking. By capturing these shared geometric understandings, the system can reuse knowledge across diverse tasks, fostering continual learning.

To operationalize these motivations, the research focuses on answering two fundamental questions:
- **How to convert human-designed sim-to-real heuristics into a learnable format for neural networks?** This is addressed by reframing sim-to-real transfer as learning from human correction trajectories, making the process amenable to iterative continual learning.
- **What specific knowledge is transferable across the iterative sim-to-real transfer process?** This is addressed by positing local geometric features as the core transferable knowledge and designing architectural components to exploit them.

These motivations lead to the proposal of the GeCo-SRT method, specifically integrating a geometry-aware Mixture-of-Experts (Geo-MoE) module for feature extraction and adaptation, and a Geometry-expert-guided Prioritized Experience Replay (Geo-PER) module to protect accumulated knowledge from catastrophic forgetting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GeCo-SRT method, "Geometry-aware Continual Adaptation," is designed to accumulate knowledge across iterative sim-to-real transfers for effective and efficient adaptation to novel tasks. It consists of three main components: Sim-to-real Transfer with Human Correction, Geometry-aware Mixture-of-Experts (Geo-MoE), and Geometry-expert-guided Prioritized Experience Replay (Geo-PER).

1. **Sim-to-real Transfer with Human Correction:**
 - **Base Policy Training in Simulation**: For each task *i*, a base policy $\pi^b_i$ is initially trained in a simulation environment. The policy utilizes 3D point clouds as the primary visual modality to mitigate visual sim-to-real gaps (e.g., texture, lighting differences common with RGB images). Depth images from two cameras are transformed and aggregated into a complete scene point cloud, which is then downsampled and used as input to the policy network. The policy, parameterized as a noise prediction network $\epsilon_\theta$, is trained using behavior cloning with an L2 diffusion loss on expert trajectories ($D^e_{i_{sim}}$) generated in simulation.
 - **Continual Learning with Human Correction**: The trained base policy $\pi^b_i$ is deployed in the real world under human supervision through a shared-autonomy framework. Human operators intervene with teleoperated actions ($a^h_t$) when anticipating a policy failure. These interventions, along with the corresponding observations, are recorded as real-world human correction trajectories ($D^h_{i_{real}}$). The key innovation here is that this human correction data, along with a subset of simulation data, forms a continually growing replay buffer ($D^m_{i_{buf}}$). While the parameters of the base policy $\pi^b_i$ are frozen, a shared "perception residual module" ($E^s_p$) is continually updated using this hybrid buffer. This module learns to align point cloud observation features, thus bridging the sim-to-real gap incrementally across tasks.

2. **Geometry-Aware Mixture-of-Experts (Geo-MoE):**
 - **Core Idea**: The Geo-MoE module serves as the perception residual module ($E^s_p$). It is built on the premise that local geometric features (e.g., planarity, linearity, saliency) are domain-invariant and task-invariant, making them suitable for transferable knowledge.
 - **Mechanism**: The input point cloud is first processed to extract local point groups ($g_i$) using k-Nearest Neighbors. For each group, local geometric features are estimated via PCA. A lightweight gating network (G) takes these geometric features as input and dynamically generates routing weights ($w_i$) for M parallel expert networks. Each expert specializes in processing distinct types of geometric knowledge. The output for each group ($g'_i$) is a weighted sum of the expert outputs. These per-group features are then aggregated into a single corrective residual vector ($g'_{res}$). This residual vector is concatenated with the output of the frozen base point cloud encoder ($E^b_{i_p}(o)$) to form a corrected feature vector ($\hat{f}$). Finally, this $\hat{f}$ is passed to the diffusion policy head ($\pi^b_{i_h}$) to produce the final action ($\hat{a}$).
 - **Training**: During training, only the Geo-MoE module is updated (the base policy remains frozen). The loss function includes an MSE term between the predicted action ($\hat{a}$) and the ground truth action ($a$), plus a standard auxiliary loss ($L_{balance}$) to ensure balanced utilization across experts and prevent gating collapse.

3. **Geometry-Expert-Guided Prioritized Experience Replay (Geo-PER):**
 - **Challenge**: In a continual learning setup, adapting to new tasks sequentially can lead to "catastrophic forgetting," where the model loses its ability to perform previous tasks. Standard Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) methods often prioritize samples based on task loss, which may not adequately preserve the specialized knowledge residing in individual experts within an MoE architecture, especially if those experts are idle for the current task.
 - **Approach**: Geo-PER modifies the prioritization strategy. A unified replay buffer (R) stores historical samples from all previous tasks, along with their associated expert activation vectors ($W_i = \{w_{i,1}, \dots, w_{i,M}\}$). When adapting to a new task (task N), the average expert utilization ($U^{new} = \{u^N_1, \dots, u^N_M\}$) for the current task is computed. The sampling priority ($P_i$) for each historical sample $i \in R$ is then dynamically calculated as proportional to the sum of its stored expert activations, weighted by the *reciprocal* of the current task's utilization of those experts: $P_i \propto \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_{i,j} \cdot \frac{1}{u^{new}_j + \epsilon}$. This ensures that historical samples that strongly activated experts *under-utilized* by the current task are prioritized for replay. This mechanism actively combats forgetting by compelling gradient updates for the parameters of idle experts, thus preserving their specialized geometric knowledge.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of GeCo-SRT was evaluated across a sequence of four robotic manipulation tasks: Pick Cube, Stack Cube, Pick Banana, and Plug Insert, utilizing both ManiSkill simulation and a real-world setup involving an Xarm5 arm and RealSense D435 cameras for point cloud observations. Evaluation metrics included Success Rate (SR) for forward transfer ability and Normalized Negative Backward Transfer (N-NBT) for quantifying catastrophic forgetting.

#### 4.1. Single Task Sim-to-Real Transfer

- **Direct Deploy** yielded a 3.1% average SR, confirming the existence of a substantial sim-to-real gap.
- **Action Residual** achieved 9.2% average SR, suggesting that only addressing action-level discrepancies is insufficient without also bridging the observation gap.
- **Transic**, a data-driven transfer method, reached 38.3% average SR.
- **Geo-MoE** (our method's perception component in isolation) outperformed all baselines with a 50.0% average SR. This performance was attributed to its dynamic utilization of specific geometric knowledge, enabling fine-grained adaptation.

#### 4.2. Cross-Task Continual Sim-to-Real Transfer

- **Naive Fine-tuning** exhibited significant catastrophic forgetting, with an average N-NBT of 75.0%, and a low average SR of 9.2%.
- Conventional continual learning baselines, **Transic + PER** and **Geo-MoE + EWC**, remained suboptimal with average SRs of 40.0% and 38.3%, respectively, and notable forgetting.
- Replacing Transic's backbone with Geo-MoE (**Geo-MoE + PER**) significantly improved performance, achieving 55.7% average SR and reducing average N-NBT to 29.6%. This indicated that the Geo-MoE architecture is inherently beneficial for continual learning by facilitating knowledge reuse through specialized experts.
- **Ours (GeCo-SRT)**, combining Geo-MoE with the proposed Geo-PER, achieved the highest performance with 63.3% average SR and the lowest average N-NBT of 26.5%. This was attributed to Geo-PER's expert-level prioritization, which actively preserves specialized knowledge in experts, including those under-utilized in the current task.

#### 4.3. Cross-Task Transfer Efficiency

- GeCo-SRT demonstrated significant data efficiency. For the Pick Cube task, it achieved 76.6% success with only 20 human correction trajectories, a performance level that nearly matched the from-scratch baseline trained with 60 trajectories (3x the data). A similar trend was observed for the Plug Insert task.
- This confirmed that GeCo-SRT effectively learns and reuses transferable geometric knowledge, enabling rapid adaptation to new tasks with limited new data.

#### 4.4. Ablation Results

- The **Observation Residual component** (point cloud encoder) was found to be crucial. Without it, performance was low (3.3% to 9.2% SR). Its introduction alone boosted average SR to 45.8% and reduced N-NBT to 37.0%, providing a stable feature bridge for visual alignment.
- The **Geo-MoE module** demonstrated a synergistic role. Building upon the Observation Residual component, its addition further improved average SR to 55.8% and reduced N-NBT to 29.6%. This indicates that Geo-MoE effectively specializes adaptation and preserves knowledge by routing geometric patterns to distinct experts.

#### 4.5. Cross-Task Transfer Results (Task Similarity)

- Task geometric similarity was identified as a critical factor for transfer effectiveness. Positive transfer was observed when learning Stack Cube after Pick Cube (40.0% SR with 10 trajectories, outperforming a 26.7% from-scratch baseline), demonstrating the reuse of knowledge from geometrically similar tasks.
- Conversely, negative transfer occurred when transferring from the geometrically dissimilar Plug Insert task to Stack Cube.
- Beneficial transfer was also observed from Pick Banana to Plug Insert, attributed to shared geometric challenges related to grasping non-cuboid objects.
- This further supports that GeCo-SRT learns and leverages foundational geometric understanding for rapid, low-data adaptation.

#### 4.6. Additional Qualitative and Quantitative Analysis (Supplementary Material)

- **Action Adaptation Visualization**: Showed that human corrections rectify initial failures, and in continual learning, Geo-MoE reuses knowledge to succeed with fewer new corrections.
- **Computational Efficiency & Robustness**: The method demonstrated a real-time inference latency of 26.1ms, comparable to baselines, and was robust to variations in the number of experts. A fixed-size replay buffer (50% of historical data) maintained performance with Geo-PER, managing memory growth.
- **MoE Interpretability**: Visualization indicated that experts specialized in distinct geometric features (e.g., edges, corners, planar surfaces). Routing collapse under Out-of-Distribution (OOD) observations was identified as a failure mode.
- **Task Complexity and Diversity**: GeCo-SRT demonstrated strong zero-shot performance on novel tasks (Open Faucet, Tabletop Tidying) and higher success rates in continual learning compared to training from scratch, even handling complex geometries and transparent objects (Pick Clear Bottle) through integration with depth-completion techniques.
- **RGB Inputs**: The framework generalized to RGB-only inputs, achieving reasonable performance, though point clouds remained superior due to their direct geometric representation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards addressing the inefficiencies and limitations of current sim-to-real transfer methods in robotics. By introducing a continual cross-task sim-to-real transfer paradigm, the work enables knowledge accumulation across iterative transfers, rather than treating each task in isolation.

The proposed GeCo-SRT method offers several contributions:
- **Paradigm Shift**: It moves beyond the prevalent "isolated sim-to-real" approach, advocating for a cumulative learning strategy that leverages prior transfer experiences. This is important for robotic systems that need to operate in dynamic, multi-task environments.
- **Effective Knowledge Transfer**: The identification and utilization of local geometric features as domain-invariant and task-invariant knowledge provide a robust foundation for bridging the sim-to-real gap and facilitating cross-task learning.
- **Enhanced Performance and Efficiency**: GeCo-SRT demonstrated an average performance improvement of 52% over baselines in success rate and substantially reduced catastrophic forgetting. Crucially, it achieved significant data efficiency, adapting to new tasks with only one-sixth of the data required by from-scratch training. This efficiency is critical for real-world robotic applications where data collection is costly and time-consuming.
- **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting**: The Geometry-expert-guided Prioritized Experience Replay (Geo-PER) module represents a targeted solution for protecting specialized knowledge within the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, ensuring that previously acquired skills are retained while new ones are learned.
- **Interpretability**: The specialization of experts within the Geo-MoE module towards distinct geometric primitives offers a degree of interpretability, providing insight into the types of knowledge being learned and transferred.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for the field of robot learning and deployment:
- **Accelerated Development**: By making sim-to-real transfer more efficient and cumulative, GeCo-SRT can significantly accelerate the development and deployment cycles of robotic systems in diverse real-world applications.
- **Reduced Cost**: The improved data efficiency directly translates to lower costs associated with real-world data collection and the need for extensive, repeated fine-tuning for each new task.
- **More Adaptable Robots**: The ability of robots to continually adapt to novel tasks without forgetting prior skills could lead to the development of more versatile and autonomous robotic agents capable of operating in complex and unstructured environments.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work explicitly calls for future research into leveraging multiple modalities, such as semantic relationships between tasks, to further enhance cross-task sim-to-real transfer, indicating a promising direction for the community. While the current focus is on geometric features, the framework's extensibility to other modalities could broaden its applicability beyond purely geometric sim-to-real gaps.
