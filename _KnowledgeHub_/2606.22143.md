# Physics-Informed Eikonal Caging for Whole-Arm Manipulation Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Idiap Research Institute, EPFL, and KTH Royal Institute of Technology developed a method called Physics-Informed Eikonal Caging to improve the robustness of whole-arm manipulation. It uses a differentiable escape-time field, learned via a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN), to bias planning towards configurations that geometrically resist object escape, demonstrating effective sim-to-real transfer despite significant model mismatches.

## Problem

- Planning contact-rich whole-arm manipulation faces challenges due to complex contact dynamics, multi-contact transitions, and the necessity for accurate contact models.
- "Object escape," where a manipulated object slips or reorients beyond the robot's control, is a common failure mode in contact-rich manipulation.
- Existing robotic caging formulations are predominantly static or sampling-based, making them difficult to integrate into continuous, dynamic trajectory planning frameworks.

## Method

- Caging is reformulated as a minimum-time optimal control problem for an adversarial object to escape, defining a continuous "escape-time field" that satisfies an eikonal equation.
- A ==Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN)== is employed to approximate this high-dimensional, differentiable escape-time field, trained self-supervised on the eikonal equation and boundary conditions.
- The learned escape-time field is integrated into continuous trajectory optimization for whole-arm manipulation, biasing plans towards robot configurations that maximize the object's escape time.

## Results

- The PINN accurately approximated the escape-time field for both simplified planar arms and a realistic Franka arm, producing differentiable outputs suitable for optimization.
- Whole-arm manipulation plans using the caging objective demonstrated superior robustness to random object disturbances and increased tolerance to contact-model mismatches (e.g., dynamics order, object shape under-approximation, friction variations) in simulation.
- Optimized trajectories successfully transferred to open-loop execution on a physical Franka Panda arm, maintaining robust manipulation despite multiple simultaneous real-world model discrepancies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic robotic caging can be precisely characterized as a minimum-time escape problem whose solution is an eikonal equation, yielding a continuous and differentiable robustness metric.
> - Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) provide an effective way to learn this complex escape-time field in high-dimensional robot-object configuration spaces, enabling its direct use in gradient-based optimization.
> - By promoting geometric enclosure through an escape-time objective, manipulation planning achieves intrinsic robustness to contact model mismatches and unmodeled disturbances, reducing dependence on highly accurate dynamic models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Planning contact-rich manipulation, particularly when involving extended robot geometry in "whole-arm manipulation," presents significant challenges in robotics. Traditional approaches often struggle with the complexity of contact dynamics, multi-contact mode transitions, and the necessity for accurate contact models. Simplifications, such as restricting interaction to end-effectors or using smoothed quasi-static/dynamic approximations, can limit robot capabilities and lead to performance degradation or failure during execution due to model mismatch. A central failure mode identified in this context is "object escape," where an object slips or reorients beyond the robot's control.

Robotic caging, a geometric concept introduced to describe situations where an object is robustly confined by surrounding robot geometry, has been explored as a means to counter object escape. Prior work on caging has primarily focused on static analysis, grasp certification, and enclosure reasoning. These formulations often rely on sampling, graph search, or certification procedures, making their integration into continuous optimization-based trajectory planning for dynamic manipulation difficult.

Separately, methods based on eikonal equations and their neural approximations have been utilized in motion planning and navigation to characterize shortest paths or traversal costs in configuration spaces. These approaches typically leverage the gradients of learned fields to guide robot motion. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have emerged as a technique for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) in a self-supervised manner, without extensive labeled data.

In contact-rich manipulation, various strategies have been employed. End-effector-centric methods simplify dynamics but limit versatility. Whole-arm manipulation, while expanding capabilities, introduces more complex multi-contact problems. Efforts to address this include reinforcement learning (which often requires extensive task-specific data and struggles with generalization) and model-based optimization with smoothed contact models (which inevitably introduce modeling errors). The need for robust planning that explicitly tolerates contact-model mismatch and real-world disturbances is thus a recognized imperative. Previous robust planning efforts have involved belief-space planning, worst-case formulations, and game-theoretic approaches, often focusing on initial state uncertainty or end-effector manipulation.

This research contributes to this landscape by introducing a novel formulation of caging that is dynamic and differentiable, explicitly designed for integration into whole-arm manipulation planning. By reformulating caging as a minimum-time escape problem and leveraging its eikonal equation characterization, the authors develop a geometric robustness primitive. This primitive is approximated by a PINN, enabling a smooth, differentiable representation that can be optimized directly within continuous whole-arm trajectory planning. This approach aims to reduce the reliance on highly accurate contact models by biasing planning towards robot configurations that geometrically resist object escape, thereby addressing the challenge of contact-model mismatch in whole-arm manipulation, a complementary focus to existing belief-space or end-effector-centric robust planning methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of whole-arm manipulation planning in contact-rich scenarios by introducing a novel, geometry-centric approach to caging. The primary motivations and specific objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Address Challenges of Contact-Rich Whole-Arm Manipulation:** The authors highlight the inherent difficulty in planning for whole-arm manipulation due to complex contact dynamics. Interactions involving extended robot geometry lead to intricate contact dynamics that are challenging to model accurately, resulting in a need for planning principles that are less dependent on such precise models.
2. **Mitigate Object Escape as a Failure Mode:** A significant practical concern in contact-rich manipulation is "object escape," where the manipulated object slips or reorients, moving out of the robot's controllable region. The research aims to develop a planning framework that explicitly guards against this failure mode.
3. **Reformulate Robotic Caging for Dynamic Planning:** Existing robotic caging formulations, while providing a geometric notion of robustness, are predominantly static or sampling-based. This characteristic makes them difficult to incorporate into continuous optimization-based manipulation planning, which requires smooth and differentiable objectives. A key objective is therefore to reformulate caging as a dynamic problem suitable for such integration.
4. **Derive a Continuous and Differentiable Robustness Metric:** The authors seek to transform the binary or discrete notion of caging into a continuous, quantitative measure of "escape difficulty." This measure should be smooth and differentiable with respect to robot-object configurations, enabling its use in gradient-based optimization.
5. **Leverage Eikonal Equations and Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs):** To achieve a continuous and differentiable escape difficulty metric, the research proposes reformulating caging as a minimum-time escape optimal control problem. It then aims to demonstrate that the resulting "escape-time field" satisfies an eikonal equation. Subsequently, the objective is to approximate this field using a PINN, thereby producing a smooth, differentiable representation without requiring extensive precomputed labels or repeated numerical solutions.
6. **Integrate Geometric Robustness into Whole-Arm Manipulation Planning:** The core objective is to embed this learned, differentiable escape-time field directly into continuous trajectory optimization problems for whole-arm manipulation. The goal is for the planning objective to favor robot configurations that maximize the object's escape time, thereby promoting geometric enclosure.
7. **Improve Robustness to Contact Model Mismatch and Disturbances:** By explicitly optimizing for geometric enclosure, the research aims to improve the robustness of manipulation plans to inevitable contact model mismatches (e.g., due to quasi-dynamic approximations, simplified object geometry, or friction errors) and unmodeled disturbances encountered during execution.
8. **Enable Planning with Simplified Contact Models:** A practical motivation is to show that, by improving intrinsic robustness through geometric caging, the planner can effectively operate with simplified, less accurate contact dynamics and geometry models, thereby making manipulation planning more tractable and computationally efficient.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this work integrates concepts from optimal control, geometric modeling, physics-informed neural networks, and trajectory optimization to achieve robust whole-arm manipulation planning. It can be categorized into three main components: Eikonal Caging Formulation, Physics-Informed Neural Approximation, and Whole-Arm Caging Manipulation Planning.

#### 3.1. Eikonal Caging Formulation

The foundation of the approach is a dynamic reformulation of robotic caging.
- **Minimum-Time Escape Problem:** Caging is defined as a minimum-time optimal control problem. For a given robot configuration $q_r$, an adversarial object (modeled as a point in configuration space, $q_o$) seeks to reach a predefined "escape region" $\Omega_g$ in the shortest possible time. The object's motion is governed by a bounded maximal velocity ($||\dot{q}_o|| \le u_{max}$), and it must remain in the collision-free configuration space ($C_{free}$) formed by the robot and environmental obstacles.
- **Escape-Time Field:** The optimal time $T(q_o; q_r)$ for the object to escape from an initial configuration $q_o$ (given robot configuration $q_r$) defines a continuous scalar field. If $T(q_o; q_r) = \infty$, the object is completely caged. Finite positive values of $T$ quantify "partial caging" or the difficulty of escape.
- **Eikonal Equation Relationship:** This escape-time field $T(q_o; q_r)$ is shown to satisfy an eikonal equation: $||\nabla T(q)|| = 1/u_{max}$ for $q \in C_{free} \setminus \Omega_g$, with boundary conditions $T(q)=0$ for $q \in \Omega_g$. This equation describes the propagation of a wavefront originating from the escape region with constant speed $u_{max}$ through the collision-free space.
- **Whole-Arm Caging Configuration Planning:** The goal is to find a robot configuration $q_r$ that maximizes this escape time $T(q_o; q_r)$ for a given object $q_o$, subject to collision avoidance ($\phi_r(q_o, q_r) \ge 0$) and robot joint limits. This is interpreted as a min-max game where the object seeks minimum escape time and the robot seeks maximum escape time.

#### 3.2. Physics-Informed Neural Approximation

To make the escape-time field tractable and differentiable for high-dimensional robot-object configuration spaces, a neural approximation is employed.
- **Collision-Free Boundary Representation:** The robot's geometry is represented using a kinematics-aware Robot Signed Distance Field (RDF). This allows efficient and differentiable computation of signed distances from any point in space to the robot's surface, defining the collision boundary $\phi_r(q_o, q_r)$.
- **Point-Based Object Approximation:** To generalize to arbitrary object shapes, the object's escape time is approximated by the average escape time of a set of $K$ key points sampled on the object's surface: $\hat{T}(q_o; q_r) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^K T_p(p_i, q_r)$. This point-based approximation is theoretically shown to be a conservative lower bound of the true rigid-body escape time, providing a valid objective for optimization.
- **Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN):** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with a non-negative activation function parameterizes $T_p(p, q_r)$, taking as input the object key point $p$ and robot configuration $q_r$. This network is trained in a self-supervised manner to satisfy the eikonal equation and boundary conditions directly through a loss function $L_{eik} = \lambda_{pde}L_{pde} + \lambda_{bc}L_{bc}$:
 - $L_{pde}$ enforces the eikonal equation using the squared difference between the gradient magnitude of $T_p$ and the inverse of a spatially varying maximum escape speed $u_{max}(p, q_r)$. This speed field slows wave propagation near the robot and blocks it in collision regions.
 - $L_{bc}$ enforces the boundary condition $T_p = 0$ for points within the escape region $\Omega_g$.
- **Sampling Strategy:** During PINN training, mini-batches of robot configurations are sampled, and training points are drawn from three streams: near the robot surface (where geometry is most restrictive), from the broader collision-free workspace, and from the outer workspace boundary (for boundary conditions).

#### 3.3. Whole-Arm Caging Manipulation Planning

The learned escape-time field is integrated into a trajectory optimization framework for dynamic manipulation.
- **Trajectory Optimization Formulation:** The problem is cast as a finite-horizon optimization: $\min_{\{u_r\}} \sum (\text{task\_cost}(q_o, q_r, u_r) - \hat{T}(q_o, q_r) + \text{control\_effort}(u_r))$, subject to robot-object dynamics, collision constraints, and joint limits. The $-\hat{T}$ term acts as a reward, encouraging configurations with high escape time.
- **Contact Dynamics Modeling:** Robot-object interaction is modeled using an optimization-based quasi-dynamic contact formulation. At each time step, given the current state and robot control input, object and robot velocities are obtained by solving a quadratic program (QP) that incorporates contact forces, friction, and compliance. This first-order model simplifies full rigid-body dynamics but captures key contact behavior.
- **Optimizer:** Trajectories are parameterized using key points over the planning horizon. CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) is used for optimization, treating the learned escape-time field as a black-box objective.
- **Robustness Evaluation:** The methodology includes extensive evaluation in simulation (planar and realistic Franka arm) and real-world experiments. This involves assessing performance under random object motion disturbances and systematic contact-model mismatches (e.g., dynamics order, object shape under-approximation, friction parameter variations). The "whole-arm contact" baseline is used to isolate the contribution of the escape-time objective from the general benefits of whole-arm engagement. Real-world experiments use open-loop execution to assess sim-to-real transfer and robustness to multiple simultaneous mismatches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research produced several findings related to the formulation, learning, and application of physics-informed eikonal caging for whole-arm manipulation.

#### 4.1. Eikonal Caging Formulation and Neural Approximation

- **Unified Caging Metric:** The reformulation of robotic caging as a minimum-time escape problem yields a continuous escape-time field that satisfies an eikonal equation. This field provides a unified metric for both complete caging (infinite escape time) and partial caging (finite escape time, quantifying escape difficulty).
- **PINN Accuracy:** The Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) successfully approximates this escape-time field. Comparisons with ground-truth solutions obtained via the Fast Marching Method (FMM) demonstrate that the learned model accurately captures both scalar field values and the underlying geometric structure of optimal escape paths, for both simplified planar arms and realistic Franka arm geometries.
- **Differentiability for Optimization:** The PINN produces a smooth and differentiable representation of the escape-time field. This characteristic is crucial, as it allows the metric to be embedded directly into continuous optimization problems for robot configurations and trajectories.
- **Point-Based Approximation Validity:** The use of point-based object approximation for the escape-time field is theoretically shown to be a conservative lower bound for the true rigid-body escape time, validating its practical use as an objective for optimization.

#### 4.2. Whole-Arm Caging Configuration Planning

- **Effective Enclosure Generation:** Optimization of the learned escape-time field successfully biases the robot toward enclosing configurations. Both gradient-free (CMA-ES) and gradient-based (SQP) optimizers converged to arm postures that effectively wrapped around objects, maximizing the predicted escape time.
- **Transfer to Realistic Geometries:** The method effectively identifies whole-arm caging configurations for a Franka arm manipulating realistic everyday objects (e.g., water jug, box, kitchen paper pack). The optimizer consistently found configurations that utilized the full arm geometry to obstruct escape routes, demonstrating its scalability beyond simple primitives.

#### 4.3. Whole-Arm Caging Manipulation (Simulation Studies)

- **Enhanced Coordinated Whole-Arm Use:** Compared to baselines (minimizing minimum distance or average distance), the caging-aware objective promoted sustained, coordinated contact across multiple robot links. This led to more consistent geometric enclosure throughout manipulation rather than localized contact or transient proximity.
- **Superior Robustness to Random Disturbances:** The caging-aware planner exhibited significantly higher robustness to random object motion disturbances. Object trajectories remained tightly concentrated around the nominal path, and final object positions were consistently closer to the goal, indicating that the induced geometric enclosure effectively mitigated the effects of unmodeled perturbations.
- **Increased Tolerance to Contact-Model Mismatch:**
 - **Dynamics Order Mismatch:** Trajectories planned using a simplified quasi-dynamic model with the caging objective remained effective when executed under more complex second-order MuJoCo dynamics, showing reduced sensitivity to unmodeled inertial and transient effects.
 - **Object Shape Under-approximation:** The method demonstrated robustness to planning with a simplified circular object model and executing with polygonal objects (octagon, hexagon, square) of increasing geometric discrepancy. Trajectories remained stable, suggesting that geometric enclosure can compensate for systematic shape modeling errors.
 - **Friction Mismatch:** Plans generated with a nominal friction coefficient maintained task-level performance when executed with varying friction coefficients, indicating reduced sensitivity to friction mis-specification.
- **Isolation of Caging Contribution:** Comparisons between a "Whole-Arm Contact" baseline (using full arm geometry but optimizing for minimal distance) and "Whole-Arm Caging" (using full arm geometry and optimizing escape time) showed that the caging objective provides additional, significant robustness benefits beyond merely increasing contact area.
- **Trade-off in Trajectory Tracking:** In trajectory-tracking tasks where the task objective could conflict with maximizing enclosure, the robot temporarily relaxed enclosure to satisfy tracking requirements. Despite this, the inherent robustness provided by the caging objective ensured that object trajectories remained bounded under disturbances and model mismatches, allowing for successful tracking.

#### 4.4. Simulation-to-Real Transfer

- **Successful Open-Loop Transfer:** Trajectories optimized in simulation using the simplified quasi-dynamic contact model successfully transferred to open-loop execution on a physical Franka Panda arm. This occurred despite the presence of multiple simultaneous contact-model mismatches inherent in the real system (e.g., initial object placement errors, differences between quasi-dynamic and true second-order dynamics, uncalibrated friction, and shape approximations).
- **Robustness to Severe Mismatches:** The physical robot demonstrated robust manipulation even under progressively more severe geometric discrepancies, including the introduction of an additional, unmodeled object. The manipulated object was consistently pushed into the goal region across all tested real-world mismatch scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel, geometry-centric approach to robustness in whole-arm manipulation, offering several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Reduced Reliance on Exact Contact Models:** A primary impact is the demonstration that explicitly optimizing for geometric enclosure can significantly mitigate the need for highly accurate and complex contact dynamics models during planning. This allows for effective planning even with simplified, first-order quasi-dynamic models, which are computationally less demanding and less prone to numerical instabilities associated with hybrid, non-smooth dynamics. This simplification can enable faster planning and potentially real-time adaptation in complex scenarios.

2. **Enhanced Robustness to Uncertainty and Disturbances:** The proposed escape-time metric directly quantifies the geometric difficulty of object escape. By biasing robot trajectories towards configurations that maximize this metric, the system gains intrinsic robustness to unmodeled object motion disturbances, variations in contact dynamics parameters (like friction), and geometric mismatches between planning models and physical realities. This is crucial for reliable robot operation in unstructured and unpredictable environments.

3. **Expanded Whole-Arm Manipulation Capabilities:** The framework provides a practical and differentiable objective for leveraging the full robot arm geometry for manipulation. This moves beyond the limitations of end-effector-centric approaches, enabling robots to handle larger, heavier, or more irregularly shaped objects through distributed contact. The ability to plan for coordinated whole-arm enclosure opens up new possibilities for dexterous and versatile robotic manipulation.

4. **Complementary Robustness Paradigm:** The work presents a geometric robustness primitive that complements existing robust planning approaches such as belief-space planning or robust model predictive control. Instead of explicitly modeling uncertainty distributions or optimizing against fixed parameter sets, it focuses on shaping the robot's physical interaction geometry to make escape inherently difficult. This could be integrated with feedback control or belief-space methods to achieve even greater robustness in diverse and challenging manipulation tasks.

5. **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful open-loop transfer of manipulation trajectories from simulation to a real robotic system, even under multiple simultaneous model mismatches, highlights the practical utility of this approach. This indicates that planning with an escape-time objective can bridge the simulation-to-reality gap more effectively by building in resilience to inevitable discrepancies.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework lays a foundation for several future research directions:
 - **3D Manipulation with Anisotropic Models:** The current planar focus can be extended to three-dimensional manipulation by incorporating anisotropic eikonal formulations, which can model gravity and other direction-dependent mobility factors, leading to gravity-aware 3D whole-arm caging.
 - **Integration with Advanced Control:** Combining the escape-time objective with Model Predictive Control (MPC) or differentiable simulation for closed-loop policies could further enhance robustness in dynamic and interactive settings.
 - **Simplified Heuristics:** Future work might explore simpler, more computationally efficient heuristic objectives that approximate the benefits of full eikonal caging while retaining similar robustness properties.
 - **Multi-Object and Clutter Scenarios:** Extending the caging concept to multi-object manipulation or cluttered environments, possibly with occlusions, could enable robots to perform more complex tasks.

In summary, this research contributes a principled and practical method for inducing geometric robustness in whole-arm manipulation, with the potential to significantly simplify planning, improve reliability, and expand the capabilities of robots in real-world applications.
