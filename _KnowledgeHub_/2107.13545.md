# Fully Autonomous Real-World Reinforcement Learning with Applications to Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Berkeley AI Research developed Reinforcement Learning for Mobile Manipulation (ReLMM), a system that enables mobile manipulators to autonomously learn combined navigation and grasping skills directly in real-world, uninstrumented environments. The system achieved 88-93% object collection rates in diverse office settings after 25-50 hours of autonomous training, outperforming scripted and random baselines.

## Problem

- Existing real-world robotic reinforcement learning often requires extensive instrumentation, human supervision for episodic resets, or pre-computed maps, limiting deployment in uninstrumented, open-world environments.
- Achieving full autonomy and continuous learning for mobile manipulation is challenging, as it requires balancing navigation and interaction in dynamic settings without human intervention.
- Learning combined navigation and manipulation from ego-centric sensing faces a "chicken-and-egg" problem, where an untrained manipulation policy provides poor feedback for navigation, and vice-versa.

## Method

- A modular policy architecture (ReLMM) decomposes the control problem into an ensemble grasping policy and a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) trained navigation policy, both continually learning from onboard sensors.
- Uncertainty-driven exploration guides the grasping policy by sampling actions from a Boltzmann distribution derived from optimistic estimates of grasp success and ensemble variance.
- Autonomous "pseudo-resets" enable continuous, reset-free learning by having the robot perform random navigation while holding an object, then placing it and navigating away, to reconfigure the environment.

## Results

- ReLMM, using a stationary curriculum, achieved 88-**93%** object collection rates in diverse real-world office environments after 25-50 hours of autonomous training, consistently outperforming hand-coded and random baselines.
- The system demonstrated continual improvement with extended training, showing its capacity for lifelong learning and adaptation beyond initial deployment.
- Simulation ablations confirmed the necessity of hierarchical decomposition, joint policy training, and uncertainty-based exploration for efficient learning, with a discretized grasping policy training over twice as fast.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modular decomposition of mobile manipulation into distinct navigation and grasping policies significantly improves training performance and manageability of the large exploration space.
> - Uncertainty-based exploration, utilizing an ensemble of grasping policies, is effective for efficient discovery of successful manipulation strategies in real-world sparse reward settings.
> - Autonomous pseudo-resets provide a practical mechanism for continuous data collection and environment rearrangement, eliminating the need for human intervention and supporting varied training experiences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively exploring learning-based approaches, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), to enable robots to operate in uninstrumented, open-world environments. This is especially pertinent for mobile manipulators, which require both navigation and object interaction capabilities in dynamic settings like homes and offices.

A significant challenge in real-world robotic RL is the practical difficulty of deploying and training systems autonomously. Existing real-world RL systems often necessitate extensive instrumentation (e.g., AR tags, external cameras for state estimation), human supervision for task monitoring and resets between trials, or pre-computed maps and object poses. These requirements limit their applicability to controlled laboratory settings and prevent continuous, autonomous learning in natural environments.

Prior work has explored mobile manipulation, but often relies on human effort, hand-coded controllers, demonstrations, or external instrumentation. Simulation-based approaches for mobile manipulation have achieved success but often require millions of timesteps to train and may not directly transfer to the complexities of the real world. For real-world learning, existing efforts have focused either on large-scale navigation tasks without manipulation or on stationary manipulation tasks (e.g., grasping in a bin) without mobility. Some works have addressed reset-free learning in stationary manipulation or simulation, but extending this to mobile manipulation in the real world remains an area of active research.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the gap of fully autonomous, reset-free, real-world reinforcement learning for mobile manipulation. It aims to develop a system that can learn complex skills combining navigation and grasping solely from on-board sensors, without external support or human intervention, thereby enabling continual learning in realistic, uninstrumented environments. This contrasts with methods that achieve partial autonomy or focus on only one aspect (navigation or manipulation) in isolation, or those that require extensive data from simulated environments that are difficult to replicate in the real world.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to devise a robotic reinforcement learning system capable of learning combined navigation and manipulation skills autonomously in the real world. The authors aim to achieve this without requiring human intervention, extensive environment instrumentation, or access to privileged information such as global maps or precise object positions.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several practical and theoretical challenges in current robotic reinforcement learning:
1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap and Open-World Deployment:** Current RL systems frequently require highly controlled, instrumented environments or extensive simulation for training. The authors argue that for robots to operate effectively in open-world settings like homes and offices, they must learn directly in these environments without specialized infrastructure. This capability is crucial for mobile manipulators that need to interact with objects while navigating dynamic spaces.
2. **Achieving Full Autonomy and Continual Learning:** Many real-world RL deployments are episodic, requiring human intervention for task resets (e.g., repositioning objects or the robot) or monitoring. This significantly hinders the scalability and practicality of deploying such systems for continuous operation. The researchers are motivated to create a system where a robot can be placed in an environment, initiate learning, and continuously improve its skills over its lifetime without human oversight. This implies the need for autonomous data collection, self-evaluation, and environment rearrangement.
3. **Efficiency for Real-World Training:** Learning in the real world is inherently sample-inefficient and time-consuming. The authors emphasize the need for a system that can learn complex mobile manipulation skills within a practical timeframe (e.g., days, not months or years of robot operation) to make real-world deployment feasible.
4. **Learning from Ego-Centric Sensing:** To avoid external instrumentation, the system must rely entirely on the robot's onboard sensors (e.g., ego-centric cameras) for both action selection and reward computation. This limits the available information compared to systems that use overhead cameras or oracle state information.
5. **Addressing the "Chicken-and-Egg" Problem in Hierarchical Tasks:** For tasks involving both navigation and manipulation, an untrained manipulation policy provides poor feedback for the navigation policy, and vice-versa. This mutual dependency makes end-to-end learning challenging. The authors aim to address this by designing a robust system that can bootstrap and stabilize the joint training of these interdependent skills.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to advance the state of real-world robotic reinforcement learning towards a paradigm of "drop-in, learn-and-improve" robots that can perpetually adapt and acquire new skills in uninstrumented, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system, named Reinforcement Learning for Mobile Manipulation (ReLMM), is designed to enable fully autonomous, real-world learning for tasks involving navigation and grasping. It addresses the challenges of real-world RL through a modular policy design, uncertainty-driven exploration, autonomous pseudo-resets, and curriculum learning.

#### 3.1. Modularized Policy Architecture

ReLMM decomposes the control problem into two main components: a **grasping policy** ($\pi_g$) and a **navigation policy** ($\pi_n$). Both policies are implemented as neural networks and are continuously trained together with the shared objective of successfully grasping objects.
- **Grasping Policy ($\pi_g$):** This policy is responsible for, given an image observation ($o_g$), predicting the likelihood of grasp success and selecting an appropriate grasping action ($a_g$). The grasping action space is discretized into a 15x15 grid in the x-y plane, simplifying the control problem to a single-step, top-down action. An ensemble of $M=6$ independent grasp policies ($G_1, \ldots, G_M$) is trained using a cross-entropy loss (Equation 1), where $r_g$ is 1 for a successful grasp and 0 otherwise. Successful grasping is determined by presenting the gripper to the onboard camera.
 - **Uncertainty-Based Exploration:** To facilitate efficient exploration for grasping, the system constructs a Boltzmann distribution for action sampling. This distribution is derived from optimistic estimates of grasp success, which combine the mean prediction of the ensemble with a multiple of its variance (Equation 2). This formulation encourages exploration of actions where the ensemble is less certain, thereby discovering new successful grasp configurations.
- **Navigation Policy ($\pi_n$):** This policy controls the forward and turn velocities of the mobile robot base ($a_n$) based on a full RGB image observation ($o$). At each timestep, the system decides whether to attempt a grasp or continue navigating. This decision is probabilistic, based on the maximum optimistic grasp success probability predicted by the grasping ensemble (Equation 3). If a grasp is attempted and successful, a reward is provided to the navigation agent.
 - **Reward Function for Navigation:** Two options for navigation rewards ($r_n$) are explored:
 1. Direct optimization for task success: $r_n(o) = r_g(o) - 1$, where $r_g$ is the actual grasp success (1 for success, 0 for failure).
 2. Reward for reaching states where the grasp ensemble *would choose* to grasp: $r_n(o) = (P[\text{grasp}|o] - 1)$. This reward depends on the grasp ensemble's prediction, not actual success, and is used to relabel states for policy updates, potentially improving sample efficiency.
 - **Training Algorithm:** The navigation policy is trained using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm known for its sample efficiency.

#### 3.2. Autonomous Pseudo-Resets

To eliminate the need for human intervention for episodic resets, ReLMM implements an automated "pseudo-reset" mechanism. After a successful grasp, instead of dropping the object in place (which could lead to local optima), the robot performs random navigation actions while holding the object. It then places the object down in this new, randomly chosen location and navigates randomly away. This process effectively rearranges the environment, continually creating new object configurations for the agent to practice on, thus ensuring varied training experiences and preventing stagnation. The overall training process integrating these components is summarized in Algorithm 2.

#### 3.3. Training Curricula

To address the "chicken-and-egg" problem where an untrained grasping policy provides poor rewards for navigation, two types of curricula are designed:
- **Stationary Curriculum:** Initially, a single object is placed in front of the robot. The robot practices grasping it for $N_{st}=2000$ steps (Algorithm 1 with $st=\text{True}$). After each successful grasp, the robot randomly places the object nearby. This phase requires occasional human intervention (approximately 5% of the time) if the object moves out of reach. This curriculum is designed for time efficiency in bootstrapping the grasping policy.
- **Autonomous Curriculum:** For fully autonomous learning, this curriculum prioritizes collecting grasping data early. After each navigation step, the robot attempts a predefined number of grasps ($N_{grasp}$). If a grasp is successful, the robot continues to practice with that object until $N_{stop}$ unsuccessful grasps occur. This initial phase continues until $N_{max}$ total grasps are completed. This curriculum aims for maximum autonomy, potentially at the cost of increased training time. (Detailed in Algorithms 3 and 4 in Appendix B).

#### 3.4. Robotic System and Sensors

The system uses a small-scale, low-cost mobile manipulation platform based on the LocoBot design, consisting of an iClebo Kobuki mobile base and a WidowX200 5-DoF arm. The robot relies on an Intel RealSense D435 camera (providing RGB and depth) for observations and bump sensors on the base. An onboard Intel NUC is used for local computation, with wireless connectivity to a server for data processing and training. Safety during autonomous operation is supported by the robot's physical characteristics (small, light, arm auto-stop on resistance) and a depth camera-based collision avoidance system that rotates the base if obstacles are detected in the graspable area. The robot learns in real-world office spaces with varied lighting and textures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ReLMM through real-world experiments and simulation ablations, focusing on its autonomy, the effectiveness of its hierarchical control, and its performance compared to other methods.

#### 4.1. Real-World Evaluation

ReLMM was evaluated on a room cleanup task, where the robot navigates to and picks up scattered items. The evaluation metric was the percentage of objects collected within a 15-minute period, normalized by room surface area. Experiments were conducted in four distinct real-world environments differing in size, layout, obstacles, and object types.
- **Autonomous Learning Capability:** ReLMM demonstrated the ability to learn mobile manipulation skills autonomously in real-world environments.
 - **ReLMM-StatCurr:** Using the stationary curriculum, the system achieved high collection rates (88% in "no obst" to 93% in "obst" rooms) after 25-50 hours of autonomous training (Table 1, Table 2). This curriculum required minimal human intervention for occasional object repositioning.
 - **ReLMM-AutoCurr:** The fully autonomous curriculum achieved comparable performance (77% in "no obst" and 75% in "diverse") but required longer training times (63-99 hours) due to its more conservative exploration strategy (Table 1, Table 2).
- **Performance Against Baselines:**
 - **Random Baselines (Rand all, Rand nav):** Both performed poorly (2-52% collection rates), highlighting the necessity of learned, directed navigation behavior rather than random movement (Table 1). The Rand nav baseline, which used a pre-trained grasp policy but random navigation, demonstrated that effective grasping alone is insufficient without intelligent navigation.
 - **Scripted Baseline:** A hand-coded controller specifically engineered for the task achieved reasonable performance (56-88%). However, ReLMM-StatCurr consistently outperformed this baseline in all tested environments, and crucially, the scripted controller cannot autonomously improve (Table 1). This was particularly evident in environments with diverse objects and challenging backgrounds (e.g., "diverse" and "obst+rugs"), where ReLMM adapted to identify and grasp objects more effectively.
- **Continual Improvement:** The evaluation of ReLMM-StatCurr in the "obst+rugs" room showed that performance steadily improved with extended training (up to 50 hours), indicating the system's capacity for lifelong learning and continuous improvement beyond initial deployment (Figure 5).

#### 4.2. Simulation Analysis and Ablation Studies

Detailed ablations in a PyBullet simulation provided insights into the design choices:
- **Hierarchical Decomposition:** Training a single, joint policy for both navigation and grasping resulted in a failure to make progress, indicating that the modular decomposition used by ReLMM significantly improves training performance and addresses the large exploration space (Figure 6, Right).
- **Joint Training of Policies:** Freezing the grasp policy after initial pre-training ("Pretrain Online") and only training navigation led to poorer performance compared to training both policies concurrently. This demonstrates the interdependent relationship and benefits of joint optimization for navigation and grasping (Figure 6, Right).
- **Uncertainty-Based Exploration:** Removing the uncertainty bonus (setting $\beta=0$) from the grasping policy led to significantly poorer performance, confirming its role in efficient exploration for grasping (Figure 6, Right).
- **Curriculum Learning and Reward Relabeling:**
 - A strong final navigation and grasping policy could be learned with only 500 stationary grasps, especially when applying reward relabeling to the navigation rewards (Figure 6, Left).
 - The autonomous curriculum, with properly tuned hyperparameters, approached the sample efficiency of the stationary curriculum, offering a pathway to fully automated training (Figure 6, Center).
 - Reward relabeling, which rewards navigation for reaching states where the grasp ensemble *predicts* a grasp, improved learning speed and final policy quality, particularly in environments with diverse objects (Figure 9, Left).
- **Discrete vs. Continuous Action Space:** A discretized grasping policy was found to train more than twice as fast as a continuous one, supporting the design choice for simplifying the grasping control (Figure 8, Left).
- **Reset Mechanism Comparison:** ReLMM's simpler pseudo-reset method was found to be as effective or more effective than a prior learning-based perturbation method for reset-free learning, without requiring additional learning complexity (Figure 6, Right).
- **Comparison to HRL4IN:** In a simulation comparison, ReLMM achieved strong performance (90% objects collected) in approximately 30,000 environment steps. In contrast, HRL4IN, a hierarchical RL method, required around 30 million environment steps (1000x more) to achieve good performance in a dense reward environment, highlighting ReLMM's significant sample efficiency for sparse real-world tasks (Appendix D.2).

#### 4.3. Additional Tasks

The ReLMM system's generality was demonstrated by its adaptability to a simulated pick-and-place task. By modifying the reward structure to reward placing objects on designated target areas, the system could learn this more complex mobile manipulation task (Figure 7).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper, ReLMM, offers a significant contribution to the field of robotic reinforcement learning, particularly in the context of mobile manipulation in real-world environments.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Autonomous Robotic Learning

The primary significance lies in demonstrating a system that can autonomously learn complex mobile manipulation skills (combining navigation and grasping) directly on real hardware, without reliance on external instrumentation, human intervention for resets, or privileged information. This addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic RL, moving towards systems that can operate and learn in open-world settings.

#### 5.2. Enabling Continual and Lifelong Learning

The system's ability to continuously improve with more training, as shown in the real-world experiments, suggests its potential for lifelong learning. Robots deployed in real-world settings could perpetually refine their skills, adapt to changes in their environment, and acquire new capabilities over their operational lifetime, rather than being limited by a fixed, pre-trained policy.

#### 5.3. Practicality and Accessibility

By minimizing the need for specialized laboratory setups and human oversight, ReLMM enhances the practicality and accessibility of real-world robot learning. This could enable wider deployment of learning-based robotic systems in less structured environments like homes, offices, or warehouses, making advanced robotic capabilities more accessible to a broader range of users and applications.

#### 5.4. Design Principles for Real-World RL

The paper provides a set of validated design decisions for real-world reinforcement learning:
- **Modular Policy Decomposition:** The hierarchical structure separating navigation and grasping proves effective for managing the complexity of joint action spaces and improving sample efficiency.
- **Uncertainty-Driven Exploration:** Leveraging ensemble uncertainty for efficient exploration in grasping helps the robot discover successful manipulation strategies more quickly.
- **Autonomous Pseudo-Resets:** The method for automatically rearranging the environment after successful interactions offers a practical solution to the challenge of episodic resets, allowing for continuous data collection without human involvement.
- **Curriculum Learning:** The use of both stationary and autonomous curricula demonstrates effective strategies for bootstrapping and stabilizing the training of interdependent skills in sparse reward environments.

#### 5.5. Future Directions

The success of ReLMM opens avenues for future research, including extending the system to more complex and diverse manipulation tasks beyond simple grasping, incorporating more sophisticated forms of interaction with the environment, and exploring long-term autonomy in even more varied and dynamic real-world settings.

In summary, ReLMM represents a step towards truly autonomous, self-improving robotic systems that can learn and adapt directly from their real-world experiences, holding implications for the widespread adoption of intelligent mobile manipulators.
