# When Does Perceptual Alignment Benefit Vision Representations?

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from MIT and collaborators investigates how fine-tuning large vision models with human perceptual judgments influences their general-purpose representations. It demonstrates that aligning models with mid-level human similarity data consistently improves performance across a diverse range of downstream tasks, including dense prediction, retrieval-augmented generation, object counting, and instance retrieval.

## Problem

- Despite advancements in large-scale vision models, their representations often misalign with human perceptual judgments, particularly regarding the weighting of various visual attributes.
- The broader utility of aligning vision models with human feedback for general-purpose representations remains unclear, with previous work often showing task-specific benefits or potential representation distortion.
- Different categories of human preference labels (low-level, mid-level, high-level) yield distinct learning signals, and it's not well understood which are most effective for broad applicability.

## Method

- A "==second pretraining stage==" is proposed, fine-tuning feature representations from state-of-the-art Vision Transformer (ViT) backbones like DINO, DINOv2, and CLIP.
- ==Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)== is used to fine-tune models on the NIGHTS dataset, which provides 20,000 human-annotated image triplets focused on mid-level perceptual similarity.
- The approach utilizes a ==triplet loss== formulation, applying it to both global image-level (CLS token) and aggregated patch-level features to adapt representations.

## Results

- Human-aligned models consistently improved performance across dense prediction tasks: DINO-HA showed boosts in **16 of 20** cases for semantic segmentation and outperformed base DINO in **27 of 36** depth estimation cases.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) classification accuracy of VLMs improved when using examples retrieved by human-aligned backbones across varied data domains.
- Human-aligned versions of six different backbones outperformed their original counterparts in **35 out of 36** cases for object counting, and showed significant gains in instance retrieval on DeepFashion2 (e.g., Ensemble-HA Top-1 recall of **23.39%** vs. **13.54%** for base Ensemble).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aligning vision models with human *mid-level* perceptual judgments provides a beneficial inductive bias, leading to more robust and versatile general-purpose representations.
> - The benefits of perceptual alignment extend to local feature-dependent tasks like dense prediction, even when trained with global image-level similarity annotations, by effectively propagating human biases to patch tokens.
> - Not all types of human feedback are equally beneficial; mid-level perceptual variations (NIGHTS) are more effective for generalization than low-level pixel changes or high-level conceptual similarities, and excessive fine-tuning can lead to overfitting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Contemporary computer vision has witnessed significant advancements through the development of large-scale pretrained models, such as CLIP, DINO, and DINOv2. These models, often trained using self-supervised learning, contrastive learning, or image-text pairing, have demonstrated the ability to learn rich, multipurpose image representations that generalize across various visual tasks. This success has led to a growing interest in further enhancing these representations by aligning them with human perception.

The concept of aligning models with human feedback has precedents in other domains, notably in large language models (LLMs) with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which has resulted in more helpful and robust models. In vision, similar trends are emerging, with efforts directed at incorporating human perceptual judgments to improve aesthetic qualities in image generation, enhance retrieval capabilities, and boost the performance of vision-language models on specific downstream tasks. Previous studies have explored the use of human preference data for alignment, particularly focusing on image similarity judgments, which are considered a stable measure of human visual perception.

However, the existing research indicates a nuanced understanding of human alignment's benefits. While it is acknowledged that alignment can enable specific objectives (e.g., predicting image similarity), its utility in fostering *general-purpose* representations remains less clear. Some work suggests that naive incorporation of human perceptual knowledge can distort representations, necessitating strong regularization to preserve downstream performance. Furthermore, the type of objective function and training data, rather than architecture or model size, appear to be critical for effective alignment. Different categories of human preference labels—such as low-level pixel variations versus mid-level perceptual similarity or high-level semantic associations—also produce distinct learning signals, each benefiting different specific tasks.

This paper addresses the gap in understanding whether aligning models to human perceptual judgments results in more general-purpose representations that benefit a wide array of visual understanding tasks, rather than just improving performance on the similarity prediction task itself. It investigates if human-aligned representations, like human perception, lead to broader improvements in visual capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the observation that human perception of visual similarity is a complex process, influenced by a diverse range of attributes including scene layout, object location, camera pose, color, and semantic content. While advanced vision models have developed a broad understanding of semantic abstractions, they often fail to appropriately weigh these varied attributes, leading to inferences that can be misaligned with human perceptual judgments. This misalignment suggests a potential area for improvement in current vision representation learning.

The key objectives of this study are:

1. **Investigate the impact of perceptual alignment on general-purpose vision representations**: The primary goal is to determine how fine-tuning vision model representations on human perceptual judgments affects their utility across a diverse spectrum of computer vision tasks. This moves beyond merely improving performance on similarity prediction and assesses the broader applicability of such aligned representations.
2. **Evaluate whether human alignment provides a beneficial inductive bias**: The research aims to ascertain if leveraging human-provided similarity labels acts as a positive inductive bias that enhances model performance across tasks, or if such alignment might compromise the model’s original representational power by diverting it towards a specialized task.
3. **Identify the scope of benefits**: The study seeks to determine if human-aligned representations offer improvements primarily in tasks directly related to similarity judgments, or if they generalize to tasks requiring diverse notions of visual understanding, such as dense prediction, counting, and retrieval-augmented generation.
4. **Explore the optimal level of perceptual detail for alignment**: Through ablation studies, the authors aim to identify what type of human similarity annotation (e.g., low-level, mid-level, or high-level semantic) is most effective in yielding beneficial general-purpose representations.

The overarching hypothesis guiding this work is that introducing an inductive bias derived from human perceptual knowledge into vision models can lead to the development of more robust and broadly applicable representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a "second pretraining stage" where feature representations from large, existing vision models are fine-tuned to align with human perceptual judgments.

**Vision Model Backbones**: The study employs several state-of-the-art Vision Transformer (ViT) backbones, specifically the base-sized (ViT-B) versions of DINO, DINOv2, CLIP, OpenCLIP, and SynCLR. Additionally, an "ensemble" model combining features from DINO, CLIP, and OpenCLIP is used. For DINO, DINOv2, MAE, and SynCLR, the CLS token from the final layer is utilized, with specific handling for layer normalization. For CLIP and OpenCLIP, representations from the image encoder are used.

**Human Similarity Annotations**: The primary dataset for fine-tuning is NIGHTS, which comprises 20,000 synthetically generated image triplets. Each triplet is annotated with two alternative forced-choice human similarity judgments, gathered with strict consensus (6-10 unanimous ratings) to minimize ambiguity. Crucially, images within these triplets share similar semantic content but vary in mid-level attributes such as pose, layout, shape, color, and object count. This design ensures that the perceptual judgments reflect shared visual appearance properties rather than high-level semantic knowledge.

**Fine-tuning Strategy**: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is used to fine-tune the model parameters. This method was selected for its efficiency and demonstrated effectiveness in achieving alignment.

**Objective Functions**:
1. **Image-level Objective**: For global image understanding tasks, the model `f_θ` is fine-tuned using a triplet loss formulation. Given a reference image `x` and two variation images `x̃₀` and `x̃₁`, where `y ∈ {0, 1}` indicates which variation is more similar to `x`, the objective minimizes the cosine distance between representations of the more similar pair (`x`, `x̃_y`) and maximizes the distance for the less similar pair (`x`, `x̃_(1-y)`). The loss function is `L_alignment(θ) = max(0, m - ∆d · ȳ)`, where `∆d` is the difference in cosine distances between the pairs, `ȳ` maps `y` to `{-1, 1}`, and `m` is a margin set to 0.05.
2. **Patch-level Objective**: For dense prediction tasks that require local feature understanding, a modified objective is employed. While NIGHTS provides global image-level annotations, these judgments often arise from local attributes. To propagate this global human annotation to individual patch tokens, the approach involves extracting both the global CLS token and spatial patch embeddings from the Vision Transformer backbones. The patch embeddings are spatially average-pooled, and then concatenated with the CLS token. The same alignment loss (Equation 2) is applied to these concatenated features `cat[CLS, pool(PATCH)]`, resulting in a feature vector of double the original dimension. This objective is specifically used for experiments requiring local feature awareness.

**Implementation Details**: Models are trained for 8 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0003 and a batch size of 16. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is selected for evaluation.

**Evaluation Benchmarks**: The fine-tuned models (termed "human-aligned") are evaluated against their original pretrained backbones on a diverse set of downstream computer vision tasks:
- **Dense Prediction**:
 - **Semantic Segmentation**: A single linear layer is trained on frozen, perceptually-aligned CLS and averaged-pooled patch tokens. Evaluated on Pascal VOC, ADE20k, Cityscapes, COCO, and DAVIS2017 using mIoU and Pixel Accuracy.
 - **Depth Estimation**: A linear layer is trained on frozen patch tokens to predict a depth map using a scale-invariant log loss and gradient-matching term. Evaluated on NYUv2, SUN-RGBD, and for out-of-distribution generalization on the 4D Light Field dataset. Metrics include RMSE, AbsRel, log10, and δ accuracy thresholds.
- **Retrieval-augmented Generation (RAG)**: Human-aligned backbones are used to retrieve few-shot examples for large vision-language models (VLMs), specifically OpenFlamingo (and IDEFICS2 in appendix), in a classification setting. Classification accuracy on various VTAB datasets (e.g., DMLab, Diabetic Retinopathy) is measured.
- **Counting**: k-Nearest Neighbors accuracy on frozen representations is used to evaluate performance on object counting tasks, utilizing FSC147, CARPK, and Clevr-Count datasets. Metrics are Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE).
- **Instance Retrieval**: Evaluated on the DeepFashion2 dataset's Consumer-to-Shop benchmark, assessing the ability to retrieve images of shared subjects under varying conditions using cosine similarity. Top-1, Top-3, and Top-5 recall scores are reported.

**Ablation Studies**:
- **Type of Human Similarity Annotation**: To investigate if performance gains are due to data quantity or the perceptual qualities of NIGHTS, the fine-tuning process is replicated using three other image triplet datasets of equivalent size (13,900 triplets): BAPPS (low-level distortions), THINGS (high-level conceptual similarity), and a specially constructed ImageNet dataset (class-based similarity).
- **Training Steps**: The effect of the extent of alignment is studied by varying the number of fine-tuning steps on DeepFashion2.
- **Perceptual Alignment for CNNs**: The benefits of perceptual alignment are also assessed on CNN architectures (ResNet50 and ConvNeXt-B) by training MLPs on their frozen final layer features, as LoRA implementations for CNNs were not readily available.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the benefits of aligning vision model representations to human perceptual judgments:

**Widespread Benefits Across Diverse Tasks**:
The study consistently demonstrated that human-aligned models, fine-tuned on NIGHTS, generally improved performance over their original pretrained backbones across a variety of downstream computer vision tasks. This suggests that perceptual alignment contributes to more general-purpose representations beyond mere similarity prediction.

**Dense Prediction Tasks**:
- **Semantic Segmentation**: Human-aligned DINO (DINO-HA) and DINOv2 (DINOv2-HA) largely outperformed their non-aligned counterparts. DINO-HA showed performance boosts in 16 out of 20 cases across tested datasets (Pascal VOC, ADE20k, Cityscapes, COCO, DAVIS2017), often achieving the highest mIoU and Pixel Accuracy. DINOv2-HA also improved on COCO and DAVIS2017. The propagation of global image-level human similarity annotations to ViT patch tokens proved beneficial for these dense prediction tasks.
- **Depth Estimation**: Human-aligned models outperformed base models in 27 out of 36 cases across NYUv2, SUN-RGBD, and a cross-domain evaluation (NYUv2 to 4D Light Field). DINO-HA consistently surpassed base DINO on all metrics and datasets, frequently achieving the highest performance overall. These results indicated strong generalization capabilities of human-aligned models, even on out-of-distribution domains.

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**:
Classification accuracy of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like OpenFlamingo (and IDEFICS2 in supplementary experiments) improved when using in-context examples retrieved by human-aligned backbones. This was observed across varied data domains, including specialized ones like medical imagery (Diabetic Retinopathy) and 2D renders (DMLab). This finding suggests that human-aligned models retrieve more informative examples, thereby enhancing the few-shot generalization of downstream VLMs.

**Object Counting**:
Across six different backbones (DINO, DINOv2, CLIP, OpenCLIP, SynCLR, and Ensemble), the human-aligned versions outperformed their original counterparts in 35 out of 36 cases (MAE and RMSE on FSC147, CARPK, and Clevr-Count). This improvement was notable given that NIGHTS comprises image-level similarity judgments, while counting requires local object awareness. The authors hypothesize this is due to the prevalence of triplets with varying object counts within the NIGHTS dataset, implicitly embedding counting awareness into the global labels.

**Instance Retrieval**:
Human-aligned models demonstrated significant performance gains over base models on the DeepFashion2 dataset (Consumer-to-Shop benchmark), with improvements across Top-1, Top-3, and Top-5 recall scores for all backbones. For example, the Ensemble-HA achieved a Top-1 recall of 23.39%, compared to 13.54% for the base Ensemble. This finding aligns with prior work suggesting that training on NIGHTS improves retrieval of similar images.

**Ablation Study: Type of Human Similarity Annotation**:
The study investigated whether the observed performance gains were due to the specific perceptual qualities of NIGHTS or merely additional training data. The results indicated that fine-tuning on NIGHTS, a dataset of mid-level perceptual judgments, yielded the largest improvements across counting and instance retrieval tasks. In contrast, fine-tuning on THINGS (high-level conceptual similarity) generally worsened performance, while BAPPS (low-level pixel variations) and ImageNet (class boundaries) had minimal impact. This pattern was also consistent for semantic segmentation and depth estimation. These findings suggest that mid-level perceptual judgments are particularly beneficial for generalization.

**Ablation Study: Training Steps**:
An ablation on the number of fine-tuning steps showed that while a modest amount of alignment to NIGHTS was beneficial, performance on DeepFashion2 instance retrieval began to decline after approximately 1,000 steps, indicating potential overfitting to the perceptual judgments.

**Perceptual Alignment for CNNs**:
Preliminary experiments on CNN architectures (ResNet50 and ConvNeXt-B) also showed that human-aligned CNNs generally outperformed their pretrained versions on counting and instance retrieval, suggesting that the benefits of perceptual alignment are not exclusive to ViT models.

**Identified Limitations**:
- **Standard Image Classification**: Perceptual alignment using NIGHTS did not consistently improve performance on standard image classification tasks within the VTAB benchmark, and in many natural image datasets, it led to worse results. Possible explanations include that mid-level judgments might not provide the most suitable inductive bias for classification tasks, where high-level semantic associations might be more appropriate, or that classification accuracy metrics do not adequately capture the visual features derived from human similarity judgments.
- **Dataset Specificity and Overfitting**: Not all human preferences are beneficial for performance. Fine-tuning on datasets with solely high-level (THINGS) or low-level (BAPPS) variations could degrade performance. Additionally, excessive fine-tuning steps can lead to overfitting and harm downstream performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research significantly contributes to the understanding of how human perceptual alignment can influence and improve general-purpose vision representations. By systematically evaluating the effects of fine-tuning on human similarity judgments across a diverse array of downstream tasks, the study moves beyond task-specific alignment and sheds light on the broader utility of incorporating human perceptual knowledge.

The findings demonstrate that injecting an inductive bias derived from mid-level human perceptual judgments, as exemplified by the NIGHTS dataset, leads to more robust and versatile vision representations. This is evident in the consistent performance gains observed in tasks such as semantic segmentation, depth estimation, retrieval-augmented generation, object counting, and instance retrieval. The ability of global image-level similarity annotations to improve local patch-level tasks (like dense prediction) is a notable insight, suggesting that human perceptual biases can be effectively propagated across different levels of visual understanding.

The practical implications of this work are substantial. The observed benefits can inform future strategies for training and fine-tuning vision models, guiding model developers to consider human perceptual alignment as a valuable "second pretraining stage." This could lead to the development of more human-centric AI systems that better interpret and interact with the visual world. Furthermore, the findings highlight the importance of carefully curated human feedback datasets, emphasizing that mid-level perceptual variations may be particularly potent for enhancing generalization capabilities, compared to purely low-level or high-level semantic annotations.

The identified limitations, such as the mixed or negative impact on standard image classification and the risk of overfitting, are equally significant. They delineate the boundaries of this alignment approach and underscore that not all types of human feedback or all levels of abstraction are universally beneficial. These limitations open avenues for future research, prompting investigations into optimal alignment strategies tailored to specific tasks and the development of metrics that better capture human visual preferences.

Finally, the discussion of societal impacts acknowledges the broader ethical considerations, particularly the potential for human preference data to embed biases (e.g., gender, racial). This highlights the ongoing need for diverse annotator groups and rigorous data filtering to ensure that perceptually aligned models reflect equitable and desirable visual interpretations. The research thus not only advances technical capabilities but also contributes to the critical discourse on responsible AI development.
