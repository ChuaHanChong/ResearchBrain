# TeleOpBench: A Simulator-Centric Benchmark for Dual-Arm Dexterous Teleoperation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai AI Laboratory and collaborating institutions developed TeleOpBench, a simulator-centric benchmark for dual-arm dexterous teleoperation, which unifies evaluation across 30 tasks and four distinct interfaces. The benchmark demonstrates a strong correlation between simulated and real-world performance, enabling reliable assessment of diverse teleoperation systems.

## Problem

- The absence of a standardized benchmark for evaluating and comparing diverse dual-arm dexterous teleoperation systems.
- Difficulty in objectively assessing and reproducing results across various hardware platforms and task environments.
- High cost and inherent uncertainties of real-world robotics experiments hinder systematic progress in teleoperation research.

## Method

![[2505.12748-method.png]]
*TeleOpBench overview: four operator interfaces (VR, RGB camera, IMU MoCap, exoskeleton) mapped to upper-body and dexterous-hand joint angles, evaluated in both simulation and the real world.*

- Introduces **TeleOpBench**, a simulator-centric benchmark in ==NVIDIA Isaac Sim== featuring 30 high-fidelity bimanual manipulation tasks of varying complexity.
- Integrates ==four representative teleoperation modalities== (inertial MoCap, VR, arm-hand exoskeletons, monocular vision tracking) into a unified, modular framework.
- Validates the benchmark's fidelity through comparative real-world experiments on physical robot platforms, demonstrating ==correlation between simulated and hardware performance==.

## Results

- Xsens MoCap-based teleoperation consistently achieved the **highest success rates** and **fastest completion times** across a suite of **10 tasks** in both simulation and real-world environments.
- Vision-based teleoperation generally exhibited the longest completion times and lowest success rates, particularly failing on complex bimanual tasks (**0% success** on 'ball bimanual' and 'pen brushpot').
- A **strong positive correlation** was observed between performance metrics (completion time and success rate) in simulated and physical tasks, validating TeleOpBench's utility as a predictive benchmarking tool.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulation-based benchmarks can reliably predict real-world performance for complex dual-arm dexterous teleoperation, reducing the need for extensive physical testing.
> - Different teleoperation modalities exhibit distinct performance profiles: high-fidelity MoCap systems offer superior precision and speed, while vision-based systems face challenges with occlusion and accuracy.
> - Standardizing evaluation environments is critical for fostering fair comparisons and accelerating innovation in advanced robot learning and human-robot interaction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied robot learning has increasingly relied on human demonstration data to train sophisticated control policies. Teleoperation serves as a primary method for acquiring such data, offering precise and natural manipulation trajectories. While single-arm grippers have been a focus, the advancement towards humanoid dual-arm dexterous manipulators presents opportunities for executing more intricate and fine-grained tasks. This increased capability, however, introduces heightened kinematic complexity and demands for coordinated bimanual motion, thereby making teleoperation more challenging.

Recent developments in dual-arm dexterous teleoperation have led to the proliferation of various operator interfaces. These include inertial and optical motion-capture systems, virtual reality (VR) controllers, upper-body exoskeletons, and vision-based trackers. Each of these modalities offers different trade-offs in terms of fidelity, latency, affordability, and ease of deployment. Despite these advancements and impressive demonstrations, the robotics community has lacked a standardized benchmark for rigorously and comprehensively comparing these diverse teleoperation approaches. The absence of such a benchmark means that each system is often evaluated within its own specific hardware, robot platform, and task environment, which can obscure objective performance assessments and hinder progress in the field.

In the broader context of robotics research, well-designed benchmarks are recognized as critical for providing standardized, reproducible, and equitable environments for evaluating different methodologies. Given the inherent uncertainties in real-world robotics experiments—such as variations in hardware setups, lighting conditions, and task configurations—simulation-based benchmarks have emerged as viable alternatives. These simulation platforms are widely used in areas like robotic reinforcement learning and imitation learning. This work addresses the specific gap in bimanual dexterous teleoperation by proposing a simulation-centric benchmark. By fixing the robot morphology and task environment within a simulator, the proposed benchmark aims to eliminate hardware and scene variability, enabling systematic assessment and fair comparison of different teleoperation systems. It builds upon the established practice of using simulation for benchmarking while extending it to the specific challenges of complex bimanual dexterous manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind TeleOpBench is the observed lack of a unified, standardized benchmark for evaluating and comparing dual-arm dexterous teleoperation systems. Current research in this area features diverse hardware pipelines, including inertial motion-capture gloves, exoskeletons, and vision-based interfaces, each often coupled with a unique robot platform and task environment. This variability hinders fair, reproducible comparisons across different approaches, making it difficult to objectively assess their performance and ultimately impeding systematic progress in dexterous teleoperation research. The development of new hardware and algorithms for teleoperation is challenged by the absence of a common evaluation framework.

In response to this gap, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Introduce a dedicated benchmark:** To establish TeleOpBench, a simulator-centric benchmark specifically designed for dual-arm dexterous teleoperation. This benchmark is intended to enable rigorous, fair, and comprehensive comparisons between competing teleoperation systems by standardizing the robot morphology and task environment.
2. **Provide diverse and challenging task environments:** To incorporate 30 high-fidelity task environments within TeleOpBench. These tasks are designed to span a broad spectrum of difficulty, encompassing pick-and-place operations, tool use, and collaborative manipulation, and covering a range of kinematic and force-interaction complexities. The tasks are hierarchically organized to allow for graduated evaluation of various teleoperation modalities.
3. **Implement representative teleoperation modalities:** To integrate four distinct and representative teleoperation modalities—inertial motion capture (MoCap), VR devices, arm–hand exoskeletons, and monocular vision tracking—into a unified, modular framework. This implementation allows researchers to evaluate different teleoperation pipelines under identical conditions.
4. **Validate simulator fidelity with real-world experiments:** To conduct mirrored experiments on a physical dual-arm platform to ascertain if performance in simulation is predictive of real-world behavior. The objective is to demonstrate a strong correlation between simulator and hardware performance, thereby confirming the external validity and practical value of TeleOpBench as a rigorous benchmarking tool.
5. **Foster open and reproducible research:** To release all code and task assets open-source, aiming to promote transparency, reproducibility, and accelerate future algorithmic and hardware innovation in dexterous teleoperation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TeleOpBench is designed as a simulator-based benchmark for the evaluation of dual-arm teleoperation systems. The methodology integrates a diverse set of task environments with multiple teleoperation interfaces within a unified framework, validated through comparative real-world experiments.

**Simulation Platform and Task Environments:**
The benchmark utilizes NVIDIA Isaac Sim as its simulation platform, leveraging its PhysX engine for physics fidelity and photorealistic renderer for environment realism. Each simulated scene features a humanoid robot with bimanual dexterous hands, interacting with task-relevant objects.
- **Humanoid Robots:** Three commercially available humanoid platforms are supported for comprehensive hardware evaluation: Unitree H1-2 (7 DoF arms, Inspire Dexterous Hands with 5 fingers/6 DoF), Fourier GR1-T2 (7 DoF arms, Fourier native dexterous hands with 5 fingers/6 DoF), and Unitree G1 (compact form factor, 3-finger hands with 4 DoF). This selection covers a range of robot scales and hand designs.
- **Task Setting:** TeleOpBench includes 30 bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks, organized hierarchically by complexity. These tasks range from simple pick-and-place to long-horizon routines involving coordinated two-hand interaction, such as lifting pot lids and transferring fruit. Each task has explicit success criteria and aims to replicate real-world physics (e.g., object masses, friction coefficients). A modular, extensible asset library with APIs is provided for customization and extension.
- **Observation Data:** For each teleoperation episode, a rich set of data is logged, including robot-state vectors (joint positions, angles, velocities, kinematics), camera streams (RGB from head-mounted and third-person perspectives), and task-level environment metadata (object positions and orientations). This data is intended to support downstream imitation-learning studies.

**Modular Teleoperation Interfaces:**
Four representative teleoperation pipelines are implemented under a unified, modular interface:
- **Vision-based:** This pipeline decouples arm/wrist pose estimation from hand keypoint estimation.
 - *Human-body parameter estimation and scaling:* A keypoint-constrained parameter–scaling model, based on SMPL, is used to minimize alignment error between human landmarks (pelvis, shoulders, wrists, head) and robot landmarks in a T-pose. This yields optimal scaling parameters (s*) between human and robot joint links.
 - *Upper-body limb motion control:* SMPLer-X captures the operator’s SMPL pose, which is then scaled by s* to obtain robotic translations. PINK-based inverse kinematics (IK) is used to compute all arm DoF, excluding fingers.
 - *Hand control:* Scheme 2, adopted for all experiments, uses MediaPipe for finger keypoint tracking and applies vector optimizers via the Dex-Retargeting library to generate robotic hand DoF values. A Kalman filter smooths the robot’s DoF to reduce jitter.
- **MoCap-based (Xsens MVN):**
 - *Hardware:* An Xsens MVN system with seventeen IMUs tracks limb movements, and Manus Metagloves provide 20 DoF per hand for intricate hand motions.
 - *Arm control:* Raw Xsens data is transformed from its global coordinate system to the robot’s pelvis-centered coordinate system. A real-time, joint-specific rescaling method adjusts joint lengths to account for skeletal differences. Closed-Loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) computes robot joint poses.
 - *Hand control:* Detailed axis-angle data (MCP, PIP, DIP joint movements) from Manus gloves are mapped to the defined joint angle constraints of the robotic dexterous hand.
- **VR-based (Apple VisionPro):**
 - *Upper-body limb motion control:* Apple VisionPro provides hand, wrist, and head tracking. Poses are transformed into the robot’s coordinate frame, and the wrist offset relative to the head is converted to an offset relative to the pelvis. Pink-based IK computes non-finger DoF using wrist translation data.
 - *Hand control:* Distal phalanx lengths of the operator’s fingers are measured and proportionally scaled to match robotic finger segments, yielding a scaling factor s* (∈ R^5). Vector-based optimizers, leveraging the AnyTeleop/Dex-Retargeting framework, generate robot-hand joint commands.
- **Exoskeleton-based:** This system proposes isomorphic exoskeletons customized to specific humanoid kinematics.
 - *System Design:* Based on principles from HOMIE, each exoskeleton replicates the target humanoid’s upper body kinematic structure with servo-driven joints. Motion-sensing gloves with Hall-effect sensors provide 15 DoF per hand.
 - *Control:* Operator kinematics are directly mapped to the humanoid’s joints, bypassing IK approximations to enhance operational bandwidth and positional accuracy.

**Experiments and Evaluation Protocol:**
A user study involving four participants was conducted.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Task success rates (%) and completion times (s) are recorded for each teleoperation modality.
- **Task Selection:** Ten representative tasks of varying difficulty were selected from TeleOpBench for detailed quantitative comparison (e.g., push cube, ball trashcan, pot tomato plate).
- **Real-world Validation:** The selected task suite was reproduced on physical robots, and all four teleoperation pipelines were evaluated using the same metrics. This allowed for a direct comparison of performance between simulation and the real world.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TeleOpBench involved qualitative and quantitative analyses across the four implemented teleoperation modalities in both simulated and real-world environments.

**Qualitative Observations:**
Figure 3 provides qualitative results illustrating the capabilities of the four teleoperation modalities. The vision-based system demonstrated successful grasping, such as placing a ball into a trash bin. The VR-based system facilitated precise placement of a pen into a holder. The exoskeleton-based system enabled bimanual object transfer, like passing a ball from one hand to another. The Xsens motion-capture system also supported fine-precision manipulation, exemplified by placing a slender pen. These observations indicate that all four systems are capable of performing various dexterous manipulation tasks to some extent.

**Quantitative Results in Simulation Tasks:**
Table 2 summarizes the performance of the teleoperation systems across ten selected tasks in the simulation environment, based on a user study with four participants.
- **Vision-based Teleoperation:** This method generally exhibited the longest completion times and the lowest success rates, particularly for more complex tasks. For instance, it achieved a 40% success rate in "uprear cup" (Task 4) and 0% in "ball bimanual" (Task 7) and "pen brushpot" (Task 10). The limitations were attributed to a low frame rate, coarse wrist orientation estimates, and susceptibility to occlusion.
- **VR-based Teleoperation:** This modality demonstrated stronger grasping capabilities and generally lower completion times than the vision-based system. It achieved 100% success on several tasks but entirely failed Task 7 ("ball bimanual") due to issues with hand-over-hand occlusion breaking pose estimation.
- **Exoskeleton-based Teleoperation:** With its kinematically aligned design and direct mapping of DoF to the robot, this method provided smooth control and performed well across most tasks, with high success rates. However, it showed increased time consumption in Task 1 ("push cube"), which was attributed to limited lateral elbow movement.
- **Xsens MoCap-based Teleoperation:** This system consistently demonstrated the highest precision and smoothness in motion, leading to the fastest completion times and typically the highest success rates across the tasks. It achieved 100% success on most tasks, including Task 7 and Task 10. The paper notes that this superior performance comes at a higher cost.

**Quantitative Results in Physical Tasks and Correlation with Simulation:**
Table 3 presents the quantitative performance metrics for the same ten tasks performed on physical robots. The results largely mirrored those observed in the simulation environment.
- **General Trends:** The vision-tracking interface consistently required the longest execution times and had the lowest success rates on challenging tasks (e.g., 0% success on Tasks 7 and 10 in the real world). The inertial MoCap pipeline (Xsens) remained the fastest and most reliable. The VR and exoskeleton interfaces clustered in between these two extremes, showing intermediate performance.
- **Correlation:** Figure 4 illustrates the completion-time and success rate curves for both simulated and real-world tasks. The curves show a strong positive correlation between the two domains. Tasks where one teleoperation system achieved less than 20% success were excluded to ensure reliability of the curves. This alignment between simulated and real-world performance supports the conclusion that TeleOpBench reliably predicts practical outcomes of teleoperation systems.

In summary, the experiments demonstrated varying performance levels among the teleoperation modalities, with Xsens MoCap generally excelling in precision and speed, followed by exoskeletons and VR, and then vision-based systems. The strong correlation between simulation and real-world results validates TeleOpBench's utility as a predictive benchmarking tool.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TeleOpBench contributes a standardized and reproducible platform for the evaluation of dual-arm dexterous teleoperation systems, addressing a previous gap in the research landscape.

The introduction of TeleOpBench establishes a common yardstick for comparing diverse teleoperation hardware and algorithmic approaches. By providing 30 high-fidelity task environments and implementing four representative teleoperation modalities within a unified, modular framework, the benchmark facilitates fair and rigorous comparisons. This standardization is expected to enable researchers to objectively assess the strengths and weaknesses of different systems, which can lead to more informed design choices and accelerate the development of improved teleoperation technologies.

The validation of TeleOpBench's external fidelity, demonstrated by the strong correlation between simulated and real-world performance across a range of tasks, is a significant aspect of this work. This correlation suggests that researchers can utilize the simulation environment for iterative development and evaluation with confidence that the results will generalize to physical robotic systems. This capability can reduce the time and cost associated with real-world experimentation, making advanced robotics research more accessible and efficient.

Furthermore, the modular design of TeleOpBench and the authors' commitment to open-sourcing the code and task assets are intended to foster collaborative research and community contributions. This openness can encourage the integration of new teleoperation pipelines, robot morphologies, and task scenarios, thereby expanding the benchmark's applicability and longevity. The rich set of logged observation data from the simulation framework also provides a valuable resource for downstream robot learning tasks, such as imitation learning, which rely heavily on high-quality human demonstration data.

The potential impact of TeleOpBench extends to accelerating progress in areas such as robot learning, human-robot interaction, and remote manipulation. By providing a reliable and accessible platform for benchmarking, it can facilitate the development of more capable and dexterous robotic systems that can perform complex tasks in various applications, from industrial assembly to hazardous environment exploration.

The paper also acknowledges limitations, noting that TeleOpBench currently focuses on upper-body teleoperation in tabletop settings. Future work outlined, such as building loco-manipulation benchmarks and incorporating haptic-feedback interfaces, suggests directions for further broadening the benchmark's applicability and advancing the field towards more comprehensive whole-body teleoperation and finer force control.
