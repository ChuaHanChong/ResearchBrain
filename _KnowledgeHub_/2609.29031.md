# Simple Torque-Observation Alignment for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Grasping with a Direct-Drive Gripper

> [!summary] Summary
> Simple torque-observation alignment lets a simulation-trained policy grasp with a three-fingered direct-drive gripper using only joint positions and torque changes. Dynamometer calibration fixes the current-to-torque scale, temporal differencing of torque cancels domain-dependent offsets, and measurement-derived Gaussian noise is injected during training. The distilled student transfers zero-shot, reaching 100% grasp success on nine in-distribution objects and 99% over 21.

## Problem

- Simulated actuator torque and current-derived real torque differ in scale, additive bias, and noise, so simulation-trained policies misread contact onset and load changes at deployment.
- Implicit robustness methods like dynamics randomization and torque perturbation never establish a calibrated correspondence between simulated and real torque, and depend heavily on chosen randomization ranges.
- Explicit fixes either need actuator-specific model identification or network training, or, in the closest observation-alignment work, extra fingertip contact-force and tactile sensing during grasping.

## Method

- **Dynamometer torque-constant calibration**: regress measured shaft torque on quadrature current per motor type to identify K*, removing the scale factor of the affine cross-domain torque model.
- **Temporal torque differencing**: use ==Delta-tau== equals current minus previous torque as policy input in both domains, cancelling constant baselines and attenuating slowly varying gravity, friction, current offsets.
- ==Dynamometer-residual Gaussian noise injection== plus teacher-student distillation: **PPO** teacher on privileged state, transformer student behavior-cloned onto joint positions and torque differences only.

## Results

- Full method reaches **100.0%** real-world success on nine in-distribution objects, while absolute-torque variants reach **26.7%** and **10.0%**, despite all torque policies scoring **100%** in simulation.
- Ablations isolate each component: adding scale calibration to Bias+Noise gains **44.4 points** from **55.6%**, and adding noise injection to Scale+Bias gains **37.8 points** from **62.2%**.
- Zero-shot on twelve unseen objects gives **98.3%**, and **99.0%** over 21 objects; a position-only student drops to **68.9%** simulation and **15.6%** real.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Differencing a sensor signal cheaply deletes unknown domain offsets, but discards sustained levels, so faster or non-quasi-static tasks may still need absolute torque.
> - Per-motor-type calibration matters: unequal scale errors (38.0% versus 7.8%) distort relative torque patterns across joints, breaking multifinger coordination more than a uniform gain error.
> - Measured residuals, not guessed ranges, set the noise level; explicit alignment combined with measurement-informed randomization beats either alone and needs no learned actuator model.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A three-finger, nine-joint direct-drive gripper must grasp, lift, and hold objects whose properties are unknown. Torque feedback could help it detect contact and changing load. A policy trained on simulated torque may misread real motor-current measurements, however, because the two can differ in scale, baseline offset, and noise.

The authors address this in three steps. They calibrate the motor-current-to-torque scale. They feed the policy changes between consecutive torque readings rather than absolute readings. They also add measurement-informed noise during simulation training. The resulting simulation-trained policy uses joint positions and torque changes to grasp, lift, and hold objects, without vision or dedicated tactile or force sensors at deployment.

"Direct-drive" means the motors act on the joints without a gearbox. The authors argue that this makes motor-current changes more directly informative about interaction loads.

Representative sequences with torque-change (Δτ) traces: success mode (full method), grasping/lifting failure (Scale + Bias), and holding failure (Bias + Noise). The paper describes the full method's traces as stabilizing after contact and the ablations as continuing to redistribute torque.

### 2. Calibrating what the gripper feels

In simulation, the policy receives actuator torque computed from simulated dynamics. The real gripper instead estimates torque from motor current. A motor-specific torque constant converts current to torque, and dynamometer calibration measures an effective constant for each motor type. For this gripper, datasheet-based estimates exceed the calibrated estimates by about 38.0% for GL60 KV25 and 7.8% for GL40 KV70.

The second step subtracts consecutive torque readings in both domains. This temporal differencing cancels a constant offset and attenuates slowly varying baselines, while retaining changes that can signal contact or load changes. It does not remove arbitrary time-varying bias.

Third, the authors inject Gaussian noise during simulation training. The dynamometer regression residuals set the scale of that noise. At deployment, the student policy observes joint positions and torque differences.

An overview of the proposed method: dynamometer calibration, calibration residuals with a Gaussian fit for noise injection, and torque differencing in the real and simulated domains, with aligned observations passed to the policy alongside joint positions.

### 3. What the ablation establishes

Each trial starts from the same prescribed non-contact initial condition. A trial is successful when the gripper grasps the object, lifts it fully off the support, and holds it until the end of the 10-second episode. The real-world variants combine three choices: calibrated versus datasheet torque constant, absolute torque versus torque difference, and noise injection on versus off. Each variant ran 10 trials on each of nine in-distribution (ID) objects.

The most direct comparison is Scale + Bias against the full method. Scale + Bias uses calibrated torque differences without noise injection and achieved 62.2% success. The full method achieved 100.0%.

Simulation results did not reveal this gap. All three torque-based policies reached 100.0% in simulation, even though their real-world performance differed.

These controlled variants support the components in this tested setup. They do not establish that one factor alone causes any particular failure.

Real-world ablation on nine ID objects. Average success: Noise Only 10.0%, Scale + Noise 26.7%, Bias + Noise 55.6%, Scale + Bias 62.2%, Our Method 100.0%. Error bars appear only for Average and denote ±1 standard error of the equally weighted mean over the nine objects.

### 4. Transfer beyond training shapes

A separate evaluation tested 21 objects. Twelve of them were out-of-distribution (OOD) objects with unseen shapes, materials, and surface properties, and all objects fit within the gripper workspace. Each object was evaluated over 10 trials from the same prescribed initial condition.

Grasp–lift–hold success averaged 100.0% on ID objects and 98.3% on OOD objects, for an overall success rate of 99.0%. Two trials failed: a tennis ball slipped during lifting, and a light bulb fell during holding.

These were fixed-base, single-object tests. The authors leave arm-mounted grasping to future work.

### 5. Torque changes, not torque levels

The policies were trained entirely in simulation and deployed without fine-tuning or adaptation. Dynamometer measurements still informed the calibration and the simulated noise. The contribution is an observation-alignment method for the policy.

The method has limits. The deployed policy does not directly observe sustained torque levels. The calibration and noise parameters were measured under specific dynamometer conditions, and more dynamic tasks may require additional compensation.
