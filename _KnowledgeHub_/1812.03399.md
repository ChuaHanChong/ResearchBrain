# Efficient transfer learning and online adaptation with latent variable models for continuous control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Uber AI Labs developed a hierarchical probabilistic model-based control framework that integrates auxiliary latent variables with online Bayesian inference for continuous control. This framework demonstrated faster learning, robust generalization to novel environments, and rapid adaptation to dynamic conditions in HalfCheetah tasks, achieving strong performance with less data compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning methods often lack data efficiency, particularly model-free approaches requiring extensive observations.
- Existing model-based RL struggles with robust generalization to new environments and adaptation to dynamic changes in underlying physics.
- Agents frequently overfit to training conditions, leading to diminished performance when test environments deviate.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical probabilistic model-based control== framework that introduces ==auxiliary latent variables== to capture environment-specific dynamics properties.
- An ==ensemble of probabilistic neural networks== models state transitions, predicting the latent variable as a Gaussian distribution, and quantifies uncertainty to improve robustness.
- ==Online Bayesian inference== updates the latent variable posterior using only the latest transition, enabling rapid, real-time adaptation to dynamic environmental changes.

## Results

- The variational approach showed faster learning and positive transfer during training, matching or exceeding specialist agent performance.
- It significantly outperformed baselines in dynamic environments, demonstrating online adaptation to mid-episode changes in gravity direction.
- The method generalized effectively to novel static environments, especially in low-data regimes, and learned latent representations that accurately captured and extrapolated underlying physical parameters.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Introducing auxiliary latent variables within a shared dynamics model facilitates positive knowledge transfer and robust generalization across diverse, related environments.
> - Rapid online Bayesian inference of these latent variables allows agents to adapt to dynamically changing environmental conditions with minimal data.
> - The unsupervised learning of latent embeddings effectively captures and extrapolates the underlying physical parameters of various environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces several challenges, including achieving data efficiency, robust generalization to new scenarios, and effective adaptation to changing environmental conditions. Model-free RL methods have demonstrated high performance in various tasks but typically require a large number of observations during training, often millions, which can be impractical for real-world applications such as robotics. Conversely, model-based RL methods are generally more sample-efficient as they learn a model of the environment's dynamics. However, they frequently exhibit poorer performance compared to model-free approaches and struggle with generalization. A significant limitation of traditional model-based methods is their inability to adapt to subtle variations in underlying dynamics, such as differences in physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction, actuator gain) across different agents or over time. Both model-free and model-based methods are susceptible to overfitting to their training conditions, leading to diminished performance when test conditions deviate.

This paper addresses these limitations by focusing on enhancing the adaptability and generalization capabilities of model-based RL. It builds upon previous work in hierarchical probabilistic models, which facilitate the incorporation of prior knowledge and the transfer of learning between related tasks. Specifically, the authors connect to research on latent variable Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), noting that prior work in this area often involved small, discrete action spaces or exhibited lower sample efficiency compared to the approach proposed here. The paper also positions its use of deep ensembles for uncertainty quantification as a pragmatic alternative to computationally intensive Bayesian neural networks, aligning with advancements that leverage ensembles to improve model-based RL performance and address model bias. The concept of online adaptation to dynamically changing conditions without the need for extensive replay buffers also forms a crucial part of the existing research landscape, with this work seeking to provide an efficient solution through variational inference.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were to develop a reinforcement learning algorithm that satisfies three key properties: efficient learning with minimal data, robust generalization to novel environments, and rapid adaptation to dynamic, changing conditions. The authors' motivation stems from the deficiencies observed in both model-free and traditional model-based RL approaches regarding these properties.

Specifically, the paper aimed to overcome the following challenges:

1. **Limited Generalization in Model-Based RL:** Traditional model-based methods struggle when environment dynamics vary, even in systematic ways (e.g., changing physical parameters like gravity, friction, or mass). The goal was to enable an algorithm to learn these relationships autonomously and transfer knowledge for faster learning and more robust control, rather than relying on human specification of how environment conditions impact dynamics.
2. **Sample Inefficiency:** While model-based methods are generally more sample-efficient than model-free ones, there was still a need to optimize learning further, especially when transferring knowledge across different, yet related, environments. The objective was to achieve efficient learning within tens of episodes.
3. **Lack of Online Adaptability:** Agents need to adjust their behavior in real-time when environment conditions change dynamically, without requiring a complete re-training or maintaining large buffers of past experience. The authors sought to equip agents with the ability to infer and adapt to such changes on the fly.

To address these motivations, the research proposed a hierarchical probabilistic model-based control framework with specific design elements:
- **Auxiliary Latent Variables:** Introduce explicit latent variables (`e_k`) to represent unknown, instance-specific properties of environment dynamics. This allows a shared neural network dynamics model to adapt to different environments by inferring the appropriate latent variable, thereby facilitating efficient knowledge transfer and generalization.
- **Online Bayesian Inference:** Implement a mechanism for rapid, online inference of these latent variables. This enables the agent to quickly update its beliefs about the current environment based on new observations, supporting adaptation to non-stationary or dynamically changing conditions without retaining extensive historical data.
- **Uncertainty Quantification:** Utilize neural network ensembles to model environment dynamics and quantify uncertainty effectively. This helps in mitigating model bias and improving the robustness of the learned dynamics model, which is crucial for model-based control.

In summary, the core motivation was to create a robust, data-efficient, and adaptive RL agent capable of performing well in continuous control tasks across a spectrum of related and dynamically changing environments, by explicitly modeling and inferring environment-specific latent properties.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates latent variable models with an ensemble-based dynamics model and online Bayesian inference within a model predictive control (MPC) framework.

#### 3.1. Dynamics Models with Auxiliary Latent Variables

The core idea is to introduce a latent variable `e_k` ∈ ℝ^d_e to represent the unknown, instance-specific parameters of an environment's transition dynamics. The overall probability model for state-action trajectories and the latent variable for a specific environment `k` is defined as:
`p(s_{0:T+1}, a_{0:T}, e_k) = p(e_k)p(s_0) ∏_{t=0}^T p(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t, e_k)p(a_t|s_t, e_k)`
Here, `p(e_k)` is a standard normal prior `N(0, I)`, and `p(s_0)` is the initial state distribution. The transition dynamics `p(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t, e_k)` are modeled by an **ensemble of probabilistic neural networks**. Each network in the ensemble predicts the change in state (`∆s = s_{t+1} - s_t`) as a Gaussian distribution `N(μ_θ(s_t, a_t, e_k), Σ_θ(s_t, a_t, e_k))`, where `θ` represents the network parameters. Using an ensemble helps capture uncertainty and reduce overfitting, serving as a practical alternative to full Bayesian neural networks.

#### 3.2. Bayesian Learning with Variational Inference

For a given dataset of transitions `D`, the intractable true posterior `p(e_k|D)` is approximated by a variational distribution `q_φ(e_k)`, chosen to be a multivariate normal distribution parameterized by `φ = {μ_q, Σ_q}`. The learning objective for both the dynamics model parameters `θ` and the variational parameters `φ` is to maximize the evidence lower bound (ELBO) to the marginal likelihood `log p(D)`.
The ELBO for training the dynamics model (Eq. 4) is given by:
`log p(D) ≥ E_{e_k~q_φ(e_k)} [∑_{t=0}^T log p_θ(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t, e_k)] - KL(q_φ(e_k)||p(e_k))`
This objective optimizes the dynamics model `θ` and the latent variable `e_k` for a given environment.

#### 3.3. Online Bayesian Inference for Adaptation

To enable rapid online adaptation and avoid retaining large replay buffers, the paper employs an Online Bayesian Inference approach. When new data `D_t+1` arrives, the posterior from the previous time step `p(e_k|D_t)` is used as the prior for the current step. However, instead of storing `D_t`, the parametric approximation `q_φ(e_k)` is used to represent this partial posterior.
For online learning, where dynamics network parameters `θ` are fixed (e.g., during test time), a modified objective (Eq. 5) is optimized to find an approximate posterior `q_φ*(e_k)`:
`q_φ*(e_k) = argmax_φ E_{e_k~q_φ(e_k)} [log p_θ(s_{t+1}|s_t, a_t, e_k)] - KL(q_φ(e_k|D_{t+1})||p(e_k|D_t))`
This allows for continuous updates of the latent variable distribution `q_φ(e_k)` based on the most recent transition, effectively adapting to changing environment conditions on the fly.

#### 3.4. Control with Auxiliary Variable Models (Model Predictive Control)

The agent uses Model Predictive Control (MPC) for action planning. At each time step, MPC plans an optimal sequence of actions over a future horizon `h` but only executes the first action, then re-plans at the next step. This strategy helps mitigate the accumulation of modeling errors.
The **Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)** is utilized as the optimizer within MPC to find the action trajectory `a_{t:t+h}` that maximizes the expected reward. CEM iteratively samples action sequences from a proposal distribution, evaluates their rewards, and updates the proposal distribution based on the top-performing samples.
To account for uncertainty about the environment, the control strategy employs **Trajectory Sampling (TS)**. During planning, the latent variable `e_k` is sampled from the current approximate posterior `q_φ(·|D_t)` for each particle state prediction. This incorporates the agent's uncertainty about the environment into the planning process.

#### 3.5. Learning Procedure (Algorithm 1)

The overall learning and control process involves:
1. **Data Collection:** Initial data is collected using a random policy.
2. **Episode Loop:** For each episode:
 - An environment `k` is sampled.
 - If in the training phase, the ensemble dynamics model `f_θ` is trained using all collected data `D` and the ELBO objective (Eq. 4).
 - **MPC Loop:** For each time step `t` within an episode:
 - CEM is used to determine the best action `a_t`. This involves sampling action sequences, sampling `e_k` from `q_φ(e_k)`, propagating states using `f_θ` and TS, and evaluating rewards.
 - The determined action `a_t` is executed.
 - The resulting transition `{(s_t, a_t), s_{t+1}}` is recorded.
 - The approximate posterior `q_φ(e_k|D_t)` is updated online using the single latest transition (Eq. 5).
 - The episode's data is added to the overall dataset `D`.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

The experiments were conducted in the HalfCheetah Mujoco simulator. The environment dynamics were varied by changing the direction of gravity (`θ_g`).
- **Training environments:** `θ_g` values of `{-12°, -6°, 0°, 6°, 12°}`.
- **Test environments:** `θ_g` values of `{-15°, -9°, -3°, 3°, 9°, 15°}`, including both interpolated and extrapolated angles.
- **Continual Learning Test:** A dynamic environment where `θ_g` changes mid-episode (from `-6°` to `6°`).
- **Baselines:**
 - **Specialist agent:** An ensemble dynamics model trained independently for each specific environment, without latent variables or transfer learning. This serves as a strong upper-bound baseline for individual environment performance.
 - **Generalist agent:** An ensemble dynamics model trained on data from all training environments, but without auxiliary latent variables. This evaluates the ensemble's generalization capability without the proposed latent variable mechanism.
Experiments were run with 5 random seeds. During test time, the neural network parameters `θ` are fixed, and only the variational parameters `φ` are updated online after every time step using the last transition.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed approach across various settings:

1. **Training Performance and Positive Transfer (Figure 2a & 2b):**
 - The "variational" approach, which incorporates auxiliary latent variables, exhibited faster learning compared to both the "specialist" and "generalist" baselines during the initial training episodes (Figure 2a). This suggests that the latent variable model facilitates positive transfer of learned dynamics across different training environments.
 - The variational model achieved performance levels comparable to or exceeding the specialist agents in individual training environments, especially in the early stages of training (Figure 2b). As training progressed and more data was accumulated, the performance gap between the variational model and the specialist often diminished, as specialist models can eventually learn to model their specific system effectively with sufficient data.

2. **Online Adaptation to Dynamically Changing Environments (Figure 3, Top):**
 - On an environment where the gravity direction (`θ_g`) changed from -6° to 6° mid-episode, the variational approach significantly outperformed the generalist baseline. This result directly supports the efficacy of online Bayesian inference of latent variables for rapid adaptation to changing environmental conditions within a single episode.

3. **Generalization to Novel Static Environments (Figure 3, Bottom):**
 - When evaluated on novel test environments (including interpolated and extrapolated `θ_g` values), the variational approach demonstrated superior performance compared to the generalist in the low-data regime (after fewer training episodes, e.g., 15 episodes). This indicates that the latent variable model enhances generalization capabilities and enables positive transfer to unseen environments without sacrificing overall performance.
 - However, the ability of the variational model to extrapolate to the most extreme test angles did not substantially exceed that of the generalist. This suggests that while the method improves generalization, the capacity for extreme extrapolation might require further algorithmic enhancements or more diverse training data.

4. **Learned Latent Variable Representation (Figure 4):**
 - The unsupervised latent embeddings (`e_k`) learned by the model accurately captured the relative order of the underlying physical parameter `θ_g` across both training and test environments.
 - Crucially, the inferred `e_k` values for novel, steeper test environments were situated outside the range of the `e_k` values observed for the training environments. This demonstrates that the latent space can extrapolate beyond the training distribution, allowing the model to represent and adapt to unseen physical conditions.

5. **Generalization from Fewer Training Environments (Appendix C):**
 - An additional experiment using only two training environments (`θ_g = ±6°`) showed that the variational method's advantage over the generalist grew with more training data. It remained competitive with the specialist on most novel environments, highlighting its robustness even with a more limited set of training tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by proposing an effective approach to address the critical challenges of data efficiency, generalization, and online adaptation in continuous control tasks.

1. **Enhanced Adaptability and Generalization:** The work demonstrates that incorporating auxiliary latent variables into ensemble dynamics models, coupled with online Bayesian inference, enables agents to learn quickly across environments with varying physical parameters and to control robustly in novel or dynamically changing environments. This capability is significant for real-world robotic applications where conditions are rarely static and precise dynamic models are often unavailable.
2. **Improved Data Efficiency:** By facilitating positive transfer of learned dynamics through a shared model and latent representations, the method achieves strong performance within a relatively small number of episodes. This is a critical advantage over sample-inefficient model-free methods, making it more practical for scenarios where data collection is costly or time-consuming.
3. **Explicit Environment Representation:** The learned latent variable `e_k` effectively captures relevant degrees of freedom of the true environment dynamics in an unsupervised manner. This provides an explicit, interpretable belief about the current state of the environment, which can be beneficial for diagnostics, understanding agent behavior, and potentially for human oversight.
4. **Alternative to Meta-Learning:** The proposed method offers a distinct approach to learning adaptive models across environment dynamics, providing an alternative perspective to popular meta-learning techniques like MAML. Its reliance on online inference avoids the need for sample-inefficient recurrent neural network policies for handling non-stationarity.
5. **Foundation for Future Work:** The results suggest that this combination of modeling elements can be extended to harder and more varied environments to further explore its performance benefits. Furthermore, the framework's structure allows for the potential incorporation of other forms of sensory information (e.g., from diverse robot perceptions) or informative priors, which could enhance its applicability and performance in complex real-world systems.

The ability to relax constraints on environment stationarity and achieve high-performance generalization on high-dimensional continuous control tasks positions this work as a relevant step towards developing more robust and autonomous intelligent agents.
