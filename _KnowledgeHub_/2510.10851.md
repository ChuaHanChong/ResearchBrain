# Preference-Conditioned Multi-Objective RL for Integrated Command Tracking and Force Compliance in Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Tsinghua University developed a preference-conditioned multi-objective reinforcement learning framework for humanoid locomotion, enabling a single policy to smoothly trade off between velocity command tracking and external force compliance. The approach introduces a velocity-resistance model and an encoder-decoder for privileged reconstruction, demonstrating adaptable and robust performance in simulation and on a physical humanoid robot, requiring only 10 N for human guidance compared to over 25 N for baseline policies.

## Problem

- Current reinforcement learning policies for humanoid locomotion prioritize robustness and command tracking, leading to rigid behaviors that resist external forces and hinder natural human-robot interaction.
- There is an inherent conflict between achieving precise command tracking and responsive external force compliance, making it difficult for humanoids to operate safely and compliantly in human-centered environments.
- Existing learning-based methods often compromise one objective for the sake of the other, or rely on complex architectures, failing to provide a unified, adaptable policy for continuous trade-offs.

## Method

- Formulating humanoid locomotion as a ==preference-conditioned multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL)== problem, where a single policy can adapt its behavior based on user-specified preference weights for command tracking and force compliance.
- Introducing a ==velocity–resistance model== that maps sustained external forces to equivalent velocities, allowing for consistent and comparable reward design for both velocity commands and external forces.
- Employing a ==one-stage privileged reconstruction== approach using an ==asymmetric actor–critic== architecture with an ==encoder-decoder== to infer task-relevant external forces from deployable observations for real-world application without explicit force sensors.

## Results

- The MORL policy demonstrated a continuous and stable trade-off between command tracking and force compliance in simulation, smoothly interpolating between rigid tracking and compliant yielding, even under online preference switching.
- On the Booster T1 humanoid robot, the MORL policy required approximately **10 N** of force for smooth human guidance, significantly less than baseline policies which needed over **25 N** and showed instability.
- The policy exhibited enhanced robustness against unexpected perturbations, with MORL variants maintaining a **50%** success rate under **50 N** impacts where baseline policies completely failed, and successfully navigating diverse outdoor terrains with human guidance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A velocity–resistance model effectively unifies velocity commands and external forces, allowing for direct comparison and integration of these objectives within a multi-objective reward function.
> - Preference-conditioned policies can learn a continuous spectrum of behaviors, enabling smooth, online trade-offs between conflicting objectives like command tracking and force compliance without requiring multiple policies or complex switching mechanisms.
> - An encoder-decoder structure can infer unobservable external forces from standard robot observations during training, allowing the policy to adapt to forces during deployment without specialized force/torque sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid locomotion research, particularly using reinforcement learning (RL), has advanced significantly in developing robust policies for navigation and stable walking on challenging terrains, with capabilities such as push recovery and stylized motion generation. Existing RL approaches often incorporate random force perturbations during training to enhance robustness and sim-to-real transfer. This typically results in policies that resist external forces, prioritizing stability and accurate command following.

However, this emphasis on resistance creates a gap in the ability of humanoids to interact naturally and compliantly with humans or their environment. Force compliance, where a robot responds to external guidance rather than resisting it, is a critical capability for deployment in human-centered spaces, enabling safer and more intuitive physical human-robot interaction. The inherent conflict between precise command tracking and responsive force compliance has not been explicitly addressed in many learning-based methods, often leading to compromises in one objective for the sake of the other.

This research addresses this specific trade-off by framing humanoid locomotion as a multi-objective optimization problem. It integrates command tracking and force compliance within a unified policy, departing from methods that primarily focus on robustness against disturbances. While multi-objective RL (MORL) has been applied in various robotics contexts (e.g., balancing speed, energy, stability in legged robots, or fidelity and style in motion generation), this work specifically focuses on the practical and often conflicting demands of velocity command tracking and continuous external force compliance in omnidirectional humanoid locomotion. Furthermore, it aims to achieve this within a single, adaptable policy without relying on complex hierarchical architectures or multi-stage training pipelines, distinguishing it from prior work that might use such structures for force adaptation in quadrupedal or loco-manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this work is the recognized need for humanoid robots to operate effectively and interact safely in human-centered environments. Current robust locomotion policies, often developed using reinforcement learning, prioritize accurate command tracking and disturbance rejection. While this robustness is essential for navigation and stability, it frequently leads to behaviors that are overly rigid and resistant to external forces, hindering natural physical human-robot interaction. For instance, a robot designed solely for robustness might resist a human's attempt to guide it, potentially leading to unsafe or unnatural responses.

The key objectives of this research are:

1. **Formulate Humanoid Locomotion as a Multi-Objective Optimization Problem**: To explicitly address the conflict between accurate velocity command tracking and responsive external force compliance, the authors aim to re-frame humanoid locomotion within a multi-objective optimization paradigm. This involves defining distinct reward components for each objective.
2. **Introduce a Velocity–Resistance Model**: To enable consistent and comparable reward design for both velocity commands and external forces, the research seeks to develop a unified representation. This model maps sustained external forces into equivalent velocities, allowing both objectives to be expressed within the same physical space.
3. **Develop a Preference-Conditioned Multi-Objective RL (MORL) Framework**: The core objective is to design and implement an MORL framework that can train a single, omnidirectional locomotion policy. This policy should be capable of smoothly trading off between command following and force compliance based on a user-specified preference input, without requiring multiple policies or complex switching mechanisms.
4. **Enable Deployable Policies through Privileged Reconstruction**: Recognizing that external forces are typically not directly measurable on real robots without specialized sensors, an objective is to integrate an encoder-decoder structure. This structure should infer task-relevant privileged features (like external forces) from deployable observations during training, allowing the policy to adapt to forces during deployment without explicit force sensing.
5. **Validate the Approach in Simulation and on Hardware**: The final objective is to empirically demonstrate the effectiveness and practical applicability of the proposed framework. This involves rigorous validation in simulation, comparing its performance against baseline methods, and subsequently deploying and testing the learned policy on a physical humanoid robot to show its adaptability and force-compliance capabilities in real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method addresses the challenge of balancing command tracking and force compliance in humanoid locomotion through a preference-conditioned multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) framework.

**A. Velocity-Resistance Modeling of External Force:**
To unify velocity commands and external forces for consistent reward design, a velocity–resistance model is introduced. This model assumes that sustained external forces induce an equivalent velocity in a steady-state condition, similar to viscous damping where resistive forces are proportional to velocity (F_res = -B * v). By simplifying this, external forces (F_ext) are mapped to an equivalent velocity (v_ext = k * F_ext), where 'k' is a scaling factor chosen to align the magnitude of equivalent force velocities with typical commanded base velocities. This formulation allows for direct comparison and integration of both objectives within the reward function, promoting stable and intuitive compliant behavior.

**B. Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning Formulation:**
Humanoid locomotion is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where the reward function is extended to a vector capturing multiple objectives. A preference-conditioned policy π(a|o, w) is adopted, where 'w' is a user-specified weight vector (w_c, w_f, w_r) that dictates the trade-off between objectives. The policy aims to maximize the expected return J(π; w) = E[Σγ^t * r(s_t, a_t) * w_i].

Three objectives are defined:
- **Command Tracking (r_c)**: Measures the accuracy of the robot's linear and angular velocity (v) in following the commanded velocity (v_c). Expressed as an exponential function of the squared L2 norm of the difference ||v - v_c||^2.
- **Force Compliance (r_f)**: Quantifies the robot's compliance to external forces. Based on the velocity–resistance model, it measures how well the robot's velocity (v) matches the equivalent velocity induced by the external force (k * F_ext). Also expressed as an exponential function of the squared L2 norm of the difference ||v - k * F_ext||^2.
- **Regularization (r_r)**: A sum of additional rewards for desirable behaviors (e.g., maintaining base height, minimizing energy cost, stability penalties).

The weights w_c and w_f are constrained such that w_c + w_f = 2, allowing a continuous trade-off between command tracking and force compliance.

**C. Policy Training with Privileged Reconstruction:**
The training framework employs a one-stage privileged reconstruction approach, building on an asymmetric actor–critic architecture with an encoder–decoder module.

1. **Observation and Action Space**:
 - **Actor**: Receives deployable observations (o), including proprioception (joint positions, velocities), velocity commands, gait features, projected gravity, angular velocity, and the preference vector 'w'.
 - **Critic**: Additionally accesses privileged information (o_p), available only in simulation, such as external forces, linear velocities, body center of mass, and body mass.
 - **Action**: Desired joint positions (q_des) that are tracked by a low-level PD controller.

2. **Network Architecture and Training Pipeline**:
 - **Encoder–Decoder**: The encoder processes stacked historical observations (o_H) to produce a latent embedding (z_t). The decoder then reconstructs privileged states (o_p_t) from z_t. This forces the encoder to learn task-relevant features, particularly those related to external forces, which are not directly observed by the robot during deployment.
 - **Actor**: Conditioned on the current deployable observation (o_t), the latent embedding (z_t), and the preference vector (w_t).
 - **Critic**: Receives both deployable and privileged observations (o_t, o_p_t).
 - **Optimization**: Actor and critic are updated using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), leveraging the MORL rewards.
 - **Curriculum Learning and Domain Randomization (DR)**: Training starts on flat terrain with increasing velocity perturbations. After half the training, the environment switches to uneven terrain. External forces are randomly applied and resampled during training, initially from U(-50, 50) N/Nm and later narrowed to U(-20, 20) N/Nm to increase sensitivity to interactions. The preference weight w_c is resampled each episode from U(0, 2) (with w_f = 2.0 - w_c), and w_r from U(1, 2) to enhance robustness.
 - **Reconstruction Losses**: The encoder–decoder is optimized jointly with the policy using L_rec = ||o_p_hat - o_p||^2 for general privileged state reconstruction and L_rec,force = ||F_ext_hat - F_ext||^2 to specifically emphasize force-related features.

**Deployment**: At deployment, only the trained encoder and actor networks are used, allowing the robot to adapt its behavior online based on real-time observations and the user-specified preference vector, without requiring direct force sensing. The regularization weight w_r is fixed at 1 for consistency during evaluation.

The training is performed in Isaac Gym with 4096 parallel environments, and the learned policies are validated via sim-to-sim transfer to MuJoCo and real-world experiments on the Booster T1 humanoid robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented extensive evaluation in both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating the capabilities of the proposed preference-conditioned MORL framework.

**A. Simulation Experiments and Analysis:**

1. **Trade-off Performance across Different Preferences**:
 - **Opposite Setting**: When commanded to walk forward and subjected to backward forces (or commanded yaw and external torques), the MORL policy exhibited clear trade-off curves between command tracking and force compliance as the preference weight (w_c) varied. High w_c resulted in better command tracking but less compliance, while low w_c led to higher compliance at the expense of tracking. The resulting velocity responses (Figure 3) confirmed that the policy smoothly interpolated between rigid tracking and compliant yielding, a continuous solution space. The MORL policy with w_c = 2.0 achieved tracking accuracy comparable to the baseline, but significantly outperformed the baseline in compliance at smaller w_c values.
 - **Orthogonal Setting**: When commanded forward velocity with lateral external forces, the MORL policy (Figure 4) generated diagonal walking, effectively combining both objectives. In contrast, the baseline policy primarily tracked the command and failed to adapt to the lateral force.
 - The MORL policy consistently operated without instability or falls across all preference settings.

2. **Adaptation to Online Preference Switching**:
 - The MORL policy demonstrated the ability to smoothly transition between behaviors when preference weights were changed online during a trial (Figure 5). For example, a robot experiencing a constant forward command and backward force would dynamically adjust its compliance or tracking behavior as w_c was altered, without instability. This capability supports dynamic task priorities in interactive deployment.

3. **Ablation Study: MORL vs. Single-Objective RL (SORL)**:
 - Comparison with a standard SORL pipeline (fixed weights w_c=w_f=w_r=1) showed that MORL exhibited more stable training behavior across metrics like total reward, tracking reward, force compliance reward, average survival steps, and auxiliary reconstruction loss (Figure 6).
 - The MORL policy developed adaptive locomotion skills, whereas the SORL policy, trained with a fixed combined objective, showed limited robustness, suggesting that MORL provides a more stable and flexible framework for conflicting objectives.

4. **Robustness under Instant Perturbation**:
 - Under mild perturbations (30N), all policies (baseline, MORL-c [w_c=2, w_f=0], MORL-m [w_c=1, w_f=1], MORL-f [w_c=0, w_f=2]) performed equally well.
 - At higher force magnitudes (40N, 50N), MORL-based policies, particularly MORL-f and MORL-m, showed significantly higher success rates than the baseline. For 50N perturbations, MORL-m and MORL-f maintained a 50% success rate, while the baseline failed completely (0% success).
 - MORL-f consistently achieved the highest success rates and generally exhibited lower peak joint torques, indicating a more compliant response that distributed forces more effectively (Table II).

**B. Real-World Experiments:**

1. **Adaptability to Different Preferences**:
 - On the Booster T1 humanoid, the robot displayed qualitatively distinct behaviors based on preference weights. Higher w_f led to reduced resistance and easier yielding to human guidance, while higher w_c resulted in more precise command execution and increased resistance. The baseline policy lacked this flexibility and became unstable under sustained human-applied forces.

2. **Cross-Directional Walking**:
 - With a balanced preference (w_c=1.5, w_f=0.5, w_r=2.0), the robot successfully performed diagonal walking when commanded forward and subjected to orthogonal external forces (Figure 1b), demonstrating the policy's ability to synthesize multiple objectives in real-time.

3. **Force-Compliance Locomotion**:
 - Quantitative measurements using an external dynamometer (Figure 7) showed that the MORL policy (w_c=0.0, w_f=2.0, w_r=2.0) required only about 10 N of force to move smoothly and efficiently. The baseline policy, in contrast, required over 25 N and sometimes exceeded 30 N, and exhibited abrupt, unmeasurable reactions to applied torque. This indicated lower force requirements and improved responsiveness for human-guided locomotion with the MORL policy.
 - The policy also demonstrated robust force-compliant walking in diverse outdoor environments (Figure 8), including rough ground, soccer fields, and raised surfaces, responding reliably to subtle horizontal guiding forces from a human operator without additional stabilization.

4. **Robustness to Instant Perturbation (Hardware)**:
 - The MORL policy (w_c=1.0, w_f=1.0, w_r=2.0) on the hardware could withstand impacts from suspended balls weighing up to 5 kg (Figure 9). The robot maintained balance, sometimes by taking backward steps, and absorbed the force through compliant motion, aligning with simulation observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a preference-conditioned multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) framework that significantly advances humanoid locomotion capabilities, particularly in the context of human-robot interaction.

The primary significance lies in its ability to **integrate two inherently conflicting objectives—command tracking and external force compliance—into a single, unified locomotion policy**. This is achieved without the need for complex hierarchical controllers, multiple distinct policies, or staged training processes, simplifying the policy architecture and deployment. The introduction of a **velocity–resistance model** provides a consistent framework for designing rewards related to both velocity commands and external forces, offering a novel approach to handle these disparate physical quantities.

A key technical contribution is the **preference-conditioned policy design**, which allows for a continuous spectrum of behaviors ranging from rigid command following to highly compliant responses to external guidance. This adaptability, demonstrated through both online preference switching in simulation and real-world experiments, provides practical flexibility for operators to tune robot behavior based on task requirements or interaction context.

Furthermore, the integration of an **encoder-decoder architecture for privileged reconstruction** is crucial for real-world deployability. By inferring force-related latent features from standard onboard observations, the policy circumvents the need for explicit tactile or force/torque sensors at the interaction point, which often adds complexity and cost to robot hardware.

The robust performance demonstrated in both challenging simulation scenarios and diverse real-world outdoor environments on an adult-sized humanoid robot (Booster T1) highlights the framework's practical utility. The quantitative results on force-compliance, showing significantly reduced force requirements for human guidance compared to baseline policies, underscore its potential for safer and more intuitive human-robot co-working or guidance scenarios. The observed robustness against unexpected perturbations, both in simulation and with physical impacts, suggests that this approach does not compromise the robot's fundamental stability.

The potential impact of this work extends to various applications where humanoids need to coexist and interact physically with humans. This includes domestic assistance, industrial co-workers, and even rescue operations where compliant physical guidance might be necessary. By enabling humanoids to be both precise in following commands and responsive to human touch, this research paves the way for more natural, safe, and collaborative human-robot ecosystems. Future work could explore integrating this framework with richer tasks, higher-dimensional objectives, and more complex forms of human interaction.
