# CAAT: Contact-Aware Attention Scaling and Tactile Masking for Data-Efficient Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> CAAT is a plug-and-play module that injects contact-stage priors into Transformer visuo-tactile policies. Contact-Aware Attention Scaling reweights modality-specific action-query readouts, favoring vision before contact and touch during contact, while Dynamic Tactile Masking drops tactile patches unchanged from a non-contact reference. It lifts ACT by 18 points on UniVTAC and reaches 60% real-world success across three backbones.

## Problem

- Transformer visuo-tactile policies concatenate tactile tokens, forcing the model to infer both when touch matters and how to fuse it from demonstrations alone.
- Contact events occupy only a small fraction of each trajectory, so sparse informative tactile signals are drowned out by abundant non-contact observations during training.
- Learnable gating and adaptive fusion still estimate modality weights from the same scarce demonstrations, needing large datasets and degrading in data-limited contact-rich settings.
- Encoding full tactile images passes static sensor background into the policy, wasting capacity on appearance rather than contact-induced deformation.

## Method

- **Contact-Aware Attention Scaling** splits post-==self-attention== tokens by modality, forms separate ==action-query cross-attention== readouts, and sums them with ==contact-conditioned factors==, 0.7/0.3 vision-tactile flipping at contact.
- **Dynamic Tactile Masking** zeroes tactile ==ViT patch tokens== whose ==cosine similarity== to a frozen non-contact reference token exceeds 0.8, retaining only deformation-bearing regions.
- A lightweight ==CNN-MLP binary classifier== on the absolute difference between current and reference tactile images estimates the ==contact state==, thresholded at probability 0.5.
- A frozen ==ViT-VQGAN== tactile encoder pretrained across GelSight, DIGIT, and 9DTact yields 256 pooled patch tokens; an isomorphic two-finger ==UMI-style tactile gripper== collects demonstrations and deploys policies.

## Results

- On UniVTAC with ACT, fixed numerical scaling averages **69.6%** success, beating direct concatenation by **18.0** and learnable gating by **10.0 percentage points**.
- Real-world across ACT, Diffusion Policy, and pi-0, CAAT averages **60.0%**, raising pi-0 from **36.7%** to **66.7%** and exceeding the strongest baseline by **21.1 points**.
- Fixed numerical scaling (**69.6%**) beats binary (**51.8%**) and learnable (**50.6%**) variants; masking ablation adds **21.5** simulation and **16.7** real-world points to CAAT.
- Under **100 demonstrations** CAAT reaches **38.4%**, exceeding masked ACT by **16.2** and gated ACT by **10.6 points**, with consistent leads at **25** and **50** demos.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An explicit, hand-set structural prior can beat a learned gate when the phenomenon it encodes is rare in the training distribution.
> - Soft 0.7/0.3 weighting outperforms hard binary switching, suggesting suppressed modalities still carry useful signal during off-phase segments.
> - Differencing a sensor stream against a quiescent reference is a cheap, calibration-free way to isolate task-relevant events from static background.
> - Applying modality weighting after joint self-attention preserves cross-modal representation learning that pre-fusion gating destroys.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving physical contact, presents challenges due to the complex interplay of visual and tactile sensory information. Traditional robotic control often relies on precise models and predefined movements, which struggle with the uncertainties and variations inherent in real-world contact-rich scenarios. Recent advancements in deep learning and imitation learning, especially with Transformer-based architectures, have enabled robots to learn complex policies directly from demonstrations. However, integrating multimodal sensory data, such as vision and touch, into these policies remains an open problem.

Existing Transformer-based visuo-tactile policies commonly employ two main strategies for multimodal fusion: direct token concatenation or learnable gating mechanisms. While expressive, these methods often require extensive datasets to implicitly learn the context-dependent utility of each modality. Specifically, visual information is highly valuable for navigation and coarse alignment in free space, providing global context and object locations. Conversely, tactile sensing becomes critical during contact phases for precise force control, slip detection, and local interaction guidance, where visual information might be occluded or insufficient. The challenge lies in the fact that informative tactile signals are often sparse, occurring only for a small fraction of a manipulation trajectory. This sparsity can lead to data inefficiency, as models struggle to discern when and how to prioritize tactile information from limited demonstrations, potentially overwhelming salient tactile signals with abundant non-contact visual observations.

This work addresses this limitation by proposing a framework that explicitly incorporates contact-aware priors into Transformer-based policies. Instead of relying solely on end-to-end learning to infer modality importance, CAAT introduces structural biases that reflect the known phase-dependent roles of vision and touch. This approach aims to improve data efficiency and robustness in learning contact-rich manipulation policies, especially in data-scarce settings. It builds upon the understanding that distinguishing contact and non-contact phases is relatively straightforward, allowing for a more directed allocation of sensory contributions, thereby augmenting and improving upon generalist Transformer architectures for multimodal learning in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-efficient framework for learning effective visuo-tactile policies in contact-rich manipulation tasks. The authors aim to overcome the limitations of standard Transformer-based fusion strategies, such as direct token concatenation or learnable gating, which often lack explicit contact-aware priors and struggle with the phase-dependent sparsity of informative tactile feedback.

The motivation behind this work stems from several key observations:
- **Phase-Dependent Modality Importance:** In contact-rich manipulation, visual observations are primarily useful for guiding movements in free space and for coarse alignment, providing global information. In contrast, tactile observations become critical during contact phases, providing local physical interaction cues necessary for tasks like grasping, preventing slip, and regulating forces during insertion or pulling. Existing fusion methods often treat modalities uniformly, failing to leverage this inherent phase-dependent utility.
- **Data Inefficiency with Sparse Tactile Signals:** Standard end-to-end learning approaches require models to infer when and how to integrate tactile information from demonstrations. Since informative contact events constitute only a small fraction of a typical manipulation trajectory, learning reliable modality allocation from limited demonstrations is challenging. This can lead to sample inefficiency and degraded performance in data-scarce environments.
- **Suppression of Static Background Noise:** Raw tactile observations often contain static background information that is not indicative of contact-induced deformation. Directly encoding these full observations can burden the policy with distinguishing relevant contact signals from sensor appearance, further hindering learning efficiency.

To address these issues, the authors propose the CAAT framework. The core idea is to explicitly incorporate contact-aware priors into the learning process. This involves emphasizing visual information before contact and tactile information during contact, thereby reducing the burden on the model to learn these allocation rules implicitly from sparse demonstrations. Additionally, the framework aims to filter out uninformative tactile background data, allowing the policy to focus on significant contact-induced changes. The overarching goal is to enable Transformer-based policies to more effectively and efficiently utilize multimodal sensory information for robust and dexterous manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CAAT (Contact-Aware Attention Scaling and Tactile Masking) framework is designed to integrate contact-aware priors into Transformer-based visuo-tactile action prediction models. It comprises two main components: Dynamic Tactile Masking (DTM) and Contact-Aware Attention Scaling. CAAT is modular and designed to be compatible with existing Transformer-based policy architectures without requiring modifications to their action decoders.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as an action sequence prediction task. At each timestep `t`, the policy observes a history of visual inputs (`I`), tactile inputs (`T`), and additional conditioning variables (`Υ`, e.g., proprioception). The objective is to predict a horizon of future actions `a` that will complete the manipulation task.

#### 3.2. Tactile Preprocessing

- **Tactile Token Representation:** Raw tactile observations (`T_t`) are first converted into a sequence of spatial patch tokens (`X_τ_t`) using a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder (`E_τ`). This encoder maps the tactile image (e.g., 256x256 pixels) into a grid of features, which are then pooled (e.g., to 16x16) to generate 256 tactile tokens of a specified dimension. The `E_τ` is pre-trained using a ViT-VQGAN framework on a large-scale, multi-sensor corpus, and is frozen during policy training.

- **Dynamic Tactile Masking (DTM):** This component is designed to suppress static non-contact background tokens in the tactile input, allowing the policy to focus on contact-relevant deformations.
 1. A reference tactile observation (`T_0`) is captured during an initial non-contact phase.
 2. Both the current tactile observation (`T_t`) and the reference observation (`T_0`) are encoded into patch tokens (`X_τ_t` and `X_τ_0`) using the pre-trained tactile encoder.
 3. For each tactile patch token, its cosine similarity (`c_t,n`) to the corresponding reference token is computed.
 4. A binary mask (`M_t,n`) is generated based on a similarity threshold (`ρ`). Tokens with similarity greater than or equal to `ρ` are considered static background (masked out with 0), while those below `ρ` are considered contact-induced changes (retained with 1).
 5. The masked tactile token (`˜x_τ_t,n`) is obtained by element-wise multiplication of the original token with the binary mask. The resulting sequence of masked tactile tokens (`˜X_τ_t`) is then used as input to the Transformer policy.

#### 3.3. Contact-Aware Attention Scaling

This component adaptively regulates the contributions of visual and tactile information based on the estimated contact state, emphasizing vision before contact and tactile during contact.
1. **Token Concatenation and Self-Attention:** Visual tokens (`X_v_t`), masked tactile tokens (`˜X_τ_t`), proprioceptive tokens (`X_Υ_t`), and action tokens (`X_a_t`) are concatenated to form a complete input sequence (`X_t`). This sequence is then processed by Transformer self-attention layers to produce contextualized encoded tokens (`H_t`).
2. **Modality-Specific Token Splitting:** The encoded tokens (`H_t`) are split back into their original modality-specific groups: `H_v_t`, `H_τ_t`, `H_Υ_t`, and `H_a_t`.
3. **Modality-Specific Cross-Attention Readouts:** An action query (`Q_a_t`) is derived from the contextualized action tokens. This query then attends separately to the keys and values of each modality (`v`, `τ`, `Υ`) to produce modality-specific readouts (`r_m_t`).
4. **Contact State Estimation:** A lightweight CNN-MLP binary classifier is used to estimate the contact state (`z_t`). It takes the absolute difference between the current tactile observation (`T_t`) and a non-contact reference observation (`T_ref`) as input. The classifier outputs a contact probability (`p_t`), and `z_t` is set to 1 (contact) if `p_t` > 0.5, and 0 (non-contact) otherwise. This classifier is trained with binary cross-entropy using ground-truth contact labels.
5. **Contact-Aware Scaling:** The modality-specific readouts are combined using contact-aware scaling factors (`γ`) that depend on the estimated contact state (`z_t`). The final representation (`ˆr_t`) is computed as: `ˆr_t = γ_v(z_t)r_v_t + γ_τ(z_t)r_τ_t + r_Υ_t`. The scaling functions `γ(·)` are defined such that `γ_v(0) > γ_τ(0)` (vision emphasized before contact) and `γ_τ(1) > γ_v(1)` (tactile emphasized during contact). The paper explores binary (0, 1), learnable, and fixed numerical (0.7, 0.3 or 0.3, 0.7) scaling variants.
6. **Action Prediction:** The scaled multimodal representation (`ˆr_t`) is then passed to the chosen policy's action decoder to predict the sequence of future actions.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Platforms:** Evaluation is conducted in both simulation (UniVTAC benchmark) and real-world settings. For real-world experiments, a custom UMI-inspired human-operated two-finger tactile gripper is developed, featuring PPTac tactile sensors and Dynamixel motors.
- **Policy Backbones:** CAAT is integrated with three representative Transformer-based imitation learning backbones: Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), Diffusion Policy (DP), and π₀.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against direct concatenation of visual and tactile features and fusion via a learnable modality gate.
- **Data:** Models are trained using 150 successful demonstrations per task in both simulation and real-world experiments. DTM is applied consistently across all fusion methods for fair comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents empirical evaluations in both simulation and real-world environments to assess the effectiveness of the CAAT framework.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results on UniVTAC

- **Overall Performance:** On the UniVTAC simulation benchmark, CAAT with fixed numerical scaling achieved an average success rate of 69.6% across five manipulation tasks using ACT as the policy backbone. This represents an 18.0 percentage point improvement over the direct-concatenation baseline (ACT + DTM) and a 10.0 percentage point improvement over the learnable-gating baseline (Gated ACT + DTM). Notable gains were observed in tasks like "Lift Bottle" and "Put Bottle in Shelf."
- **Comparison of Scaling Strategies:** Among the three scaling implementations tested (binary, learnable, and fixed numerical), fixed numerical scaling demonstrated the highest average success rate (69.6%). The binary and learnable variants achieved 51.8% and 50.6% respectively. This suggests that pre-defined numerical scaling can provide a more balanced phase-dependent preference than entirely suppressing a modality or learning weights from limited demonstrations. The fixed numerical scaling strategy was subsequently adopted for real-world experiments.
- **Data Efficiency:** CAAT consistently outperformed both baselines across various demonstration budgets (25, 50, 100, and 150 demonstrations). With 100 demonstrations, CAAT achieved 38.4% average success rate, surpassing ACT + DTM by 16.2 percentage points and Gated ACT + DTM by 10.6 percentage points. This indicates that the contact-aware prior enables more reliable policy learning, particularly in data-limited scenarios.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results

- CAAT demonstrated consistent performance improvements across three diverse contact-rich real-world manipulation tasks ("Lift Bottle," "Open Box," "Powerbank Extraction") and three different policy backbones (ACT, Diffusion Policy, π₀).
- Compared to direct concatenation, CAAT increased the average success rate across tasks by 30 percentage points (from 25.0% to 55.0%) for ACT, by 21.6 percentage points (from 36.7% to 58.3%) for Diffusion Policy, and by 30 percentage points (from 36.7% to 66.7%) for π₀.
- The improvements were particularly significant on the "Open Box" task, where CAAT achieved 50%–60% success rates, compared to 10%–35% for baselines. For "Powerbank Extraction," CAAT achieved up to 90% success with the π₀ backbone. These results suggest that CAAT generalizes across different policy architectures and effectively leverages tactile feedback in real-world conditions.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Analysis of CAAT Attention

Visualizations of Transformer readout attention weights revealed that CAAT modulates multimodal attention across interaction phases as intended. Before contact, the policy primarily attended to visual tokens, using global appearance and spatial information for approach guidance. Upon contact, attention shifted towards tactile tokens, enabling the policy to utilize contact-induced deformations and local interaction cues. This phase-dependent attention shift confirms the effectiveness of Contact-Aware Attention Scaling in directing the policy's focus.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Dynamic Tactile Masking (DTM):** Ablation studies confirmed that DTM consistently improved performance across all three fusion strategies (direct concatenation, learnable gating, and CAAT). Masking increased the average success rate for direct concatenation by 12.8 and 11.1 percentage points in simulation and real-world respectively. Learnable gating showed similar gains (10.0 and 13.3 percentage points). CAAT exhibited the largest gains, with 21.5 percentage points in simulation and 16.7 percentage points in the real world. This indicates that DTM effectively suppresses static background noise, allowing the policy to focus on informative contact changes, and is broadly beneficial.
- **Contact-Aware Attention Scaling:** Removing contact-aware attention scaling (while retaining DTM) led to substantial performance degradation in both simulation and real-world settings, with a more pronounced reduction in real-world experiments. This suggests that dynamically adjusting the relative importance of visual and tactile information is crucial, especially given real-world contact variations and sensory uncertainties. Attention scaling allows for increased tactile contribution during contact-critical phases while maintaining visual guidance in non-contact phases, a capability not present in fixed-fusion approaches.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that both Dynamic Tactile Masking and Contact-Aware Attention Scaling contribute synergistically to improving the data efficiency and overall success rates of visuo-tactile policies across various tasks and architectures, validating the explicit incorporation of contact-aware priors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CAAT framework represents a development in the domain of contact-rich robotic manipulation, particularly for learning policies from limited demonstrations. By explicitly incorporating contact-aware priors, CAAT addresses known limitations of generic multimodal fusion strategies in Transformer-based architectures.

The significance of this work stems from several points:
- **Improved Data Efficiency:** The core contribution of CAAT is its ability to achieve higher success rates with fewer demonstrations compared to baselines. This is critical in robotics, where collecting large, high-quality demonstration datasets can be resource-intensive and time-consuming. By injecting prior knowledge about the phase-dependent utility of vision and touch, CAAT reduces the burden on the learning algorithm to discover these fundamental allocation rules from scratch, leading to more efficient learning.
- **Robustness in Contact-Rich Tasks:** Contact-rich manipulation tasks are inherently challenging due to the need for precise local interaction control. CAAT's emphasis on tactile information during contact phases and visual information during non-contact phases enables policies to leverage the most relevant sensory input at each stage, contributing to more robust task execution.
- **Generalizability Across Architectures:** The modular, plug-and-play nature of CAAT allows it to be integrated into diverse Transformer-based policy backbones (ACT, Diffusion Policy, π₀) without modifying their core action decoders. This demonstrated generalizability enhances the potential for widespread adoption and application across different imitation learning frameworks.
- **Enhanced Interpretability:** The qualitative analysis of attention heatmaps indicates that CAAT's components function as intended, directing the policy's attention to visual cues before contact and tactile cues during contact. This phase-dependent shift provides a degree of interpretability regarding how the multimodal information is being processed, which can be valuable for understanding and debugging robotic policies.
- **Hardware and Data Collection Tool:** The development of the UMI-inspired two-finger tactile gripper for real-world demonstration collection is a practical contribution. By providing an isomorphic platform for both human demonstration and robot deployment, it helps to mitigate the embodiment gap, which is often a challenge in sim-to-real transfer and real-world robot learning.

The potential impact of this research is broad. It could facilitate the deployment of robots in complex real-world scenarios requiring dexterous, contact-rich manipulation, such as industrial assembly, domestic assistance, or surgical tasks. By reducing the data requirements for learning, CAAT could lower the barrier to entry for developing and training new robotic manipulation skills. The principles of contact-aware attention scaling and dynamic tactile masking could also inspire similar context-aware fusion strategies in other multimodal learning domains where sensory relevance varies dynamically over time or across different task phases.
