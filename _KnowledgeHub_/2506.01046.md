# STATE-NAV: Stability-Aware Traversability Estimation for Bipedal Navigation on Rough Terrain

> [!summary] Summary
> The STATE-NAV framework introduces a learning-based approach for bipedal robots to navigate rough terrain by predicting locomotion instability and its uncertainty. Utilizing a Transformer network (TravFormer) and a novel Body-to-Stance-Foot Angle (BSFA) instability metric, the system defines traversability as a stability-aware command velocity, enabling robust and efficient navigation in both simulations and real-world outdoor environments with a Digit robot.

## Problem

- Existing learning-based traversability methods are primarily designed for wheeled or quadrupedal robots, lacking specific models for bipedal instability.
- Bipedal robots rely heavily on manually designed rules for traversability, which often fail to accurately reflect locomotion stability and generalize across diverse, complex terrains.
- Navigation planning for bipedal robots frequently overlooks the critical aspect of motion aggressiveness and often fails to incorporate predicted instability and its uncertainty for risk-aware path selection.

## Method

- Identified the Body-to-==Stance-Foot Angle (BSFA)== as a reliable self-supervised metric for bipedal instability, demonstrating its strong correlation with fallover risk.
- Developed TravFormer, a Transformer-based neural network, to predict BSFA instability and its uncertainty from terrain elevation maps and robot command velocities.
- Defined traversability as a stability-aware command velocity, determined by an iterative search that ensures risk-sensitive predicted instability (using Value at Risk) remains below a user-defined limit, and integrated this into a hierarchical planning framework (TravRRT* global planner and MPC local planner).

## Results

- TravFormer achieved an RMSE of **0.7065** and accurate uncertainty estimation (|∆PICP| of **0.0007**) for instability prediction, outperforming simpler architectures.
- In simulation, STATE-NAV achieved **100%** success rates with lower maximum instability (**4.82**-**5.37**) on rough and ramp terrains, compared to baselines (70-**90%** success, **7.09**-**8.31** max instability) that often traversed hazardous areas.
- Successfully validated on a Digit humanoid robot in three diverse outdoor real-world environments, navigating goals while maintaining maximum BSFA instabilities between **1.7** and **3.4** in real time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Body-to-Stance-Foot Angle (BSFA) instability provides a highly effective and bipedal-specific self-supervised signal for training traversability estimators.
> - Integrating command velocity and terrain features via attention mechanisms in a Transformer architecture (TravFormer) significantly improves the prediction of bipedal instability and its associated uncertainty.
> - Defining traversability as a stability-aware command velocity, rather than a simple cost, enables more robust, environment-agnostic planning by allowing the robot to adapt its speed to maintain stability, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of mobile robotics has seen significant advancements in autonomous navigation, particularly for wheeled and quadrupedal platforms. These platforms often benefit from static stability or larger support polygons, simplifying traversability estimation. Existing research in this area has extensively utilized learning-based methods, often employing self-supervised signals derived from proprioceptive features like traction, IMU variances, or energy consumption, to quantify terrain difficulty.

However, bipedal robots, while offering advantages in traversing human-centered and complex, rough environments, face unique challenges in maintaining stability due to their limited support area and higher center of mass. Traditional approaches to bipedal traversability have largely relied on manually designed rules based on geometric features (e.g., slope, height differences). These rule-based methods often provide an incomplete representation of locomotion stability and performance, struggling to generalize across diverse terrains and to account for the complex dynamics of bipedal walking. Furthermore, direct application of self-supervised features developed for wheeled or quadrupedal robots to bipedal systems is not straightforward due to fundamental differences in locomotion strategies.

This work addresses the gap in learning-based, stability-aware traversability estimation specifically for bipedal robots. It introduces a data-driven approach to identify a suitable instability metric for bipedal locomotion, develops a neural network to predict this instability with uncertainty, and integrates this estimation into a hierarchical planning framework. By focusing on bipedal-specific instability and defining traversability as a stability-aware command velocity, the research aims to provide a more robust and adaptive navigation solution compared to previous rule-based or general learning-based methods, particularly in diverse and rough terrain. It contributes to the growing body of work seeking to enhance the autonomy and robustness of humanoid robots in challenging environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are motivated by the inherent challenges bipedal robots face when navigating diverse and rough terrains, specifically their increased risk of failure compared to more stable mobile platforms. The authors identify several limitations in existing approaches that their work aims to overcome:

- **Lack of Bipedal-Specific, Learning-Based Traversability Estimation:** Current learning-based traversability methods are predominantly developed for wheeled or quadrupedal robots, using locomotion features (e.g., traction, IMU variances) whose relevance to bipedal instability is uncertain. Bipedal robots currently rely heavily on manually designed rules based on terrain geometry, which often fail to accurately reflect actual locomotion stability and performance in complex environments. A key objective is therefore to develop the first learning-based traversability estimator specifically for humanoid robots that directly accounts for locomotion stability.
- **Need for an Effective Self-Supervised Instability Signal:** For a data-driven approach, identifying a reliable, self-supervised signal that robustly correlates with bipedal fallover risk is crucial. Existing features from other robot types are not directly applicable. Thus, a significant objective is to identify and validate a locomotion feature that best reflects bipedal instability and can serve as a supervisory signal for training a neural network.
- **Incorporating Instability and Uncertainty into Navigation Planning:** Traditional navigation planning for bipedal robots often overlooks the critical aspect of motion aggressiveness, such as command velocity, which is crucial for safety. Furthermore, ignoring uncertainty in stability predictions can lead to risky paths. The authors aim to integrate predicted bipedal instability and its uncertainty directly into a navigation framework to enable risk-aware and adaptive planning.
- **Developing a Robust and Environment-Agnostic Planning Framework:** Many planning approaches use traversability as a tunable cost term, requiring constant re-tuning of weights for different environments. This is particularly problematic for bipedal robots where stability is paramount. The objective is to define traversability in a manner that inherently prioritizes stability and enables consistent performance across diverse terrains without extensive hyperparameter tuning. This involves developing a hierarchical planning framework that leverages this stability-aware traversability.

In summary, the motivation is to advance bipedal robot navigation from heuristic-based, limited stability awareness to a data-driven, learning-based paradigm that explicitly models and leverages bipedal instability with uncertainty for safe, efficient, and robust locomotion across a wide range of challenging terrains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, STATE-NAV, integrates a learning-based instability estimator (TravFormer) with a hierarchical planning framework for bipedal navigation.

**A. Instability and Fallover Prediction (Self-Supervised Signal Identification)**
To develop a bipedal-specific instability estimator, the authors first identified a suitable self-supervised signal.
- **Data Collection:** Walking data was collected from a Digit humanoid robot in a MuJoCo simulator across various rough terrains (slopes, bumps, surface roughness) with varying linear and angular command velocities, controlled by a terrain-aware ALIP controller.
- **Feature Analysis:** Various locomotion features (e.g., energy, IMU signals, traction, Center of Pressure) were computed as Root-Mean-Square (RMS) values per gait cycle. Each cycle was labeled for a fallover event (1 if fall within two steps, 0 otherwise). Features were normalized by their RMS during in-place stepping on flat ground.
- **Correlation Analysis:** Logistic regression was used to evaluate the correlation between each feature and fallover events, using McFadden's R² and AUC-ROC scores.
- **Body-to-Stance-Foot Angle (BSFA) Instability:** The Body-to-stance-foot angle (BSFA) was identified as providing the highest prediction accuracy for fallover risk, indicating its strong correlation with bipedal instability. This metric, specifically its RMS per cycle (referred to as BSFA instability δ), was selected as the supervisory signal.

**B. Learning Instability with TravFormer**
A Transformer-based neural network, TravFormer, was designed to predict BSFA instability (δ) and its uncertainty (σ) from environmental geometry and robot command.
- **Inputs:** The model takes a 0.64m x 0.64m robot-centric (yaw-aligned) 2.5D elevation map patch ($m_{ego}$) as geometric input and a command vector ($a = [v, \omega]^T$) consisting of linear and angular velocities.
- **Outputs:** TravFormer predicts the mean ($\hat{\delta}$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$) of the BSFA instability, modeling δ as a Gaussian distribution. σ represents aleatoric uncertainty from perception noise and unmodeled effects.
- **Architecture:**
 - **Terrain Feature Extraction:** A ResNet-18 backbone processes the elevation map patch to extract spatial features.
 - **Attention Mechanisms:** Spatial attention is used within the ResNet, and deformable self-attention further refines these features.
 - **Command Integration:** A Transformer Decoder treats command embeddings as queries and terrain features as keys/values to contextually integrate command inputs with terrain features.
 - **Output Heads:** MLPs produce the final $\hat{\delta}$ and $\sigma$.
- **Training:** A two-phase training schedule was employed:
 1. **Phase 1 (MSE Training):** Initial training minimizes the Mean Squared Error between predicted $\hat{\delta}$ and the collected BSFA instability labels. This establishes a strong foundation.
 2. **Phase 2 (Gaussian Negative Log-Likelihood - NLL Training):** After initialization from Phase 1, the network is extended to predict both $\hat{\delta}$ and $\sigma$. Training minimizes the Gaussian NLL loss, optimizing both the mean prediction and the uncertainty estimation.

**C. Traversability as Stability-Aware Command Velocity**
Instead of a simple cost, traversability is defined as the maximum command velocity ($a^* = (v^*, \omega^*)$) that keeps the risk-sensitive predicted instability below a user-defined limit ($\delta_{limit}$), accounting for uncertainty.
- **Risk-Sensitive Criterion:** Value at Risk (VaR) is employed, ensuring that the predicted instability does not exceed $\delta_{limit}$ at a specified risk level ($\alpha$). This mitigates overestimation of safe velocities, especially when uncertainty increases with higher command velocities.
- **Iterative Search:** For each local map patch, $v^*$ and $\omega^*$ are determined by iteratively sweeping command velocities downwards from a maximum candidate (e.g., 0.5 m/s, 0.75 rad/s) until the VaR-based stability criterion is met. If even zero velocity exceeds the threshold, minimal velocities (0.001 m/s, 0.001 rad/s) are assigned to penalize traversal. This approach allows for environment-agnostic tuning of $\delta_{limit}$, which is a robot-specific hyperparameter.

**D. Hierarchical Bipedal Navigation Planning**
A hierarchical planning framework combines global and local planners.
- **Traversability-Informed RRT\* (TravRRT\*) Global Planner:**
 - **Objective:** Generates a navigation-time-optimal global path while maintaining instability below the defined limit.
 - **Informed Sampling:** Samples nodes with probability proportional to local traversability (stability-aware command velocity), guiding the search towards safer, more navigable regions.
 - **Cost Calculation:** The cost of an edge between nodes is defined as the expected traversal time, incorporating both linear and angular displacements and the stability-aware linear ($v^*_k$) and angular ($\omega^*_k$) velocities at intermediate points along the edge.
- **MPC Local Planner:**
 - **Objective:** Ensures stable, dynamically feasible execution of the global path generated by TravRRT\* by tracking waypoints.
 - **Dynamics Model:** Utilizes the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) to derive discrete dynamics for CoM position and velocity changes as a function of footstep length and heading change. These local dynamics are transformed into 2D world coordinates.
 - **Optimization Problem:** Formulates an MPC problem to minimize a cost function (terminal cost for reaching the goal, running cost for smooth motion) over a prediction horizon.
 - **Stability Constraint:** Critically, the stability-aware command velocities ($v^*$, $\omega^*$) from the TravFormer are incorporated as a constraint on the local CoM velocity and heading change, ensuring that the robot's commanded velocities remain within stability limits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, evaluating the performance of the TravFormer instability estimator and the complete STATE-NAV navigation framework.

**A. Instability Learning with TravFormer**
An ablation study on TravFormer variants was conducted to assess the contribution of different architectural components to instability prediction and uncertainty estimation.
- **Performance Comparison (Table II):** The full TravFormer model, incorporating spatial attention and deformable self-attention, achieved an RMSE of 0.7065 and a |∆PICP| of 0.0007.
- **Contribution of Components:** Variants with the Transformer Decoder (full model, w/o spatial attention, w/o deformable self-attention) consistently outperformed the MLP decoder variant by approximately 11.8% in RMSE, indicating the effectiveness of the attention mechanisms for integrating command inputs. The full model demonstrated the best uncertainty estimation capabilities, achieving the lowest |∆PICP|.

**B. Planner Benchmarking in Simulation**
The STATE-NAV framework was benchmarked against three baselines in MuJoCo simulation across diverse terrains (rough terrain, ramp, challenging terrain):
- **Baselines:**
 - **Learned Instability (LearnedInS):** Uses TravFormer's instability prediction (with a fixed maximum linear command) as a cost.
 - **Manual Traversability Cost Design for Bipeds (ManualBiped):** Relies on manually designed rules based on slope and height differences.
 - **Quadrupedal Foothold Score (QuadFoothold):** A method originally for quadrupeds, adapted for cost.
- **Metrics:** Success rate, maximum instability, path smoothness (mean angular acceleration), and navigation time. Baselines were tested with different trade-off weights (w=0.5, 3) for traversability vs. path length, while STATE used a fixed $\delta_{limit}$=3.

**1. Benefit of Traversability Learning for Bipedal Locomotion:**
- **Rough Terrain (Fig. 4, Table III):** QuadFoothold and ManualBiped baselines failed to accurately assess risk in stepped regions, resulting in paths traversing hazardous areas, leading to lower success rates (70-90%) and higher maximum instability (7.09-8.31). In contrast, STATE and LearnedInS (with w=3) accurately identified stepped areas as dangerous, planning paths that avoided them, achieving 100% success rates and lower maximum instability (4.82-5.37). This highlights the advantage of a bipedal instability-supervised estimator.

**2. Benefit of Stability-Aware Command Velocity-based Traversability in Global Planning:**
- **Rough Terrain vs. Ramp (Figs. 4 & 5, Table III):** STATE consistently planned safe and efficient paths across both rough terrain and ramp environments with a single hyperparameter ($\delta_{limit}=3$). LearnedInS, however, showed environment dependency: w=3 performed well on rough terrain but resulted in unsafe paths (80% success rate, 7.39 max instability) and longer navigation times on the ramp due to the different scales of path length and traversability score. The authors note that LearnedInS required extensive re-tuning or modifications (e.g., using p=2 in the cost function, which then made it overly conservative on other terrains) to achieve stability on the ramp, whereas STATE's $\delta_{limit}$ is intuitive and robot-specific, not environment-specific.

**3. Benefit of Stability-Aware Command Velocity-based Traversability in MPC Local Planning:**
- **Challenging Terrain (Fig. 6, Table IV):** On challenging terrain where global paths were nearly identical for STATE and LearnedInS (w=3), STATE achieved a higher success rate (80% vs. 60% for LearnedInS) while maintaining a better balance between maximum instability (6.54) and navigation time (61.6 s). This indicates that the adaptive velocity regulation enabled by the stability-aware command velocity in the local MPC planner allows for more effective adaptation to terrain conditions, modulating speed to maintain stability where necessary and accelerating on favorable terrain.

**C. Real-World Validation**
The framework was validated on a Digit humanoid robot in three outdoor real-world environments (Fig. 7).
- **Setup:** The robot used a ZED 2i stereo camera for perception and localization, with algorithms running on an external laptop. Minor modifications (MPC horizon to 7, TravRRT\* iterations to 400) were made for hardware transfer.
- **Performance:** The robot successfully navigated diverse terrains, including ground obstacles and slopes. It reached goals in 54s, 32s, and 34s, maintaining maximum BSFA instabilities of 3.4, 2.0, and 1.7, respectively. The system demonstrated real-time performance with continuous updates of the traversability map and global plan (every 5s) and MPC local planner (3Hz), illustrating the practicality of the framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable advancement in the field of bipedal robot navigation, particularly for operation in complex, unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Novelty in Bipedal Traversability Estimation:** The introduction of TravFormer as the first learning-based traversability estimator specifically designed for humanoid robots on diverse rough terrain represents a departure from traditional rule-based methods. This data-driven approach, leveraging self-supervised learning with an identified bipedal-specific instability metric (BSFA), is designed to provide a more accurate and nuanced understanding of terrain difficulty for bipedal locomotion.
- **Enhanced Stability and Risk Awareness:** By predicting instability with uncertainty and integrating this into a Value at Risk (VaR) framework, the system enables risk-sensitive planning. This capability is crucial for bipedal robots, which inherently have a higher failure risk than other mobile platforms. The stability-aware command velocity definition intrinsically prioritizes safety, allowing the robot to adapt its speed to terrain conditions rather than relying on generic cost functions.
- **Robust and Environment-Agnostic Planning:** The definition of traversability as a stability-aware command velocity allows for optimization of navigation time while maintaining stability within a robot-specific threshold ($\delta_{limit}$). This approach reduces the need for extensive, environment-specific hyperparameter tuning, which is a common challenge in traditional cost-based planning. This robustness could simplify deployment across varied operational environments.
- **Demonstrated Real-World Applicability:** The successful validation of the framework on a physical humanoid robot (Digit) in outdoor environments underscores its practical utility. This transition from simulation to hardware, with minimal modifications, suggests the potential for wider adoption in real-world applications requiring bipedal robots to navigate challenging terrains.

The potential impact of this research includes:
- **Increased Autonomy for Humanoid Robots:** By enabling more reliable and safer navigation, this work contributes to the development of highly autonomous humanoid robots capable of operating in human-centered and complex environments such as disaster zones, industrial facilities, or urban areas.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The method provides a foundation for future investigations into bipedal locomotion. Future directions mentioned, such as active exploration using the humanoid's field of view and incorporating semantic perception for terrain property estimation, indicate avenues for further enhancing robustness and adaptability.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction and Trust:** Robots that can safely and reliably navigate their environments are more likely to be accepted and trusted by human operators and the public. Improved stability and reduced failure rates contribute to this trust.

Overall, this research addresses a critical gap in bipedal robotics, offering a comprehensive and validated approach to stability-aware navigation that could significantly advance the capabilities and deployment of humanoid robots in practical applications.
