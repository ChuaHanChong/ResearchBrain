# H-Zero: Cross-Humanoid Locomotion Pretraining Enables Few-shot Novel Embodiment Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> H-Zero introduces a cross-humanoid locomotion pretraining pipeline that learns a generalizable policy across diverse robot designs. This approach allows for efficient few-shot transfer to novel embodiments, reducing controller development effort from tens of thousands of training epochs to a few hundred or thousand.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) controllers are typically tailored to specific humanoid robot designs, requiring extensive retraining for each new variant.
- The high degrees of freedom and complex dynamics of humanoid robots make control development resource-intensive and unscalable.
- A lack of efficient algorithms exists to achieve robust and versatile locomotion across the rapidly proliferating diverse humanoid embodiments.

## Method

- A pretraining pipeline learns a generalizable locomotion policy by exposing it to a diverse set of existing humanoid and quadruped robot models.
- Unified control semantics and embodiment descriptors standardize observation and action spaces across heterogeneous robot morphologies, leveraging descriptors as privileged information for the critic.
- An embodiment-aware learning strategy, including extended domain randomization, adaptive exploration, and dynamic loss reweighting, enhances generalization and balances learning progress across diverse robots.

## Results

- Pretrained policies achieved up to 81% mean normalized episode length on unseen robots when trained with broader embodiment diversity and extended domain randomization.
- H-Zero enables few-shot transfer, adapting to novel robots in hundreds to a few thousand fine-tuning epochs (approximately 30 minutes), a substantial reduction compared to tens of thousands of epochs for training from scratch.
- Qualitative evaluations on real-world robots like Aliengo, T1, and Adam Lite confirm that few-shot fine-tuning from the pretrained policy yields stable and responsive locomotion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pretraining on a diverse set of real-world robot models, combined with extended domain randomization, is critical for a policy to generalize to unseen embodiments.
> - Providing embodiment-specific physical parameters as 'privileged information' to the critic, rather than directly to the policy, significantly improves performance and stability.
> - Adaptive exploration sampling variances and dynamic loss reweighting ensure balanced learning progress across morphologically diverse robots during the pretraining phase.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is undergoing a rapid transformation, particularly with the advent of diverse and customized humanoid robot designs. This rapid proliferation has created an urgent demand for control algorithms that can adapt robustly and efficiently across various embodiments. Traditionally, developing controllers for humanoid robots, characterized by their anthropomorphic structure, high degrees of freedom (DoFs), and complex dynamics, has been a significant challenge. Existing Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) solutions, while powerful, are predominantly tailored to specific robot designs. This morphology-specificity necessitates extensive, resource-intensive retraining and fine-tuning of reward functions, physical parameters, and training hyperparameters for each new robot variant. Such an approach is neither scalable nor practical given the increasing diversity of humanoid platforms.

H-Zero directly addresses this critical bottleneck by positioning itself within the emerging landscape of generalizable robot control. Prior research in legged locomotion via DRL has demonstrated remarkable agility and robustness for specific robots, including quadrupeds- and humanoids-, often leveraging techniques like domain randomization and sim-to-real transfer. However, these methods typically treat each robot as a distinct learning problem.

The broader research landscape has seen efforts to move beyond single-robot specificity. Some approaches explore specialized network architectures, such as Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), to capture morphological structure, or transformer-based methods- to handle flexible observation and action dimensions. While effective, transformer-based solutions can be computationally demanding. Other works have investigated procedural generation of randomized embodiments, to improve policy generalization, but this often requires considerable expert knowledge to define the distribution of generated robots and faces challenges in real-robot application.

H-Zero differentiates itself by proposing a novel pretraining pipeline that aims to learn a *generalizable humanoid base policy* by leveraging a diverse *set of existing robot models* rather than purely procedurally generated ones. This approach, termed "targeted extrapolation from real-world designs," seeks to strike a balance between broad randomization and practical applicability. By introducing "unified control semantics" and "embodiment descriptors," H-Zero provides a standardized interface for learning across heterogeneous robot designs, thereby addressing the incompatibility of action and observation spaces that plagues multi-robot control. This method aligns with the growing trend of "pretraining" in AI, seeking to create foundational models that can be quickly adapted to novel tasks or embodiments, much like large language models in NLP, but applied to the complex physical domain of robotics. The work aims to offer a more efficient and less resource-intensive path to robust humanoid control, moving beyond the traditional paradigm of "training from scratch" for every new robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind the H-Zero project stems from the burgeoning diversity of humanoid robot designs and the inherent inefficiencies of current control development paradigms. As the Introduction succinctly states, "The rapid proliferation of novel and customized humanoid robot designs has intensified the demand for control algorithms capable of enabling robust and versatile locomotion across diverse embodiments." Humanoid robots, with their high degrees of freedom and anthropomorphic structures, offer immense versatility but simultaneously pose significant challenges for controller development due to complex dynamics and varying morphologies.

Current Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) solutions, while powerful in training controllers for complex tasks like bipedal walking and dynamic balancing, are typically "tailored to specific robot designs." This specialization means that even minor variations in joint layouts, mass distributions, or limb dimensions necessitate "resource-intensive retraining from scratch," involving extensive tuning of reward functions, physical parameters, and training hyperparameters. This lack of generalizability represents a significant bottleneck for the scalable deployment and adoption of new humanoid platforms.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the H-Zero research are clearly defined:

- **Develop a Generalizable Humanoid Base Policy:** The primary objective is to create "H-Zero, a novel pretraining pipeline for developing generalized locomotion policies for humanoid robots." This pipeline aims to learn a single, robust base policy capable of controlling multiple humanoid models concurrently.
- **Enable Zero-shot and Few-shot Transfer:** A critical goal is to demonstrate that this pretrained policy can adapt to novel humanoid robots with "minimal fine-tuning" and "minimal additional training time." Specifically, the paper aims to show "zero-shot and few-shot transfer to novel humanoid robots."
- **Reduce Development Resources:** By enabling rapid adaptation, the project seeks to "significantly reduc[e] the need for extensive retraining" and minimize the data and computational resources required for deploying controllers on new hardware.
- **Ensure Robust and Stable Locomotion:** The ultimate aim is to ensure the generalizable policy can enable "stable and efficient locomotion" across diverse platforms, both in simulation and on real hardware, thereby addressing the fundamental need for reliable robotic movement.

In essence, H-Zero is motivated by the desire to accelerate the development and deployment of humanoid robots by making their control systems highly adaptable and efficient, moving away from a costly, robot-specific development cycle towards a more unified and scalable approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

H-Zero proposes a comprehensive cross-humanoid locomotion pretraining pipeline built on several innovative technical components designed to foster generalization across diverse robot embodiments.

**A. Extended POMDP Formulation:**
The framework extends the standard Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) formulation for locomotion control by augmenting the state space `S` to include not only the robot's kinematic states (`Q`) but also embodiment-specific parameters (`E`). This explicitly incorporates morphological and physical variations into the learning problem: `S = Q × E`. During training, an embodiment `e ∈ E` is sampled and fixed per rollout, exposing the policy to a continuous range of morphologies.

**B. Unified Control Semantics:**
To enable a single policy to control diverse robots, H-Zero introduces a "unified control representation." This is achieved by:
- **Standardizing Joint Semantics:** Instead of relying on robot-specific joint configurations, a hardware-agnostic joint state space is defined. This space comprises current positions, velocities, and target positions for key rotational joints (e.g., head, shoulders, knees, ankles), identified by their kinematic roles.
- **Bidirectional Transformation (M):** A matrix `M` is used to map between a robot's physical joint space (`q_phy`) and this unified environment space (`q_env`). This mapping handles varying degrees of freedom by encoding index assignments and zero-padding.
- **Kinematic Alignment:** To ensure that control commands produce equivalent physical motions across different robots, a kinematic alignment step applies scaling (`s`) and offset (`b`) to joint directions and neutral positions, standardizing them to an upright pose.

**C. Embodiment Descriptors:**
A key innovation for achieving embodiment-aware control is the introduction of "embodiment descriptors." These are compact, informative features that encode crucial physical properties of each robot, including:
- **Kinematics:** Joint limits, rotation axes.
- **Topology:** Hierarchical joint structures.
- **Geometry:** Link lengths, shapes.
- **Dynamics:** Mass, inertia, stiffness, damping.
These descriptors are structured according to the unified joint space for consistent semantics. Critically, H-Zero finds that treating these descriptors as "privileged information" (accessible only to the value function in an asymmetric actor-critic architecture) performs better than incorporating them directly into the policy observation space. This allows the policy to implicitly adapt to the robot's physical properties without explicit, potentially confounding, raw parameter inputs.

**D. Embodiment Training Set and Extended Domain Randomization (DR):**
Unlike approaches that procedurally generate entirely randomized hardware, H-Zero curates a diverse set of *existing* robot models (e.g., Unitree H1, N1, G1, GR1, Go2, A1, etc.) for pretraining. This set encompasses a variety of kinematic structures and physical properties. To further enhance generalization, the method employs "extended domain randomization":
- The perturbation bounds for rigid-body and joint dynamics (e.g., friction, mass) are significantly broadened (e.g., doubled) compared to single-robot training.
- Structural diversity is introduced by mixing these distinct existing robot models.
This approach aims for "targeted extrapolation from real-world designs," allowing the training distribution to cover a wider range of dynamics and morphologies that are more likely to be encountered in novel, real-world robots (as visualized in Figure 3).

**E. Embodiment-Aware Learning Strategy:**
Recognizing the increased learning challenges posed by diverse robot dynamics, H-Zero implements an adaptive training strategy:
- **Unified Reward Formulation with Tailored Coefficients:** While the reward function structure remains identical across robots (e.g., velocity tracking, base height maintenance), its coefficients (e.g., nominal base height, stance width) are dynamically tailored to each embodiment.
- **Adaptive Exploration:** Independent exploration sampling variances (`σ`) are assigned to each robot type. These variances are adaptively updated based on gradients from each embodiment's learning progress. Robots showing slower learning receive higher variance to encourage broader action sampling, while more stable ones use reduced variance.
- **Dynamic Loss Reweighting:** The total loss is dynamically reweighted based on the average episodic return of each embodiment (`L_total = Σ w_i L_i`, where `w_i` increases for underperforming robots). This ensures that robots struggling with the task receive increased gradient emphasis, promoting balanced learning across the diverse set.

**F. Cross-Embodiment Transfer Protocol:**
The pretrained policy, leveraging the unified semantics and embodiment-aware learning, serves as a "robust base policy." For novel robots, H-Zero proposes:
- **Zero-shot Inference:** Directly deploying the pretrained policy without any fine-tuning.
- **Few-shot Fine-tuning:** Adapting the pretrained policy using a small set of trajectories specific to the novel robot. A crucial design choice here is to use a low action variance during initial sampling to prevent destabilizing valid locomotion behaviors learned during pretraining, leading to faster adaptation.

By integrating these components, H-Zero constructs a powerful framework that enables a single policy to learn generalizable locomotion skills across a diverse range of humanoids, facilitating rapid and efficient transfer to unseen robot embodiments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of H-Zero focuses on three key research questions: the scalability of the pretrained policy with training embodiment diversity, its few-shot adaptation efficiency, and its generalization to real-world deployment. The experiments are conducted in the Isaac Gym simulator using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, with policies trained for 50,000 epochs. Performance is quantified using normalized episode length and tracking errors for linear and angular velocities.

**A. Cross-Embodiment Pretraining Performance:**
- **Effect of Embodiment Training Set:** Ablation studies (Table II) demonstrate a clear correlation between the diversity and number of training embodiments and the policy's generalization performance on unseen robots. Policies trained on a broader set, especially those including morphologically distinct robots (e.g., mixing humanoids with quadrupeds like A1 and Go2), consistently achieve higher normalized episode lengths (up to 81% mean for unseen robots) compared to policies trained on fewer or less diverse sets. This highlights the importance of comprehensive embodiment coverage during pretraining. Figure 5 further validates that dynamic adjustment of sampling variances and loss scales across embodiments leads to more balanced and effective learning progress.
- **Impact of Domain Randomization (DR):** Ablation B confirms that expanding the domain randomization range (e.g., doubling or quadrupling perturbation bounds) significantly improves transferability to unseen robots. Visualization via t-SNE (Figure 3) illustrates that extended DR broadens the coverage of the training distribution, making trajectories from unseen robots without randomization overlap more with the generalized training data, thus facilitating better transfer. While DR is crucial, Figure 4 shows that even with strong randomization, embodiment parameters for different robots form distinct, non-overlapping clusters, implying that randomization perturbs *within* an embodiment's characteristics rather than blurring the fundamental differences *between* embodiments.
- **Embodiment Descriptors:** Ablation C reveals that policies using *privileged* embodiment descriptors (input to the critic only) perform substantially better than those with *observable* descriptors (input to the policy). This suggests that providing explicit, raw physical parameters directly to the policy can be detrimental, possibly due to the disjoint nature of these parameters across different robot types (Figure 4), making it challenging for the policy to learn a unified representation from them. The critic, with its higher-level role, can better leverage this privileged information.
- **Action Space Size:** Ablation D indicates that limiting the action space to only leg joints (12 DoFs) yields better performance than incorporating whole-body control (32 DoFs, including waist and upper-body). This is likely due to the increased dimensionality of the control problem and potentially weak reward coupling for upper-body joints, signaling the need for more targeted objectives or regularization for full whole-body control.

**B. Few-shot Transfer Efficiency:**
The few-shot transfer evaluations (Table III) demonstrate a significant advantage of pretraining. Policies initialized from H-Zero's pretrained weights (P) adapt to novel unseen robots (e.g., Adam, T1, H1-2, AGo) much more rapidly than policies trained from scratch (S). For instance, a pretrained policy could match or exceed the performance of a scratch-trained policy (e.g., 52 mean return for Adam with P 2k vs S 50k epochs) with only *hundreds* to *a few thousand* epochs of fine-tuning, compared to *tens of thousands* of epochs required for training from scratch. This translates to substantial savings in training time and computational resources (e.g., 30 minutes of fine-tuning for few-shot transfer). Furthermore, Figure 6 highlights that using a reduced initial action sampling variance during fine-tuning is crucial for faster adaptation, as it preserves the valid locomotion behaviors learned during pretraining.

**C. Real-world Performance:**
Qualitative evaluation on multiple real hardware platforms (Aliengo, T1, H1-2, Adam Lite) provides compelling evidence for the practical utility of H-Zero (Figure 7).
- **Zero-shot Transfer:** The pretrained base policy shows "moderate adaptability" to unseen real robots but often exhibits "jittery motion, velocity drift, and instabilities" at higher velocity commands.
- **Few-shot Adaptation:** Fine-tuning the pretrained policy for the specific target robot, even for a few iterations, significantly mitigates these issues, resulting in "more stable locomotion and responsive control." This confirms that while the base policy provides a strong foundation, minimal robot-specific adaptation is key for robust real-world deployment.

In summary, H-Zero successfully demonstrates that cross-embodiment pretraining enables efficient zero-shot and superior few-shot transfer to novel humanoid and even quadrupedal robots in both simulation and the real world, substantially reducing the effort required for new robot controller development.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The H-Zero framework represents a significant step forward in the field of humanoid robotics and reinforcement learning, with far-reaching implications for the design, development, and deployment of complex robotic systems.

**A. Significance:**
- **Addresses a Critical Scalability Challenge:** H-Zero directly tackles one of the most pressing issues in humanoid robotics: the inability of current DRL approaches to scale efficiently with the proliferation of diverse robot designs. By providing a method for learning a generalizable base policy, it mitigates the need for costly and time-consuming retraining from scratch for every new embodiment.
- **Pioneering Pretraining for Humanoids:** The work underscores the power of pretraining paradigms, drawing parallels with the success seen in large language models, and adapting them to the complex, continuous control domain of embodied AI. This shift from robot-specific learning to generalizable foundational policies is transformative.
- **Novel Technical Contributions:** The introduction of "unified control semantics" and "embodiment descriptors" (especially as privileged information) offers elegant solutions to standardize policy inputs and outputs across heterogeneous robot morphologies. The "extended domain randomization" strategy, combined with "embodiment-aware learning" (adaptive exploration and dynamic loss reweighting), further enhances the policy's robustness and generalization capabilities to unseen robots.
- **Strong Empirical Validation:** The comprehensive evaluation, including ablation studies and real-world deployment on multiple hardware platforms, provides compelling evidence of the method's effectiveness. Demonstrating few-shot adaptation in minutes rather than days or weeks is a practical breakthrough.

**B. Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Robot Development and Deployment:** H-Zero has the potential to dramatically speed up the prototyping, testing, and deployment cycles for new humanoid robot designs. Researchers and manufacturers can quickly get new robots walking stably with minimal effort, allowing them to focus on higher-level functionalities or new hardware iterations.
- **Lowered Barrier to Entry:** By simplifying the control development process, H-Zero could make humanoid robotics more accessible to a wider range of researchers, small businesses, and educational institutions, fostering innovation in the field.
- **Enabling More Agile Robot Systems:** The ability to rapidly adapt to new robot morphologies means that future humanoid systems could be designed with greater modularity and customization, where a common control intelligence can be quickly tailored to specific task requirements or hardware configurations.
- **Towards General-Purpose Robot Intelligence:** This work moves closer to the vision of general-purpose robot intelligence, where a single, versatile policy can serve as a foundation for various physical instantiations, ultimately leading to more adaptable and intelligent robotic agents.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the challenges of whole-body control with larger action spaces, open clear avenues for future research, including curriculum-based embodiment selection and more sophisticated randomization scheduling, paving the way for even more capable and robust humanoid controllers.

In conclusion, H-Zero makes a substantial contribution by offering a practical, efficient, and scalable solution for cross-humanoid locomotion control. Its methodology and impressive empirical results position it as a foundational work that will likely influence the trajectory of generalizable robot learning for years to come.
