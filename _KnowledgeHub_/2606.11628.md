# LUCID: Learning Embodiment-Agnostic Intent Models from Unstructured Human Videos for Scalable Dexterous Robot Skill Acquisition

> [!summary] Summary
> LUCID introduces a two-stage framework that learns robot manipulation intent from unstructured internet-scale human videos and robust sensorimotor control policies in massively parallel simulation. This approach enables scalable dexterous robot skill acquisition, demonstrating successful real-world performance on multiple tasks and facilitating transfer across different robot embodiments by decoupling task intent from robot-specific control.

## Problem

- Traditional robot manipulation relies on costly, embodiment-specific data (teleoperation, structured human demos) which limits scalability and generalization.
- Unstructured human videos, while abundant, lack direct robot action labels, while simulation often requires manual reward engineering for high-level task intent.
- Existing methods, such as open-loop planners or policies trained on extracted trajectories, struggle with generalization beyond demonstrated scenes or adapting to execution deviations.

## Method

- A two-stage framework, **LUCID**, is proposed, consisting of an embodiment-agnostic ==intent model== and an embodiment-specific sensorimotor policy, communicating via short-horizon object flow and palm pose references.
- The intent model is trained on diverse unstructured human videos, processed through a multi-stage pipeline for camera reconstruction, object/hand segmentation, and 3D tracking, to predict future object motion and palm poses.
- A generalist sensorimotor policy is trained in massively-parallel simulation using goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, distilled from a teacher policy, to robustly follow the predicted short-horizon references.

## Results

- LUCID achieved an average real-world success rate of **73%** across stirring, wiping, and binning tasks, significantly outperforming an open-loop video-generation planner which reached **28%** success.
- Intent model performance and real-world task success rates for binning showed a clear correlation with the volume of human video supervision, increasing proportionally as data scaled from 1k to 20k clips.
- The same intent model successfully transferred across a dexterous hand and a parallel-jaw gripper, yielding comparable aggregate success rates (19/30 trials each) on push-T and cable routing tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling manipulation intent from robot control allows for scalable learning from abundant human video data and robust control from simulation, overcoming data scarcity bottlenecks.
> - A closed-loop intent prediction system, continually re-querying the scene, enables robots to adapt to execution deviations and recover from errors, offering greater robustness than open-loop planning.
> - The learned embodiment-agnostic intent model can drive different robot hardware (dexterous hand, parallel-jaw gripper) with minimal adaptation, enhancing generalizability across robot platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation has traditionally relied on intensive data collection methods, such as teleoperated robot demonstrations or structured human demonstrations using specialized equipment (e.g., motion capture, multi-view cameras, wearables). These approaches are characterized by high costs, require purpose-built infrastructure, and are often tied to specific robot embodiments or interfaces, which limits their scalability and generalizability.

Existing research has explored alternative data sources to overcome these limitations. Unstructured human videos, particularly from internet-scale datasets, offer a vast and diverse repository of manipulation demonstrations across various objects, scenes, and strategies. However, these videos lack direct robot action labels. Concurrently, physical simulation environments provide a means to generate action-labeled data at scale, but often necessitate manual reward engineering for each task, a challenge particularly for defining high-level task intent.

Prior efforts to leverage these data sources have faced distinct shortcomings:
- **Policies trained on extracted human-video trajectories** often learn specific behaviors rather than generalizable task intent, struggling to generalize beyond the exact demonstrated scene.
- **Open-loop planners** that use pretrained video models to generate a plan at the start of an operation cannot adapt or recover if execution deviates from the initial plan.
- **Pretrained video models** used as policy backbones still typically require additional per-task and per-embodiment robot-specific data.
- **Generalist sensorimotor policies** trained in simulation, while capable of generalizing across objects under aggressive randomization, typically require external references (e.g., from motion capture, teleoperation, or static video poses) at inference time.

LUCID addresses these challenges by proposing a two-stage framework that aims to bridge the gap between unstructured human video data and scalable robot skill acquisition. It integrates the strengths of internet-scale human videos for embodiment-agnostic intent learning with massively-parallel simulation for robust, task-agnostic sensorimotor control. The work positions itself as an approach that decouples intent from control and operates in a closed-loop manner, distinguishing it from prior open-loop or robot-data-dependent methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind LUCID is to enable scalable dexterous robot skill acquisition by overcoming the data scarcity and embodiment specificity inherent in traditional robot learning pipelines. The authors aim to leverage readily available unstructured human videos and large-scale simulation to train robots for complex manipulation tasks without requiring robot demonstrations or structured human data collection.

The key objectives of this research can be summarized as follows:

1. **Decouple Manipulation Intent from Robot Control**: To separate the "what" (task intent) from the "how" (robot actions). This involves learning an embodiment-agnostic intent model from unstructured human videos and a separate, embodiment-specific sensorimotor control policy in simulation. The intent model is designed to predict short-horizon scene changes (object flow and palm pose) which can then be realized by different robot controllers.

2. **Achieve Scalability through Data Sources**: To demonstrate that internet-scale unstructured human videos can serve as a scalable source of task intent supervision, and massively-parallel simulation can provide scalable, action-labeled data for training robust sensorimotor policies. This avoids the bottleneck of collecting expensive, robot-specific demonstrations.

3. **Enable Closed-Loop Intent Prediction**: To develop an intent model that continually re-queries the live scene during deployment, rather than generating a static, one-shot plan. This is motivated by the need for robustness and adaptability to unexpected execution deviations or changes in the environment.

4. **Demonstrate Zero-Shot Transfer to Novel Scenes and Object Instances**: To evaluate the intent model's ability to generalize to new scenes, camera viewpoints, and previously unseen object instances without further adaptation.

5. **Facilitate Embodiment-Agnostic Intent Transfer**: To show that the same learned intent model can drive different robot embodiments, such as a dexterous hand and a parallel-jaw gripper, with minimal embodiment-specific adaptation required for the control policy.

6. **Investigate the Impact of Training Video Volume**: To quantify how increasing the amount of human video supervision affects the performance of the intent model (measured by intent loss) and downstream task success in real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LUCID proposes a two-stage framework comprising an intent model ($f_\theta$) and a sensorimotor policy ($\pi$), which communicate through a short-horizon reference ($R$) that encapsulates object flow and a palm pose. This separation allows for embodiment-agnostic intent learning from human videos and embodiment-specific control learning in simulation.

#### 3.1. Intent Model ($f_\theta$)

The intent model predicts short-horizon object motion and a rough palm pose from a current RGB-D observation.
- **Inputs**: A stack of $F$ recent RGB-D frames ($I_t$), current 3D positions of $N$ query points on the target object ($\{x_{trk_{n,0}}\}^N_{n=1}$), and the current palm pose ($(p_{palm_0}, R_{palm_0})$).
- **Outputs**: A short-horizon reference $R$, consisting of future 3D trajectories for the $N$ object query points ($\{x_{trk_{n,\tau}}\}^{N,T}_{n=1,\tau=1}$) and future palm poses ($\{(p_{palm_\tau}, R_{palm_\tau})\}^T_{\tau=1}$) over $T$ future steps.
- **Architecture**: It adapts Co-Tracker3 as a point-token transformer. Key modifications include:
 1. Conditioning on frozen DINOv3 patch tokens and fusing depth via a residual adapter.
 2. Predicting forward in time, unlike standard point trackers.
 3. Attaching a single palm-pose token alongside object tokens for joint prediction.
- **Training**: Trained with mean-squared-error losses on object flow ($L_{trk}$), palm position ($L_{palm,p}$), and palm rotation ($L_{palm,r}$), averaged over $T$ future steps. Extensive augmentation of human pixels in $I_t$ is applied to ensure the model infers object motion independent of human hand appearance.
- **Supervision from Unstructured Video**: For each task, 20k clips from public video datasets (e.g., Panda-70M, Action100M, Something-Something-V2, EPIC-Kitchens, LVP) or self-collected smartphone videos (for underrepresented tasks) are used. A four-stage pipeline extracts supervision targets for each sliding window of video:
 1. **Camera and depth reconstruction**: ViPE reconstructs per-frame camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and metric depth.
 2. **Object and hand segmentation**: SAM 3.1 produces object and human masks, with human masks subtracted from object masks.
 3. **Object flow tracks**: DenseTrack3Dv2 samples and tracks $N$ query pixels within the object mask, projecting them to 3D.
 4. **Hand reconstruction**: WiLoR returns a MANO hand mesh, which is rigidly fitted to the depth data within the human mask to yield the palm pose.

#### 3.2. Generalist Sensorimotor Policy ($\pi$)

The sensorimotor policy maps onboard sensing to motor commands, trained via goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (RL) in Isaac Lab using massively-parallel simulation. A separate $\pi$ is trained for each embodiment (e.g., dexterous hand, parallel-jaw gripper).
- **Setup and Training Data**: $\pi$ is trained to follow references in $R$'s format, generated procedurally. Each episode involves a procedurally generated object (union of primitives with randomized scale, mass, friction) and a reference trajectory $R$. Trajectories consist of four segments (approach, in-hand motion, goal, disengage) with randomly sampled grasp, waypoint, and goal poses, and varying hand-coupling modes. This broad distribution aims to yield a task-agnostic policy.
- **Action Space**: The policy outputs arm joint-position deltas, eigen-grasp coefficients (derived from a human grasp basis to bias exploration), and per-joint hand residuals. Actions are EMA-smoothed and clipped.
- **Observations and Goal**: Both policies receive $R$ (palm-pose component and object-flow component) and proprioception (joint configuration, previous joint target).
 - **Teacher Policy ($\pi_T$)**: Trained on privileged simulator state, observing full-surface object flow, joint velocities, and object pose.
 - **Student Policy ($\pi_S$)**: Distilled from $\pi_T$, observing a smaller, external-camera-visible subset of object flow, and a wrist-mounted depth image for close-range geometry. External camera locations are randomized during training.
- **Reward**: A single task-agnostic reward balances object tracking, palm-pose following, finger contact (shaping rewards), and regularization (penalizing large actions and unsafe joint configurations).
- **Policy Architecture**: Both $\pi_T$ and $\pi_S$ build on AME, a cross-attention architecture. Modifications include:
 1. Scene tokens from query points on the object.
 2. $\pi_S$ tokenizes the wrist-depth image and joins these tokens with point-trajectory tokens.
 3. A self-attention block runs over joint tokens before cross-attention.
- **Training and Sim-to-Real**:
 - $\pi_T$ is trained with PPO on privileged simulator state under an adaptive curriculum (ADR) that gradually increases environmental difficulty (gravity, perturbations, success tolerances) as performance improves.
 - $\pi_S$ is distilled from $\pi_T$ using a hybrid objective ($L_{PPO}(\pi_S) + \lambda_D \| \mu_S - \mu_T \|_2^2$), combining PPO on on-policy rollouts with MSE regression to the teacher's actions. The distillation weight $\lambda_D$ is annealed. $\pi_S$ is trained with realistic sensor noise. System identification calibrates simulator dynamics to the real robot.

#### 3.3. Real-World Execution

The robot operates with a slow intent cycle around a fast control loop.
- **Sensors**: A fixed external RGB-D camera and a wrist-mounted depth camera provide observations.
- **Intent Cycle (1 Hz)**: SAM 3.1 refreshes the object mask, query points are sampled and back-projected, and $f_\theta$ is re-queried with these, $I_t$, and the current palm pose (from forward kinematics) to produce a fresh $R$. Intent model outputs (in external-camera frame) are transformed to the robot base frame.
- **Control Loop (50 Hz)**: $\pi_S$ consumes current points, flow and palm-pose reference from $R$, wrist depth, and proprioception to emit actions.
- **Point Tracking**: Between intent cycles, a sliding-window 3D point tracker keeps current points consistent with the live scene, and a lookahead window advances over $R$'s 1-second flow horizon.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LUCID focused on four claims across five real-world manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Real-World Capability on Web-Scraped Tasks (Q1)

LUCID was evaluated on stirring, wiping, and binning, each supervised by 20k web-scraped human video clips.
- **Performance**: LUCID (dexterous hand) achieved 73% average success across these tasks and scenarios. An open-loop video-generation planner (Veo 3.1) achieved 28% success.
- **Robustness**: LUCID's closed-loop intent prediction enabled recovery from initial grasp misses or mid-rollout object shifts, a key differentiator from the open-loop baseline which struggled when execution diverged from its initial plan.
- **Object Handling**: The sensorimotor policy handled deformable objects (e.g., tissue, towel) despite being trained only on rigid objects in simulation.
- **Emergent Composition**: In the wiping task, the intent model could cause the robot to pick up a soiled tissue and deposit it in a nearby bin, an emergent behavior attributed to incidental binning demonstrations within the broader wiping video dataset.
- **Failure Modes**: Failures for LUCID often stemmed from perception issues (e.g., object occlusion by the hand) or unrecoverable states from unstable grasps. The open-loop baseline predominantly failed due to stale references after execution divergence or hallucinated scene details.

#### 4.2. Intent Data Scaling (Q2)

The binning task was used to investigate the impact of human video supervision volume, sweeping from 1k to 20k clips.
- **Correlation**: Held-out intent loss decreased steadily as the corpus size increased, following a power law. Real-world success rates for binning increased proportionally with the training data volume.
- **Behavioral Improvement**: At low data volumes (1k-2k clips), intent models produced poor references. In the 5k-10k range, container localization emerged, but placement accuracy remained weak.

#### 4.3. Intent Transfer Across Robot Embodiments (Q3)

LUCID was evaluated on push-T and cable routing tasks, with the intent model trained on 1 hour of self-collected smartphone video for each. The same intent predictions were used with two sensorimotor policies: the LEAP hand and a parallel-jaw gripper.
- **Cross-Embodiment Performance**: Both the dexterous hand and the parallel-jaw gripper achieved comparable aggregate success rates (19/30 trials each) on these tasks, demonstrating the transferability of the embodiment-agnostic intent model.
- **Task-Specific Suitability**: Cable routing was observed to favor the parallel-jaw gripper due to its morphology being better suited for grasping thin cables compared to the dexterous hand.
- **Failure Modes**: Failures on these tasks were often linked to out-of-distribution states where the intent model's predictions were insufficient, likely due to the limited 1-hour smartphone video corpus.

#### 4.4. Sensorimotor Policy Ablations (Q4)

Ablation studies were conducted in simulation to assess key design choices for the sensorimotor policy.
- **MLP Encoder**: Replacing the attention-based policy encoder with a simple MLP encoder resulted in reduced performance, as it lost the fine-grained per-point detail necessary for stable grasp contacts and in-hand manipulation.
- **No Eigen-Grasp Basis**: Removing the eigen-grasp basis, which guides exploration toward stable grasps, led to a significantly larger exploration space and lower episode rewards.
- **DAgger-BC Distillation**: Substituting the hybrid PPO + distillation objective with a DAgger-BC mixture resulted in lower performance. This was attributed to DAgger-BC primarily mimicking the teacher without fully exploiting the student's unique input modalities.
- **No Wrist Camera**: Removing the wrist-mounted depth camera from the student policy caused object geometry to be unresolved, leading to performance comparable to the MLP encoder ablation.
- **Query-Points Count**: Increasing the number of camera-visible query points improved episode reward with diminishing returns, with 16 points identified as a balance between performance and training speed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LUCID represents a contribution to scalable robot skill acquisition by addressing limitations in data collection and embodiment specificity. By effectively decoupling task intent from robot control and leveraging diverse, unstructured human video data alongside massively parallel simulation, the framework offers an alternative to costly and specialized data pipelines.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Scalability**: The framework demonstrates that robot-agnostic intent can be learned from internet-scale human videos, a highly abundant data source, and control can be learned in simulation, which provides limitless action-labeled data. This addresses a major bottleneck in robot learning.
- **Robustness through Closed-Loop Intent**: The continuous re-querying of the intent model from live scene observations during execution enables the robot to adapt to deviations and recover from errors, contrasting with the fragility of open-loop planning approaches.
- **Embodiment Agnosticism**: The ability to transfer the same intent model across different robot embodiments (dexterous hand and parallel-jaw gripper) without re-training the intent model is a step towards more generalizable robot intelligence, reducing the effort required to deploy skills on new hardware.
- **Emergent Capabilities**: The observation of emergent task composition (e.g., binning a wiped tissue without explicit supervision) suggests that broad, unstructured video datasets can implicitly teach complex interactions.

**Potential Impact:**
The LUCID framework could accelerate the development and deployment of robot manipulation skills in diverse real-world environments. By reducing the reliance on robot-specific demonstrations, it has the potential to democratize access to advanced robot capabilities, making it easier for researchers and practitioners to develop and test new skills on various robotic platforms. This could broaden the range of tasks robots can perform, from household chores to industrial applications, and foster more general-purpose manipulation systems.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors identify several limitations that also point to future research avenues:
1. **Pipeline Brittleness**: The modular nature of the system, relying on multiple off-the-shelf perception components (SAM, DenseTrack3Dv2, ViPE, WiLoR), introduces potential failure points. An end-to-end trained intent model from raw video could create a more robust single learned representation.
2. **Task-Condition Gap**: Tasks without a clear, verifiable end condition can lead to indefinite loops. Manual corpus filtering for new tasks also limits scalability. Future work could explore text conditioning or a high-level planner to prompt the intent model and stitch sub-tasks, enabling scaling to thousands of tasks.
3. **Lossy Explicit Interface**: The hand-designed 3D object flow and palm-pose interface between the intent model and sensorimotor policy may discard important information (e.g., precise finger configurations, fine contact cues) present in human demonstrations. A jointly optimized latent intermediate representation could allow the interface to adapt to the policy's specific needs, potentially leading to a more complete reproduction of human skills.

In conclusion, LUCID presents a method for scaling dexterous robot skill acquisition by leveraging readily available, unstructured human video data for intent learning and simulation for control. Its approach of decoupling intent from control and operating in a closed-loop manner demonstrates progress towards more robust, generalizable, and scalable robot manipulation capabilities.
