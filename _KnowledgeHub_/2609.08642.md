# SUN: Reaching for Novelty in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> SUN (SUccessor-to-Novelty) is a goal-selection framework for exploration in reinforcement learning that scores candidate goals as the product of a successor-value-function reachability estimate and a pseudocount novelty term, with an adaptive reselection trigger and an O(1) buffer-based pseudocount. It beats AdaGoal and DISCOVER on coverage and visitation entropy across gridworlds, classic control and goal-conditioned locomotion, at roughly 3.5x lower runtime.

## Problem

- Density-based goal selection such as MEGA, Skew-Fit and GoalGAN scores goals purely by novelty, committing the agent to rare states it cannot actually reach.
- Distance and success-probability methods optimize reachability alone, so the top-scoring goal is the agent's current state and exploration stalls without visiting rare achievable goals.
- Ensemble-based methods estimate novelty from critic disagreement, which is costly to train and query and leaves the novelty-reachability balance poorly calibrated, collapsing toward one signal.

## Method

- **SUN** indicator: a multiplicative score, ==successor value function== times ==pseudocount novelty==, whose shared zero and common unit scale remove the tuning coefficient an additive combination needs.
- ==Adaptive goal-selection==: keep the current goal until the learned successor value at the current state falls below its value at selection time, then resample candidates from the replay buffer.
- Lightweight pseudocount: ==fixed-radius nearest-neighbour counts== in standardized feature space, maintained incrementally at buffer-insertion time, so each candidate novelty query costs O(1) instead of a density-model evaluation.

## Results

- Averaged over environments, adaptive multiplicative SUN lifts coverage AUC by **15.1%** over AdaGoal, **21.0%** over DISCOVER and **67.1%** over random, with entropy gains of **7.6%**, **9.2%** and **26.5%**.
- Indicator ablation: the additive variant loses entropy against baselines (**-5.0%** versus AdaGoal) as its novelty bonus saturates and selections cluster near start states; reachability-only barely explores at all.
- Pseudocount overhead is negligible: SUN averages **3.54x** faster than AdaGoal and **3.43x** faster than DISCOVER on gridworlds and classic control, despite scoring candidate batches far more often.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multiplying two bounded signals that share a zero removes the scale-calibration coefficient additive bonuses need, fixing the trade-off via the discount instead of a re-tuned hyperparameter.
> - Epistemic uncertainty is a poor novelty proxy near unreachable regions: disagreement resolves only after many wasted episodes, so uncertainty-seeking agents chase goals they cannot reach.
> - Monotone value growth along a trajectory is a free self-diagnostic for bad goal commitments; count-based novelty still degenerates in image-scale spaces where every state is unique.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Exploration is a central problem in reinforcement learning because an agent must gather informative experience before it can learn effective behavior. Standard methods such as epsilon-greedy action selection and entropy regularization inject randomness but generally do not account for the structure of the environment. More structured methods include count-based exploration, density estimation, intrinsic motivation, maximum-entropy exploration, and goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL).

The paper focuses on GCRL, in which an agent learns a policy conditioned on a desired goal state or state-action configuration. Exploration is then driven by selecting goals that cause the agent to visit underexplored parts of the state space. Existing goal-selection methods often emphasize one of two properties:

1. **Novelty:** Goals that have rarely been visited receive high priority. Density-based methods such as MEGA, Skew-Fit, GoalGAN, and hindsight goal generation follow this general approach. Their weakness is that a highly novel goal may be unreachable from the agent’s current state.
2. **Reachability:** Goals are selected based on distance, estimated success probability, or value. These approaches avoid impossible targets but may repeatedly select nearby or already familiar states.

The paper argues that effective exploration requires both properties simultaneously: a goal should be unusual relative to the visitation history but sufficiently reachable to produce useful experience.

SUN is particularly related to AdaGoal and DISCOVER, which also use successor value functions and seek to balance reachability with an exploration signal. However, those methods use disagreement among ensembles of critics as a proxy for novelty or uncertainty. The authors argue that ensemble disagreement is computationally expensive and does not reliably calibrate reachability against novelty. SUN instead uses a multiplicative combination of successor-value reachability and a lightweight count-based novelty estimate.

The work also sits between two broader formulations of reward-free exploration. One seeks a state-visitation distribution with high entropy or broad coverage. The other seeks to visit all goals that are reachable within some horizon. SUN adopts a practical, greedy GCRL formulation rather than attempting to solve either objective exactly in large or continuous state spaces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Addressing the Novelty–Reachability Trade-off

The primary objective is to develop a goal-selection rule that explicitly favors goals that are both novel and reachable. The motivation is illustrated by environments containing isolated rooms or irreversible transitions. A novelty-only method may select an infrequently visited state in a disconnected room, while a reachability-only method may select the agent’s current state because it is easiest to reach. Neither choice creates useful exploration.

The paper seeks a mechanism that rejects unreachable states regardless of how novel they are, while still allowing rare but achievable goals to be selected over familiar ones.

#### 2.2. Avoiding Manual Signal Calibration

Additive combinations of reachability and novelty generally require a coefficient to determine the relative scale of the two terms. This coefficient may need to be retuned across environments or during training because estimated value scales and novelty magnitudes change. SUN is designed to avoid this explicit calibration by multiplying the two signals. Since both factors are nonnegative and bounded, a goal receives a low score if either its reachability or novelty is low.

#### 2.3. Supporting Large and Continuous Goal Spaces

A second objective is to make novelty estimation computationally inexpensive. Classical density models or kernel density estimators can be costly when many candidate goals are scored repeatedly during an episode. The authors therefore construct a pseudocount whose computational cost is largely paid when replay-buffer samples are inserted. Novelty queries can then be answered in constant time for stored candidates.

#### 2.4. Handling Changing Reachability During an Episode

A selected goal may become unreachable after a stochastic transition, an irreversible action, or an inaccurate value estimate. The authors aim to preserve the stability of committing to a goal while still allowing the agent to abandon a failed commitment. This motivates an adaptive reselection rule based on whether the estimated successor value of the current goal decreases relative to its value when selected.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. The SUN Indicator

SUN, or **SUccessor-to-Novelty**, is defined as

\[
\mathrm{SUN}(g \mid s)=V^\pi(s,g)\nu(g),
\]

where \(V^\pi(s,g)\) is a successor value function (SVF) measuring the discounted likelihood of reaching goal \(g\) from state \(s\), and \(\nu(g)\) is a novelty score. The selected goal is the candidate with the largest SUN score.

The SVF is trained using hindsight experience replay (HER). The goal-conditioned reward is one when the agent reaches the goal and zero otherwise. HER relabels trajectories with future states as goals, producing positive training examples even when the originally commanded goal was not reached. SUN is implemented with DQN in discrete-action gridworld and classic-control environments and with TD3 in continuous-action GCRL tasks.

The candidate set is sampled from the replay buffer rather than searched over the entire goal space. This makes the method compatible with off-policy learning and limits selection to states the agent has already observed.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Properties

The paper establishes three structural properties, primarily under an oracle setting with exact SVFs and counts.

First, if novelty is defined as \(\nu(g)=1/n_g\), where \(n_g\) is the visit count, SUN is equivalent to the value of a goal-specific count-bonus reward. Thus, the product is not presented merely as an arbitrary heuristic; it corresponds to optimizing a discounted reward for reaching rarely visited goals.

Second, the SVF provides a short-horizon hitting-probability bound:

\[
\Pr^\pi[\tau_g\leq n]\geq V^\pi(s,g)-\gamma^{n+1}.
\]

A high SVF therefore implies that the goal has a substantial probability of being reached within a finite horizon.

Third, if a goal is unreachable under the policy class, its SVF is zero and hence its SUN score is zero, independent of its novelty. This formalizes the method’s ability to suppress unreachable goals.

The paper also analyzes the multiplicative form in log space. The logarithm of SUN separates into a reachability term and a rarity term, with the discount factor determining the implicit trade-off. This removes the free scaling coefficient required by additive formulations, although it does not eliminate the underlying trade-off itself.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Goal Selection

SUN begins an episode by selecting a goal from a sampled candidate batch. It then retains that goal while acting according to the goal-conditioned policy. At each step, the algorithm checks whether the current SVF estimate has declined relative to the value at the time of selection. If it has, the goal is discarded and a new candidate set is sampled. A new goal is also selected when the current goal is reached.

This strategy is intended to combine the stability of episodic selection with the error recovery of per-step selection. Under deterministic dynamics and exact values, the authors show that the SVF should increase along a trajectory directed toward the goal, so reselection should not occur. With stochastic transitions or approximate values, a decrease in the SVF acts as a signal that the goal may no longer be reliable.

#### 3.4. Lightweight Pseudocount

For each replay-buffer entry, SUN stores the number of other entries within a radius \(\rho\) in standardized feature space. When a new state is inserted, its neighborhood count is computed and the counts of neighboring entries are incremented. Novelty is then obtained as the reciprocal of the stored count.

Feature standardization makes a single radius meaningful across dimensions. The authors also floor feature standard deviations to avoid amplifying noise in nearly constant dimensions. The novelty query is \(O(1)\), while insertion costs \(O(NM)\), with \(N\) denoting buffer size and \(M\) goal dimensionality. The buffer is assumed in the experiments not to evict samples; the appendix discusses how counts could be decremented if eviction occurs.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Design

The experiments use 20 random seeds and compare SUN with random exploration, AdaGoal, and DISCOVER. They include three newly designed gridworlds with isolated rooms, irreversible traps, stochastic movements, and narrow passages. They also include MountainCar, Pendulum, LunarLander, Acrobot, CartPole, PointMaze, AntMaze, and ArmPush tasks. The benchmarks cover discrete and continuous action spaces, hard-to-reach states, unreachable configurations, obstacles, mazes, high-dimensional observations, and unbounded regions.

Performance is measured using state-space coverage and normalized Shannon entropy of visitation counts. For continuous spaces, the authors discretize dimensions into bins for most metrics; for the eight-dimensional LunarLander variant, they use a k-nearest-neighbor differential entropy estimator.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The reported results show that SUN achieves higher coverage and visitation entropy than the evaluated baselines across the benchmark suite. In gridworlds, all methods often eventually reach most or all visitable tiles, but SUN produces a more uniform distribution. This distinction is important because coverage alone can hide severe concentration in a small region.

In ThreeRoom, SUN avoids selecting rarely visited states in isolated or effectively inaccessible regions while progressively expanding toward less familiar areas. In FourRoomStuck, episodic methods can become trapped after entering a room from which the previously selected goal is unreachable. Adaptive SUN and per-step SUN recover by changing goals. Adaptive selection performs better because it avoids the continual goal switching observed with per-step selection.

In GridMaze, SUN is more likely to traverse the narrow passage needed to reach the broader maze. AdaGoal and DISCOVER tend to concentrate near easier regions or fail to balance progress with exploration.

The same pattern appears in continuous-control tasks. SUN expands beyond initial-state regions in MountainCar, CartPole, LunarLander, Acrobot, and the maze environments. In MountainCar, the selected goals become progressively farther away as the policy learns the action sequences required to build momentum. SUN also performs well in the eight-dimensional LunarLander setting, supporting the practical value of the pseudocount in moderately high-dimensional continuous spaces.

Pendulum is an important exception. SUN’s entropy decreases even as its coverage increases because some rare configurations can only be reached by repeatedly revisiting common intermediate configurations to build momentum. This reflects a structural limitation of optimizing goal rarity alone: reaching the tails of the visitation distribution may require concentrated revisits to central states.

The ablations support the paper’s central claims. Reachability-only selection tends to remain near the current state and produces little exploration. Novelty-only selection works in environments where most goals are reachable but fails in environments with disconnected or irreversible regions. An additive SUN variant initially benefits from novelty but later becomes dominated by reachability as the novelty term diminishes. The multiplicative variant avoids this saturation behavior.

The reported area-under-the-curve comparisons across environments show that adaptive multiplicative SUN improves coverage AUC by approximately 15.1% over AdaGoal, 21.0% over DISCOVER, and 67.1% over random exploration. Its entropy AUC improvements are approximately 7.6%, 9.2%, and 26.5%, respectively.

Theoretical claims should be interpreted within their stated assumptions. The main proofs characterize an oracle or frozen-count setting and do not provide a complete sample-complexity guarantee for the practical deep-RL algorithm. The appendix’s SUN-UCB analysis is explicitly presented as a structural connection and leaves unresolved both frontier discovery and the relationship between historical policy averages and optimal policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SUN contributes a modular exploration mechanism that can be attached to off-policy GCRL algorithms. Its main conceptual contribution is to treat novelty and reachability as jointly necessary rather than as sequential or independently tuned criteria. The formal unreachability result gives a clear explanation for why the method should avoid a common failure mode of density-based exploration.

The pseudocount design may reduce the computational burden associated with critic ensembles or repeated density queries, especially when goals are reconsidered during an episode. This could make reachability-aware exploration more practical in continuous-control settings and in algorithms that already maintain replay buffers.

Potential applications include sparse-reward robotics, autonomous navigation, manipulation, and reward-free pretraining, where broad experience collection is valuable before a downstream task is specified. However, the limitations identified by the authors constrain the likely scope of impact. Count-based novelty becomes weak in very large spaces such as raw image observations, where most states are effectively unique. SUN also selects goals only from the replay buffer and does not account for the novelty of intermediate states along a path. Future extensions could combine its reachability factor with learned curiosity, latent representations, temporal-distance metrics, or cumulative path-based objectives.
