# Towards Concise and Adaptive Thinking in Large Reasoning Models: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey provides a comprehensive overview of methods for achieving concise and adaptive thinking in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs). It categorizes existing research into training-free and training-based approaches, detailing how models can shorten verbose reasoning chains and dynamically adjust their 'thinking' depth based on input difficulty, thereby improving efficiency and practical usability.

## Problem

- Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) like OpenAI O1 and DeepSeek R1 generate excessively long and redundant Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning sequences, even for simple queries.
- This 'overthinking' leads to substantial waste of inference resources, increased response times, and higher computational costs, hindering practical LRM deployment.
- LRMs lack the ability to adapt their reasoning depth based on input difficulty, failing to balance 'fast' (System 1) and 'slow' (System 2) thinking.

## Method

- The survey categorizes and analyzes existing methodologies into two main approaches: ==training-free methods== and ==training-based methods==.
- Training-free methods include ==prompt-guided==, ==pipeline-based architectures==, decoding manipulation techniques, and model merging strategies to control reasoning length without additional model training.
- Training-based methods involve fine-tuning strategies (e.g., data construction, variable-length datasets, DPO variants) and reinforcement learning (e.g., with ==length penalties==, difficulty awareness) to learn adaptive reasoning.

## Results

- Training-free methods offer ease of implementation but often exhibit inconsistent performance (e.g., prompt-guided), introduce latency (pipeline-based), or require careful design to avoid errors (decoding manipulation).
- Training-based methods, while powerful for teaching adaptive length, are heavily reliant on high-quality variable-length CoT data and face challenges like **'Format Collapse'** in RL approaches.
- A lack of unified evaluation standards across research efforts, including inconsistent base models, datasets, and metrics, hinders direct comparison and standardization of findings in the field.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) widely exhibit an 'overthinking phenomenon,' generating unnecessarily lengthy reasoning chains for many tasks.
> - Optimal reasoning lengths are non-uniform and vary with input difficulty, necessitating adaptive thinking to balance performance and efficiency.
> - Current research on concise and adaptive LRM thinking falls into two main paradigms: training-free methods (e.g., prompting, pipeline-based, decoding manipulation) and training-based methods (e.g., fine-tuning, reinforcement learning).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This survey paper is situated at the intersection of large language models (LLMs), advanced AI reasoning, and computational efficiency. Recent advancements have seen the emergence of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), such as OpenAI's O1 and DeepSeek R1, which demonstrate exceptional performance on complex tasks like mathematics and programming. This prowess largely stems from their ability to generate extensive Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning sequences, often likened to human "System 2" (slow, deliberate) thinking, in contrast to the "System 1" (fast, intuitive) thinking of traditional LLMs.

However, this impressive capability comes at a significant cost: LRMs frequently exhibit an "overthinking" phenomenon, generating unnecessarily lengthy and redundant reasoning chains even for simple queries. This leads to substantial waste of inference resources, increased response times, and practical limitations for real-world deployment. The broader research landscape is therefore grappling with the challenge of harnessing LRMs' reasoning power without incurring prohibitive computational overhead.

This survey specifically addresses the imperative to "shorten lengthy reasoning chains and learn adaptive reasoning between fast and slow thinking based on input difficulty." It distinguishes itself from other existing surveys on efficient reasoning for LRMs by concentrating precisely on "concise and adaptive thinking." The authors argue that this focus is "essential to build a unified model that possesses both strong chat and reasoning capabilities." The paper explicitly scopes its contribution by outlining what it *does not* cover, such as general LLM efficiency techniques (distillation, quantization, speculative decoding), model architecture optimization, or efficient reasoning in multimodal models. This clear demarcation positions the survey as a specialized and in-depth review of methodologies aimed at making LRMs intelligent *and* efficient in their reasoning processes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this survey is to address a critical inefficiency in current Large Reasoning Models (LRMs): their tendency to "overthink" by generating "unnecessarily lengthy and redundant reasoning chains" even for simple problems. This "overthinking phenomenon" leads to a substantial waste of computational resources (inference tokens), increases latency for user queries, and ultimately hinders the practical deployment and widespread application of LRMs in real-world products. The authors highlight that while long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning is vital for complex tasks, it's not always necessary and can be detrimental when overused. Empirical evidence suggests there are "optimal reasoning lengths for different tasks that allow the model to think adaptively based on input difficulty."

Given this problem, the key objectives of the research surveyed, and consequently, the motivation for this survey itself, are twofold:
1. **Concise Thinking:** To develop methods for "shortening the verbose and lengthy CoT to more concise" forms.
2. **Adaptive Thinking:** To enable LRMs to "adaptively trigger slow thinking based on input difficulty," thereby achieving a balance between fast (System 1) and slow (System 2) thinking.

The specific objective of *this survey* is to provide a "comprehensive overview of recent cutting-edge research in concise and adaptive thinking of LRMs." This includes reviewing various methodologies, relevant benchmarks, and identifying remaining challenges for future exploration. The authors explicitly state their hope that the survey will "help researchers quickly understand the landscape of this field and inspire novel adaptive thinking ideas to facilitate better usage of LRMs."

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a survey paper, the methodology primarily involves a systematic and comprehensive review, categorization, and critical analysis of existing literature on concise and adaptive thinking in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs). The authors categorize the vast array of approaches into a clear, hierarchical taxonomy, enabling a structured understanding of the field.

The core of their approach involves:

- **Categorization of Methods:** The methods are broadly divided into two main paradigms:
 - **Training-free methods:** These approaches aim to achieve concise and adaptive thinking without requiring additional model training. Sub-categories include:
 - **Prompt-Guided:** Utilizing carefully crafted prompts (e.g., direct instructions, token budgets, thinking modes, or "no thinking" directives) to influence the model's reasoning output.
 - **Pipeline-Based:** Designing modular workflows, often employing routers, to strategically offload computational overhead by directing queries to appropriate models or reasoning strategies based on perceived difficulty.
 - **Decoding Manipulation:** Dynamically adjusting the model's decoding process to control reasoning length (e.g., budget forcing, early exiting based on confidence, token logits manipulation, or activation steering).
 - **Model Merging:** Combining a slow-thinking LRM with a fast-thinking LLM through weight averaging or other parameter fusion techniques to create a hybrid model.
 - **Training-based methods:** These involve fine-tuning or reinforcement learning to teach LRMs to adapt their reasoning length. Sub-categories include:
 - **Fine-tuning:**
 - **Data Construction:** Generating diverse CoT datasets with heterogeneous reasoning lengths, often through sophisticated sampling, pruning, or distillation techniques.
 - **Variable Length Datasets:** Identifying and summarizing publicly available datasets specifically designed for variable-length CoT.
 - **Fine-tuning Approaches:** Applying various fine-tuning strategies such as long CoT compression, short CoT selection, implicit CoT (internalizing reasoning without explicit steps), DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) variants for multi-objective optimization, and other hybrid fine-tuning paradigms.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Using RL frameworks to optimize the trade-off between reasoning conciseness and accuracy. Sub-categories include:
 - **RL with Length Penalty:** Incorporating penalties for verbose outputs into the reward function.
 - **RL with GRPO-Variant:** Adapting Generalized Policy Optimization (GRPO) to address issues like "Format Collapse" and integrate external guidance.
 - **RL with Difficulty-awareness:** Training models to adjust reasoning length based on estimated problem difficulty.
 - **RL with Thinking Mode:** Explicitly training models to switch between distinct "thinking" and "no thinking" modes.
 - **Other RL:** Miscellaneous RL innovations, including novel reward designs or training frameworks.

- **Evaluation Review:** The survey dedicates a significant section to existing evaluation practices. It outlines commonly used base models and datasets (primarily in mathematics and coding), and provides a comprehensive list and explanation of various metrics beyond simple accuracy and token count. These include `pass@k`, latency, reasoning verbosity, CoT trigger rate, outcome/process efficiency, various concise accuracy metrics (HCA/SCA/CCA), E3 (Efficiency-Aware Effectiveness Evaluation), InfoBias/InfoGain, and Overthinking Score. This highlights the authors' commitment to a thorough appraisal of the field's assessment tools.

- **Identification of Challenges and Future Directions:** Based on their comprehensive review, the authors synthesize current limitations and propose key areas for future research, including model capability-aware reasoning, human preference-aware reasoning, and ensuring trustworthy reasoning (hallucination, safety, instruction following) in adaptive LRMs.

By meticulously structuring the existing knowledge and providing critical analysis of each approach's strengths and weaknesses, the survey offers a valuable roadmap for researchers navigating the complex landscape of LRM efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a survey paper, its "findings" are a synthesis of the state of the art, observations from current research, and identified challenges, rather than experimental results. The main findings and insights presented in the survey include:

- **Ubiquitous Overthinking in LRMs:** A central observation is the pervasive "overthinking phenomenon" in current LRMs, where models generate excessively long and redundant Chain-of-Thought (CoT) sequences even for simple problems. This confirms the critical need for solutions that balance reasoning depth with efficiency.
- **Non-Uniform Optimal Reasoning Lengths:** The survey highlights that extremely long reasoning chains do not consistently improve performance across all tasks. Instead, there exist optimal reasoning lengths that vary based on the input difficulty, underscoring the necessity of adaptive thinking.
- **Diverse Methodological Landscape:** The paper successfully categorizes the wide array of approaches to concise and adaptive thinking into two principal categories: training-free and training-based methods, each with further detailed sub-categories. This structured classification is a key finding in itself, providing a coherent framework for understanding the field.
- **Trade-offs Across Approaches:**
 - **Training-Free Methods:**
 - **Prompt-Guided:** Simple to implement but often inconsistent as LRMs may not reliably follow instructions, and can introduce hidden inaccuracies or increase output instability.
 - **Pipeline-Based:** Effective in offloading computation but introduce additional inference overhead (latency) due to routing mechanisms. The efficiency of the router is crucial.
 - **Decoding Manipulation:** Non-invasive and widely applicable, allowing dynamic intervention in generation. However, hard token budgets can lead to errors, while dynamic early-exiting methods require careful design to avoid added computational costs from frequent checks. Activation steering shows promise but needs refinement for accurate control. Parallel scaling can trade more compute for faster initial correct answers.
 - **Model Merging:** Can effectively balance long and short reasoning for mid-scale models, but struggles with very small or very large models and offers limited fine-grained control over reasoning depth.
 - **Training-Based Methods:**
 - **Fine-tuning:** Powerful for teaching adaptive length, but heavily reliant on the quality and diversity of variable-length CoT data. Approaches like implicit CoT can improve efficiency but sacrifice interpretability. DPO variants offer multi-objective optimization but face challenges in preference data construction and maintaining reasoning depth.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Highly effective for optimizing reasoning efficiency, particularly with length penalties and GRPO variants. However, RL methods can suffer from issues like "Format Collapse" (over-reliance on a single reasoning format), require careful difficulty calibration, and are computationally intensive.
- **Inconsistencies in Evaluation Standards:** A significant challenge identified is the lack of unified evaluation standards across research efforts. This includes variations in base models, datasets (often limited to reasoning-focused benchmarks), metrics (a diverse but often incomparable set), and hyperparameters for inference. This discrepancy hinders direct comparison and standardization of research findings.
- **Emerging Evaluation Metrics:** Researchers are developing more nuanced metrics beyond simple accuracy and token count, such as reasoning verbosity, CoT trigger rate, and various efficiency/conciseness scores (HCA/SCA/CCA, E3, InfoBias/InfoGain, Overthinking Score), reflecting a growing understanding of LRM efficiency.

In essence, the survey's main "result" is a clear, detailed, and critically analyzed map of the current landscape of concise and adaptive thinking in LRMs, highlighting both the progress made and the inherent trade-offs and challenges that remain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey paper holds significant importance for the field of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) and the broader development of intelligent AI systems. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

- **Bridging the Efficiency-Capability Gap:** The most direct and immediate impact is addressing the critical inefficiency of LRMs. By synthesizing methods to achieve concise and adaptive thinking, the survey provides a roadmap for reducing the "overthinking phenomenon." This is crucial for lowering the exorbitant inference costs associated with LRMs and decreasing response latency, thereby making these powerful models more economically viable and practically usable in real-world applications. This can accelerate the adoption of LRMs in various industries, from scientific research and software development to customer service and education.

- **Guiding Future Research:** The comprehensive and structured overview of existing methodologies, along with their strengths, weaknesses, and identified trade-offs, serves as an invaluable resource for researchers. By clearly categorizing approaches (training-free vs. training-based, and their sub-types), the survey helps new entrants and experienced practitioners alike to quickly grasp the landscape, identify promising avenues, and avoid redundant efforts. The explicit discussion of "Challenges and Future Directions"—specifically model capability-awareness, human preference-awareness, and trustworthy reasoning—provides concrete guidance for future research agendas, inspiring novel ideas and fostering a more targeted approach to LRM development.

- **Advancing AI Cognition and Architecture:** The paper situates adaptive thinking within the cognitive science framework of "System 1" (fast, intuitive) and "System 2" (slow, deliberate) thinking. By exploring how to enable LRMs to dynamically switch between these modes, the research contributes to a deeper understanding of AI cognitive architectures. The long-term impact is the potential to build "unified models that possess both strong chat and reasoning capabilities," moving closer to Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) by allowing models to intelligently allocate computational effort based on task complexity.

- **Improving Trustworthiness and User Experience:** The emphasis on aligning reasoning processes with human preferences highlights a crucial, often overlooked, aspect of LRM deployment. Ensuring that reasoning is not only concise but also interpretable and aligned with human cognitive patterns, especially in sensitive domains (e.g., medical, legal), can significantly enhance user trust and satisfaction. Furthermore, by flagging the need for more systematic evaluation of how adaptive thinking impacts hallucination, safety, and instruction-following, the survey contributes to the development of more robust and reliable AI systems. Addressing "unthinking vulnerability" and the trade-off between reasoning capability and instruction adherence are vital for safe and controlled AI deployment.

In summary, this survey is not merely a summary of existing work; it is a critical analysis that illuminates the path forward for developing smarter, more efficient, and ultimately more usable large reasoning models.
