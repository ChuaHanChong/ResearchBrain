# Towards Concise and Adaptive Thinking in Large Reasoning Models: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey provides a comprehensive overview of methods for achieving concise and adaptive thinking in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs). It categorizes existing research into training-free and training-based approaches, detailing how models can shorten verbose reasoning chains and dynamically adjust their 'thinking' depth based on input difficulty, thereby improving efficiency and practical usability.

## Problem

- Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) like OpenAI O1 and DeepSeek R1 generate excessively long and redundant Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning sequences, even for simple queries.
- This 'overthinking' leads to substantial waste of inference resources, increased response times, and higher computational costs, hindering practical LRM deployment.
- LRMs lack the ability to adapt their reasoning depth based on input difficulty, failing to balance 'fast' (System 1) and 'slow' (System 2) thinking.

## Method

- The survey categorizes and analyzes existing methodologies into two main approaches: ==training-free methods== and ==training-based methods==.
- Training-free methods include ==prompt-guided==, ==pipeline-based architectures==, decoding manipulation techniques, and model merging strategies to control reasoning length without additional model training.
- Training-based methods involve fine-tuning strategies (e.g., data construction, variable-length datasets, DPO variants) and reinforcement learning (e.g., with ==length penalties==, difficulty awareness) to learn adaptive reasoning.

## Results

- Training-free methods offer ease of implementation but often exhibit inconsistent performance (e.g., prompt-guided), introduce latency (pipeline-based), or require careful design to avoid errors (decoding manipulation).
- Training-based methods, while powerful for teaching adaptive length, are heavily reliant on high-quality variable-length CoT data and face challenges like **'Format Collapse'** in RL approaches.
- A lack of unified evaluation standards across research efforts, including inconsistent base models, datasets, and metrics, hinders direct comparison and standardization of findings in the field.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) widely exhibit an 'overthinking phenomenon,' generating unnecessarily lengthy reasoning chains for many tasks.
> - Optimal reasoning lengths are non-uniform and vary with input difficulty, necessitating adaptive thinking to balance performance and efficiency.
> - Current research on concise and adaptive LRM thinking falls into two main paradigms: training-free methods (e.g., prompting, pipeline-based, decoding manipulation) and training-based methods (e.g., fine-tuning, reinforcement learning).
