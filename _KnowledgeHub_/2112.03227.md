# CALVIN: A Benchmark for Language-Conditioned Policy Learning for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> CALVIN introduces an open-source simulated benchmark for developing language-conditioned robot policies capable of executing complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks using continuous control. The benchmark includes diverse environments, multimodal sensor data, and natural language instructions, with a baseline model achieving 53.9% success on single tasks but only 0.08% on long-horizon sequences, highlighting current limitations.

## Problem

- Enabling general-purpose robots to understand and act on natural language instructions for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- Lack of standardized benchmarks for language-conditioned continuous visuomotor control, hindering reproducible research and cross-comparison of methods.
- Difficulty for robot policies to generalize acquired skills and language understanding to novel environments and unseen instructions in a zero-shot manner.

## Method

- **CALVIN** provides an open-source simulated benchmark featuring a ==7-DOF robot arm== interacting in four distinct, varied environments with common manipulation objects and ==continuous control==.
- It includes a ==multimodal observation space== (RGB-D, tactile, proprioception) and a dataset of approximately 24 hours of unstructured ==teleoperated play data==, with 1% annotated by over 400 crowd-sourced natural language instructions for 34 tasks.
- The **CALVIN Challenge** defines evaluation protocols for ==multi-task language control== and ==long-horizon tasks==, including demanding ==zero-shot generalization== settings to unseen environments.

## Results

- The MCIL baseline achieved a **53.9%** success rate on individual manipulation tasks (MTLC) when trained and tested in the same environment using static camera RGB-D.
- Long-horizon performance was notably low, with the MCIL baseline achieving only a **0.08%** success rate for chains of five language instructions (LH-MTLC) even within the same environment.
- Generalization to unseen environments (zero-shot) severely impacted performance, dropping single-task success to **38.6%** and effectively **0%** for instruction chains of two or more, indicating poor abstract concept generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The design of CALVIN with diverse tasks, multimodal inputs, and zero-shot generalization capabilities reveals the significant challenges for current robot learning methods in bridging language and continuous control.
> - Unstructured "play" data combined with sparse language annotations can be used to create a realistic and scalable dataset for language-conditioned policy learning.
> - Establishing a common evaluation framework is critical for advancing research in general-purpose, language-driven robot manipulation, as it highlights current limitations and encourages innovation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robots that can seamlessly coexist and assist humans in daily environments represents a grand challenge in robotics. A critical aspect of this challenge is enabling robots to interpret and act upon human language instructions, which offers an intuitive and scalable way for non-expert users to command robots.

Historically, robot learning has often focused on acquiring individual tasks, with multi-task learning approaches typically relying on simplified task specifications like goal images or one-hot skill selectors, which are impractical for real-world human instruction. The field has seen significant advancements in natural language processing (NLP) and vision-and-language grounding, leading to interactive systems for object localization or grounding spatial relations for pick-and-place tasks. However, much of this prior work has been limited to restricted environments, simplified actuators with discrete motion primitives, or short-horizon tasks. While there's a growing body of work on learning language-conditioned policies for continuous visuomotor control via imitation learning or reinforcement learning, a significant barrier has been the lack of standardized benchmarks and algorithm implementations, making cross-comparison and reproducible research difficult.

CALVIN directly addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive, open-source simulated benchmark. It pushes the boundaries beyond existing benchmarks like ALFRED, which focuses on combined navigation and manipulation with predefined action primitives. In contrast, CALVIN emphasizes learning a diverse repertoire of general-purpose skills from unconstrained language instructions for continuous control. It also extends the complexity beyond prior work like Lynch et al. (on which its baseline is built) by including more subtasks, longer long-horizon evaluation sequences, a wider range of sensor modalities, and crucial zero-shot generalization capabilities to unseen environments and novel language instructions. This focus on long-horizon, continuous control, multimodal inputs, and strong generalization makes CALVIN a unique and timely contribution to the robotic learning landscape, aspiring to bridge the gap between language understanding and complex physical interaction in realistic settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of the CALVIN benchmark is to enable the development of agents capable of solving many robotic manipulation tasks over a long horizon, from onboard sensors, and specified only via human language. The authors articulate several key motivations:

- **Bridging Language and Action:** To allow general-purpose robots to understand and relate human language to their perceptions and actions for a wide range of daily tasks.
- **Acquiring General-Purpose Skills:** To facilitate the learning of diverse skill repertoires that can be composed to perform long-horizon tasks, moving beyond single-task learning or limited multi-task approaches.
- **Scalable Task Specification:** To overcome the limitations of current robot instruction methods (e.g., goal images, one-hot selectors) by enabling intuitive task specification through unconstrained natural language, making robots accessible to non-expert users.
- **Addressing Complexity:** To create a benchmark that features tasks significantly more complex than existing datasets in terms of sequence length, action space, and language variability.
- **Supporting Multimodal Learning:** To enable the development of agents that can leverage diverse onboard sensor observations (RGB-D, proprioceptive, tactile) to improve multimodal perception and control.
- **Standardization and Reproducibility:** To provide a standardized, open-source framework for training, prototyping, and validating language-conditioned continuous control policies, thereby fostering more reproducible research and facilitating comparisons between different approaches in the robotics community.
- **Enabling Generalization:** To explicitly evaluate agents' ability to generalize, not only to novel language instructions but critically, to novel and unseen environments in a zero-shot manner, mimicking human-like abstract concept generalization.

In essence, CALVIN aims to accelerate progress towards truly general-purpose, language-driven robots that can autonomously acquire knowledge and execute complex instructions in diverse, real-world-like environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CALVIN is designed as a comprehensive benchmark, consisting of three main components: the CALVIN Environment, the CALVIN Dataset, and the CALVIN Challenge.

#### 3.1. The CALVIN Environment

The environment simulates a 7-Degrees-of-Freedom (DOF) Franka Emika Panda robot arm with a parallel gripper interacting with a desk setup. It features four distinct yet structurally related environments (A, B, C, D) to facilitate general play and specific task evaluations. These environments contain common manipulation objects like a sliding door, a drawer, a button, a switch, and three differently colored and shaped rectangular blocks. Crucially, the textures and static element positions vary across environments to test generalization, while the robot and static camera positions remain consistent. Physics are simulated using the PyBullet engine for fast data collection.

- **Observation Space:** CALVIN supports a rich multimodal observation space including:
 - RGB-D images from a static camera (200x200 resolution).
 - RGB-D images from a gripper camera (84x84 resolution).
 - Vision-based tactile sensing (120x160 resolution).
 - Continuous proprioceptive information (EE position/orientation, gripper width, joint positions).
- **Action Space:** The robot performs closed-loop continuous control at 30Hz. CALVIN supports flexible action spaces:
 - Absolute Cartesian pose (EE position, EE orientation, gripper action).
 - Relative Cartesian displacement (EE position, EE orientation, gripper action).
 - Joint action (joint positions, gripper action).
- **Tasks:** 34 specific manipulation tasks are defined (e.g., "rotate red block right," "open drawer," "stack blocks," "turn on light bulb"). The environment includes an automatic task detection mechanism, which checks for changes in environment state between initial and final steps, enabling sparse reward for reinforcement learning or efficient labeling of offline data.

#### 3.2. The CALVIN Dataset

- **Unstructured Demonstrations:** The dataset comprises approximately 24 hours of teleoperated "play" data, collected in all four environments (6 hours per environment). This amounts to ~2.4 million interaction steps. The data collection was unscripted, allowing untrained operators to explore and interact freely without predefined tasks, generating diverse and task-agnostic behaviors, including exploratory and sub-optimal actions valuable for learning robust representations.
- **Language Instructions:** Instead of relying solely on crowd-sourced annotations, CALVIN combines procedural labeling with crowd-sourced language. Over 400 crowd-sourced natural language instructions, with around 11 synonymous instructions per task, are provided for the 34 tasks. These instructions are automatically aligned with task-relevant segments of the robot interaction data by leveraging the environment's state-based task detection. Importantly, only 1% of the total robot interaction data is annotated with language instructions to simulate real-world data sparsity. Precomputed MiniLM embeddings (384-dimensional vectors) are also provided for all instructions, though users are free to use other language models.

#### 3.3. The CALVIN Challenge: Evaluation Protocols

The CALVIN Challenge defines a set of evaluation protocols of varying difficulty to assess the learned language-conditioned continuous control policies.

- **Training and Test Environments:**
 - **Single Environment:** Training and testing in the same environment (e.g., D → D).
 - **Multi Environment:** Training in all four environments and testing in one specific environment (e.g., A,B,C,D → D).
 - **Zero-Shot Multi Environment:** Training in three environments and testing in a completely unseen fourth environment (e.g., A,B,C → D). This is the most challenging setting, requiring generalization to different textures and static element placements.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Multi-Task Language Control (MTLC):** Evaluates generalization to 34 manipulation tasks using unseen language instructions. For each task, 10 rollouts are performed from different valid starting states.
 - **Long-Horizon MTLC (LH-MTLC):** Assesses the policy's ability to accomplish sequences of five sequential language instructions. This is highly challenging as it requires chaining subgoals. 1000 unique instruction chains are used, and the robot is reset to a neutral position after each sequence, forcing reliance on language. The agent only progresses to the next instruction if the current task is successfully completed.
- **Sensor Combinations:** Evaluations are conducted across various sensor combinations, including different subsets of static camera RGB-D, gripper camera RGB-D, and tactile sensing, to study their impact on performance.

#### 3.4. Baseline Model: Multi-Context Imitation Learning (MCIL)

The paper establishes baseline performance using the Multi-Context Imitation Learning (MCIL) approach, which was previously shown effective for long-horizon language-conditioned manipulation tasks. MCIL addresses the challenge of learning from unstructured "play" data. It works by relabeling short trajectories from the play data with "goal states" (treating visited states as reached goals). For language conditioning, MCIL adapts this by pairing a small fraction of the play data with crowd-sourced language instructions. It trains a single latent goal-conditioned policy over multiple datasets (goal images and language instructions) simultaneously, using a sequence-to-sequence conditional variational auto-encoder (seq2seq CVAE) to encode contextual demonstrations into a latent "plan" space. The policy then reconstructs actions conditioned on current state, goal, and inferred plan. The baseline implementation follows Lynch et al., using an Adam optimizer, specific network architectures for visual and tactile encoders, and early-fusion for depth images. Absolute Cartesian actions are used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate that the MCIL baseline model performs poorly on the complex and long-horizon tasks presented in CALVIN, highlighting the significant challenges that the benchmark poses for current state-of-the-art methods.

- **Single-Task Performance (MTLC):**
 - When trained and tested on the same environment (D → D) using static camera RGB-D, the MCIL baseline achieved a success rate of **53.9%** for the 34 individual manipulation tasks (MTLC evaluation).
 - Adding a gripper camera, depth channels, or tactile sensing did not lead to substantial improvements in this setting.
- **Long-Horizon Performance (LH-MTLC):**
 - The performance dropped dramatically for long-horizon tasks. The best MCIL model achieved a mere **0.08%** success rate when following chains of five language instructions in a row (LH-MTLC) even when training and testing on the same environment (D → D).
 - While the policy could solve the first subtask of a chain in 48.9% of cases (similar to its single-task performance), it struggled to chain these successes into complete long-horizon tasks.
- **Generalization to Novel Environments:**
 - Performance generally decreased significantly when evaluating in multi-environment settings (training on A,B,C,D and testing on D) and especially in zero-shot multi-environment settings (training on A,B,C and testing on D, an unseen environment).
 - For instance, in the zero-shot setting (A,B,C → D) with static camera RGB-D, the MTLC success rate dropped to 38.6%, and the LH-MTLC (1 instruction) success rate fell to 20.2%, with effectively 0% for chains of 2 or more instructions.
- **Qualitative Observations:**
 - The authors noted that the baseline sometimes confused object colors (e.g., red and blue blocks) in instructions, suggesting difficulties in visual grounding.
 - Performance was observed to be sensitive to the robot's initial position, implying that the agent might rely on context rather than learning to disentangle initial states and tasks.

**Summary of Challenges Highlighted:** The results clearly indicate that the MCIL baseline struggles with:
1. **Long-horizon planning and execution:** Chaining multiple skills remains a significant hurdle.
2. **Robust perceptual grounding:** Accurately identifying objects based on language (e.g., distinguishing colored blocks) given visual input.
3. **Generalization to unseen environments:** Adapting to new textures and object placements, especially in a zero-shot manner.
4. **Effective multimodal sensor fusion:** The baseline did not significantly benefit from additional sensor modalities, suggesting that more sophisticated fusion strategies are needed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CALVIN represents a crucial step forward in robotic learning research due to its comprehensive and challenging nature, which directly exposes the limitations of current state-of-the-art approaches. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Standardization and Reproducibility:** By providing an open-source, standardized benchmark with detailed environments, datasets, and evaluation protocols, CALVIN will significantly foster reproducible research in language-driven robotics. This allows researchers to compare different models and approaches on a common ground, accelerating progress.
- **Driving Innovation:** The poor baseline performance of MCIL on CALVIN, especially in long-horizon and zero-shot generalization tasks, underscores that significant research gaps exist. CALVIN explicitly challenges the community to develop more innovative agents that can learn to:
 - Acquire diverse, general-purpose skills.
 - Perform robust object detection and referring expression grounding.
 - Achieve robust action grounding from natural language.
 - Master task-agnostic continuous control.
 - Plan and execute complex, long-horizon sequences of actions.
 - Generalize abstract concepts and skills to unseen entities and environments, mimicking human cognitive abilities.
- **Realistic Simulation:** The benchmark's support for multimodal, high-dimensional onboard sensor inputs (RGB-D, tactile, proprioception) and 7-DOF continuous control in varied, yet structurally related, simulated environments brings it closer to real-world complexities. This design encourages the development of methods that are more likely to transfer to physical robots.
- **Focus on Generalization:** The explicit inclusion of zero-shot multi-environment evaluation is a critical feature. This pushes research towards agents that can truly generalize, rather than merely memorizing training scenarios, which is essential for service robots operating in diverse daily settings.
- **Catalyst for Future Research:** CALVIN is poised to become a foundational benchmark for the field. It will undoubtedly inspire new research in areas such as:
 - Novel language-conditioned policy architectures.
 - Advanced multimodal sensor fusion techniques.
 - Robust imitation learning or reinforcement learning algorithms capable of handling long horizons and sparsity.
 - Techniques for improved visual-language grounding and disentangling causal factors.
 - Domain adaptation and meta-learning strategies for strong zero-shot generalization.

In conclusion, CALVIN is more than just a dataset or an environment; it is a meticulously designed challenge that defines the frontier for language-driven robotics. By highlighting the current limitations, it serves as a powerful catalyst for the development of scalable, general-purpose, and truly intelligent robotic systems that can interact with humans through natural language in complex, dynamic environments.
