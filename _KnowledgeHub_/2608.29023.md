# Teaching Robot Policies to Humans Using Erroneous Examples

> [!summary] Summary
> This work extends a demonstration-based robot policy teaching loop with two classroom techniques: self-explanation, where learners verbalize reasoning, and erroneous examples, where learners critique and correct a suboptimal robot trajectory. A 39-participant user study across taxi and skateboard gridworld domains found no significant immediate gains, but both interventions improved policy retention on a delayed post-test, and IRL-style reasoners predicted policies best.

## Problem

- Prior demonstration-based policy teaching shows only optimal robot trajectories, hitting a ceiling on how well people grasp nuanced, multi-feature reward functions.
- Existing teaching loops measure only immediate information gain, never testing whether humans retain a learned robot policy hours or days later.
- Belief-modeling frameworks assume humans reason like inverse reinforcement learners, but that assumption rested on sparse optional survey data, never directly validated.

## Method

- **Erroneous-example** unit tests present a suboptimal trajectory the learner must explain as wrong, then correct and justify, mirroring math-pedagogy prompt-response structure.
- **Self-explanation** condition keeps standard trajectory-prediction tests but adds an open-ended text box requiring learners to verbalize reasoning before feedback.
- Erroneous examples are mined from prior study data by selecting the majority incorrect trajectory per unit test, or grouping equal-reward errors sharing one dominant misconception.

## Results

- No significant condition effect on optimal responses immediately (**F(1,39)=0.65, p=0.528**) or on the delayed post-test (**F(1,30)=0.136, p=0.873**); regret likewise null.
- Only the control group declined from immediate to delayed post-test; self-explanation and erroneous-example groups improved, with self-explanation showing the largest regret drop.
- Coded explanations (**363** responses, **78%** coder agreement, Cohen's kappa **0.63**) were dominated by IRL-style reasoning, and IRL-style learners beat other styles on both metrics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pedagogical interventions may pay off in retention rather than immediate accuracy, so policy-transparency studies without a delayed post-test can miss the real effect.
> - Humans learning robot policies spontaneously reason about feature costs and tradeoffs, empirically supporting IRL-based human belief models used in machine teaching.
> - Extra explanation prompts did not raise self-reported cognitive load, but learners rated belief-contradicting erroneous examples as less informative, confusion can masquerade as poor teaching.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of human-robot collaboration (HRC), specifically focusing on the critical aspect of human understanding of robot policies. As autonomous agents become more prevalent in diverse environments, from warehouses to medical settings, ensuring that humans can comprehend and trust their behavior is paramount. This work directly addresses the challenge of explainability and transparency in artificial intelligence, particularly for sequential decision-making models like those based on reinforcement learning (RL).

Previous research in explainable RL has explored various methods to convey robot policies to humans, including directly exposing numerical rewards, using inherently interpretable models, saliency maps, and demonstration-based explanations. Demonstration-based explanations, which involve showing examples of a robot's behavior in different environments, have been a significant focus due to their utility in conveying complex internal reward functions. These methods have often drawn inspiration from educational literature, applying teaching techniques like scaffolding (incrementally increasing difficulty) and remedial education (correcting misconceptions) to human-robot interaction.

However, the existing literature indicates a "ceiling" on how well humans understand nuanced policies, even with various interventions, particularly when only optimal robot behaviors are demonstrated. The current work identifies a gap in existing robot policy teaching frameworks, which primarily focus on presenting optimal behavior or "positive examples." It posits that no single teaching method has been universally proven effective across varying domains, difficulties, or learner types.

This paper extends the existing demonstration-based policy teaching framework by integrating concepts from mathematical pedagogy: the use of *erroneous examples* and *self-explanation*. Erroneous examples, which involve reflecting on and correcting incorrect responses, have been shown in traditional education to help learners understand common pitfalls and improve long-term retention. Self-explanation, the verbalization of one's reasoning, is also a recognized pedagogical tool for enhancing comprehension and retention. By applying these techniques to the teaching of robot policies, this research aims to overcome the limitations of purely optimal demonstrations and advance the methods by which humans learn robot behaviors, thereby contributing to more effective, safer, and trustworthy HRC. The work also builds upon the assumption, often made in modeling human learning of robot policies, that humans use an inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) approach to infer underlying reward functions from observed behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance human understanding and retention of robot decision-making processes (policies) by integrating pedagogical tools proven effective in human education: erroneous examples and self-explanations. The overarching motivation is to improve human-robot collaboration by fostering greater transparency and trust, which are considered foundational for effective and safe interaction with autonomous agents.

Specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Addressing Limitations of Current Teaching Methods:** Existing methods for teaching robot policies, predominantly demonstration-based explanations that showcase optimal behavior, have demonstrated limitations in conveying complex or nuanced policies. There is a need for approaches that can help humans grasp the intricacies of robot decision-making beyond what optimal examples alone can provide. The paper highlights that, similar to traditional classrooms where only showing correct solutions might not be sufficient, only showing optimal robot behaviors may hinder comprehensive understanding.
- **Leveraging Educational Psychology Principles:** The research is motivated by findings from educational literature, particularly in mathematics pedagogy, which indicate that self-explanation and the critical analysis of erroneous examples significantly improve concept retention, reasoning, and the understanding of common misconceptions. The authors aim to investigate whether these benefits, observed in human-to-human learning contexts, transfer to human learning from robots.
- **Integrating Novel Pedagogical Tools:** The core objective is to integrate self-explanation and erroneous examples into an existing robot policy teaching framework. This involves modifying the traditional policy teaching loop, which typically consists of demonstrations, unit tests, and feedback, to incorporate prompts for verbalizing reasoning and confronting suboptimal robot trajectories.
- **Empirically Evaluating Effectiveness:** A key objective is to conduct a user study to empirically assess the effectiveness of these integrated pedagogical tools. The study is designed to measure whether viewing incorrect demonstrations and articulating one's reasoning improves a human's ability to predict robot actions, particularly focusing on long-term retention of the learned policy. The inclusion of a delayed post-test is specifically motivated by educational literature, which suggests that the benefits of erroneous examples often manifest in long-term retention rather than immediate performance gains.
- **Characterizing Human Learning Styles:** The research also aims to contribute to understanding how humans learn robot policies by characterizing different learning or explanation styles adopted by participants. This includes investigating whether humans naturally adopt Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)-like reasoning, which is often assumed in computational models of human belief about robot policies.
- **Validating Modeling Assumptions:** By collecting and analyzing qualitative data from participant explanations, the study seeks to validate the underlying assumptions made in human belief modeling, specifically the premise that humans use IRL-like reasoning to infer a robot's reward function. Such validation can inform the development of more accurate and effective machine teaching algorithms.

In essence, the research seeks to bridge insights from educational theory with practical challenges in human-robot interaction to develop more robust and effective methods for human comprehension of robot behavior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This study employs a mixed-methods approach, combining a quantitative user study with qualitative analysis of participant responses, underpinned by theoretical frameworks from machine teaching and explainable reinforcement learning.

**Theoretical Foundations:**
The methodology draws on the machine teaching framework, which posits that a robot teacher can summarize its policy by selecting informative demonstrations to help a learner recover the optimal policy. A core assumption maintained from prior work is that human learning of robot policies can be modeled through Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL), where the human approximates the robot’s underlying reward function from demonstrations and then uses planning to predict future behavior.

**Human Belief Modeling (Particle Filter):**
To model human understanding and generate a curriculum, the study utilizes a particle filter-based method. This filter tracks a distribution of possible reward weights (representing human beliefs about the robot's policy). Particles are updated Bayesian-style based on constraints derived from demonstrations and unit tests. These constraints are half-spaces in the belief space, distinguishing beliefs consistent with the observed behavior from inconsistent ones. A custom probability distribution function, combining a uniform distribution for consistent beliefs and a von Mises-Fischer distribution for inconsistent beliefs, is used for particle weight updates. This particle filter was used to generate a fixed curriculum for the user study, ensuring consistency across participants, while also being capable of adaptive sampling for personalized teaching. The curriculum was designed using scaffolding (incremental difficulty) and counterfactual reasoning (targeting common misconceptions).

**Robot Policy Teaching Loop:**
The study adapts a partial feedback policy teaching loop from existing literature. This loop consists of:
1. **Demonstrations:** Visualized action-by-action optimal trajectories of the robot in sample environments.
2. **Unit Tests:** Opportunities for learners to predict the robot’s optimal trajectory in new, unseen environments.
3. **Feedback:** If a learner's response is incorrect, they are shown the optimal trajectory alongside their provided response.
This loop is repeated until all knowledge components of the policy's reward function are presented.

**User Study Design:**
A user study was conducted with a *between-subjects* design for the treatment conditions and a *within-subjects* design for the domains. All participants experienced an identical teaching curriculum of demonstrations and tests, with the difference being the format of the unit tests.

- **Treatment Groups:**
 - **Control Group:** Participants received standard unit tests, asking them to predict the optimal trajectory in a new environment. This served as a benchmark, replicating previous teaching loop designs.
 - **Self-Explanation Group:** Participants predicted the optimal trajectory and were then required to provide a text-based explanation of their reasoning.
 - **Erroneous Example Group:** Participants were shown an *incorrect* robot trajectory in a new environment. They were asked to explain why it was incorrect, then provide the correct trajectory, and finally explain their reasoning for the correct trajectory. This mirrored the prompt structure used in educational literature on erroneous examples.

- **Domains:** Participants were taught policies for two visually masked grid-world Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), presented in randomized order:
 - **Taxi Domain:** A 4x3 grid where a robot picks up and delivers a package, with rewards for delivery and recharge stations, and penalties for mud. Described as less complex.
 - **Skateboard Domain:** A 6x5 grid where a robot reaches a destination, with reduced action costs when using a skateboard. Described as more complex with tighter tradeoffs.

**Erroneous Example Selection:**
Erroneous examples for the third treatment group were derived from common misconceptions observed in prior studies using the same curriculum. For each unit test, if a singular incorrect trajectory represented the majority of incorrect responses, it was selected. If not, a dominant misconception was identified by analyzing the reward of incorrect trajectories that could stem from the same underlying incorrect belief about the reward function.

**Measures:**
The study collected both quantitative and qualitative data:
- **M1. Optimal Response:** Binary score (optimal/not optimal) for test trajectories.
- **M2. Regret:** Real-valued score quantifying the difference in total reward/cost between the participant's trajectory and the optimal trajectory.
- **M3. Improved Understanding Rating:** 5-point Likert scale rating provided after each demonstration and test/feedback interaction.
- **M4. Cognitive Load:** 5-point Likert scale rating provided after the training phase.

**Assessments:**
All participants took an immediate post-test and a delayed post-test (24-36 hours later). Both post-tests used the control group's format (predict optimal trajectory) to measure policy understanding and retention.

**Participants:**
39 participants were recruited from Prolific for the initial study phase. 30 of these participants returned for the delayed post-test. Participants were randomly assigned to treatment groups and domain order.

**Qualitative Analysis of Explanations:**
Open-ended text responses from the self-explanation and erroneous example groups were categorized into five styles:
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** References to previous demonstrations.
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** References to environmental features, costs, and tradeoffs.
- **Procedural:** References to task mechanics or instructions.
- **Understanding:** Statements about confidence or confusion.
- **Other:** Responses not fitting other categories.
Two independent coders categorized these responses, achieving a Cohen's kappa of 0.63, indicating moderate inter-coder agreement. Participants were then classified into learning styles based on their most frequent explanation type.

**Statistical Analysis:**
Mixed ANOVAs were used to analyze the quantitative measures, assessing the effects of test condition, domain, and their interaction on optimal responses, regret, understanding ratings, and cognitive load. Comparisons between immediate and delayed post-test performance were also conducted.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the effectiveness of self-explanation and erroneous examples, the influence of domain complexity, and the nature of human reasoning about robot policies.

**Performance on Post-Tests (H1 & H2):**
- **Optimal Responses:** There was no statistically significant effect of the test condition (control, self-explanation, erroneous example) on the number of optimal responses in either the immediate or delayed post-tests.
- **Regret:** Similarly, no significant effect of test condition was found on the sum of regret in either post-test.
- **Domain Effect:** A significant effect of the domain was observed on both optimal responses and regret in the immediate post-test. Participants performed better and exhibited lower regret in the Taxi domain, which was characterized as less complex, compared to the more complex Skateboard domain. This domain effect persisted for regret in the delayed post-test, but not for optimal responses, potentially due to ceiling effects in the Taxi domain and floor effects in the Skateboard domain.

**Change Between Immediate and Delayed Post-tests (H3 & H4):**
- Although statistical significance was not achieved, observed trends suggested differences in policy retention over time across groups.
- The self-explanation and erroneous example groups showed an improvement in mean optimal responses and a decrease in cumulative regret from the immediate to the delayed post-test.
- In contrast, the control group exhibited a decline in mean optimal responses and an increase in cumulative regret over the same period.
- These trends, while not statistically significant in this study, align with findings in educational literature which indicate that self-explanation and erroneous examples can foster better long-term concept retention.

**Improved Understanding (H5):**
- No statistically significant effect of test condition was found on participants' self-reported ratings of improved understanding after demonstrations or unit tests.
- A possible explanation suggested is that erroneous examples, by potentially contradicting existing beliefs, might have been perceived by participants as causing confusion rather than improving understanding, despite their potential long-term benefits.

**Cognitive Load (H6):**
- No statistically significant effect of test condition on participants' self-reported cognitive load was observed.
- This outcome was unexpected, given that the self-explanation and erroneous example conditions required additional cognitive effort for analysis and explanation. Potential reasons include the framing of the cognitive load question or participants allocating minimal effort to the explanation prompts.

**Analysis of Explanation Responses (Qualitative Data):**
- **Prevalence of Reasoning Styles:** Qualitative analysis of the 363 open-ended explanations revealed that Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)-based explanations were the dominant type of reasoning across both the self-explanation and erroneous example groups. These explanations referred to environmental features, their associated costs or rewards, and the tradeoffs involved (e.g., "Grabbing green would significantly increase the distance taken to get to the goal, so it is not worth it").
- **Learning Style Categorization:** Most participants in the experimental groups were categorized as IRL learners, meaning their reasoning predominantly focused on inferring the underlying reward function.
- **Validation of IRL Assumption:** The prevalence of IRL-based reasoning among participants provides empirical evidence to support the assumption, often made in modeling human understanding of robot policies, that humans tend to use an IRL-like process. This represents a novel contribution of the study.
- **Performance by Learning Style:** Participants categorized as IRL learners demonstrated superior performance on both immediate and delayed post-tests, in terms of higher optimal responses and lower regret, compared to participants exhibiting other reasoning styles (e.g., imitation learning or procedural reasoning). This suggests that IRL-like reasoning is an effective strategy for learning robot policies.

In summary, while the quantitative results did not show statistically significant differences in immediate performance or self-reported understanding/cognitive load between the intervention groups and the control, there were observed trends suggesting improved long-term retention for the self-explanation and erroneous example groups. Crucially, the qualitative analysis provided strong evidence for the widespread use of IRL-like reasoning by human learners and indicated that this reasoning style correlates with better policy prediction performance, validating a foundational assumption in the field.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of human-robot collaboration by proposing and evaluating novel pedagogical interventions for teaching robot policies to humans. While the study acknowledges limitations in statistical power and sample size, its findings offer several implications for advancing human-robot interaction.

The observed trends in improved policy retention for the self-explanation and erroneous example groups from immediate to delayed post-tests, despite the lack of statistical significance, align with established principles in educational literature. This suggests that incorporating these methods could be crucial for developing robust training curricula for human collaborators, particularly when long-term memory of robot policies is essential, such as in high-stakes environments like surgical assistance or complex industrial tasks. The ability to retain policy understanding over time, rather than just immediate comprehension, is critical for building enduring trust and facilitating sustained, effective collaboration.

A significant contribution of this work lies in its qualitative analysis of human explanation responses. The findings validate the long-standing assumption in machine teaching and human-robot interaction research that humans learning robot policies often employ Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)-like reasoning. Participants predominantly verbalized their understanding in terms of environmental features, costs, and rewards, actively attempting to infer the robot's underlying reward function. Furthermore, the correlation between IRL-style reasoning and higher performance on policy prediction tasks provides empirical support for the effectiveness of this cognitive approach. This validation can guide future research in more accurately modeling human learning processes and designing more targeted and effective machine teaching algorithms that explicitly cater to IRL-like inference.

The study also provides insights into the challenges of teaching complex policies, consistent with prior work. The difficulty differences between the Taxi and Skateboard domains highlight that teaching interventions may need to be tailored to the inherent complexity and subtlety of the robot's reward function.

Future work, specifically with larger sample sizes, could establish the statistical significance of the observed retention benefits, providing stronger evidence for integrating erroneous examples and self-explanation into robot teaching frameworks. Exploring different formats for explanations (e.g., multiple-choice, fill-in-the-blank) and the timing of erroneous example presentation (e.g., as standalone demonstrations) could further refine the pedagogical approach. Additionally, investigating how these interventions differentially impact learners with varying levels of prior knowledge about autonomous agents could lead to adaptive teaching strategies.

Ultimately, by advancing methods for human understanding and long-term retention of robot policies, this research directly contributes to the broader goals of increasing transparency, fostering trust, and enhancing safety in human-robot collaboration, which are increasingly critical as autonomous agents become more integrated into daily life.
