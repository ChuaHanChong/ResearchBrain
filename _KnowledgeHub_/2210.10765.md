# When to Ask for Help: Proactive Interventions in Autonomous Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework named Proactive Agent INTerventions (PAINT) enables autonomous reinforcement learning agents to proactively detect and manage irreversible states in complex environments. This approach substantially reduces the need for human interventions and label queries while achieving comparable or superior task performance across various continuous control benchmarks.

## Problem

- Autonomous reinforcement learning agents struggle in real-world environments characterized by irreversible states from which they cannot recover independently, requiring constant human supervision.
- Existing safe RL methods often assume prior knowledge of safety labels or require a high frequency of human feedback (e.g., querying labels at every time-step), which is impractical and costly.
- The high cost of human monitoring and manual resets limits the scalability and practical deployment of RL agents, particularly in physical robotic systems.

## Method

- **PAINT** modifies the reward function to strongly penalize actions leading to ==irreversible states==, assigning them a fixed, extremely low Q-value to discourage their visitation.
- A neural network classifier learns to estimate ==state reversibility==, utilizing a highly label-efficient ==binary search algorithm== that queries only ==O(log T) labels per trajectory== instead of O(T).
- The agent proactively requests a human reset when its learned classifier predicts it has entered an irreversible state, optionally performing additional ==exploration steps== before the actual intervention.

## Results

- PAINT significantly reduced human interventions (e.g., **80 resets** in Tabletop Organization vs. **3000+** for baselines) and label queries (e.g., **3,260 labels** for **200,000 states** in Maze vs. **200,000** for baselines) across various tasks.
- The framework achieved task success rates comparable to or better than existing safe RL and reversibility-aware methods, with intervention counts **plateauing over training**, indicating effective learning to avoid irreversible states.
- Ablation studies confirmed the **critical role** of post-prediction exploration steps in enhancing the learned reversibility classifier and the overall efficiency of the PAINT framework.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - By modifying the Bellman update to assign a fixed, low Q-value to actions leading to irreversible states, the agent is effectively incentivized to avoid them without requiring an oracle.
> - The sequential nature of irreversible states within a trajectory allows for a binary search-based labeling strategy, drastically reducing the number of expert queries needed to identify the point of irreversibility.
> - Performing short, random exploration steps after the agent predicts an irreversible state but before a human reset provides valuable data to refine the reversibility classifier, improving its accuracy and the agent's overall learning efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous reinforcement learning (RL) agents that can learn and interact in real-world environments is a significant long-term objective in the field. A fundamental challenge in achieving such autonomy is the presence of irreversible states. These are states from which an agent cannot recover independently and require external assistance, such as a human supervisor resetting a robot or an object in its workspace.

Current RL approaches often necessitate continuous human monitoring to identify irreversible states and initiate interventions. This paper addresses this limitation by proposing a framework that enables agents to proactively detect and manage irreversible states, thereby reducing the need for constant human supervision.

This work relates to several existing research areas:

- **Autonomous RL**: Prior research in autonomous RL often focuses on minimizing human-provided resets at the end of episodes. However, many of these methods assume the environment is fully reversible. Exceptions like the "Leave No Trace" algorithm (Eysenbach et al., 2017) check for return to initial states and request intervention otherwise, but this paper's approach differs by basing resets on estimated state reversibility. The current work specifically tackles environments where irreversibility is a core characteristic, going beyond the assumption of a fully reversible environment.
- **Safe RL**: Safe RL algorithms aim to learn policies that minimize visits to unsafe regions of the state space. They typically achieve this by formulating constrained optimization problems or assigning low rewards to unsafe states. A common assumption in safe RL is the availability of safety labels for states, often queried on demand at every time-step. This work diverges by addressing scenarios where the agent initially lacks knowledge of irreversible states and aims to learn to detect them while significantly minimizing the number of explicit label queries required from a human supervisor.
- **Reversibility-aware RL**: Some studies have explored reversibility and reachability in RL, focusing on avoiding actions leading to irreversible states or guiding exploration towards difficult-to-reach states. While prior methods might be self-supervised, this paper introduces a method that leverages a limited number of binary reversibility labels to achieve greater intervention efficiency, particularly in non-episodic settings.
- **Human-in-the-loop learning**: This field involves incorporating human feedback to train RL agents. However, many interactive RL algorithms are feedback-intensive, relying on input at every time-step. More feedback-efficient algorithms learn reward models from preferences, but this paper introduces a different mode of feedback—reversibility labels—and a mechanism to acquire them efficiently.

By focusing on learning to detect and proactively intervene in irreversible environments with high label and intervention efficiency, this research contributes to bridging the gap between theoretical RL advancements and practical deployment in complex, real-world robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research is to advance the autonomy of reinforcement learning agents, particularly those deployed in real-world environments where irreversible situations can arise. The paper identifies a critical impediment to full autonomy: the existence of states from which an agent cannot recover on its own, such as a robot physically displacing an object beyond its reach. In such scenarios, external intervention, often from a human supervisor, is currently indispensable for the learning process to continue.

The limitations of existing paradigms drive the specific objectives of this work:

1. **Reduce Human Monitoring**: Traditional RL agents require constant human observation to detect when an irreversible state is entered and when an intervention is necessary. This continuous monitoring is resource-intensive and limits scalability. A primary objective is to liberate human supervisors from this ongoing task.
2. **Enable Proactive Intervention**: Instead of passive monitoring, the goal is to design agents that can actively recognize irreversible states and autonomously request assistance *before* prolonged unproductive interaction. This shifts the burden from continuous human vigilance to an on-demand, agent-initiated request for help.
3. **Learn to Detect Irreversible States**: Since the set of irreversible states is typically unknown a priori in realistic environments, the agent must develop an internal mechanism to identify these states. This involves learning a model that can predict the reversibility of a given state.
4. **Learn to Avoid Irreversible States**: Beyond detection, the agent should also learn to adapt its policy to minimize visits to irreversible states, thereby preventing the need for interventions in the first place. This requires integrating reversibility awareness directly into the policy learning process.
5. **Achieve Label and Intervention Efficiency**: Current safe RL methods often assume that safety labels can be queried for every state, leading to a high demand for human input. This work aims to significantly reduce this labeling burden. Similarly, it seeks to minimize the total number of interventions over the entire training period, making the learning process more practical and less costly. The paper aims for superior "sample-efficiency" (how quickly the agent learns the task) and "intervention-efficiency" (minimizing human resets) compared to existing methods.

In summary, the motivation stems from the need for RL agents to operate more independently in complex, irreversible environments by empowering them to detect and avoid detrimental states and proactively solicit help when required, all while minimizing the associated human supervision and data labeling effort.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named **Proactive Agent INTerventions (PAINT)**, is designed to enable RL agents to learn autonomously in environments with irreversible states while minimizing human supervision. PAINT integrates three core components: a modified reward function for penalizing irreversible states, an efficient mechanism for estimating state reversibility, and a proactive intervention strategy.

#### 3.1. Penalizing Visitation of Irreversible States

To encourage the agent to avoid irreversible states, the framework modifies the conventional reward function `r(s,a)` to a surrogate reward function `~r(s,a)`.
- If the next state `s'` is reversible (`R_ρ(s') = 1`), the agent receives the standard environment reward `r(s,a)`.
- If `s'` is irreversible (`R_ρ(s') = 0`), the agent receives a significantly low constant reward, `R_min - ε`, where `R_min` is the minimum possible environment reward and `ε` is a small positive hyperparameter. This value is designed to be worse than any achievable reward, making irreversible states highly undesirable.

This modification ensures that actions leading to irreversible states consistently have lower Q-values. The Q-value for any state-action pair that leads to an irreversible state `s'` will converge to `(R_min - ε) / (1 - γ)`, where `γ` is the discount factor. This observation allows for a modified Bellman backup operator `B_π` that directly regresses to this fixed low value for irreversible states, bypassing the need for recursive backups. The Bellman update equation is adapted to incorporate `R_ρ(s')` (the true reversibility label) to determine whether to use the standard Q-value update or the fixed low Q-value. A theoretical analysis (Theorem A.1 and A.2 in Appendix) demonstrates that this modification ensures actions leading to reversible states are preferred over those leading to irreversible ones under certain conditions.

#### 3.2. Estimating Reversibility

Since the true reversibility function `R_ρ(s)` is unknown, PAINT trains a neural network classifier, `ˆR_ρ(s): S →`, to estimate the probability of a state being reversible. The `ˆR_ρ` classifier is trained using binary cross-entropy loss on a dataset of visited states with their corresponding reversibility labels.

A crucial aspect of PAINT is its **label-efficient scheme** for acquiring these `R_ρ(s)` labels:
- **Property of Irreversible States**: The method leverages two key observations: (a) any state following an irreversible state is also irreversible, and (b) any state preceding a reversible state is also reversible. This implies that any trajectory can be conceptually divided into an initial reversible segment and a subsequent irreversible segment (which may be empty).
- **Binary Search Labeling**: To identify the transition point from reversible to irreversible states within a trajectory `τ` of length `T`, a binary search algorithm is employed. Instead of querying `O(T)` labels for all states in a trajectory, this binary search identifies the first irreversible state (or confirms full reversibility) with at most `O(log T)` queries.
- **Batch Labeling**: Labels are not queried continuously. Instead, all new states visited since the previous intervention are collected into a trajectory. At the moment an intervention is requested (or at the end of an extended episode in some settings), this entire batch of states is labeled using the binary search procedure. This significantly reduces the frequency of human interaction for labeling.
- **Theoretical Guarantee**: Theorem 5.1 and Lemma A.4 show that the suboptimality of the learned policy is bounded by `δ`, the `L_∞` norm difference between the true and estimated reversibility functions `R_ρ` and `ˆR_ρ`. This implies that a more accurate `ˆR_ρ` leads to a policy closer to the optimal.

#### 3.3. Proactive Interventions

To enable proactive behavior, the agent uses its learned `ˆR_ρ(s)` classifier to decide when to request human intervention.
- **Intervention Rule**: The agent executes a special `a_reset` action (requesting a reset) if its reversibility classifier predicts `ˆR_ρ(s) < 0.5`. This threshold indicates that the agent believes it has entered an irreversible state.
- **Exploration Steps**: After detecting an irreversible state and deciding to request an intervention, the agent can optionally perform a fixed number of uniform-random exploration steps before the environment is actually reset. This provides additional data points about irreversible states, which can further improve the `ˆR_ρ` classifier.

#### 3.4. Putting It Together (PAINT Algorithm)

The overall PAINT framework operates in trials. During each trial:
1. The environment starts from an initial state `s ~ ρ_0`.
2. The agent interacts with the environment, executing actions based on its current policy.
3. The critic's Q-value function is updated using the modified Bellman operator `ˆB_π`. For states that have already been labeled (from past interventions), the true `R_ρ` is used in the Bellman update; for unlabeled states (from the current trial), the classifier's prediction `ˆR_ρ` acts as a pseudo-label.
4. Continuously, the agent monitors the current state `s` with `ˆR_ρ(s)`.
5. If `ˆR_ρ(s) < 0.5`, the agent initiates the intervention process: it performs exploration steps and then requests a reset.
6. Upon an intervention (either agent-requested or an environment-imposed reset, if applicable), all newly collected state-action-reward-next_state tuples since the last intervention are processed. The binary search algorithm (Alg. 1) is applied to label these new states for reversibility.
7. The `ˆR_ρ` classifier is then trained or updated using *all* available labeled data (new and old).
8. The environment is reset to `s ~ ρ_0`, starting a new trial.

PAINT is designed to be compatible with any value-based RL algorithm and can be applied in both episodic and non-episodic (e.g., building upon MEDAL for forward-backward learning) settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates PAINT across a suite of continuous control environments, including Maze (episodic), Tabletop Organization (forward-backward), Peg Insertion (forward-backward), and Half-Cheetah Vel (continuing). The main findings focus on the efficiency of PAINT in terms of required interventions and label queries, as well as its overall task success compared to baseline methods.

#### 4.1. Intervention and Sample Efficiency

- **Tabletop Organization & Peg Insertion (Forward-Backward Tasks)**: PAINT demonstrated superior intervention efficiency. In Tabletop Organization, PAINT required approximately 80 resets (roughly one intervention per 25,000 steps) and in Peg Insertion, 124 resets, significantly fewer than Leave No Trace (LNT) and Reversibility-Aware Exploration (RAE). These numbers also plateaued over training, indicating the agent increasingly learned to avoid irreversible states. MEDAL, without an intervention rule, used fewer interventions but failed to make meaningful task progress.
- **Half-Cheetah Vel (Continuing Task)**: PAINT achieved performance comparable to SMBPO with Oracle Termination (an idealized baseline that knows true reversibility and intervenes immediately), converging after around 750 resets. In contrast, a standard episodic RL agent (receiving resets every 2,000 steps) and RAE learned substantially slower in terms of interventions.
- **Maze (Episodic Task)**: PAINT achieved comparable task success to safe RL methods (SMBPO, SQRL) while requiring significantly fewer labels.

#### 4.2. Label Efficiency

- **Significant Reduction in Label Queries**: One of the most prominent findings is the drastic reduction in human-provided reversibility labels.
 - In the Maze task, PAINT required an average of 3,260 label queries to process 200,000 visited states, whereas safe RL baselines like SMBPO and SQRL required 200,000 labels (one per time-step).
 - For Tabletop Organization, PAINT required approximately 1,021 labels, and for Peg Insertion, 2,083 labels.
 - In the Half-Cheetah task, PAINT used around 8,748 labels over 3 million steps, compared to the 3 million labels required by SMBPO with Oracle Termination.
- **Effective Reversibility Classifier Learning**: The reversibility classifier learned by PAINT was effective at identifying irreversible regions. For example, in the Maze task, it accurately identified the path to the goal as reversible while classifying other (task-irrelevant) regions, even some that were technically reversible, as irreversible. This conservative classification strategy was beneficial for the policy as it reduced the exploration space.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Sensitivity Analysis (from Appendix)

- **Impact of Exploration Steps**: Performing random exploration steps after the agent's classifier predicts an irreversible state (but before the human reset) was found to be crucial. This exploration significantly increased the diversity and quantity of irreversible state data, leading to improved classifier learning and overall task success. Without this, PAINT's learning efficiency decreased.
- **Robustness to Demonstrations**: PAINT and SMBPO demonstrated robustness to variations in the number of provided expert demonstrations, maintaining relatively high success rates even with fewer demonstrations.
- **Pseudo-labels for Unlabeled States**: When updating the agent's critic with `Eqn. 9`, using the reversibility classifier's predictions as pseudo-labels for unlabeled states (from the current trial) was more effective than assuming all unlabeled states were reversible.
- **Classifier Threshold Sensitivity**: A threshold of 0.5 for the reversibility classifier (`ˆR_ρ(s) < 0.5` to request intervention) generally yielded robust performance across tasks, balancing success rates with intervention frequency.
- **Confidence-Based Label Querying**: Further label reduction was possible by only querying expert labels when the classifier's confidence was low (e.g., predictions close to 0.5). In the Maze, this reduced queries from 3,000 to under 100 while maintaining similar success.
- **Noisy Labels and Transitions**: A robust variant of PAINT, which queries neighboring states in a window to account for noisy human labels, was shown to tolerate some level of false positive and false negative label noise. The method also demonstrated robustness to Gaussian noise in environment transitions up to a certain magnitude.

In summary, PAINT consistently outperforms baselines in terms of reducing human interventions and label requirements, while achieving comparable or superior task performance in environments characterized by irreversible states.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, PAINT, that contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by addressing the critical challenge of operating in environments with irreversible states, a common characteristic of real-world scenarios, particularly in robotics. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Autonomy in RL**: PAINT directly contributes to the long-term goal of building truly autonomous RL agents. By enabling agents to proactively detect irreversible states and request intervention only when necessary, it reduces the reliance on continuous human monitoring. This shift from constant supervision to on-demand assistance represents a substantial step towards self-sufficient learning systems.
- **Improved Efficiency in Real-World Deployment**: The demonstrated gains in label-efficiency (reducing human label queries from `O(T)` to `O(log T)` per trajectory, and significantly fewer overall) and intervention-efficiency (requiring substantially fewer resets than prior methods) are highly impactful for practical applications. Human supervision and manual resets are costly and time-consuming. Reducing these requirements makes the deployment and training of RL agents on physical robots or in complex simulations more feasible and scalable.
- **Generalizability of the Approach**: The framework's ability to adapt any value-based RL algorithm, in both episodic and non-episodic settings, broadens its applicability. This suggests that existing robust RL algorithms can be augmented with reversibility awareness without fundamental redesign.
- **Safety and Robustness**: By explicitly learning to identify and avoid irreversible states, PAINT implicitly enhances the safety of RL agents. The robust performance under noisy labels and transitions further suggests its potential for reliability in imperfect real-world conditions. The learned reversibility classifier also aids in better understanding the environment's dynamics from the agent's perspective.
- **Future Research Directions**: The identified limitations, such as the potential for false positives with late-encountered irreversible states and the scope for more sophisticated querying strategies (e.g., based on classifier confidence), open avenues for future research. Validating these techniques on real robotic systems is a crucial next step, moving from simulation to physical embodiment.

In conclusion, PAINT offers a practical and efficient solution for a fundamental bottleneck in autonomous RL. By reducing human oversight and data requirements, it helps bridge the gap between theoretical RL capabilities and the demands of real-world application, paving the way for more independent and scalable robotic learning systems.
