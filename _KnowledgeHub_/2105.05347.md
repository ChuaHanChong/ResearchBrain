# Return-based Scaling: Yet Another Normalisation Trick for Deep RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Return-based scaling introduces a hyper-parameter-free mechanism that normalizes temporal-difference error scales by leveraging problem-side statistics of rewards and returns. Applied to a multi-head R2D2 agent on Atari, it significantly improved overall performance and value accuracy by effectively balancing diverse learning objectives.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning suffers from widely varying temporal-difference error scales due to diverse reward magnitudes, discount factors, and non-stationarities.
- These varying scales lead to challenges like excessive hyper-parameter tuning, learning instability, and slowdowns in RL training.
- In multi-objective learning settings, disparate error scales between components sharing a neural network cause significant interference and imbalanced learning.

## Method

- A =="return-based scaling"== mechanism is proposed that linearly normalizes temporal-difference (TD) errors by dividing them by an adaptive scale factor, $\sigma$.
- The scale factor $\sigma$ is derived from intrinsic problem-side statistics: the variance of rewards ($V[R]$), variance of the discount factor ($V[\gamma]$), and expected squared returns ($E[G^2]$).
- These statistics are estimated robustly from the agent's cumulative learning history, ensuring the approach is hyper-parameter-free, algorithm-agnostic, and preserves problem semantics.

## Results

- Return-based scaling effectively normalized TD error and loss scales across **57** Atari games and different learning stages, reducing their range by **orders of magnitude**.
- In a multi-head R2D2 agent setup with **10** different discount and reward objectives, the method achieved substantial performance increases across all human-normalized score metrics compared to unscaled baselines.
- The approach consistently improved value accuracy across all heads, particularly for low-discount factors, and outperformed Pop-Art by avoiding instability issues — Pop-Art produced **repeated performance collapses** on Atari `asterix` under a large reward spike.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Normalizing error scales based on intrinsic problem statistics (rewards, discounts, returns) is more robust and agent-agnostic than error-tracking methods, which can lead to numerical instability.
> - Existing normalization "tricks" often fall short by ignoring crucial contextual information (like discounts) or fundamentally altering the problem's semantics (like reward clipping).
> - Balancing the relative scales of multiple learning objectives within a shared neural network is critical for maximizing performance, especially in complex multi-head RL architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work addresses a persistent practical challenge in deep reinforcement learning (RL): the issue of varying error scales in temporal-difference (TD) learning. Learning value functions, often through regression with neural networks, is fundamental to most model-free deep RL algorithms. Unlike supervised learning, where standard preprocessing like data whitening is common, deep RL typically lacks a universally applied method for normalizing the scales of learning targets.

The scales of TD errors are influenced by several factors, including the magnitude and density of rewards, the discount factor, and non-stationarities arising from changing policies, data distributions, or the discovery of new rewards. These variations can span many orders of magnitude and can cause significant practical problems such as increased hyper-parameter tuning effort, learning instabilities, slowdowns, and interference when multiple learning objectives are trained within a shared network.

Existing mitigation strategies for these scaling issues include reward clipping, gradient clipping, the use of specific discount factors, non-linear reward or value transforms (e.g., Pop-Art, non-linear Bellman equations), separating value and advantage networks (e.g., Dueling DQN), or employing separate networks for different components. While these "tricks of the trade" often alleviate symptoms, they can be ad-hoc, introduce new hyper-parameters, alter the problem semantics (e.g., reward clipping), or increase architectural complexity.

This paper revisits the topic of scale normalization, identifies specific scenarios where existing simple fixes are insufficient, and proposes a novel, data-driven scaling mechanism. The proposed method aims to provide a robust, simple, and hyper-parameter-free solution that integrates into existing deep RL frameworks, particularly emphasizing its utility in multi-objective learning settings where balancing different loss scales is crucial. It positions itself as a complementary approach to adaptive optimizers and within-network normalizations, offering a foundational preprocessing step for learning targets.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical difficulties encountered by practitioners of reinforcement learning due to significant variations in error scales. These variations are pervasive across different domains, tasks, and stages of the learning process, sometimes differing by many orders of magnitude. The authors highlight several specific factors contributing to these scale issues:
- **Reward Scales and Density:** The inherent magnitudes and frequency of rewards vary widely across different environments.
- **Discount Factor:** As values are cumulative quantities, the discount factor directly influences their scale.
- **Non-stationarity:** Changes in the agent's policy, data distribution, and the discovery of new rewards during learning can cause error scales to shift dynamically.
- **Multi-component Systems:** Training multiple components with disparate error scales within a single neural network (e.g., an actor and a critic, auxiliary prediction heads, multiple value heads for different discounts or reward types) can lead to significant interference and imbalanced learning.

These issues translate into tangible problems for deep RL agents:
- **Excessive Hyper-parameter Tuning:** Practitioners often spend substantial effort tuning learning rates and other optimization parameters to cope with varying scales.
- **Learning Instability and Slowdowns:** Imbalanced error scales can lead to erratic learning dynamics and hinder convergence.
- **Interference between Learning Objectives:** When multiple objectives share a network, components with larger error scales can dominate the learning process, impeding progress on other tasks.

Given these challenges, the paper sets out several key objectives for a desirable normalization scheme:
- **Robustness Across Scenarios:** The method should produce sensible results in diverse scenarios that affect reward and return scales, including linear scaling, additive reward offsets, sequential structure variations, reward sparsity, different discount factors, and changes in temporal resolution.
- **Simplicity and Efficiency:** The mechanism should be simple to implement, have low computational overhead, and avoid introducing new hyper-parameters.
- **Algorithm-Agnosticism:** It should not require access to agent internal workings or specific architectural modifications.
- **No Tuning or Clipping:** The method should operate without the need for manual hyper-parameter tuning or arbitrary clipping operations that alter the problem's semantics.
- **Mitigation of Interference:** A specific objective is to balance the scales of multiple learning objectives within a shared neural network, thereby alleviating interference.
- **Semantics Preservation:** While rescaling errors, the method should ideally preserve the true semantics of the prediction targets, allowing values to be interpretable at their original scales if needed.

The paper aims to develop a "preprocessing-like scaling mechanism" that meets these criteria, ultimately seeking to improve learning speed, stability, and overall performance in deep RL, particularly in complex multi-objective settings.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The proposed method, termed "return-based scaling," introduces a linear scale factor to normalize temporal-difference (TD) errors. This factor is derived from problem-side statistics of rewards and returns, aiming for agent-agnosticism and hyper-parameter-free operation.

**Intuitions and Scenarios:**
The authors begin by illustrating the need for a robust scaling method through six toy scenarios, each varying a key aspect of a reference reward sequence. These scenarios demonstrate how rewards and returns can be affected by:
1. **Linear scaling:** Multiplicative changes to rewards.
2. **Additive offset:** Constant additions to rewards.
3. **Sequential order:** Rearranging rewards while preserving total returns.
4. **Reward sparsity:** Differences in reward density.
5. **Discount factor (γ):** How different discounts alter the effective horizon and value scale.
6. **Time-resolution:** Changes due to mechanisms like action repeats.

These examples reveal that simple scaling approaches, referred to as "false friends" (e.g., normalizing by standard deviation of rewards, standard deviation of returns, or 1/(1-γ), or using reward clipping), often fail in one or more scenarios by either ignoring crucial contextual information (like discounts or sequential structure) or altering the problem's fundamental semantics.

**Point of Scaling:**
The paper argues for applying the scale factor as "upstream as possible" to maximize agent-agnosticism, but also "semantics-preserving" to maintain the meaning of prediction targets. This leads to the choice of rescaling TD errors, which are defined as $\delta_t = R_t + \gamma_t V'_{t+1} - V_t$.

**Derivation of the Scale Factor:**
The core of the methodology is to derive an adaptive scale factor, $\sigma$, for the TD error $\delta_t$. The scaled TD error is defined as $\bar{\delta}_t := \delta_t / \sigma$. The derivation starts by approximating the variance of the TD error, $V[\delta]$, in the transient learning regime where errors are characteristic of the problem rather than near zero.
The variance of the TD error is decomposed using approximate independence assumptions between rewards ($R$), discount factors ($\gamma$), and values ($V$):
$V[\delta] \approx V[R] + \bar{\gamma}^2 V[V' - V] + V[\gamma]E[(V' - V)^2] + (1 - \bar{\gamma})^2 V[V] + V[\gamma]E[V^2]$
By assuming that value statistics (V) can be approximated by return statistics (G) early in learning ($V[V] \approx V[G]$ and $E[V^2] \approx E[G^2]$) and applying further approximations for one-step differences ($G' - G = R - (1 - \gamma)G$), the derivation simplifies to:
$\sigma^2 := V[R] + V[\gamma]E[G^2] \approx V[\delta]$
This final expression uses three problem-side statistics: the variance of rewards ($V[R]$), the variance of the discount factor ($V[\gamma]$), and the expected squared returns ($E[G^2]$). These components collectively capture information about reward scales, discounting, and offsets, allowing the method to address the identified toy scenarios.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Estimation of Statistics:** The statistics $V[R]$, $V[\gamma]$, and $E[G^2]$ are estimated using all data the agent has encountered throughout its learning history. This conservative approach prioritizes robustness and simplicity over potentially faster adaptation in highly non-stationary environments. Double precision (FLOAT64) is noted as important for accuracy.
- **Initialization and Stability:** To prevent division by zero or large updates when insufficient data is available or a first large reward is encountered, the scaled TD error is computed as $\bar{\delta}_t := \delta_t / \max(\sigma, \sigma_V, \sigma_{batch})$. $\sigma_V$ represents the noise level of the value function at network initialization (typically $10^{-2}$), and $\sigma_{batch}$ is a batch-specific estimate of $\sigma$ to handle immediate large reward events.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Agent Architecture:** The method is validated on an R2D2 agent, a high-performing distributed value-based RL algorithm.
- **Domain:** The suite of 57 Atari games, known for its diversity in reward scales, densities, and episode lengths, serves as the testbed.
- **Training Protocol:** Experiments run for $10^9$ environment frames, a smaller data regime than the original R2D2, taking approximately 12 hours per experiment.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Human-normalized mean, median, and capped mean scores are used.
- **Experimental Variants:**
 - **Vanilla (1-head) case:** An R2D2 agent with a single discount (γ=0.997) optimizing for unclipped rewards.
 - **Multi-head variant:** The neural network features 10 separate heads. These heads estimate value functions for 5 different discount factors (γ ∈ {0, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, 1}) and two reward types (clipped and unclipped rewards) for each discount, totaling 10 heads. Experience is used to train all heads, and a bandit algorithm selects which head's policy is used for experience generation and evaluation, based on expected undiscounted, unclipped returns. Return-based scaling is applied separately to each head.
- **Comparisons:** The method is compared against its unscaled baseline, reward clipping, Pop-Art, and non-linear value transforms.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several findings regarding the effectiveness and characteristics of return-based scaling:

**1. Normalization of Loss Scales:**
The primary finding confirms that return-based scaling effectively normalizes error and loss scales. Figures 1 (right) and 3 illustrate that the method maps loss scales across 57 Atari games, various discount factors, and different stages of learning into a significantly narrower range, often by many orders of magnitude. This intrinsic normalization implies that scale-related tuning and stability concerns are substantially reduced. For the 10-head setup, Figure 5 demonstrates that average per-head loss scales are well-balanced across different discounts and between clipped and unclipped reward objectives, in contrast to the widely varying unscaled losses.

**2. Impact on Overall Performance:**
The impact of return-based scaling on aggregate performance varied depending on the experimental setup (Figure 4):
- **1-head setup:** In the single-head R2D2 configuration, return-based scaling did not lead to a meaningful performance difference compared to the unscaled baseline. The authors attribute this to the adaptive optimizer, Adam, which already mitigates many scale differences at the parameter update level (as further explored in Appendix B).
- **10-head setup:** In the multi-head scenario, return-based scaling resulted in a substantial performance increase across all metrics (mean human-normalized score, median human-normalized score, and mean human-capped score) compared to both the unscaled 10-head baseline and all 1-head experiments. This indicates that appropriately balancing the relative scales of multiple learning objectives within a shared network is highly beneficial for overall learning performance. The unscaled 10-head setup performed similarly to the 1-head baseline, suggesting that without proper scaling, the benefits of multi-head learning were not fully realized.

**3. Improved Value Accuracy:**
Return-based scaling consistently led to better value accuracy across all heads (Figure 6). This improvement was particularly pronounced for low-discount heads. The authors hypothesize that the balanced loss contributions across heads, facilitated by return-based scaling, contribute to this enhanced accuracy (Figure 13).

**4. Comparisons to Other Scaling Methods:**
- **Reward Clipping:** While widely used in Atari, reward clipping (capping rewards to [-1, 1]) consistently achieved the best aggregate mean human-normalized score among single-head methods (Figure 12). However, it fundamentally alters the problem semantics, potentially making optimal solutions unattainable in some games (e.g., BOWLING or SKIING) and obscuring the agent's sensitivity to large reward events (Figure 14).
- **Pop-Art:** Pop-Art also mitigated the range of loss scales, but not as effectively as return-based scaling (Figure 3). In the 10-head setup, Pop-Art showed benefits over the unscaled variant but still lagged behind return-based scaling, particularly in median score (Figure 11). A notable issue with Pop-Art was its instability in environments with large, sudden reward spikes (e.g., ASTERIX), where its rapid adaptation of scales could lead to performance collapses (Figure 8).
- **Non-linear Value Transforms:** These methods non-linearly squash large-magnitude errors. While they influence scales, Figure 3 indicates they were less effective at normalizing the overall range of loss scales compared to return-based scaling.

**5. Discussion Points from the Paper:**
- **Return-based vs. Error-based Scaling:** The authors argue that return-based scaling (using problem-side statistics) is superior to direct error-tracking methods because it is agent-agnostic, not affected by learner competence or optimization dynamics, and avoids the risk of "noise amplification." Error-tracking methods can continually amplify residual approximation errors as accuracy improves, potentially leading to numerical instabilities (Figure 10).
- **Initial Value Offset:** Preliminary experiments on SKIING (Figure 7) demonstrated that initializing the value network's bias weight to the expected return, based on initial statistics, could significantly speed up learning by addressing large negative reward offsets, allowing the agent to reach human-level performance faster.
- **Interplay with Regularization and Multi-head Correlations:** The paper suggests potential future benefits of return-based scaling in simplifying the tuning of regularization terms and enabling more sophisticated joint normalization across multiple correlated heads. It notes that return-based scaling is complementary to existing advanced methods for adaptive loss weighting.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of return-based scaling represents a notable contribution to the practical implementation of deep reinforcement learning. Its significance stems from addressing a fundamental and pervasive challenge—the management of varying error scales—with a mechanism that offers several advantages over existing solutions.

**Key Strengths and Impact:**
- **Simplicity and Accessibility:** Return-based scaling is derived from basic statistical properties of rewards and returns, making it conceptually straightforward and easy to implement. Crucially, it introduces no new hyper-parameters that require tuning, which directly tackles a frequent pain point for RL practitioners.
- **Robustness and Generalizability:** The method's foundation in problem-side statistics (rewards, discounts, returns) makes it inherently robust across a wide array of environments and learning scenarios. Its algorithm-agnostic nature suggests broad applicability to various value-based RL algorithms without requiring deep modifications to their internal workings.
- **Enhanced Stability and Efficiency:** By normalizing TD errors into a consistent range, the method contributes to more stable learning dynamics and potentially faster convergence. This reduces the burden on adaptive optimizers or the need for manual hyper-parameter sweeps.
- **Mitigation of Interference in Multi-objective Learning:** The most impactful result is the substantial performance gain observed in the multi-head Atari setup. By balancing the loss contributions from different value heads (corresponding to various discount factors and reward types), return-based scaling enables the agent to effectively learn multiple, diverse objectives simultaneously within a shared representation. This capability is critical for complex tasks where agents might need to optimize for short-term vs. long-term rewards, or pursue multiple auxiliary tasks. It allows for the exploitation of rich, multi-faceted learning signals without one objective dominating or undermining others.
- **Preservation of Problem Semantics:** Unlike reward clipping, which alters the original problem by capping reward values, return-based scaling acts purely as a normalization factor for errors, allowing the agent to learn the true magnitudes of values. This is important for optimal policy learning in environments where large rewards are meaningful.
- **Complementary to Existing Techniques:** The method is presented as a foundational preprocessing step, complementary to adaptive optimizers like Adam and within-network normalizations. This suggests it can be integrated into advanced RL systems to further enhance their performance and robustness.

**Potential Future Directions:**
The paper opens avenues for future research, such as investigating faster time-scales for tracking scale statistics in highly non-stationary environments, exploring joint normalization across multiple correlated heads, and further analyzing its interplay with regularization terms. The finding regarding initial value offset also highlights a simple, yet potentially impactful, initialization strategy for certain environments.

In conclusion, return-based scaling offers a practical, principled, and easily adoptable solution to a significant challenge in deep RL. Its ability to stabilize and balance learning, particularly in multi-objective settings, positions it as a valuable "trick" that could simplify the development and improve the performance of a broad range of value-based RL agents.
