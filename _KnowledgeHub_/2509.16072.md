# I-FailSense: Towards General Robotic Failure Detection with Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> I-FailSense introduces a Vision-Language Model framework that excels at detecting subtle semantic misalignment errors in language-conditioned robotic manipulation. The model achieved 90.64% accuracy on simulated semantic errors and generalized to control errors and new environments, outperforming specialized baselines and demonstrating effective real-world transfer with minimal fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Current VLM-based robotic control lacks robust self-failure detection capabilities, particularly for errors beyond low-level physical mistakes.
- Semantic misalignment failures, where a robot's action is physically plausible but semantically incorrect relative to the instruction, are a critical yet underexplored challenge for VLMs' fine-grained grounding and temporal reasoning.
- Existing robotic failure detection methods often focus on generative explanations or solely on control errors, lacking the discriminative accuracy required for triggering automated recovery mechanisms.

## Method

- A pipeline was developed for constructing Semantic Misalignment Failure (SMF) datasets by repurposing existing language-conditioned manipulation data, generating challenging negative examples through intentionally mismatched instructions.
- **I-FailSense**, a two-stage post-training framework built upon a pre-trained VLM (PaliGemma2-mix-3B), was designed, utilizing Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) with ==LoRA== in the first stage to adapt the VLM.
- The second stage, termed 'Grounded Arbitration,' attaches lightweight FailSense (FS) binary classification blocks to multiple internal VLM layers, training them independently and ensembling their predictions with the VLM's output for robust failure classification.

## Results

- I-FailSense achieved **90.64%** accuracy and a 0.9132 F1 score on detecting semantic misalignment failures in the D_SMF-CALVIN dataset, substantially outperforming zero-shot VLMs which performed close to random or exhibited significant precision-recall gaps.
- The framework demonstrated strong generalization by achieving an **89.0%** failure detection rate on the D_AHA dataset (predominantly control errors), surpassing specialized AHA baselines by approximately 19 percentage points.
- Effective sim-to-real transfer to real-world trajectories (D_SMF-DROID) was achieved with **74.28%** accuracy and a 0.7300 F1 score after minimal fine-tuning of only the FS blocks on real data, matching the performance of a larger zero-shot VLM like Qwen2.5-VL.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Detecting semantic misalignment errors is crucial for advancing VLM grounding capabilities, as it reveals limitations in understanding complex, nuanced instructions beyond simple physical execution.
> - A model trained on semantic misalignment errors can generalize effectively to diverse control error types and across different simulation environments, indicating that the learned failure detection patterns are robust and transferable.
> - Leveraging multi-level internal VLM representations through specialized classification heads (FS blocks) significantly enhances discriminative failure detection accuracy and robustness by grounding failure identification in language-space reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust robotic systems for open-world settings necessitates the ability to not only execute tasks accurately but also to autonomously detect failures. Recent advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have significantly enhanced capabilities in areas such as spatial reasoning, instruction following, and task planning for robots. These models serve as foundational components for language-conditioned robotic manipulation.

However, a notable limitation in the current landscape of VLM-based robotic control is the capacity for self-failure detection. Prior research in robotic failure detection has primarily focused on "control errors," which involve low-level physical mistakes such as a robot failing to grasp an object, dropping an item, or executing an incorrect rotation that can be identified from visual cues of physical interaction. Benchmarks and fine-tuning strategies, such as those introduced by Duan et al. (AHA dataset) and Gupta et al., have largely targeted these types of failures.

A less explored but critical category of errors is "semantic misalignment failures." These occur when a robot performs an action that is semantically meaningful and physically plausible, but which does not align with the given language instruction. Examples include interacting with the wrong object or performing the correct action in the wrong direction (e.g., rotating left instead of right). Such failures are not due to a lack of physical control but rather a deficiency in the robot's semantic understanding and grounding of language instructions with its visual observations and motion. This challenge is indicative of a broader struggle of state-of-the-art VLMs with fine-grained visual grounding, as well as temporal and spatial reasoning concerning motion, as documented in various studies.

Existing VLM and Large Language Model (LLM) approaches have been utilized as zero-shot or few-shot success detectors, and some have been instruction-tuned for failure detection. The AHA framework notably advanced failure analysis by enabling generative, free-form reasoning for error explanations, which can aid in recovery. However, its architecture is not explicitly designed for high-accuracy discriminative failure classification, which is a prerequisite for triggering automated recovery mechanisms.

I-FailSense positions itself within this context by addressing the challenge of detecting semantic misalignment errors, thereby aiming to improve the grounding capabilities of robotic agents. Unlike previous work that emphasizes generative explanations or focuses solely on control errors, this research proposes a discriminative classification approach specifically optimized for accurate failure detection. It also leverages multi-level internal representations of VLMs, departing from methods that might only utilize final layer features, to enhance both robustness and accuracy in identifying diverse failure modes, including those that demand intricate language grounding and temporal reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust method for detecting language-conditioned robotic manipulation failures, with a specific emphasis on a challenging category known as **semantic misalignment errors**.

The motivation behind focusing on semantic misalignment errors stems from several critical aspects:

- **Addressing a Fundamental Limitation of VLMs:** Semantic misalignment errors reveal a key shortcoming in current Vision-Language Models and the robotic agents built upon them: a lack of robust grounding between visual observations, robot motion, and high-level semantic instructions. While VLMs have demonstrated proficiency in open-world reasoning and instruction following, their ability to discern subtle discrepancies between an instruction and a semantically plausible but incorrect action remains limited.
- **Distinction from Control Errors:** Most prior failure detection research concentrates on "control errors," which are typically low-level physical mistakes (e.g., a failed grasp or an object dropping). These errors are often visually obvious and can sometimes be detected without deep semantic understanding. Semantic misalignment errors, in contrast, are not due to a lack of physical precision but rather an incorrect interpretation or execution of the instruction's intent. The robot's behavior is meaningful but wrong in the context of the given command.
- **Complexity of Detection:** Detecting semantic misalignment errors is inherently more challenging than detecting control errors. It requires a sophisticated understanding of the relationship between textual instructions and observation trajectories, necessitating reasoning about object interactions in both spatial and temporal dimensions. The model must verify if the robot's movements and interactions over time precisely match the specified language goal, rather than just checking for gross physical anomalies.
- **Impact on Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** The ability for a robot to autonomously detect its own semantic errors is crucial for achieving truly robust and general-purpose manipulation in real-world environments. Such a capability is foundational for self-correction, enabling improved motion planning, sub-task verification, reward shaping, and providing actionable feedback for iterative policy improvement, particularly for foundation models in robotics.
- **Enhancing Grounding Capabilities:** By developing models that can detect semantic misalignment, the research aims to foster improved language grounding in robotic systems. This capability would enable robots to better understand and execute complex, nuanced instructions by verifying that their actions align with the semantic intent, not just the physical feasibility.

To achieve this, the authors set forth several specific objectives:

1. **Dataset Construction Method:** Propose a pipeline for building dedicated datasets for semantic misalignment failure detection by repurposing existing language-conditioned manipulation datasets.
2. **Novel VLM Framework (I-FailSense):** Introduce an open-source VLM framework designed for discriminative failure detection. This framework is intended to leverage the internal representations of a base VLM through a specific post-training methodology.
3. **Generalization Demonstration:** Empirically demonstrate that I-FailSense can reliably detect semantic misalignment errors and, importantly, generalize this capability to broader robotic failure categories (e.g., control errors), novel simulation environments, and real-world scenarios with minimal adaptation.
4. **Architectural Contribution:** Investigate the effectiveness of using lightweight classification heads (FS blocks) attached to multiple internal layers of the VLM, aggregating their predictions through an ensembling mechanism, to explicitly ground failure detection in language-space reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on a two-stage post-training approach for a Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically adapted for language-conditioned robotic failure detection with a focus on semantic misalignment errors.

**A. Semantic Misalignment Failure Datasets (D_SMF)**

The first methodological contribution is a pipeline for constructing datasets tailored to Semantic Misalignment Failures (SMF). This process leverages existing multi-task, language-conditioned robotic manipulation datasets that provide expert demonstrations {τ, g*}, where τ is an observation trajectory and g* is the successfully achieved semantic goal. The new datasets, D_SMF = {τ, g, y}, contain trajectories τ, textual instructions g, and a binary success label y.

- **Positive Examples (y = 1):** These are created by pairing the original expert trajectories (τ) with their corresponding successful textual instructions (g*).
- **Negative Examples (y = 0):** These are generated by taking existing expert trajectories (τ) and pairing them with textual instructions (g) that are *different from the original g* but belong to the *same task category*. This design ensures that the robot's behavior in the trajectory is semantically meaningful but explicitly misaligned with the given, mismatched instruction. Examples include "lift the red block" vs. "lift the blue block" (where the robot lifts the red block), or "rotate the red block right" vs. "rotate the red block left" (where the robot rotates it right). This strategy creates challenging negative examples that require fine-grained semantic reasoning to detect the misalignment.

Three specific datasets were used or created:
- **SMF-CALVIN dataset (D_SMF-CALVIN):** Built from the CALVIN task D benchmark, comprising 34 simulated manipulation tasks grouped into six categories. This dataset was used for in-distribution training and evaluation of semantic misalignment detection.
- **AHA dataset (D_AHA):** Used for generalization evaluation (Q2 and Q3). This benchmark, based on RLBench, includes 79 simulated tasks with seven error categories (six control errors and one semantic misalignment error, "wrong target object"). The authors constructed a test set of 400 negative pairs following the AHA pipeline.
- **SMF-DROID dataset (D_SMF-DROID):** Derived from the real-world DROID dataset for sim-to-real generalization evaluation (Q4). Expert demonstrations from DROID served as positive examples, and negative examples were generated by randomly mismatching instructions from the same task category. This dataset contains 6K training and 276 test data points.

**B. FailSense: Vision-Language Model for Failure Detection (I-FailSense)**

I-FailSense is built upon a pre-trained VLM, specifically PaliGemma2-mix-3B, chosen for its balance of size and performance. The input to the model consists of an observation trajectory (τ) aggregated into a single image representation (τ ∈ R^(3x(H·N)x(W·T))) and a textual instruction (g). This combined input is formatted via a fixed prompt template. The method follows a two-stage post-training process:

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (Stage 1):**
 - **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) with LoRA:** The MLP layer connecting the frozen vision encoder (SigLIP) and the base language model is fine-tuned. Concurrently, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules are integrated into the key-query-value (KQV) projection layers of the language model's attention blocks. This strategy updates a small number of task-specific parameters while keeping most pre-trained VLM weights frozen.
 - **Loss Function:** Training minimizes the cross-entropy loss between the model's predicted token distribution and a target supervision token (<success> or <fail>).

2. **Grounded Arbitration (Stage 2):**
 - **FailSense (FS) Blocks:** After Stage 1, the VLM is frozen. Lightweight binary classification heads, termed FailSense (FS) blocks, are attached to *K* evenly distributed internal layers of the base language model. Each block takes the features (f_k) from its corresponding VLM layer.
 - **FS Block Architecture:** Each FS block comprises a hybrid attention pooling mechanism (combining MLP and Multi-Head Attention), followed by a sequence of MLP blocks with residual connections and batch normalization, concluding with a binary classification linear layer. This layer outputs a binary probability (p_k = FS_φ_k(f_k)).
 - **Training:** In this stage, only the randomly initialized FS blocks are updated, based on the binary cross-entropy loss between their predictions (p_k) and the true label (y).
 - **Voting Strategy (Inference):** During inference, each of the *K* FS blocks produces a binary prediction (y_k). The VLM's original free-form output is also converted into a binary prediction (y_vlm). A weighted voting mechanism aggregates these predictions to produce the final binary prediction (ŷ). In the experiments, K=3, FS block predictions receive a weight of 1 (ω_k = 1), and the VLM prediction receives a weight of 2 (ω_vlm = 2) to resolve ties.

**C. Experimental Setup**
- **Observation Point of Views (PoV):** Experiments were conducted using either one exocentric PoV (N=1) or a combination of exocentric and egocentric PoV (N=2) to assess the impact of visual input richness.
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against zero-shot large VLMs of comparable size (PaliGemma2-mix-3B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B) and larger models (GPT-4o). For the D_AHA dataset, the AHA model (7B and 13B) fine-tuned on D_AHA was also included as a baseline.
- **Metrics:** Standard binary classification metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score) were used for datasets with both positive and negative examples (D_SMF-CALVIN, D_SMF-DROID). For D_AHA, which contains only negative examples, accuracy (interpreted as failure detection rate) was reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of I-FailSense addressed four research questions (Q1-Q4) and included an ablation study.

**A. Semantic Misalignment Failure Detection (Q1)**
- On the in-distribution D_SMF-CALVIN dataset, I-FailSense demonstrated a significant improvement over zero-shot VLMs. Zero-shot GPT-4o and PaliGemma2-mix performed close to random (Accuracy ~0.47-0.63). Zero-shot Qwen2.5-VL showed better performance (Accuracy ~0.68-0.69) but exhibited a notable gap between recall (up to 0.92) and precision (as low as 0.63), indicating frequent false positives where misaligned trajectories were classified as successful.
- I-FailSense (after full training, "ours") achieved an accuracy of **0.9064 with one PoV** and 0.8818 with two PoV, with F1 scores of 0.9132 and 0.8909, respectively. This performance represented a substantial improvement over all zero-shot baselines and a reduction in the precision-recall gap, suggesting better identification of subtle semantic misalignments.

**B. Generalization Across Error Types (Q2)**
- I-FailSense, despite being trained exclusively on semantic misalignment errors from D_SMF-CALVIN, showed strong generalization to the D_AHA dataset, which primarily contains control errors (~85%) and a smaller fraction of "wrong target object" semantic errors.
- Zero-shot VLMs performed poorly on D_AHA (Accuracy 0.075 to 0.50). The AHA models, specifically fine-tuned on D_AHA, achieved accuracies of 0.691 (7B) and 0.702 (13B).
- I-FailSense achieved a failure detection rate (accuracy) of **0.890** on D_AHA, outperforming the AHA baselines by approximately 19 percentage points. This finding suggests that learning to align language instructions with observation trajectories for semantic misalignment detection provides transferable signals for recognizing control errors, as both necessitate verification of observed motion against instructions.

**C. Generalization to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Simulations Trajectories (Q3)**
- The strong performance of I-FailSense on the D_AHA dataset also demonstrated its ability to generalize across different simulation environments. I-FailSense was trained on D_SMF-CALVIN (CALVIN environment with a fixed set of objects and camera settings) but evaluated on D_AHA (RLBench environment with different objects and camera configurations). This cross-environment generalization indicates that the model learned robust, transferable failure detection patterns rather than being overfit to specific simulation characteristics.

**D. Generalization to Real-World Trajectories (Q4)**
- Direct transfer of I-FailSense, fully trained on simulated data (D_SMF-CALVIN), to detect failures in real-world trajectories (D_SMF-DROID) showed mixed results. While accuracy improved over zero-shot baselines, the F1 score decreased due to a significant drop in recall, indicating difficulty in recognizing real-world failures without specific adaptation.
- Fine-tuning only the FS blocks on the real-world D_SMF-DROID dataset, after the initial LoRA training on D_SMF-CALVIN, achieved the best performance on real-world data, with an accuracy of **0.7428** and an F1 score of 0.7300 (with two PoV). This performance matched that of the larger zero-shot Qwen2.5-VL model.
- This result suggests that the LoRA fine-tuning on simulated data provides a beneficial initial representation, but minimal fine-tuning of the specialized FS blocks on real-world data is crucial for effective sim-to-real transfer.
- For real-world trajectories, using both exocentric and egocentric PoV (N=2) resulted in better performance (+0.03 accuracy) compared to a single exocentric PoV, possibly due to higher visual complexity and distractors in real-world scenes necessitating richer visual input.

**Ablation Study**
- The ablation study on D_SMF-CALVIN clarified the contribution of each training stage:
 - **Base PaliGemma2-mix-3B (zero-shot):** Performed near randomly (Accuracy ~0.47-0.52), indicating its limitations in dynamic, simulated robotic failure detection without specialized training. This is attributed to its training on static real-world images and general vision-language tasks, not dynamic trajectory grounding.
 - **I-FailSense (LoRA only, Stage 1):** Achieved significant improvement, with accuracy rising to ~0.82-0.85. This highlights the effectiveness of parameter-efficient fine-tuning with LoRA for adapting the VLM to the specific task of failure detection in simulated robotic trajectories.
 - **I-FailSense (ours, full training with FS blocks, Stage 1 + 2):** Further improved performance, adding 3-5 percentage points in accuracy and 0.03-0.05 in F1 score (Accuracy ~0.88-0.90). This demonstrates that leveraging multiple levels of the VLM's internal representations through dedicated classification heads and an ensembling mechanism enhances the model's ability to understand multimodal inputs and make more robust failure predictions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The I-FailSense framework and the accompanying methodologies contribute to the field of robotic autonomy and Vision-Language Models in several ways:

- **Addressing Underexplored Failure Modes:** The research directly addresses the detection of semantic misalignment errors, a challenging class of robotic failures that has received comparatively less attention than control errors. By specifically targeting these errors, I-FailSense pushes the capabilities of robotic agents to discern subtle discrepancies between instructions and actions, which is crucial for higher-level task understanding.
- **Enhancing VLM Grounding Capabilities:** The work demonstrates that training on semantic misalignment failures, especially when combined with an architecture that leverages multi-level VLM representations, significantly improves the model's ability to ground language instructions in both the spatial and temporal dimensions of visual observations. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how VLMs can be made more robust to nuanced semantic interpretations.
- **Improved Robotic Robustness and Autonomy:** The ability for a robot to autonomously and accurately detect its own failures, particularly semantic ones, is a fundamental step towards deploying robust and adaptable robots in complex, open-world environments. This self-evaluation capability can enable faster learning from errors, facilitate proactive recovery mechanisms, and reduce reliance on constant human supervision.
- **Strong Generalization Properties:** I-FailSense exhibits strong generalization across different types of errors (semantic vs. control) and across varied simulation environments, even when trained on a distinct set of tasks and visuals. This suggests that the learned failure detection patterns are robust and less prone to overfitting to specific task configurations.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The research demonstrates that while initial training on simulated data is valuable for learning representations, minimal fine-tuning on real-world data with the specialized FS blocks is sufficient to achieve effective sim-to-real transfer for failure detection. This highlights a practical pathway for deploying such systems in real-world robotic applications.
- **Architectural Contribution:** The proposed two-stage post-training pipeline, particularly the use of FailSense blocks with grounded arbitration, provides a novel architectural approach for leveraging the internal representations of pre-trained VLMs for specific discriminative tasks. This method can potentially be adapted to other fine-grained multimodal reasoning problems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By establishing a robust framework for failure detection, I-FailSense lays the groundwork for future advancements in robotic error recovery. A robot that can reliably detect its own failures can then be equipped with mechanisms to understand the root cause, adapt its policy, or seek human intervention more intelligently, moving closer to truly intelligent and resilient language-conditioned robotic agents.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The public release of the datasets and models facilitates further research and development in language-conditioned robotic failure detection, promoting transparency and collaborative progress in the field.
