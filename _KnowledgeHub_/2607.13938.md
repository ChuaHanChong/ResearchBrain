# Discriminative Barrier Functions for Safe Adversarial Imitation Learning from Observation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Washington developed Discriminative Barrier Functions (DBFs) for safe adversarial imitation learning from observation, enabling robots to acquire imitative policies and inhibitory control from unlabeled expert data. This framework achieved 0% collision rates in novel, unseen obstacle environments during real-world experiments, while maintaining high task success.

## Problem

- Many Inverse Reinforcement Learning from Observation (IRLfO) methods lack safety guarantees during online learning, posing risks in real-world deployments and often requiring explicit safety labels or action supervision.
- Manually designing Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) for complex, high-dimensional systems is difficult, and existing data-driven CBF learning approaches typically need explicit safe/unsafe state labels or action-labeled demonstrations.
- Current imitation learning and safe reinforcement learning often struggle to generalize safety mechanisms robustly to novel, unencountered unsafe states.

## Method

- The framework integrates ==Discrete-Time Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)== into an ==Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL)== from Observation (IRLfO) setup.
- A **Discriminative Barrier Function (DBF)** is proposed where the AIL discriminator is constrained to be a CBF dynamic constraint (q_h(s, s')), enabling joint learning of an imitative policy and an interpretable barrier function from unlabeled expert observations.
- The learning objective uses a ==Wasserstein GAN== formulation to differentiate safe (expert) from unsafe (learner) transitions, complemented by a ==gradient penalty== and a ==hinge loss== to explicitly classify expert states as safe (h(s)>0) and learner states as unsafe (h(s)<0).

## Results

- DBF-based methods consistently achieved **0% collisions** when tested with unseen obstacles in simulation, outperforming standard GAIL (**20.5%** collisions) and MPAIL (**12.3%** collisions).
- In real-world robot experiments, DBF-GAIL and DBF-MPAIL demonstrated **0% collisions** across **20 trials**, a substantial improvement over non-DBF baselines (GAIL at **50%** and MPAIL at **30%**).
- During online learning, DBF-based approaches significantly reduced constraint violation rates, enabling safer exploration and learning, and DBF-MPAIL achieved a **65% success rate** in real-world tasks, far surpassing non-DBF baselines (**0%** success).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formal safety guarantees, typically requiring explicit design or labels, can be effectively learned and integrated into data-driven imitation learning solely from positive, unlabeled expert observations.
> - Constraining the adversarial discriminator with Control Barrier Function (CBF) dynamic properties enables the joint learning of an interpretable barrier function and an imitative policy that performs inhibitory control, capable of generalizing to novel unsafe states.
> - The specific choice of K-class function within the DBF formulation influences the learning process, with linear functions leading to a faster reduction in constraint violations during online learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning, particularly through imitation, has advanced significantly with Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) algorithms. IRL aims to infer an expert's reward function from demonstrations, enabling the synthesis of policies that mimic expert behavior. A common challenge in traditional IRL is its reliance on action-labeled demonstrations, which can be costly or impractical to acquire in real-world robotic scenarios. This limitation has led to the development of Inverse Reinforcement Learning from Observation (IRLfO), where policies are learned directly from state-only trajectories, mirroring human ability to learn by observing.

However, a critical limitation in many IRLfO methods is the safety of the learning process. These algorithms often involve unconstrained exploration in the environment to refine policies, which can lead to unsafe states or actions, making them unsuitable for deployment in safety-critical real-world applications. Existing IRLfO approaches primarily prioritize behavioral fidelity and typically lack explicit mechanisms for avoiding unsafe states during exploration and learning. This contrasts with human learning, which often incorporates inhibitory control to avoid hazardous situations.

Concurrently, Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) in control theory offer a formal framework for guaranteeing the safety of dynamic systems. CBFs mathematically define a safe set of states and provide a control law that ensures the system remains within this set, or returns to it if violated. While CBFs offer strong safety guarantees, their analytical design for complex, high-dimensional systems is often challenging, time-consuming, and domain-specific, akin to the reward engineering problem in Reinforcement Learning (RL).

Previous work has explored learning CBFs from data, often by discriminating between safe and unsafe regions. However, these methods frequently require explicit safe/unsafe labels, action-labeled demonstrations, or operate strictly offline, which restricts their applicability in scenarios where safety structure must be inferred from unlabeled observations alone. Similarly, existing safe RL and imitation learning methods typically rely on pre-specified constraint sets or action labels and often lack online adaptation capabilities.

This work addresses the intersection of these challenges by proposing a method to integrate the formal safety guarantees of CBFs into the data-driven learning framework of IRLfO. It specifically aims to overcome the need for action labels and pre-defined safety constraints by learning barrier functions and safe policies jointly from unlabeled expert observations, while enabling safe online learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an IRLfO framework that allows a robot to learn an imitative policy from expert observational trajectories while simultaneously performing inhibitory control to avoid unsafe states during online learning. This objective is motivated by several identified limitations in existing approaches:

1. **Addressing the limitations of action-labeled demonstrations:** Traditional IRL methods necessitate expert actions, which are often unavailable or expensive to collect in real-world robotics. The work seeks to operate within the IRLfO setting, where only state-only expert trajectories are provided.

2. **Ensuring safety during online learning and exploration:** Many IRLfO methods rely on online exploration to refine policies. This exploration can pose significant safety risks, limiting their deployment in real-world environments. The paper aims to mitigate these risks by integrating safety considerations directly into the learning process, moving beyond approaches that focus solely on behavior fidelity.

3. **Overcoming the difficulty of manual CBF design:** Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) provide robust safety guarantees but are challenging to design analytically for complex systems. The research seeks a data-driven approach to recover barrier functions automatically, thereby alleviating the burden of manual engineering.

4. **Inferring safety from unlabeled observations:** Current methods for learning CBFs or ensuring safety in imitation learning often require explicit safe/unsafe state labels or action labels. This work aims to infer the safety structure and associated inhibitory control directly from positive, unlabeled expert observations, without prior knowledge of unsafe regions or explicit non-preferred labels.

5. **Achieving generalizability to unseen unsafe states:** The goal is for the learned safety mechanism (barrier function) to be robust and generalize effectively to novel environments and unsafe states not encountered in the expert demonstrations or during initial training.

To achieve these objectives, the authors investigate the hypothesis that constraining the reward function candidacy within the IRLfO framework with the forward invariance property inherent to CBFs can lead to the joint recovery of both an interpretable barrier function and a policy capable of inhibitory control near unsafe observations through experience. The proposed Discriminative Barrier Functions (DBFs) are designed to learn these properties jointly, without requiring action supervision or hand-designed constraints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology integrates Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) into an Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) framework, specifically tailored for the Inverse Reinforcement Learning from Observation (IRLfO) setting.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The system is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), where an agent observes `o` from a world state `s_w`, perceives its state `s` from observation history, and performs action `a`. A predictive model `f: S × A → S` allows for self-prediction. The expert policy `π_E` optimizes an unknown reward `r_w(s, s')`, which may implicitly encode safety. The core problem assumes only state-only observations (`s, s'`) are available from the expert.

#### 3.2. Inverse Reinforcement Learning from Observation

The work builds upon the general IRLfO objective (Equation 1), which seeks a cost function `c(s, s')` under which the expert's performance is maximized, while other optimal policies' (learner `π`) performances are minimized. This objective incorporates entropy regularization `H(π)` to encourage exploration. The crucial component for this work is the cost regularizer `ψ`, which shapes the search space for `c(s, s')`.

#### 3.3. Discrete-Time Control Barrier Functions

Safety is formalized using Discrete-Time CBFs. A safe set `S` is defined by a specification function `h(s) ≥ 0`. A function `h` is a Discrete-Time CBF if, for any state `s` in `S`, there exists an action `a` such that `h(f(s, a)) - h(s) ≥ -α(h(s))`, where `α` is a class K function. This condition guarantees forward invariance (the system remains in the safe set) and stability (if the system is unsafe, an action can return it to safety). The paper employs a discrete form to relax continuity and differentiability requirements during training.

#### 3.4. Discriminative Barrier Functions via Cost Regularization

The core idea is to treat the barrier function `h` as a discriminator that differentiates between safe and unsafe states, similar to how an AIL discriminator distinguishes expert from learner behavior.
The authors define a Dynamic Constraint Lagrangian `q_h(s, s') = h(s') - h(s) + α(h(s))` (Equation 3). This function becomes more positive for safer transitions and more negative for less safe ones.
The key innovation is to restrict the cost function `c` in the IRLfO objective to be a CBF, i.e., `c(s, s') = q_h(s, s')`, where `h` defines the safe and unsafe sets. This restriction defines `C_CBF` (Equation 4) as the space of cost functions that are also Control Barrier Functions.
By substituting this restriction into the IRLfO objective and adopting a Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) formulation for stable training, the DBF objective (Equation 5) is derived. This objective maximizes the difference between `D_h(s, s')` (which is `q_h(s, s')`) for expert transitions (`τ_safe`) and learner transitions (`τ_unsafe`), while enforcing 1-Lipschitz continuity via a gradient penalty (`L_Gradient Penalty`). Additionally, explicit constraints `h(s) > 0` for safe states and `h(s) < 0` for unsafe states are enforced.

#### 3.5. Learning DBFs via Adversarial Imitation Learning

To compute the expectations in the DBF objective without explicit safety labels, the framework assumes that learner data `τ_unsafe` (generated by `π`) is unsafe, and expert data `τ_safe` (from `π_E`) is safe. This enables the use of Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL). The complete AIL objective with DBFs (Equation 7) includes:
- A WGAN term `λ_wgan` maximizing `D_h(s, s')` for expert data and minimizing it for learner data.
- A gradient penalty term `λ_GP` to enforce Lipschitz continuity.
- An entropy regularization term `H(π)` for the policy.
- A hinge loss term `λ_sign` that directly pushes `h(s)` to be positive for expert states and negative for learner states, thus enforcing the safety classification.

The policy `π` is trained using rewards derived from the discriminator `D_h(s, s')`. The method can incorporate known safety constraints or unsafe demonstrations if available, although the primary focus is on learning from unlabeled observations. The approach uses `ϵ`-nets to define implicit safety regions around expert states and Lipschitz constraints for robust learning, which aligns with practices for stabilizing AIL algorithms.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

The DBF objective is evaluated with three AIL algorithms: GAIL and AIRL (policy-based, model-free), and MPAIL (planning-based, model-based). The discriminator `D_h(s, s')` serves as the reward function for the learner. All experiments calculate the expected value of the discriminator off-policy.
- **Simulation Environment:** A 10-DOF ego vehicle (MUSHR) in Isaac Lab is used for navigation to a goal. Initial studies use a no-obstacle setting, followed by a maze environment with randomized horizontal walls during training to promote generalization. A novel vertical wall is introduced during testing to assess generalization to unseen obstacle configurations.
- **Real-World Experiment:** A "Real-to-Sim-to-Real" pipeline is employed. A small number of state-only observations are collected from a real robot. The method is trained in simulation with randomized obstacles, then deployed zero-shot on the real robot in a motion capture room for obstacle avoidance tasks.
- **Metrics:** Collision rate, average environmental reward, normalized reward, and cost rate (a metric for constraint-violation regret).
- **K-class Function:** Linear K-class functions are primarily used, with an analysis of linear, cubic, and exponential variants.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation addresses three primary questions regarding the performance and generalization capabilities of Discriminative Barrier Functions (DBFs).

#### 4.1. Q1: Barrier Function Robustness to Unsafe States not Present in Expert Data

- **Qualitative Analysis:** In a simple no-obstacle simulation (Figure 3), DBF-based methods, particularly DBF-MPAIL, demonstrated the ability to learn expressive discriminative boundaries around expert trajectories. The learned barrier function `h(s)` successfully modeled complex behaviors, such as circling near the goal, and generalized better than baseline AIL methods.
 When tested in a maze environment with an unseen vertical obstacle (Figure 4a,c,d,e), the barrier function learned by DBF-MPAIL adapted, correctly identifying the new obstacle as an unsafe region. This demonstrated robustness to distribution shifts and the preservation of a topologically consistent safe set around obstacles, even when expert trajectories intersected obstacle regions. The robot was observed inhibiting its control inputs to avoid obstacles in the unseen environment (Figure 4e).
- **Quantitative Comparison:** In the test environment with an unseen vertical wall (Table 2), DBF-based AIL methods (DBF-AIRL, DBF-GAIL, DBF-MPAIL) achieved 0.0 ± 0.0% collisions across five training seeds. In contrast, GAIL exhibited a 20.5 ± 25.4% collision rate, and MPAIL showed 12.3 ± 1.8% collisions. While AIRL also achieved 0.0 ± 0.0% collisions, the paper attributes this to its inherent reward structure rather than explicit safety constraints, distinguishing it from DBF-based methods. DBF-MPAIL attained the highest average environmental reward (0.338 ± 0.022) among the DBF variants, indicating effective task completion alongside safety. These results suggest that the DBF objective facilitates learning a generalizable barrier function capable of handling novel unsafe states.

#### 4.2. Q2: Safe Online Imitation Learning Performance Comparison and Safety Analysis

- **Safety (Cost Rate):** During online learning in the maze environment (Figure 5), DBF-based methods consistently demonstrated a reduction in cost rate as learning progressed, indicating improved safety and fewer constraint violations. DBF-GAIL achieved the lowest cost rate at convergence. In contrast, standard GAIL entirely collapsed, failing to imitate the expert and accumulating high constraint violations due to its inability to discriminate against unseen obstacles. MPAIL, while more robust than GAIL, still exhibited a higher cost rate compared to DBF-MPAIL. This suggests that constraining the reward function with CBF dynamic constraints, as in DBF, promotes safe imitative behavior.
- **Reward Performance:** DBF-MPAIL achieved significantly better reward than DBF-GAIL, although with a marginally higher cost rate. MPAIL achieved the highest reward but at the cost of higher constraint violations than DBF-based methods, highlighting a safety-performance trade-off. AIRL and DBF-AIRL showed comparable performance (Figure 9).
- **Effect of K-class Functions:** An investigation into the impact of different K-class functions (linear, cubic, exponential) on DBF-MPAIL's performance (Figure 6) revealed that linear K-class functions led to the fastest reduction in cost rate and the lowest accumulation of total collisions. This correlates with their steeper slope within the relevant discriminator logit range, suggesting that the choice of K-class function can modulate the inhibitory behavior learned.

#### 4.3. Q3: Real World Validation Through Real-Sim-Real Experiment

- **Deployment Success:** In real-world obstacle avoidance tasks using a Real-to-Sim-to-Real pipeline (Figure 1, Figure 7), DBF-MPAIL consistently traced paths to avoid obstacles, even when these obstacles were unseen in the expert demonstrations or positioned differently from training environments. This occurred while successfully imitating the goal-reaching behavior. The planning-based nature of DBF-MPAIL, allowing access to the optimization landscape, contributed to the interpretability of its decisions.
- **Collision Avoidance:** Quantitatively (Table 3), both DBF-GAIL and DBF-MPAIL achieved 0% collisions across all 20 real-world trials, in stark contrast to the non-DBF baselines. GAIL experienced 50% collisions, and MPAIL had 30% collisions.
- **Task Performance:** While DBF-GAIL exhibited a low success rate (5%) in reaching the goal, attributed to the sim-to-real gap and lack of planning structure, DBF-MPAIL achieved a 65% success rate. The non-DBF baselines, GAIL and MPAIL, both had 0% success rates, frequently colliding or deviating from the expert trajectory. These findings indicate that DBF-based methods enhance the safety of imitation learning algorithms in real-world safety-critical deployments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Discriminative Barrier Functions (DBFs), a novel framework that integrates Control Barrier Function (CBF) theory into Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) from observation. The work addresses several limitations in current imitation learning paradigms and contributes to the development of safer and more robust autonomous systems.

The primary significance lies in demonstrating that formal notions of safety and inhibitory control can be recovered directly from unlabeled expert observations. This removes the reliance on explicit unsafe labels or hand-engineered safety costs, which are typically difficult and time-consuming to obtain. By constraining the learned discriminator through barrier-function objectives, DBFs enable AIL methods to learn not only how to imitate expert behavior but also how to discriminate against and avoid unsafe observations, even those entirely absent from the expert data.

The findings indicate that DBFs improve the generalization capabilities of learned safe sets, allowing robots to adapt to novel, previously unseen obstacles and environmental configurations in a zero-shot manner. This enhanced generalization is critical for deploying autonomous agents in dynamic and unpredictable real-world environments. Furthermore, the demonstrated reduction in collision rates and cost rates during online learning signifies a tangible improvement in the safety of exploration. This is a crucial step towards making online learning from observation viable for safety-critical applications, as it mitigates risks associated with unconstrained environment interaction.

The comparative evaluation across both policy-based and planning-based AIL algorithms, in simulation and real-world experiments, suggests that integrating control-theoretic structure can lead to more interpretable and robust agents. Specifically, planning-based DBF-AIL methods offer increased interpretability by leveraging an explicit optimization landscape, which can be advantageous in understanding and verifying agent behavior compared to opaque black-box policies.

The potential impact of DBFs extends to various domains where autonomous systems operate under safety constraints, such as autonomous driving, aerial robotics, and industrial automation. By providing a principled method for learning safety from observation, this research paves the way for more dependable and trustworthy intelligent systems that can learn and adapt in real-world settings while adhering to critical safety imperatives. Future work, such as integrating safety-aware value functions and addressing conservatism or uncertainty in expert data, further suggests the long-term potential of this approach to advance safe and robust robotic learning.
