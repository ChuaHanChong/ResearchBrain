# Humanoid Loco-Manipulation With Discrete VLA Model

> [!summary] Summary
> Holo-M is a discrete vision-language-action model for humanoid loco-manipulation. It adds whole-body action tokens directly to the vocabulary of a Qwen3-VL-2B backbone. A tokenizer split into four body-part groups (end-effector, body, hand, kinematics) lets it train on human video and humanoid teleoperation together. Grouped discrete diffusion decoding makes real-time control possible. Holo-M scores 143/180 as a generalist and 163/180 as a specialist on SIMPLE, beating every baseline.

## Problem

- Discrete-token VLA recipes have been built almost only for robot arms, whose small action space fits one tokenizer and autoregressive or discrete diffusion decoding.
- Existing humanoid VLAs bolt continuous flow-matching or diffusion action experts onto the VLM, which then need gradient insulation to avoid corrupting the backbone's semantic representations.
- Humanoid whole-body actions are high-dimensional and mixed, so a flat tokenizer, single-source datasets and 208-token autoregressive decoding cannot meet tokenization, training or latency needs.

## Method

- **FAST-RVQ unified action tokenizer**: keeps two low-frequency ==DCT== coefficients per dimension plus four k-means ==residual vector quantization== stages, applied separately to EEF, body, hand and kinematics groups (208 tokens).
- **Vocabulary-extended backbone** with ==group-validity masking==: action, state and structural tokens join the Qwen3-VL vocabulary, and the loss skips missing body-part groups so EgoDex and Humanoid Everyday can co-train.
- **Grouped discrete diffusion decoding**, adapted from DiffusionVL: ==confidence-based parallel de-masking== inside each body-part group and ==causal decoding== across groups, cutting sequential depth 6.5x (32 steps versus 208).

## Results

- Generalist SIMPLE success is **143/180** for Holo-M and **138/180** for its autoregressive variant, against **114/180** for Psi-0 and under **80/180** for pi0.5, DreamZero and ACT.
- Specialist success is **163/180**, ahead of Psi-0 at **154/180** and ACT at **149/180**. On long-horizon Mobile Pick-and-Place, Holo-M succeeds **25/30** versus **18/30**.
- Pretraining raises closed-loop success from **53/180** with none, to **119/180** with Humanoid Everyday, to **138/180** with EgoDex added. Real G1 scores are Grasp **10/10**, Move Pick **8/10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Splitting the action space by body part, and masking missing groups out of the loss, lets partial-coverage data like human hand video supervise a whole-body policy without padding.
> - Discrete tokens in the language vocabulary can match or beat continuous action experts on humanoids, so knowledge insulation is a design choice, not a requirement.
> - Open-loop error barely moves (0.022 to 0.019) while closed-loop success rises 2.6x, so open-loop MAE is a poor stand-in for deployment performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A humanoid robot might be asked to grab a cart handle, push the cart to a desk, and then pick up a coffee cup and put it on the desk. That one language-directed task requires both locomotion and manipulation. The robot must coordinate walking, balancing, and dexterous hand use, but the actions and training data for those capabilities come in different forms.

Holo-M, from Horizon Robotics, gives a vision-language model (VLM) one shared vocabulary for language and whole-body actions. It divides those actions into four body-part groups. It fills in masked action tokens in parallel within each group and generates the groups in order.

The project page labels this real-time video “Serving coffee with a cart.” The instruction is to grab the cart handle, push forward and stop at a desk, then grasp a coffee cup and place it on the desk. This is a qualitative demonstration, not evidence for a success rate.

### 2. One vocabulary, four kinds of action

Holo-M uses Qwen3-VL-2B and extends its vocabulary with action tokens. A 30-step action chunk at 30 Hz has 96 continuous dimensions. These are encoded into 208 action tokens in a fixed order:

- end effector (EEF: wrist and fingertip poses, 100 tokens)
- body joints (62)
- hand joints (32)
- base kinematics (14)

The EEF group supplies a shared task-space representation for training. The other groups cover joint targets and base-motion commands. Each data source supervises only the groups it has, and missing groups are excluded from the loss rather than given artificial targets. The model can therefore learn from sources that supervise different parts of the robot, and no single source has to provide a complete body's actions.

The closest foil, [[2603.12263|Ψ₀]], uses a separate flow-based action expert for humanoid control. Holo-M instead predicts action tokens with the VLM's own sequence model. The authors argue this avoids the need to insulate a separate expert from the VLM. The paper does not independently measure improved semantic preservation.

Teleoperation, human-centric, and simulated data feed a unified action tokenizer with four groups: EEF, body, hand, and kinematics. At inference, image, language, and proprioceptive state are encoded. Groups are decoded by grouped discrete diffusion, parallel within a group and ordered across groups, then detokenized into whole-body controller commands.

### 3. Parallel within a group, ordered across groups

Decoding starts by initializing the current group as masked. The model predicts every masked position together and reveals higher-confidence tokens over repeated iterations. It then appends the completed group before decoding the next. EEF therefore precedes body, hand, and kinematics. Later groups condition on earlier groups, but not the reverse.

With eight de-masking iterations per group, Holo-M needs 32 sequential decoding steps. Autoregressive token-by-token decoding needs 208. This is a 6.5× reduction in sequential decoding depth, not a measured reduction in elapsed time or total computation.

On an RTX 5090, mean inference latency at eight iterations was 476.8 ± 16.9 ms per 30-timestep, one-second action chunk. The robot observes every 500 ms and has a 500 ms hold budget. If inference exceeds the hold, the robot returns to its previous pose.

### 4. What the benchmark establishes

On the SIMPLE simulation benchmark, each score counts successful rollouts across six tasks, three randomization levels, and ten trials per combination, for 180 attempts. The generalist evaluation uses one checkpoint jointly trained on the SIMPLE tasks with no task-specific fine-tuning. In that setting, Holo-M scores **143/180** and Ψ₀ scores **114/180**.

The authors also test an autoregressive version, Holo-M AR. It shares Holo-M's backbone, tokenizer, and training data, but decodes actions autoregressively instead of by grouped diffusion. It scores 138/180. This modest five-rollout difference supports the claim that grouped decoding need not cost performance. It does not show that decoding explains most of the gain over other models. The authors suggest the tokenizer and joint-training recipe account for most of it. The paper also reports no elapsed-time comparison against an autoregressive Holo-M run.

### 5. One sequence, many parts

Parallel prediction makes a long action sequence tractable while keeping the ordering between body groups. Shared action tokens let the same sequence model learn from partially overlapping sources. The benchmark evidence comes from simulation. The real-robot evaluation covers two tasks, with ten rollouts per task, and each deployed specialist was also trained on 100 task-specific teleoperation episodes collected in that deployment scene.
