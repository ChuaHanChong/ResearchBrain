# SpatiaLab: Can Vision-Language Models Perform Spatial Reasoning in the Wild?

> [!summary] Summary
> SPATIALAB, a new comprehensive benchmark, rigorously evaluates vision-language models' spatial reasoning in diverse, real-world visual environments across six categories. Its assessment reveals a considerable performance disparity between state-of-the-art models and humans, particularly when tasks require open-ended generative reasoning.

## Problem

- Vision-language models (VLMs) demonstrate inconsistent spatial reasoning, especially in complex and noisy real-world scenes.
- Prior benchmarks for spatial reasoning often rely on simplified, synthetic, or templated datasets, lacking ecological validity and comprehensive task coverage.
- Most existing evaluations use closed-ended (multiple-choice or binary) formats, which can overestimate VLM capabilities by allowing models to use shortcuts.

## Method

- The ==SPATIALAB benchmark== is introduced, comprising ==1,400 visual question-answer pairs== generated from diverse, unconstrained real-world images.
- It employs a ==hierarchical taxonomy covering six major spatial reasoning categories== (e.g., Depth & Occlusion, Spatial Navigation) and thirty fine-grained subcategories.
- The benchmark supports both multiple-choice (SPATIALAB-MCQ) and open-ended (SPATIALAB-OPEN) evaluation formats to assess discriminative accuracy and generative reasoning.

## Results

- State-of-the-art VLMs achieved a maximum of **54.93%** accuracy on SPATIALAB-MCQ (InternVL3.5-72B), considerably below the human baseline of **87.57%**.
- Performance further declined in the open-ended evaluation, with top models reaching only **40.9%** accuracy (GPT-5-mini), compared to human performance of **64.9%**.
- Qualitative error analysis revealed recurring issues including spatial mislocalization, perspective and scale mistakes, occlusion failures, and generating fluent but visually ungrounded narratives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLMs exhibit a substantial performance gap, averaging 23.0%, between multiple-choice and open-ended spatial reasoning tasks, suggesting models rely on recognition over genuine generative understanding.
> - Systematic errors persist across models in areas like occlusion handling, inconsistent scale judgments, and multi-step relational chaining, pointing to limitations in fundamental geometric grounding.
> - Traditional improvement methods (Chain-of-Thought prompting, supervised fine-tuning, multi-agent systems) provide limited, localized, or inconsistent benefits without resolving core perceptual and reasoning deficiencies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Spatial reasoning constitutes a fundamental aspect of human cognition, essential for tasks ranging from everyday navigation to complex applications in robotics, autonomous driving, and augmented/virtual reality. Contemporary Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated advancements in multimodal representation and language grounding; however, their capacity for robust spatial judgment in realistic, unconstrained environments remains an area of ongoing challenge.

Prior research on VLM spatial reasoning largely relied on synthetic datasets (e.g., CLEVR, GQA) or simplified, puzzle-like setups (e.g., EmbSpatial-Bench, Space3D-Bench, SpatialVLM, RoboSpatial). These environments typically feature reduced visual clutter, homogenized objects, and elementary task designs such as binary spatial relations or low-resolution depth categorization. While useful for controlled analysis, these controlled conditions often mask VLM failures under real-world distribution shifts, where visual noise, diverse spatial relationships, robust occlusion inference, scale-consistent judgments, and planning under partial observability are prevalent. Many existing benchmarks also predominantly employ closed-ended question-answering (QA) or binary judgment formats, which can limit the expressivity of model outputs and potentially inflate perceived performance by allowing models to exploit superficial cues or eliminate distractors rather than demonstrating genuine generative reasoning.

This work introduces SPATIALAB to address these limitations. SPATIALAB aims to provide a comprehensive, real-world benchmark that evaluates VLM spatial reasoning in unconstrained contexts, moving beyond synthetic or narrowly defined tasks. It offers a broad taxonomy of spatial challenges (30 distinct task types across six major categories) and supports both multiple-choice and open-ended evaluation formats, benchmarking VLM performance against human capabilities. By focusing on diverse, visually complex, and spatially rich real-world scenes, SPATIALAB intends to expose critical limitations in current VLMs and guide research towards more human-aligned spatial understanding. The benchmark's design, which emphasizes compositional logic, physics-aware reasoning, and multi-step inference, positions it as a diagnostic tool for understanding specific failure modes and facilitating advancements in robust spatial intelligence for artificial agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed fragility of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in performing spatial reasoning within realistic and complex visual environments, despite their progress in multimodal understanding. Existing benchmarks, as discussed, are perceived as insufficient to truly assess VLM spatial competence due to their reliance on synthetic data, simplified task designs, or limited scope, which fail to capture the nuances of real-world visual complexity, noise, and diverse spatial relationships. This inadequacy restricts the safe and reliable deployment of VLMs in critical applications such as robotics, autonomous driving, and augmented/virtual reality, where accurate spatial understanding is paramount.

The key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Develop a Comprehensive, Realistic Benchmark**: To introduce SPATIALAB, a large-scale benchmark comprising 1,400 visual question-answer pairs. This benchmark is designed to cover a broad taxonomy of spatial tasks, organized into six principal categories (Relative Positioning, Depth & Occlusion, Orientation, Size & Scale, Spatial Navigation, and 3D Geometry) and further subdivided into thirty distinct task types. The questions are derived from realistic, unconstrained visual contexts, emphasizing photographic diversity and complex spatial relational queries, and supporting both multiple-choice (SPATIALAB-MCQ) and open-ended (SPATIALAB-OPEN) evaluation formats.
2. **Conduct a Broad VLM Evaluation**: To perform an extensive evaluation of over 25 state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models. This includes a diverse range of models, encompassing open-source, closed-source, reasoning-focused, and specialized spatial reasoning models, with their performance systematically compared against human baselines to quantify the existing gap in spatial reasoning capabilities.
3. **Perform In-depth Error Analysis**: To deliver detailed error analyses and diagnostic studies. These analyses aim to uncover systematic failure modes in VLM spatial reasoning, particularly in areas like depth perception, occlusion inference, spatial navigation, and 3D geometry. The objective is to provide concrete insights and actionable directions for advancing robust, human-aligned spatial understanding in future models.
4. **Explore Performance Enhancement Strategies**: To investigate various strategies for improving spatial reasoning in VLMs. This includes exploring the effectiveness of supervised fine-tuning (SFT), Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, CoT with self-reflection, and multi-agent architectures (SPATIOXOLVER) that employ multi-step reasoning. The aim is to analyze the benefits and limitations of these interventions across both multiple-choice and open-ended tasks.

These objectives are collectively motivated by the need to establish a rigorous, standardized framework for evaluating and improving VLM spatial intelligence, pushing models beyond surface-level pattern recognition towards genuine, grounded spatial understanding required for real-world interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the construction and application of SPATIALAB, a new benchmark designed for evaluating spatial reasoning in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) under realistic conditions.

**Benchmark Construction:**

1. **Benchmark Design**: SPATIALAB comprises 1,400 visual question-answer pairs structured across six major spatial reasoning categories: Relative Positioning, Depth & Occlusion, Orientation, Size & Scale, Spatial Navigation, and 3D Geometry. Each major category is further decomposed into five subcategories, resulting in a total of 30 distinct task types. This ensures balanced coverage, with at least 25 QA pairs per subcategory and a minimum of 200 per main category. The benchmark supports both multiple-choice (SPATIALAB-MCQ) and open-ended (SPATIALAB-OPEN) evaluation formats.

2. **Image Collection**: Images were curated using a multi-pronged approach:
 - **Automated web crawling**: For large-scale visual diversity.
 - **Targeted online retrieval**: For scene-specific imagery.
 - **Manual snapshots**: Collected in diverse indoor and outdoor environments to ensure real-world variability.
 To capture naturalistic complexity, images were profiled across six meta-dimensions: lighting conditions (e.g., high contrast, shadows), texture complexity (e.g., uniform, complex), edge complexity (sharp, smooth), dominant spatial relation (e.g., stacked, scattered), material type (e.g., opaque, transparent), and gravity constraints (normal, floating). Statistical analysis of the corpus indicated high complexity, with images averaging 21.48 objects (11.88 partially visible) distributed across 3.23 depth layers, and spatial reference chains averaging 2.07 links. Approximately 16.71% of questions required mental rotation, and 56.07% required object specificity.

3. **Annotation**: Annotation involved three phases:
 - **Annotator Training**: Annotators, all engineering graduates aged 24-30, underwent structured training to ensure consistent interpretation of spatial categories, subcategories, and meta-dimensions. Training included crafting precise multiple-choice distractors and formulating compositional prompts for open-ended questions, emphasizing real-world complexity over simplified scenarios.
 - **QA Task Creation**: Each image was paired with one or more spatial QA tasks, designed to balance perceptual grounding with higher-order inference (e.g., proximity gradients, occlusion inference, stability prediction).
 - **Format Encoding**: QA pairs were encoded into both multiple-choice (four options) and open-ended formats.

4. **Review and Quality Control**: A three-tier review pipeline ensured annotation reliability:
 - **Semantic Validity**: QA items checked for alignment with designated subcategories.
 - **Correctness Verification**: Independent annotators verified question phrasing and answers, focusing on ambiguity reduction and clarity.
 - **Gold-Standard Round**: A final round established a set of high-confidence QA pairs.
 Metadata was removed from images, and copyright concerns were addressed. The process yielded 1,400 validated QA items.

**Experiments and Evaluation:**

1. **Models Evaluated**: Over 25 state-of-the-art VLMs were evaluated, categorized as:
 - **Proprietary Models**: e.g., GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5-mini, Gemini-2-Flash, Claude 3.5 Haiku.
 - **Open-source Models**: e.g., Intern-VL3.5, Qwen-VL2.5, GLM, Gemma-3, Llama-3.2, Step-3.
 - **Reasoning Models**: e.g., o4-mini, Gemini-Thinking variants, Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking.
 - **Spatial Reasoning Specialists**: e.g., SpaceOm, SpaceThinker-Qwen2.5VL-3B.
 Human baseline performance was also established for comparison.

2. **Evaluation Process and Metrics**:
 - **Metric**: Accuracy was the primary metric for both MCQ and open-ended tasks.
 - **MCQ Evaluation**: Models were directly prompted to select an option number, with responses automatically checked.
 - **Open-ended Evaluation**: Models generated free-form answers, which were then assessed by a large language model judge (Gemini-2.5-Flash). The LLM judge's performance was validated through an LLM-human agreement analysis, showing substantial agreement (Cohen’s kappa of 0.738 against majority human vote, 0.880 raw accuracy against majority).

**Improving Visual Reasoning Capabilities:**

The study explored several strategies to enhance VLM spatial reasoning:

1. **Inherent Reasoning Mechanisms**: Evaluation compared standard VLM performance with that of "reasoning-enabled" variants.
2. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**: Models were prompted to generate step-by-step reasoning before providing an answer.
3. **CoT with Self-Reflection**: An extension of CoT where models reviewed and revised their initial reasoning.
4. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: Qwen-VL2.5-3B-Instruct was fine-tuned on 40% of the SPATIALAB dataset using LoRA, with evaluation on the remaining 60%.
5. **AI Agents for Spatial Reasoning (SPATIOXOLVER)**: A multi-agent system, adapted from Xolver, was developed. It decomposes visual reasoning into specialized sub-tasks (object segmentation, attribute extraction, spatial relation mapping, grouping/symmetry, transformation tracking) managed by dedicated agents, consolidating outputs into a unified structured representation for reasoning. Gemini-2.5-Flash with low temperature was used for implementation.

**Statistical Robustness and Dataset Stability**:
The benchmark's reliability was assessed through multiple-run averages, intra-class correlation (ICC), a resampling study, and Cronbach's alpha. These analyses confirmed high statistical robustness and internal consistency, demonstrating that results reflect genuine model capabilities rather than random variations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on SPATIALAB revealed a substantial performance gap between state-of-the-art models and human capabilities, alongside specific strengths and weaknesses across different spatial reasoning tasks and intervention strategies.

**Overall Results:**

- **SPATIALAB-MCQ (Multiple-Choice Questions)**:
 - VLM accuracy ranged from approximately 30% to 55%, significantly below the human baseline of 87.57%. Random chance performance was 25%.
 - InternVL3.5-72B, an open-source model, achieved the highest VLM accuracy at 54.93%.
 - Proprietary models and reasoning-focused variants (e.g., GPT-5-mini, o4-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Thinking) generally ranked among the top performers, indicating benefits from instruction-tuning and explicit reasoning.
 - Category-wise analysis showed higher performance in Orientation and 3D Geometry for some strong models, while Spatial Navigation, Depth & Occlusion, and Size & Scale were generally more challenging.
 - Specialized spatial reasoning models (e.g., SpaceOm) achieved only mid-40s to low-40s overall accuracy, suggesting that specialization alone does not guarantee superior performance across the diverse benchmark.

- **SPATIALAB-OPEN (Open-Ended Questions)**:
 - Performance was substantially lower than in the MCQ setting, with VLM accuracy ranging from 9.6% (InternVL3.5-1B) to 40.9% (GPT-5-mini). The human baseline was 64.9%.
 - GPT-5-mini was the top performer at 40.93%.
 - Proprietary and reasoning-tuned models generally led the open-ended tasks, indicating that instruction/CoT-style tuning improves generative outputs.
 - Smaller open-source models typically scored in the low teens, while larger open-source models reached the low-20s, demonstrating modest gains from scale alone.
 - Spatial-specialist models performed poorly in open-ended generation (10-13%), suggesting architecture or task specialization did not translate to robust free-form responses.
 - Performance on Depth & Occlusion, Size & Scale, and Spatial Navigation remained consistently low, reinforcing that multi-step grounding and perceptual reasoning are bottlenecks for generative answers.

**Performance Drop in Open-Ended Evaluation Compared to MCQ:**

- Across 25 models, the average performance gap between MCQ and open-ended formats was 23.0% (standard deviation = 5.5%). This highlights that MCQ alone can overestimate practical spatial reasoning ability.
- The largest gaps were observed in specialist spatial reasoning models (approx. 27%), particularly in Spatial Navigation and Orientation. Reasoning-oriented models showed smaller gaps (approx. 19%).
- Spatial Navigation was the strongest predictor of the overall disparity (Pearson r = 0.99), indicating that multi-step referential reasoning is disproportionately affected in open-ended formats.
- Some models exhibited negative or near-zero gaps in specific categories (e.g., Llama-3.2-11B in Depth & Occlusion, o4-mini in Relative Position), suggesting that poorly designed MCQ distractors can sometimes misrepresent true competence.

**Error Analysis:**

- **SPATIALAB-MCQ**: VLMs demonstrated selective competence but lacked holistic understanding. Systematic weaknesses were identified in embodied reasoning (e.g., Tool Handedness), recursive relational chaining (e.g., Pathway Existence), and handling non-local cues (e.g., Reflective Surfaces). Apparent strengths, like in Obstacle Avoidance, were sometimes attributed to shortcut exploitation rather than robust planning. Models appeared to rely on surface-level correlations, lacking stable internal encodings of orientation, physics, and compositional logic.
- **SPATIALAB-OPEN**: Even top-performing models struggled with tasks requiring continuous or analogical spatial cues (e.g., Proximity Gradients). Failures were concentrated in occlusion handling, orientation, and multi-step relational chaining. The analysis suggested root causes included missing representations for spatial analogies, architectural limitations related to scale and training diversity, and brittle reasoning pipelines.
- **Qualitative Error Analysis**: Recurring error classes included:
 - **Spatial mislocalization and reference confusion**: Models struggled to distinguish visually similar objects in cluttered scenes, often prioritizing salient objects over explicit instructions.
 - **Perspective and scale mistakes**: Reliance on canonical object sizes or world knowledge often overrode actual image-based geometric cues, leading to errors in fit or scale comparison.
 - **Occlusion and ordering failures**: Difficulty in determining which objects are in front/behind, especially with thin or semi-transparent structures, suggesting weak retention of depth signals.
 - **Attribute confusion and semantic swap**: Conflating perceptual properties with functional roles, often influenced by language priors (e.g., assuming crosswalk safety solely from stripes).
 - **Open-ended rationalization without verification**: Generating fluent but visually ungrounded narratives, with high confidence in fabricated explanations.
 These errors were consistent across architectures, pointing to structural biases in current VLMs where multi-cue integration and grounded reasoning are lacking.

**Performance Improvement Approaches:**

- **Inherent Reasoning Mechanisms**: Reasoning-enabled models generally outperformed their standard counterparts in both MCQ and open-ended formats, particularly in relational and orientation tasks. However, improvements were uneven, and some categories experienced declines, indicating that logical consistency alone does not fully address perceptual grounding issues.
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**: CoT provided minimal benefit and often reduced accuracy, except for Orientation tasks. This suggested that step-by-step reasoning amplified flawed priors rather than correcting them when robust perceptual grounding was missing.
- **CoT with Self-Reflection**: Modest gains were observed in MCQ (especially for geometry and depth), but these failed to generalize to open-ended tasks. Reflective reasoning could not compensate for absent perceptual grounding.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: SFT consistently improved MCQ accuracy across all categories (e.g., Qwen-VL2.5-3B-Instruct gained 10.97% overall). However, it showed little to negative transfer to open-ended tasks, suggesting overfitting to task-specific answer distributions and potential "catastrophic forgetting" of linguistic priors. Multi-seed SFT dynamics confirmed a U-shaped accuracy trajectory for open-ended tasks, indicating representational instability. Despite this, SFT on SPATIALAB showed positive transfer to external benchmarks (+5.24% to +11.70%), suggesting generalizable spatial representations were learned.
- **AI Agents for Spatial Reasoning (SPATIOXOLVER)**: The multi-agent system demonstrated substantial gains in Orientation (+8.00% MCQ, +36.00% open-ended). However, it led to severe declines in other categories like Depth & Occlusion (-24.00% open-ended) and Spatial Navigation (-12.00% open-ended). This indicated that multi-step agentic reasoning could reinforce misconceptions when underlying perceptual priors were weak, failing to compensate for fundamental grounding issues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of SPATIALAB is significant as it addresses critical gaps in the evaluation of Vision-Language Models' (VLMs) spatial reasoning capabilities. By moving beyond synthetic or overly simplified datasets, SPATIALAB provides a high-difficulty, comprehensive, and real-world benchmark that more accurately reflects the complexities encountered in practical applications. Its broad taxonomy of 30 spatial subcategories across six major domains, coupled with support for both multiple-choice and open-ended evaluation, establishes a robust diagnostic tool for identifying fine-grained weaknesses in current VLM architectures.

The main findings underscore that state-of-the-art VLMs, despite their advancements, lack holistic spatial competence. Their performance consistently lags significantly behind human baselines, particularly in open-ended generative tasks where accuracy drops by an additional 10-25% compared to multiple-choice formats. This disparity highlights a fundamental limitation: current models often rely on surface-level textual or visual correlations rather than internalized 3D geometric representations and physics-aware reasoning. The detailed error analysis further illuminates these systemic failures, pinpointing specific struggles with occlusion inference, scale consistency across viewpoints, multi-step navigation planning, and the integration of multiple visual cues.

The exploration of performance improvement strategies reveals that traditional methods like scaling, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, and even supervised fine-tuning (SFT) offer only partial solutions. While reasoning-enabled models show some gains in logical consistency, and SFT can boost performance in structured tasks, these interventions often prove brittle or fail to generalize to free-form reasoning, sometimes even amplifying existing biases or leading to "catastrophic forgetting" of linguistic priors. Agentic architectures, while demonstrating promise for tasks reducible to sequential alignment, cannot compensate for a lack of fundamental perceptual grounding in complex 3D environments, leading to performance degradation in categories like depth and navigation.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for several reasons:
1. **Diagnostic Tool**: SPATIALAB provides a standardized, challenging testbed that can drive future research by enabling precise diagnosis of VLM limitations in spatial understanding.
2. **Guiding Architectural Innovation**: The consistent failure modes observed across diverse models suggest a need for architectural innovations that explicitly integrate geometric grounding, physics-aware representations, and multi-scale feature retention, rather than solely relying on increased model scale or linguistic reasoning scaffolds.
3. **Informing Training Strategies**: The findings emphasize the necessity for richer training signals, potentially incorporating embodied interaction, action-conditioned feedback, and counterfactual augmentations to foster more robust and generalizable spatial intelligence.
4. **Promoting Grounded AI**: By exposing the tendency for models to generate fluent but ungrounded rationales, the benchmark highlights the critical need for verification pipelines that enforce factual accuracy and alignment with visual evidence, especially for safety-critical applications.

In conclusion, SPATIALAB establishes a new standard for evaluating spatial reasoning in VLMs, exposing persistent bottlenecks and providing actionable insights. It serves as a foundation for developing VLMs that possess more robust, human-aligned spatial understanding, a crucial step towards creating truly intelligent agents capable of perceiving and interacting effectively within the complexities of the real world.
