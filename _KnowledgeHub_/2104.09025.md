# The MIT Humanoid Robot: Design, Motion Planning, and Control For Acrobatic Behaviors

> [!summary] Summary
> This research details an integrated system for dynamic, acrobatic humanoid robot behaviors, combining a novel hardware design, an actuation-aware motion planner, and a hierarchical landing controller. Simulations demonstrated successful execution of complex maneuvers like backflips and front flips, ensuring planned trajectories respected detailed actuator and battery limitations.

## Problem

- Enabling highly dynamic and acrobatic behaviors in humanoid robots faces challenges due to limitations in existing hardware designs (e.g., high impedance hydraulics, limited force control in compliant actuators).
- Generating feasible motion plans for complex, high-degree-of-freedom acrobatic maneuvers is difficult, with existing trajectory optimization methods often computationally expensive or failing to account for critical actuator limitations.
- Achieving stable and robust landings after high-impact aerial maneuvers requires precise control and effective energy dissipation, which is challenging with simplified models or inconsistent control frameworks.

## Method

- Developed the ==MIT Humanoid== robot, designed with torque-dense electric motors and backdrivable proprioceptive actuators, extending principles from the MIT Cheetah series to a humanoid platform.
- Introduced an Actuation-Aware Kino-Dynamic (AAKD) planner that explicitly integrates detailed, empirically validated actuator and battery dynamic limits into trajectory optimization.
- Implemented a hierarchical landing control framework combining Model-Predictive Control (MPC) for long-horizon planning with Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC) for high-bandwidth, dynamically consistent feedback.

## Results

- The MIT Humanoid robot successfully demonstrated a range of acrobatic motions in a high-fidelity simulation environment, including backflips, front flips, and 180° spinning jumps, using accurate actuator and battery models.
- The AAKD planner generated trajectories for acrobatic maneuvers that consistently adhered to all specified actuator limits, unlike standard kino-dynamic planning, with an acceptable **28.2%** increase in solve time.
- The hierarchical MPC + WBIC landing controller achieved stable recovery from high-impact landings, effectively managing body orientation and angular momentum, with WBIC enabling emergent arm motions for stabilization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Proprioceptive actuator design, previously successful in quadrupeds, can be effectively adapted for humanoid robots to enable high-impulse, dynamic behaviors while managing impacts.
> - Explicitly incorporating actuator torque, speed, and power limits, including battery voltage droop, directly into kino-dynamic motion planning is essential for generating physically executable acrobatic trajectories.
> - A hierarchical control architecture that carefully prioritizes tasks, such as body orientation over centroidal momentum, allows for robust post-impact stabilization and can lead to emergent stabilizing motions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses the challenges associated with enabling highly dynamic and acrobatic behaviors in humanoid robots, an area that has seen demonstrations (e.g., Boston Dynamics' ATLAS performing a backflip) but lacks comprehensive public documentation regarding methodologies and underlying principles.

Previous efforts in humanoid robotics, particularly those influenced by the 2015 DARPA Robotics Challenge (DRC), primarily focused on minimally dynamic tasks. These robots often employed hydraulically actuated designs or physically compliant actuators. Both paradigms present challenges for acrobatic motions: hydraulic systems can exhibit high mechanical impedance, while compliant actuators may have limited force control bandwidth. In contrast, the proprioceptive actuator design originating from the MIT Cheetah robot series (e.g., Mini Cheetah, Cheetah 2, Cheetah 3) has demonstrated success in dynamic legged locomotion through a combination of torque-dense electric motors, high-bandwidth force control, and impact mitigation via backdrivability. However, this design philosophy had not been implemented on a humanoid platform prior to this work.

Motion planning for complex, high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) humanoid robots, especially for acrobatic maneuvers, poses significant challenges. Manually generating such trajectories is impractical. Trajectory optimization methods using full-body dynamics can exploit a robot's full dynamic range but may suffer from computational expense and susceptibility to local minima. Reduced-order models, like spring-mass models, offer computational tractability but often come with restrictive assumptions that limit their generality. Centroidal dynamics-based approaches and kino-dynamic planners represent a compromise, balancing computational efficiency with dynamic expressiveness by considering the robot's center of mass (CoM) and angular momentum while integrating joint-level kinematics. Prior kino-dynamic planners, however, typically did not explicitly incorporate detailed actuator limitations, which are critical for truly pushing the physical boundaries of hardware.

Stable landing after dynamic aerial maneuvers also presents a complex control problem, requiring energy dissipation over time while enabling rapid response to disturbances or model errors. Hierarchical control frameworks combining model-predictive control (MPC) for long-time horizon optimization with whole-body impulse control (WBIC) for high-bandwidth feedback have been explored in previous work for quadruped robots. This paper extends and refines such an approach for humanoid landing, particularly in ensuring dynamic consistency when transferring optimal solutions from simplified MPC models to the more complex WBIC model.

This research contributes to the field by proposing a unified system design that integrates novel hardware development with advanced motion planning and control strategies, specifically tailored to overcome the limitations of existing approaches and enable high-impulse, acrobatic behaviors in humanoid robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive system—encompassing hardware design, motion planning, and control—that enables a humanoid robot to execute highly dynamic and acrobatic maneuvers, such as flips and spinning jumps.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing the Gap in Public Knowledge:** Following demonstrations of acrobatic humanoid robots without accompanying technical documentation, a key motivation is to provide a detailed, systematic approach that explains the underlying methodology, addresses challenges, and offers a framework for replicating similar dynamic motions.
- **Developing Capable Hardware for Dynamic Tasks:** Current humanoid robot designs often face limitations (e.g., high mechanical impedance in hydraulic systems, limited force control bandwidth in compliant actuators) that hinder high-impulse, acrobatic movements. The motivation here is to design a humanoid robot incorporating the successful proprioceptive actuator principles from the MIT Cheetah series. These principles—emphasizing torque-dense electric motors, high-bandwidth force control, and backdrivability—are crucial for generating impulses to propel the robot and for robustly managing high-speed landing impacts.
- **Enabling Actuator-Aware Motion Planning:** Generating complex, high-DoF acrobatic trajectories is computationally challenging. While trajectory optimization exists, a significant motivation is to develop a planning method that not only finds dynamically feasible trajectories but also explicitly considers and respects the physical torque, velocity, and power limits of the robot's actuators. This "actuator-aware" approach aims to ensure that planned motions are directly executable on hardware, overcoming the limitations of planners that might generate physically infeasible trajectories.
- **Achieving Stable and Robust Landing Control:** Acrobatic maneuvers culminate in high-impact landings that require precise control for stability and energy dissipation. The motivation is to develop a landing controller that effectively integrates the long-time horizon optimality of model-predictive control (MPC) with the high-bandwidth, full-body dynamics feedback of whole-body impulse control (WBIC). This integration needs to be dynamically consistent, ensuring the optimal solutions from MPC are effectively translated into real-time WBIC commands, particularly for managing body orientation and angular momentum after aerial phases.
- **Demonstrating System Viability through Simulation:** A core motivation is to validate the integrated system (hardware, planning, and control) through realistic dynamics simulations. This involves incorporating detailed actuator and battery models, derived from experimental validation, into the simulation to ensure that the demonstrated acrobatic motions are genuinely feasible for eventual hardware implementation.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to provide a practical and unified solution for achieving sophisticated dynamic behaviors in humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves an integrated system design covering robot hardware, a novel motion planner, and a hierarchical landing controller, all validated through detailed dynamics simulations.

**A. System Overview**
The proposed "MIT Humanoid" robot is designed to stand approximately 0.7 meters tall and weigh around 21 kg, with mass primarily concentrated in the torso, shoulder, and hip (75.6%). Each leg features five custom actuators (three for the hip, one for the knee, one for ankle pitch), enabling 3D ground reaction force generation and moments about the foot's pitch and yaw axes. Arms have three degrees of freedom. Belt gearing systems are used at the knee, ankle, and elbow joints for increased torque. The system is powered by a 60V, 3Ah battery, and each foot includes four wireless contact sensors. The design paradigm extends the principles of previous MIT Cheetah robots, emphasizing torque-dense electric motors, high-bandwidth force control, and backdrivability.

**B. Custom Actuator Design and Modeling**
The humanoid utilizes two custom electric motor modules, U10 and U12, adapted from the MIT Mini Cheetah actuators to meet increased torque requirements. These modules perform motor torque control without dedicated torque or force sensors, using modified Mini-Cheetah motor drivers. Specifications for mass, gear ratio, maximum torque, and speed are provided (e.g., U10: 33.6 Nm max torque, 55 rad/s max speed; U12: 68.0 Nm max torque, 45 rad/s max speed).

To validate and model the actuators, a custom dynamometer with a Futek TRS300 torque sensor was constructed. This allowed for empirical measurement of peak torque and the torque constant. The U10 module reached saturation at approximately 31 Nm, while the U12 module reached mechanical gearbox limits at 68 Nm without electrical saturation.

An accurate dynamic model for the actuators was developed by combining measured torque constant (k_t) values with inductance and resistance from an impedance analyzer. This model accounts for the relationship between produced torque, pole pairs, and q-axis current ($\tau = \frac{3}{2} p i_q \lambda$). Crucially, it incorporates the limitation that maximum current is dependent on stator voltage (V_s), which can be constrained by back-EMF at high speeds (Equation 2, 3). The motor actuator model also includes estimates for damping and friction coefficients.

Furthermore, the model integrates battery dynamics to account for voltage droop under high power draw. The relationship between total power (P) from the motors, battery impedance (R_b), initial bus voltage (V_init), and final bus voltage (V_fin) is used to calculate the actual voltage available to the motors (Equation 4, 5). This dynamically estimated voltage is then used in the torque-speed curve calculations to provide highly accurate, time-step-dependent actuator limits in simulation.

**C. Planning and Control Framework**
Dynamic aerial motions are divided into three phases: takeoff, flight, and landing, governed by a hierarchical framework (Fig. 5).

1. **Takeoff (Actuation-Aware Kino-Dynamic Planner - AAKD):**
 - **Optimization Variables:** The planner optimizes discrete trajectories for the robot's generalized position and velocity (q, $\dot{q}$), CoM position, velocity, and acceleration (r, $\dot{r}$, $\ddot{r}$), centroidal angular momentum (CAM) and its derivative (h, $\dot{h}$), contact point positions (c_i), and ground reaction forces (f_i).
 - **Motion Selector:** A high-level component selects a target motion (e.g., jump onto a platform, backflip) by specifying a terminal state and generating a reference motion via interpolation.
 - **Objective Function:** The optimization minimizes the squared error between the planned trajectory variables and a reference motion over time (Equation 8).
 - **Standard Constraints:**
 - **Dynamical:** Enforce centroidal dynamics, relating CoM acceleration to forces and CAM derivative to moments (Equation 9). Backward Euler integration ensures continuity.
 - **Kinematic:** Include joint range of motion limits and consistency between joint configuration and CoM/end-effector positions (Equation 10).
 - **Contact:** Differentiate between contacting limbs (fixed contact point, ground height constraint, friction cone limits for reaction forces - Equation 11) and non-contacting limbs (minimum ground clearance - Equation 12).
 - **Novel Actuator Limit Constraints:** A significant contribution is the inclusion of actuator limits within the kino-dynamic planning. Since joint torques are not explicit in centroidal dynamics, they are approximated from ground reaction forces ($\tau_j \approx - \sum J^T_{c,i} f_{c,i}$, Equation 14). To handle the nonlinearity of the contact Jacobian's dependence on robot configuration, the Jacobians of certain joints (hip yaw, hip ab/ad, ankle) are linearized with respect to a reference position. This approximate, configuration-dependent relationship between ground reaction forces and joint torques is then incorporated as a constraint, encoding a linear approximation of the empirically verified torque-speed curve (Fig. 4).

2. **Flight:** During the aerial phase, the robot's joints are controlled using simple PD control to maintain a configuration determined by the AAKD planner.

3. **Landing (Hierarchical Control: MPC + WBIC):**
 - **Model-Predictive Control (MPC):** Activated upon ground contact.
 - **Simplified Model:** Uses a lumped-mass model with linear time-invariant dynamics and Euler angles for body orientation. Assumptions include a nearly constant body orientation and that angular velocity about one principal axis dominates, allowing precession and nutation effects to be ignored.
 - **Dynamics:** Discrete dynamics (Equation 17-19) describe the evolution of state (orientation, CoM position, angular velocity, CoM velocity) based on reaction forces.
 - **Optimization:** A quadratic program (QP) minimizes reaction forces and the error between the state and a reference trajectory over a 1.5-second horizon (15 time steps of 0.1 s each), updated at 10 Hz (Equation 20).
 - **Constraints:** Dynamics, initial conditions, and friction cone constraints on ground reaction forces (Equation 21). Desired CoM position and body orientation are defined based on landing posture.
 - **Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC):** Runs at 500 Hz, providing high-bandwidth posture control.
 - **Integration with MPC:** WBIC receives the optimal motion and reaction force commands from MPC as references.
 - **Task Prioritization:**
 - **Body Orientation Task:** Given the concentration of the MIT Humanoid's mass around its body, the body orientation is used to represent the lumped mass orientation from MPC and is set as the highest-priority task.
 - **Centroidal Momentum (CM) Task:** Placed below the body orientation task. Its feedback law focuses on centroidal angular velocity tracking rather than orientation error correction. The Jacobian from (J_CM = I_CM^-1 A_CM) is used.
 - **Joint Posture Task:** Other tasks, such as joint posture, are projected into the null space of the higher-priority CM task, allowing for arm motions to counteract body orientation control and minimize CAM.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The viability of the proposed system design was demonstrated through simulation experiments, which rigorously incorporated the detailed actuator and battery models.

- **Simulation Environment Accuracy:** The research utilized a custom dynamics simulator from the MIT Biomimetic Robotics Lab. This simulator accounts for rotor inertias, motor torque-speed relationships, and the effect of battery voltage droop, all crucial for realistic predictions of hardware performance. The framework had been previously validated using the Mini-Cheetah robot.

- **Demonstration of Acrobatic Behaviors:** The MIT Humanoid robot successfully demonstrated a range of acrobatic motions in simulation, including backflips, front flips, and 180° spinning jumps (Fig. 7). These behaviors serve as benchmarks for the robot's dynamic capabilities.

- **Efficacy of AAKD Planner:**
 - The Actuation-Aware Kino-Dynamic (AAKD) planner effectively generated trajectories that coordinated high-speed vertical propulsion with significant angular momentum generation for acrobatic movements.
 - **Actuator Limit Adherence:** A comparison between standard kino-dynamic (KD) planning and AAKD planning for a 180° spinning jump (a worst-case scenario due to large hip joint displacements) showed that AAKD successfully ensured all actuator limits were respected, whereas the standard KD planner did not (Fig. 6). This was achieved despite the approximation of joint torques from ground reaction forces.
 - **Computational Cost:** The inclusion of actuator limits in AAKD planning resulted in an average increase of 28.2% in solve times compared to standard KD planning, which the authors considered an acceptable trade-off for ensuring feasibility (Table III).

- **Performance of Landing Control Framework (MPC + WBIC):**
 - The hierarchical control framework, comprising MPC for long-horizon planning and WBIC for high-bandwidth feedback, proved effective for both takeoff and landing phases.
 - WBIC demonstrated the ability to account for errors in the kino-dynamic and lumped-mass models and to recover from small perturbations.
 - Robust tracking performance was observed during the takeoff phase of a front flip, an action involving significant rotation and impact (Fig. 8).
 - The task prioritization within WBIC, particularly the placement of the centroidal momentum task below the body orientation task, resulted in emergent arm motions that stabilized the robot's posture without being explicitly authored in the planner (Fig. 7).

- **Validation of Simulated Actuator Model:** Extensive care was taken to ensure the simulated dynamics mirrored real hardware capabilities. The simulated joint torque profiles for all phases of a front flip (Fig. 9) consistently remained within the empirically verified actuator limits, which included the effects of battery voltage droop. The authors noted that the experimental limit did not account for field weakening, implying the actual hardware limits might be even higher in some regimes. The validity of these simulation results is contingent upon accurate inertial properties, a suitable rigid contact model for the ground, and adequate state estimation on the physical robot.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a unified and systematically developed approach for achieving highly dynamic, acrobatic behaviors in humanoid robots, addressing several critical challenges in the field.

- **Integrated System Design:** The paper's most significant contribution is the demonstration of a cohesive system design that spans hardware, motion planning, and control. This integrated methodology offers practical solutions for developing robots capable of extreme dynamic maneuvers, a capability previously demonstrated but without detailed public methodological insights.

- **Hardware Innovation and Application:** By successfully adapting the proprioceptive actuator design principles from the MIT Cheetah series to a humanoid platform, the research introduces a new class of humanoid robots capable of generating strong impulses and managing high-speed impacts. The development and empirical validation of two custom, high torque density electric motor modules are crucial steps toward realizing physically robust and dynamic humanoid hardware.

- **Actuator-Aware Motion Planning:** The introduction of the Actuation-Aware Kino-Dynamic (AAKD) planner marks an advancement in trajectory optimization for complex robots. By explicitly incorporating detailed, configuration-dependent actuator limits (derived from empirical data and battery dynamics) into the planning process, the AAKD ensures that generated trajectories are physically feasible and directly executable on hardware. This reduces the gap between simulation and reality, a common challenge in robotics.

- **Enhanced Landing Control:** The refined hierarchical control framework, integrating Model-Predictive Control (MPC) with Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC), provides a robust solution for managing stable landings after dynamic aerial maneuvers. The careful consideration of dynamic consistency in transferring MPC outputs to WBIC tasks, particularly in body orientation and centroidal momentum management, improves the stability and performance of high-impact interactions with the environment. The emergent arm motions for stabilization further highlight the framework's effectiveness.

- **High-Fidelity Simulation Validation:** The rigorous approach to validating simulated dynamics, including detailed models of rotor inertias, torque-speed curves, and battery voltage droop, enhances the confidence that the demonstrated acrobatic motions are transferable to physical hardware. This sets a standard for realistic simulation practices in dynamic robotics research.

- **Enabling Future Dynamic Behaviors:** While acrobatic motions may not have immediate direct utility, they serve as a critical benchmark. The capabilities demonstrated in this paper—high-impulse generation, robust aerial control, and stable landing—lay a foundational groundwork for future developments in more practical, yet still highly dynamic, legged robot behaviors, such as parkour, navigation over complex terrain, or agile manipulation in unstructured environments. The work directly addresses the lack of public documentation on how to achieve such movements, providing a valuable reference for the broader robotics community.
