# Bridging Language and Physics: Automated Design of Continuum Robots with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> AID-SR is an agentic framework that automates tendon-driven continuum robot design from natural-language objectives. Designer, Coder, Scene Integrator, and Judge agents iterate inside a closed loop that feeds MuJoCo-observed physical states, semantic critique, and optional human feedback back to the LLM. Across 14 benchmark tasks it reaches 96.2% simulation feasibility and 26.7% average task success after PPO training, with three designs fabricated and deployed physically.

## Problem

- LLMs reason over linguistic patterns, so physical consequences like instability, self-collision, and contact failure are hard to describe in text and remain ungrounded.
- Prior LLM robot-design work targets idealized 2D blocks or simple 3D crawlers, yielding restricted design spaces with little relevance to real manipulation or human-assistive scenarios.
- One-shot generation is brittle: directly prompting a frontier LLM for a full MuJoCo scene yields near 0% syntactically correct and physically stable files.

## Method

- ==Multi-agent pipeline==: a Designer proposes several diverse concepts per objective, a Coder emits MuJoCo XML, and a Scene Integrator merges robot with environment, decoupling morphology from environment adaptation.
- ==Physical Stability Validation== runs actuator tests in MuJoCo and applies diagnostic rules to sensor traces, detecting dead actuation, self-collision, and numerical instability as structured text feedback.
- An ==LLM Judge== gates semantic coherence and design-space adherence, followed by optional ==human-in-the-loop natural-language critique== routed back for up to eight refinement iterations.

## Results

- Across **14** tasks valid rate averages **96.2%** and success rate **26.7%**; Button Pushing peaks at **78.67%** success while Force-Controlled Brushing reaches **0%**, costing **151K** tokens and **$0.73** per design.
- Ablations show removing the Judge drops success **10.7-20** points; removing Validation collapses physical validity to **0%**; adding human feedback lifts Button Pushing from **70.7%** to **76.0%**.
- Backbone comparison on Flat Ground: GPT-5 attains **100%** valid rate versus **73.3%** for Gemini 2.5 Pro, **66.7%** for Claude Sonnet 4.5, and **0%** for Qwen3-30B.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulator diagnostics converted into structured text are what ground language-based design; without that translation layer no generated morphology instantiates at all.
> - Physical validity separates backbone model capability more cleanly than task success, which is confounded by downstream reinforcement-learning stochasticity.
> - High simulation feasibility does not imply buildability: idealized tendon routing and unbounded gear ratios still exceed what real actuators and cables permit.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Automated robot design has traditionally relied on evolutionary algorithms, gradient-based optimization, modular component search, and expert-designed parameter spaces. These methods can optimize geometry, actuation, or control parameters, but their effectiveness depends strongly on how the design space is specified in advance. Broad design spaces can be computationally expensive to search, while narrow spaces may exclude useful or unconventional morphologies.

Generative artificial intelligence has introduced alternative approaches. Diffusion models and other generative methods can produce new robot structures based on learned design distributions, although their outputs are constrained by the quality and diversity of available training data. More recent work has used large language models to generate robot descriptions, simulator files, or executable code. These systems have demonstrated the ability to construct robots in simplified two-dimensional environments and, in some cases, three-dimensional environments involving relatively simple legged or modular robots.

The present work addresses a more difficult setting: the automated design of tendon-driven continuum robots for three-dimensional tasks involving contact, deformation, grasping, locomotion, and manipulation. Continuum robots are suitable for this investigation because they offer a relatively structured, symbolic design representation while retaining complex physical behavior. Their performance depends on segment geometry, joint parameters, tendon routing, actuation, stiffness, contact, and environmental interaction.

The paper’s central contribution is not merely using an LLM to generate a robot model. It proposes a closed-loop process in which the simulator reports physical consequences back to the LLM in structured form. This places the work at the intersection of LLM-based code generation, robot morphology optimization, soft robotics, simulation-based design, and embodied or physically grounded reasoning.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether an LLM can generate physically feasible and task-relevant continuum robot designs from short, high-level natural-language objectives. An example objective is to “design a continuum robot on the robot base to press the button on the wall.” The system is expected to produce a robot morphology and a complete MuJoCo simulation scene rather than only a verbal concept.

The motivation arises from a mismatch between linguistic reasoning and physical behavior. LLMs can produce syntactically plausible code and articulate mechanical rationales, but they do not directly observe how a generated structure behaves under gravity, actuation, contact, self-collision, or numerical simulation. Consequently, one-shot generation frequently results in malformed XML, unstable structures, ineffective actuators, inappropriate scaling, or morphologies that do not correspond meaningfully to the task.

The authors therefore seek to address four related problems. First, the system must generate models that can be loaded and simulated. Second, the models must be physically stable under actuator tests. Third, the robot must be compatible with a specified environment and task. Fourth, the robot must possess sufficient functional potential for a standard controller to learn the target behavior.

The paper also investigates whether limited human feedback can improve the quality of designs without requiring an engineer to manually construct robot models or specify detailed rules. Finally, it examines whether selected simulation designs can be fabricated and transferred to physical hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Design Representation

The system receives a natural-language objective \(O\) and an environment specification \(E\) in MuJoCo XML format. Its output is a set of scenes containing continuum robot morphologies. The target robot class consists of compliant or semi-compliant structures formed from multiple body segments connected by ball or hinge joints and actuated by one or more tendons. The robots are underactuated so that passive compliance contributes to their behavior.

The LLM is given examples of the relevant mechanical units and information about MuJoCo’s modeling syntax. It selects primitive geometries, combines them into a robot, chooses joint and actuator parameters, and produces XML. The task description itself is intentionally brief, with no prescribed morphology or actuation strategy.

#### 3.2. AID-SR Agentic Workflow

The proposed framework, AID-SR, or Autonomous and Iterative Design of Soft Robots, separates design into specialized stages.

The first stage is initial design conception. A Designer agent interprets the task and proposes several distinct concepts. For locomotion, for example, it might suggest a peristaltic body, a multi-legged structure, or a ring-shaped morphology. This parallel initialization is intended to maintain design diversity.

A Coder agent then converts each concept into a MuJoCo robot file. Each design enters an independent refinement loop with a maximum of eight iterations.

The first feedback layer is deterministic physical-stability validation. The generated XML is loaded into MuJoCo, allowing the system to identify syntax errors and invalid model definitions. If loading succeeds, predefined actuator tests are applied while sensor readings and simulator states are monitored. Diagnostic rules identify problems such as ineffective actuation, constant sensor outputs, high-frequency oscillations, self-collision, unexpected simulator resets, and numerical instability. The results are returned as structured feedback to the Coder agent.

The second layer is scene integration. A Scene Integrator combines the robot file with the environment and task specification. It evaluates compatibility, adjusts placement and scale, and generates a unified scene. The combined scene is subjected to physical validation again, since a robot that is stable in isolation may fail when placed in an environment.

The third layer is semantic critique by an LLM Judge. This agent checks whether the design corresponds to a tendon-driven continuum robot, contains necessary sensors and actuators, is internally coherent, and appears relevant to the stated task. Failed designs receive textual critiques and are sent back for revision.

The fourth layer is optional human feedback. An engineer can inspect the rendered scene, logs, and XML through a web interface and provide high-level natural-language guidance. The human does not need to edit the model directly. The main benchmark excludes human feedback for consistency, while ablations measure its effect.

#### 3.3. Downstream Reinforcement Learning Evaluation

Passing physical validation is not considered sufficient evidence of functional usefulness. Each candidate is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization with a common training pipeline. Observations combine general proprioceptive information, such as base position, joint angles, and tendon measurements, with task-specific features including target distances and force readings.

Rewards are task-dependent but generally use generic task-level signals rather than morphology-specific engineering. Reaching and locomotion primarily use distance-based rewards. Button pushing and surface adaptation reward deformation toward a target. Pipe insertion rewards downward motion while penalizing excessive contact force. Drawer opening uses sequential rewards for approaching, grasping, and pulling. Wall brushing rewards trajectory following while maintaining a target contact-force range.

For symmetric grasping fingers, actuators are sometimes grouped and controlled jointly based on the LLM’s design rationale. This modification is intended to make exploration more consistent with the proposed mechanism.

A design is classified as invalid if it remains stationary, exhibits implausible motion, crashes the simulator, or fails to produce a structurally plausible continuum robot. A valid design is one that survives training and evaluation and passes manual structural inspection. A successful design is a valid design that satisfies the task-specific success criterion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The benchmark contains 14 tasks in four categories. Reaching includes button pushing, surface adapting, and pipe inserting. Grasping includes simple grasping, zero-gravity grasping, delicate grasping, and dynamic catching. Locomotion includes flat ground, stairs, obstacles, gaps, and tunnels. Manipulation includes drawer opening and force-controlled wall brushing.

Across the benchmark, the abstract reports a 96.2% simulation-feasibility pass rate. After uniform PPO training, 26.7% of generated robots reportedly complete their assigned tasks. The task-level results show substantial variation. Button pushing has a 100% valid rate and a 78.67% success rate, the highest task success rate in the table. Surface adapting has a 100% valid rate but only a 13.3% success rate, while pipe inserting has a 93.3% valid rate and a 21.4% success rate.

For grasping, simple grasping achieves a 93.3% valid rate and 33.3% success rate. Zero-gravity grasping reaches 13.3% success, delicate grasping reaches 20.0%, and dynamic catching reaches 26.7%. The authors attribute these lower results to difficulties in generating complete, stable, and appropriately fitted gripper morphologies and in modeling contact relationships.

Locomotion performs better than grasping overall. Flat-ground locomotion reaches 46.7% success, stairs 26.7%, obstacles 13.3%, gaps 53.3%, and tunnels 33.3%. These tasks require coordinated global movement, but the authors note that the uniform reward and control setup may not fully exploit the capabilities of individual morphologies.

The manipulation tasks are the most difficult. Drawer opening achieves a 100% valid rate but only 6.7% success. Force-controlled brushing has an 80% valid rate and no successful designs. This result indicates that the framework can often produce models that simulate without failure, while still failing to generate morphologies and control affordances appropriate for multi-stage or force-sensitive tasks.

The average end-to-end generation cost is approximately 151,000 tokens, $0.73, and 523 seconds per design. Force-controlled brushing requires approximately 261,000 tokens, $1.19, and 868 seconds, indicating that more complex constraints lead to additional refinement cycles.

Ablation studies support the role of the feedback layers. Removing the semantic Judge reduces task success by approximately 10.7–20% relative to the full automated framework in the tested tasks. Removing scene integration also reduces validity and success. Eliminating both components produces further degradation. Without physical validation, the reported validity rate is 0%, showing that syntactic or semantic generation alone does not reliably produce executable MuJoCo models. Adding one human-feedback iteration increases button-pushing success from 70.7% to 76.0% and flat-ground success from 53.3% to 60.0%.

Backbone-model comparisons on flat-ground locomotion show GPT-5 achieving a 100% valid rate and 46.7% success. Gemini 2.5 Pro achieves 73.3% validity with no successful designs, while Claude Sonnet 4.5 reaches 66.7% validity and 26.7% success. The tested open-source models perform less reliably, suggesting that model capacity affects multi-step correction under dense physical and syntactic constraints.

Three selected designs were fabricated and tested in the real world for dynamic catching, simple grasping, and delicate grasping. The paper reports that all three completed their corresponding tasks, with broadly consistent motion patterns between simulation and hardware. However, the authors also identify simulation assumptions that impede transfer, including unrestricted tendon routing and actuator specifications that ignore volume, mass, thermal limits, and realistic power density.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work demonstrates a method for using LLMs as participants in an iterative engineering process rather than as one-shot code generators. Its main significance lies in connecting language-based design proposals to simulator-derived diagnostics, environmental compatibility checks, semantic review, and optional expert judgment.

The framework could reduce the effort required to explore early-stage robot concepts, particularly when the design space is structurally rich but can be represented in a simulator. It may also provide a mechanism for translating informal task descriptions into candidate morphologies that human engineers can inspect and refine.

The results nevertheless indicate that physical validity and task success remain distinct challenges. The high feasibility rates contrast with relatively low success rates in grasping and complex manipulation. The system is therefore more effective as a design-generation and filtering tool than as an autonomous replacement for mechanical engineering and control design. Future progress will require more realistic actuation and tendon models, stronger task-level feedback, morphology-aware reward or controller design, broader hardware testing, and evaluations against optimization-based and human-designed baselines.
