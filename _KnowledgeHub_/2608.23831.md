# Learning to Act While Waiting: RL Finetuning of Generalist Robot Policies Under Inference Latency

> [!summary] Summary
> ARLI (Asynchronous RL with Intermediate Information) enables reinforcement-learning finetuning of large generalist robot policies despite inference latency. It keeps asynchronous inference, then conditions a lightweight noise-steering RL policy on the committed intermediate actions plus a fresher mid-inference observation taken just before the action expert runs, restoring near-Markovian structure. Real bimanual UR5e tasks rise from roughly 40% to near 100% success within 125 episodes.

## Problem

- Generalist VLAs need 100-300 ms per action generation, so synchronous execution either pauses or acts on stale observations, degrading control in reactivity-critical deployment.
- Prior latency work (action chunking, temporal ensembling, real-time chunking, delay-injected sim-to-real) targets deployment of a frozen pretrained policy and never addresses online adaptation.
- Asynchronous inference makes the effective RL state non-Markovian, since actions are selected before reaching the state they execute in, causing standard RL finetuning to fail outright.

## Method

- **ARLI** actions: the ==DSRL== ==noise-steering== policy is conditioned on the committed intermediate action chunk executing during inference, supplying predictive information about the future state the new chunk lands in.
- **ARLI** state: decomposing latency into VLM-backbone and action-expert stages lets the RL policy fire only t_rl_delay steps before execution, conditioning on a much fresher intermediate observation.
- Integration with ==real-time chunking== inpainting guidance preserves chunk continuity, plus a ==delayed-oracle-optimality-gap== result bounding the suboptimality of ==action-chunked Q-learning== under delayed observations.

## Results

- Across Kinetix mjc_swimmer/walker/car_launch (t_delay=**4**) and AlohaTransferCube with a **3.3B** flow VLA (t_delay=**20**), ARLI reaches higher final success faster than DSRL, residual RL, and RTC baselines over **3 seeds**.
- Three real bimanual UR5e tasks starting from about **40%** base success reach near **100%** within **100-125 episodes**, while synchronous and RTC DSRL variants stay below **80%** or need double the time.
- Ablations show conditioning on both intermediate state and actions beats either alone, and learning improves sharply once RL inference happens later than half the total inference delay.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Latency is not merely an execution artifact; it changes the effective MDP, so fix the RL policy's state representation before blaming the learning algorithm.
> - Heterogeneous inference stages are exploitable: small downstream modules can run later on fresher observations, buying reactivity at no extra wall-clock cost.
> - Committed future actions are cheap predictive state; any controller acting under delay can condition on its own pending commands rather than stale observations alone.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen significant advancements with the emergence of generalist robot policies, often referred to as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These policies, trained on extensive and diverse datasets, aim to equip robots with the capability to perform a wide array of tasks [1–11]. While offline training provides a foundational capability, online improvement and adaptation through reinforcement learning (RL) are increasingly recognized as critical for achieving the high precision, reliability, and adaptability required for real-world deployment [12–17].

A fundamental challenge hindering the effective online improvement of these generalist policies is inference latency. Modern VLAs are typically large, often comprising billions of parameters, which results in non-trivial delays in action generation. For instance, common open-source VLAs can take 100 to over 300 milliseconds per action generation step. Such delays manifest in real-world deployment as noticeable pauses or jerky movements, which can alter the effective dynamics of the environment and compromise policy performance, particularly in tasks demanding high reactivity. Critically, these delays can break the Markov assumption fundamental to many standard RL algorithms, leading to learning failures.

Prior research has addressed aspects of policy latency, primarily focusing on maintaining smooth policy deployment rather than enabling online learning. Strategies like action chunking, asynchronous inference, temporal ensembling, rejection sampling, diffusion inpainting, and training policies robust to delays in simulation have been proposed. However, these methods typically operate on a fixed, pre-trained policy and do not investigate how latency impacts the ability of RL-based approaches to learn effectively during deployment. Furthermore, while concurrent control and RL with delayed information [33–38] exist, they often consider generic RL settings or policies trained from scratch, rather than the specific action-chunked asynchronous inference regime characteristic of modern diffusion and VLA policies.

This work directly addresses the identified gap by focusing on enabling robust RL finetuning of generalist policies under practical inference latency constraints. It leverages recent advancements in asynchronous inference to mitigate deployment-related latency issues and builds upon Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL) as a base finetuning algorithm, which allows RL to steer the denoising process of diffusion-based policies. The paper’s contribution lies in adapting and extending these concepts to create a latency-aware framework that makes RL-based online improvement feasible for large generalist robotic policies in real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable effective reinforcement learning (RL) based improvement and finetuning of generalist robot policies under the practical constraint of significant inference latency.

The motivation stems from several key observations regarding modern robotics:
- **Increasing Reliance on Generalist Policies (VLAs):** Generalist robot policies, such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, are becoming prevalent due to their ability to perform diverse tasks based on large-scale datasets. They offer a strong foundation for robotic control.
- **Critical Need for Online Improvement:** While powerful, these pre-trained policies often require further refinement and adaptation through online experience (RL finetuning) to achieve the high levels of precision, reliability, and adaptability necessary for real-world deployment. Offline training alone may not capture all real-world complexities or provide sufficient fine-grained control.
- **Challenge of Inference Latency:** A significant obstacle to online RL improvement is the substantial inference latency inherent in large VLA models. Generating a single action chunk can take hundreds of milliseconds.
 - **Problem with Synchronous Inference:** If a robot pauses to wait for each action chunk to be computed, this leads to jerky, discontinuous, or slow movements, rendering the policy ineffective, especially in tasks requiring continuous, reactive control.
 - **Problem with Naive Asynchronous Inference:** To mitigate pauses, asynchronous inference strategies compute the next action chunk while the current one is still executing. However, this means the VLA's inference begins at an earlier state (e.g., `s_{t-t_delay}`) than when the action will actually be executed (at `s_t`). This reliance on "stale" observations creates a non-Markovian effective environment state, causing standard RL algorithms (which assume Markovian dynamics) to fail or learn inefficiently.
- **Unaddressed Gap:** Existing work on latency primarily focuses on policy deployment (e.g., smoothing actions or minimizing pauses) but does not adequately address how this latency fundamentally impacts the ability of an RL agent to learn and adapt the policy.

Therefore, the motivation is to bridge this gap by developing a framework that can overcome the non-Markovian state issue introduced by asynchronous inference, thereby allowing RL to effectively finetune generalist policies even when faced with substantial inference delays in real-world dynamic environments. This is crucial for enabling robots to continuously improve and become more capable through real-world experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **Asynchronous RL with Intermediate Information (ARLI)**, a framework designed to enable effective RL-based finetuning of generalist robot policies under inference latency constraints. ARLI is built upon asynchronous inference strategies, which interleave action generation with execution to hide latency, and extends Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL) as its core RL algorithm. DSRL specifically tunes the denoising process of diffusion or flow policies, enabling a lightweight RL policy to "steer" the actions produced by the larger pre-trained policy by selecting the input noise.

The core challenge addressed by ARLI is the incompatibility of standard RL with asynchronous inference. When the generalist policy begins inference at `t - t_delay` (based on state `s_{t-t_delay}`) but its generated actions are executed at `t`, the RL policy effectively operates on a non-Markovian state. ARLI addresses this by carefully augmenting the state information available to the RL policy (`π_rl`).

ARLI incorporates two key contributions to design a low-latency RL policy that maximizes reactivity within the inference window:

1. **Conditioning `π_rl` on Intermediate Actions (ARLI Actions):**
 - When the inference for the next action chunk begins at `t - t_delay`, the RL policy `π_rl` is provided not only with the initial state `s_{t-t_delay}` but also with the sequence of actions `a_{t-t_delay}:t` (i.e., `a_{t-t_delay}, ..., a_{t-1}`). These are the actions from the *previously computed* chunk that are still being executed during the current inference window.
 - Rationale: By observing these intermediate actions, `π_rl` gains more predictive information about the environment's state at time `t`, allowing it to better anticipate the future and adjust its steering decisions accordingly. Although the pre-trained policy (`π_pt`) cannot easily integrate this additional conditioning, the lightweight `π_rl` can learn to leverage it.

2. **Conditioning `π_rl` on an Intermediate State (ARLI State):**
 - Most VLAs are composed of two main parts: a large VLM (Vision-Language Model) backbone (`π_vlm_pt`) that processes observations and language to produce embeddings, and a smaller action expert (`π_ae_pt`) that takes these embeddings and generates the final action chunk via a denoising process. The `π_vlm_pt` typically accounts for the majority of the inference delay.
 - Since DSRL operates by selecting the input noise for the `π_ae_pt` denoising process, `π_rl` does not need to run at `t - t_delay`. Instead, `π_rl` can be executed just before `π_ae_pt` is called.
 - The authors define `t_rl_delay` as the combined inference time of `π_rl` and `π_ae_pt`. This means `π_rl` can access a more recent state `s_{t-t_rl_delay}`.
 - Rationale: This intermediate state allows `π_rl` to react to environmental disturbances or changes that occur between `t - t_delay` and `t - t_rl_delay`, which would otherwise be unobserved. The augmented RL state becomes `s_rl_t = (s_{t-t_delay}, a_{t-t_delay}:t, s_{t-t_rl_delay})`.

3. **Integration with Real-Time Chunking (RTC):**
 - ARLI incorporates RTC, a technique that improves zero-shot policy deployment under asynchronous inference by promoting temporal consistency between action chunks through diffusion inpainting.
 - Rationale: While `π_rl` could theoretically learn this temporal consistency, explicitly enforcing it through RTC ensures reliable execution and allows `π_rl` to focus on learning richer, corrective behaviors, without slowing down the learning process. DSRL selects the initial noise, and RTC's inpainting guidance is applied during denoising.

**Theoretical Guarantee:**
The paper provides a theoretical result (Proposition 1, informal) demonstrating that standard Q-learning, when applied to action chunking policies with an `t_delay` observation delay, can converge to a policy whose performance is bounded. This bound is related to a "Delayed Oracle Optimality Gap" (`ω_d`), which quantifies the sub-optimality arising from delayed decision-making. ARLI's approach of incorporating intermediate states and actions effectively reduces this `ω_d` by providing more up-to-date information, thereby improving performance beyond what is achievable with naive delayed observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ARLI involved both simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against various baselines. The core findings demonstrate that ARLI effectively enables RL finetuning under inference delays where other approaches struggle or fail, and can even match or exceed performance in idealized no-latency scenarios.

**Simulated Experiments:**
- **Tasks:** Kinetix benchmark tasks (`mjc swimmer`, `mjc walker`, `car launch`), known for requiring high reactivity, and `AlohaTransferCube`, a bimanual manipulation task.
- **Latency Settings:** `t_delay`=4 and `t_rl_delay`=1 for Kinetix; `t_delay`=20 and `t_rl_delay`=10 for `AlohaTransferCube`.
- **Baselines:** Naive DSRL (asynchronous, conditioned only on `s_{t-t_delay}`), DSRL with RTC, Residual RL, and the pre-trained policy with synchronous/RTC inference.
- **Results (Figure 4):**
 - ARLI consistently outperformed naive DSRL, achieving higher final success rates and faster learning across all tasks.
 - DSRL with RTC showed some improvement over naive DSRL, but ARLI's state augmentations (intermediate actions and state) were identified as necessary for full learning.
 - ARLI often performed effectively even without RTC, though RTC generally improved its efficiency and reliability.
 - ARLI also significantly outperformed Residual RL, suggesting that DSRL's ability to steer the denoising process, combined with ARLI's state augmentations, is more effective than simple residual corrections, despite Residual RL's inherently higher reactivity.
 - These results indicated ARLI's capability to handle asynchronous inference delays and enable effective finetuning for both high-reactivity settings and with large-scale VLAs.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- **Robot:** Bimanual UR5e robot cell with wrist and base cameras.
- **Tasks:** `Assembly` (inserting a power connector), `Shoe-in-Bag` (placing a shoe into a narrow bag), and `Bag-Placement` (positioning a bag with two arms).
- **Policy:** `π_0.5` finetuned on human demonstrations.
- **Latency Settings:** `t_delay`=10, `t_rl_delay`=7, action horizon `n`=20.
- **Baselines:** DSRL (synchronous inference), DSRL (RTC), Pre-trained Policy (synchronous inference), Pre-trained Policy (RTC).
- **Results (Figure 5):**
 - ARLI significantly improved the base policy's success rate from around 40% to nearly 100% after 100-125 episodes of training across all three tasks.
 - In contrast, DSRL with synchronous inference and DSRL with RTC struggled to reach 80% success in the same timeframe for `Assembly` and `Bag-Placement`, and took nearly twice as long for `Shoe-in-Bag`.
 - The paper highlighted that synchronous inference resulted in poor baseline policy success and failed or significantly slower RL training, emphasizing that asynchronous inference is *required* for learning on these tasks. ARLI demonstrated its role in facilitating RL finetuning under this necessary asynchronous regime.
 - ARLI also showed significantly higher policy throughput (successes/hour) compared to baselines.
 - For the `Bag-Placement` task, ARLI (Actions Only) did not converge to a high success rate, suggesting the intermediate state information was particularly crucial for this task.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Importance of Intermediate State and Actions (Figure 6, 9, 10):** Experiments isolating the components of ARLI's augmented state demonstrated that conditioning `π_rl` on *both* intermediate states and intermediate actions yielded superior results compared to using only one or neither. This underscored the importance of the full `s_rl_t` formulation.
- **Sensitivity to `t_delay` (Figure 7):** ARLI, particularly with RTC, exhibited slower performance degradation as `t_delay` increased, indicating greater robustness to varying inference delays compared to DSRL.
- **Sensitivity to `t_rl_delay` (Figure 8, 11):** The results showed that having more up-to-date information (smaller `t_rl_delay`) led to better performance. Significant learning improvements were observed when `t_rl_delay` was less than half of `t_delay`.

**Learning Dynamics of ARLI Noise Steering (Appendix C, Figure 12):**
- Analysis of ARLI's learned noise distribution on held-out `Shoe-in-Bag` trajectories revealed that ARLI learns to reshape the noise input in a trajectory- and phase-dependent manner.
- Failed episodes showed localized directional shifts in the predicted noise distribution (increased mean magnitude, decreased variance) specifically around task phases requiring significant corrective steering (e.g., alignment and insertion). This suggests ARLI becomes more confident and biased in these critical regions, learning targeted corrections based on the effective state information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work addresses a significant challenge in the practical deployment and improvement of modern generalist robot policies: enabling effective online reinforcement learning (RL) finetuning in the presence of substantial inference latency. The proposed ARLI framework represents a step towards making such RL-based improvement feasible under real-world operational constraints.

**Significance:**
The paper demonstrates that by strategically augmenting the observations provided to the RL policy, it is possible to restore a near-Markovian structure to the learning problem, thereby allowing standard RL algorithms to function effectively even when operating with asynchronous inference and significant delays. This challenges the previous limitation where severe latency would cause standard RL to fail entirely.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Real-World Robot Capabilities:** By enabling continuous improvement of generalist robot policies during deployment, ARLI can contribute to more robust, precise, and adaptive robotic systems. Robots could learn from their own experiences in diverse and dynamic real-world environments, overcoming the sim-to-real gap and inherent limitations of offline training.
- **Practicality of Large Foundation Models in Robotics:** The work makes a practical contribution to the field by showing how large vision-language-action (VLA) models, despite their computational demands, can be effectively integrated with online learning paradigms. This is crucial for realizing the full potential of foundation models in robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** ARLI provides a framework that can be built upon for further research into latency-aware RL. It opens avenues for exploring more sophisticated ways to handle delayed information and actions in learning systems, potentially leading to more efficient and reliable adaptation strategies.

**Limitations:**
Despite its demonstrated efficacy, ARLI has several identified limitations:
- **Pretrained Policy Architecture Assumption:** ARLI's effectiveness, particularly concerning the intermediate state conditioning, relies on the assumption that the pretrained generalist policy can be cleanly decomposed into a VLM backbone and an action expert, with the latter being the sole component requiring a noise input for steering. This architecture may not apply to all types of generalist policies, limiting the framework's universality. However, the intermediate action conditioning component remains applicable even without this specific decomposition.
- **Reactivity vs. Consistency Trade-off with RTC:** While integrating Real-Time Chunking (RTC) improved performance, the authors note that RTC's mechanism of enforcing old actions to maintain temporal continuity might inherently reduce the RL policy's reactivity. This introduces a potential trade-off between temporal consistency and the expressive power of the RL policy to introduce novel, reactive behaviors.
- **Dependence on DSRL:** ARLI is built upon DSRL as its core RL algorithm. Consequently, ARLI inherits any limitations or challenges DSRL might face on certain tasks, such as known difficulties in learning specific behaviors. If DSRL struggles, ARLI is likely to struggle similarly.

In summary, ARLI addresses a critical bottleneck for the real-world application of advanced robot learning, demonstrating a viable path for online policy improvement under practical inference latency constraints, while also highlighting areas for future methodological and architectural investigation.
