# Learning Correlated Reward Models: Statistical Barriers and Opportunities

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from MIT EECS demonstrate that learning correlated probit models, crucial for capturing nuanced human preferences, is statistically impossible with traditional pairwise preference data but feasible with best-of-three preference data. They provide the first identifiability guarantees and an efficient algorithm, empirically showing improved prediction accuracy and more diverse recommendations on real-world datasets.

## Problem

- Classical Random Utility Models (RUMs), like the logit model, suffer from the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption, which fails to capture context-dependent or correlated human preferences.
- The IIA assumption prevents models from accurately representing how preference for one item might correlate with preference for another, leading to coarse approximations and issues like the 'red bus/blue bus problem'.
- Fundamental questions regarding data sufficiency, identifiability, and robust estimation for correlated probit models, which explicitly account for utility correlations, were largely unaddressed.

## Method

- The authors formally establish that traditional ==pairwise preference== data is insufficient for learning correlated probit models, while best-of-three preference data is provably sufficient and necessary.
- The paper provides the first formal proofs for the unique identifiability of correlated probit model parameters (mean vector and covariance matrix) from three-way preference observation probabilities, extending from three to an arbitrary number of alternatives.
- A polynomial-time algorithm is proposed that leverages these identifiability results, constructing a sparse graph of O(n^2) triplets and using convex optimization for efficient estimation of the full n-item model with near-optimal sample complexity.

## Results

- On synthetic data, the best-of-three probit model accurately recovers ground truth covariance matrices and outperforms pairwise-trained probit models, which often learn non-existent correlations or are sensitive to initialization.
- Across various real-world datasets (sushi, jokes, Netflix, MovieLens), the best-of-three probit model consistently yields higher prediction accuracy compared to pairwise probit models, often matching or slightly surpassing logit and matrix completion models, especially when inherent correlations are present.
- In welfare maximization tasks (e.g., suggesting a menu), the correlated probit model identifies more diverse and broadly satisfying choices for the population by accounting for item correlations, unlike models assuming independence, demonstrating its practical utility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pairwise preference comparisons inherently lack the information required to discern correlational structures within probit models, revealing a critical statistical barrier.
> - By observing ranked preferences over three items, sufficient information becomes available to uniquely identify and estimate the full mean and covariance structure of correlated utility models.
> - A scalable estimation approach can be achieved by carefully selecting a sparse set of O(n^2) three-item sub-problems and aggregating their individual solutions through convex optimization, avoiding cubic complexity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Random Utility Models (RUMs) constitute a foundational framework for modeling human preferences across diverse fields such as mathematical psychology, transportation science, econometrics, and marketing. More recently, RUMs have become a critical component in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), particularly for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences.

A persistent challenge in many classical RUMs is the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption. This assumption posits a universal underlying utility function that is independent of the presence or absence of other alternatives, thus failing to capture the nuanced and context-dependent nature of human preferences. For instance, in the context of LLMs, IIA suggests a single utility function for all users, which a growing body of work indicates is insufficient for representing the full spectrum of human preferences and can lead to issues like the "red bus/blue bus problem" where the introduction of a seemingly irrelevant option can paradoxically alter relative preferences between existing options.

While some efforts, such as Nested Logit and Mixed Logit models, have attempted to relax the IIA assumption, statistical and computational guarantees for RUMs that explicitly model utility correlations, particularly the probit model, have largely been absent. Previous research has focused on scaling probit estimation, but not on the fundamental identifiability or robust estimation of the correlational structure itself. This paper addresses this gap by investigating the statistical and computational requirements for learning correlated probit models, which explicitly account for correlations between item utilities, thereby offering a more granular and accurate representation of user preferences. The work thus aims to provide theoretical foundations and practical methods for developing more sophisticated and personalized reward models in domains like RLHF.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the statistical and computational challenges associated with learning correlated utility models, specifically focusing on the correlated probit model where utilities follow a multivariate normal distribution. This endeavor is motivated by the recognized limitations of existing RUMs, predominantly the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption.

The IIA assumption, common in classical models like the logit model, simplifies human preferences by positing a universal underlying utility function. This simplification leads to a coarse approximation of the diverse range of human preferences and limits the ability of models to capture how preferences for one item might correlate with preferences for another. In practical applications, particularly within the burgeoning field of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) for Large Language Models (LLMs), the IIA assumption hinders the development of personalized and context-aware reward models. For example, it prevents past user behavior or preferences for certain content types from informing future responses or recommendations effectively. The consequence is that models aligned with human feedback based on IIA might offer suboptimal or even contradictory suggestions in complex scenarios.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out to answer two specific, fundamental questions that have remained largely unaddressed for correlated probit models:
1. **What data is needed to learn them?** This question probes the sufficiency of conventional data collection paradigms, such as pairwise preference data, for identifying and estimating the correlational structure of utility functions.
2. **How many samples do we need?** This targets the statistical efficiency and sample complexity required for an estimator to accurately learn these models from appropriate data.

By addressing these questions, the authors aim to overcome the fundamental shortcomings of models constrained by the IIA assumption. The motivation is to enable the development of more accurate reward models that can capture the full richness of human preferences, allowing for improved personalization, better counterfactual predictions, and more effective alignment of AI systems with human values and tastes. Prior to this work, even the identifiability of correlated probit models, disregarding any statistical considerations, was not established, highlighting the fundamental nature of the problems addressed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the learning of correlated probit models, where user utilities for $n$ items are modeled as a high-dimensional random vector $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$. To address inherent identifiability issues (where scaling $X$ by a positive constant or shifting all components by a constant scalar does not change choice probabilities), the authors adopt specific normalization constraints: $\langle\mu, \mathbf{1}\rangle = 0$, $\Sigma\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$, and $\text{Tr}(\Sigma) = n$. This ensures $X$ resides on the hyperplane $\mathbf{1}^\top X = 0$ and $\Sigma$ has rank $n-1$.

The methodological approach proceeds in several stages:

#### 3.1. Data Sufficiency Analysis

The authors first analyze the sufficiency of different types of preference data:
- **Pairwise Preference Data**: The paper formally establishes (Theorem 3.2) that classical pairwise preference data (i.e., comparing two items at a time, $|R|=2$) is fundamentally insufficient to learn correlational information in the probit model for $n \geq 3$. This is demonstrated by showing that for any given $(\mu, \Sigma)$, an infinite set of alternative $(\mu', \Sigma')$ pairs exists that yield identical pairwise preference probabilities, especially when $\mu = \mathbf{0}$ (where all pairwise preferences are $1/2$ regardless of $\Sigma$). This result explains the historical lack of statistical and computational guarantees in this setting.
- **Three-way Preference Data (Best-of-Three)**: In contrast, the paper demonstrates that best-of-three preference data (i.e., observing the ranked preference over three items, $|R|=3$) is provably sufficient and necessary to overcome the limitations of pairwise data. This type of data provides enough information to discern correlational structures.

#### 3.2. Identifiability (Theoretical Guarantees)

The research establishes novel identifiability results for correlated probit models:
- **Case of Three Alternatives ($n=3$)**:
 - The utility vector $X \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is first projected onto a 2-dimensional plane defined by $\mathbf{1}^\top X = 0$.
 - A linear transformation $P$ is then applied to map $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ to a bivariate normal distribution $V \sim \mathcal{N}(\dot{\mu}, \dot{\Sigma})$.
 - The model parameters are further transformed to render the distribution isotropic, $W \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{e}\mu, I)$, where $\mathbf{e}\mu$ represents the transformed mean vector and $I$ is the identity covariance matrix.
 - The core idea is to show that various probabilities (e.g., $P\{X_i \geq X_j\}$ or $P\{X_i \geq X_j \geq X_k\}$) can be expressed in terms of dot products involving $\mathbf{e}\mu$ and transformed basis vectors $\mathbf{e}c_i$.
 - Lemma 4.2 demonstrates that quantities $\alpha_i := \langle\mathbf{e}c_i, \mathbf{e}\mu\rangle$ are identifiable from observed three-way ranking probabilities (specifically, from single-preference probabilities $P\{X_i \geq X_j\}$).
 - Lemma 4.3 then shows that the dot products $\alpha_{ij} := \langle\mathbf{e}c_i, \mathbf{e}c_j\rangle$ (which encode correlational information) are identifiable from observed two-way preference probabilities within the 2D isotropic space, given the identified $\alpha_i$.
 - Theorem 4.1 leverages these identifiable quantities ($\alpha_i, \alpha_{ij}$) to uniquely reconstruct the parameters $\dot{\mu}$ and $\dot{\Sigma}$ of the bivariate normal distribution, and subsequently $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ of the original 3-item probit model.
- **Case of More Than Three Alternatives ($n > 3$)**:
 - Theorem 4.4 extends the identifiability to an arbitrary number of alternatives $n$. It achieves this by recognizing that the parameters $(\mu, \Sigma)$ for the full $n$-item model are uniquely identifiable from the collection of all 3-item sub-probit models (each identifiable via Theorem 4.1).
 - The proof involves showing how scaling factors for 3-item sub-matrices can be determined, and then how individual entries of $\Sigma$ (e.g., $\Sigma_{ii}, \Sigma_{ij}$) and $\mu$ (e.g., $\mu_i - \mu_j$) can be uniquely derived by aggregating information across different 3-item subsets, ultimately leading to the full $(\mu, \Sigma)$ matrix.

#### 3.3. Finite-Sample Guarantees (Estimation Algorithm)

The theoretical identifiability is complemented by a statistically and computationally efficient estimation algorithm with finite-sample guarantees:
- **Observability Assumption**: The algorithm requires an Assumption 5.1 (Observability), which states that for any three alternatives $i,j,k$, the probability $P\{i > j > k\}$ is bounded below by a constant $\gamma > 0$. This prevents degenerate cases where preferences are overwhelmingly skewed, making estimation impossible.
- **Estimation for Three Alternatives**: Theorem B.1 details an algorithm to estimate $(\mu, \Sigma)$ for $n=3$ from $N$ samples of three-way preferences. It extends the identifiability logic by incorporating concentration inequalities (Hoeffding's inequality, Lemma B.4) to bound the estimation error of $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_{ij}$ (Lemmas B.7, B.8). It shows that estimates converge to the true parameters with high probability given a sufficient number of samples.
- **Efficient Estimation for $n$ Alternatives**: For the general $n$-item setting, a key challenge is the naive approach would require estimating $O(n^3)$ 3-item sub-matrices. To reduce this, Lemma C.1 proposes constructing a sparse "sub-graph" of 3-item tuples, requiring only $O(n^2)$ unique 3-item experiments. This sub-graph is designed to remain connected and have a small (logarithmic) diameter, which is crucial for aggregating information efficiently.
- **Convex Optimization**: The global $(\mu, \Sigma)$ parameters are then recovered by solving a convex optimization problem that enforces consistency across the $O(n^2)$ estimated 3-item sub-matrices while adhering to normalization constraints.
- **Error Bounds**: Theorem 5.2 provides finite-sample guarantees, showing that the proposed polynomial-time algorithm returns estimates $(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\Sigma})$ that are $\varepsilon$-close to the true parameters in $\ell_\infty$ norm, with high probability, given $N \ge C n^2 \varepsilon^{-2} \gamma^{-24} \log(n/\delta) \log^6(n/(\gamma\varepsilon))$ observations.
- **Statistical Lower Bound**: Theorem 5.3 establishes a statistical lower bound of $\Omega(n^2 \varepsilon^{-2} \log(1/\delta))$ samples for any successful estimator. This demonstrates the near-optimality of the proposed algorithm in terms of sample complexity with respect to $n$, $\varepsilon$, and $\delta$.

#### 3.4. Experimental Validation

The theoretical guarantees are empirically validated using both synthetic and real-world datasets:
- **Datasets**: Synthetic data (sampled from probit models), sushi preference dataset, eigen-taste joke dataset, Netflix prize, and MovieLens datasets.
- **Evaluation Metrics and Task**: Models predict preferences over two alternatives given four as context. Accuracy is the primary metric.
- **Models Compared**: Logit, matrix completion, probit (trained with pairwise comparisons), and probit (trained with best-of-three observations). A "direct" method (computing desired quantities directly on the training set) serves as a sanity check.
- **Analysis**: Beyond accuracy, the experiments investigate the structure of learned covariance matrices and the impact on welfare maximization problems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Statistical Barriers and Opportunities

- **Insufficiency of Pairwise Data**: The research formally establishes that the conventional paradigm of pairwise preference data is fundamentally insufficient for uniquely identifying the parameters (mean vector $\mu$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$) of a correlated probit model (Theorem 3.2). This finding explains the historical lack of robust statistical and computational guarantees for learning such models from widely used data types.
- **Sufficiency of Best-of-Three Data**: In contrast, the paper demonstrates that collecting best-of-three preference data (i.e., observing the ranked order of three alternatives) provides sufficient information to overcome these identifiability shortcomings. It is shown to be both necessary and sufficient for learning correlated utility models.

#### 4.2. Theoretical Guarantees for Identifiability and Estimation

- **First Identifiability Guarantees**: The paper provides the first formal identifiability guarantees for the correlated probit model.
 - For the case of three alternatives ($n=3$), it establishes that the model parameters $(\mu, \Sigma)$ are uniquely identifiable from the three-way observation probabilities (Theorem 4.1) after appropriate normalization. This is achieved by projecting the utility space, transforming it to an isotropic distribution, and then identifying key mean and correlation parameters from specific probability events.
 - This is extended to an arbitrary number of alternatives $n$, demonstrating that the full $n$-item model parameters are uniquely identifiable from the collection of three-way observation probabilities across all possible triplets of alternatives (Theorem 4.4).
- **Statistically and Computationally Efficient Estimator**: An algorithm is devised that leverages the identifiability results.
 - For $n=3$, an estimator is presented that, given enough samples, returns accurate estimates of the model parameters (Theorem B.1).
 - For the general $n$-item setting, the algorithm constructs a sparse "sub-graph" of $O(n^2)$ triplets (Lemma C.1) to avoid the cubic complexity of considering all possible triplets. It then uses convex optimization to aggregate information from the individually estimated 3-item sub-models into a coherent estimate for the full $n$-item model.
 - The estimator is proven to be statistically and computationally efficient, providing $\ell_\infty$ error bounds for both $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\Sigma}$ (Theorem 5.2). It requires $N \geq C n^2 \varepsilon^{-2} \gamma^{-24} \log(n/\delta) \log^6(n/(\gamma\varepsilon))$ observations of rank-3 permutations to achieve an accuracy of $\varepsilon$ with probability $1-\delta$, where $\gamma$ is an observability constant.
- **Near-Optimal Sample Complexity**: A statistical lower bound is established (Theorem 5.3) indicating that any successful estimator for learning these models requires at least $\Omega(n^2)$ unique experiments (and thus samples), demonstrating the near-optimality of the proposed estimator.

#### 4.3. Empirical Validation

The theoretical findings are supported by experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets:
- **Synthetic Data Performance**: On synthetic data sampled from a probit model, the best-of-three probit model accurately recovers the ground truth covariance matrices and matches the performance of a "direct" method (which has access to the underlying utility distributions). In contrast, the probit model trained with only pairwise comparisons frequently learns non-existent correlations in uncorrelated settings or is sensitive to initialization in correlated settings (Table 1, Figure 2).
- **Real-World Data Performance**: Across various real-world datasets (sushi, jokes, Netflix, MovieLens), the probit model trained with best-of-three observations consistently shows improved prediction accuracy compared to the probit model trained with pairwise observations. It often matches or slightly outperforms logit and matrix completion models, particularly when modeling complex feature embeddings (Table 2). The gains are especially notable in settings where inherent correlations are expected.
- **Meaningful Learned Correlations**: The models successfully learn interpretable correlations. For example, in movie datasets, strong positive correlations are observed between sequels (e.g., Spider-Man (2002) and Spider-Man 2 (2004), or Kill Bill Vol. 1 (2003) and Vol. 2 (2004)). Negative correlations are found between critically acclaimed films and certain blockbusters, or between different genres. In the sushi dataset, preferences for certain types (e.g., sea urchin) are identified as divisive, and specific negative correlations are found (e.g., cucumber sushi preference negatively correlates with fatty tuna sushi preference) (Table 3, Figure 7).
- **Impact on Welfare Maximization**: In a welfare maximization experiment on the sushi dataset, the best-of-three probit model suggests different optimal menus compared to the logit model. For example, for a size-2 menu, the logit suggests toro (fatty tuna) and maguro (tuna), while the probit suggests uni (sea urchin) and maguro. This difference arises because the probit model recognizes that items like toro and maguro are highly correlated (if you like one, you likely like the other), whereas uni has high variance and correlates negatively with toro and maguro. By modeling these correlations, the probit model identifies a more diverse and broadly satisfying menu for the population (Figure 3), highlighting the practical value of correlated preference modeling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a fundamental contribution to the field of choice modeling by addressing the longstanding limitations of the Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) assumption, particularly in the context of correlated probit models.

The key significance and potential impact include:

- **Establishing Theoretical Foundations**: The paper delivers the first formal identifiability guarantees for correlated probit models from preference data. This theoretical underpinning establishes that learning such models is fundamentally possible, providing a basis for future algorithmic and application development.
- **Challenging Conventional Data Collection Paradigms**: By rigorously demonstrating the statistical insufficiency of traditional pairwise preference data, the research advocates for a shift towards higher-order preference data, specifically best-of-three comparisons. This has significant implications for how preference data is collected in various domains, suggesting deliberate amendments to data pipelines to capture richer user insights.
- **Enabling More Accurate Preference Modeling**: The ability to capture correlations between item utilities allows for a more fine-grained and accurate representation of human preferences. This moves beyond the simplifying IIA assumption, which often provides a coarse approximation, towards models that can account for context, user segments, and interdependent choices.
- **Impact on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**: This work has direct relevance to RLHF, particularly for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) and other AI systems with human values. By using correlated reward models, LLMs can potentially develop more personalized and nuanced responses that reflect the complex, often inter-dependent nature of human preferences. This could lead to AI systems that are more genuinely helpful, harmless, and aligned with diverse human needs.
- **Improved Personalization and Recommendation Systems**: In applications like recommender systems (e.g., for movies, music, products), correlated utility models can lead to more effective personalization. Understanding how preference for one item correlates with another allows for better predictions of future choices and the generation of more diverse and satisfying recommendations, as demonstrated by the welfare maximization experiments.
- **Advancing Economic and Transportation Modeling**: Beyond AI, fields such as econometrics and transportation science, which heavily rely on discrete choice models, can benefit. More accurate modeling of correlations can improve predictions of consumer behavior, travel mode choices, and the impact of new policies or infrastructure.
- **Foundation for Advanced Applications**: The authors note that modeling preference correlations is "essential for enabling more advanced applications like in-context learning and the strategic sampling of alternatives." This implies the work opens doors for developing AI agents that can learn preferences more efficiently from limited interactions or actively query for the most informative types of feedback.

In summary, this research not only addresses a fundamental theoretical gap in preference modeling but also offers a practical and empirically validated approach to building more sophisticated, personalized, and context-aware reward models, with broad implications for AI alignment and various other domains.
