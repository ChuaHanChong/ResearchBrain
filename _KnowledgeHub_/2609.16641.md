# SAVLA: Symmetry-Aware Vision-Language-Action Models for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> SAVLA is a symmetry-aware vision-language-action model that keeps a pretrained GR00T N1.5 vision-language backbone frozen and adds an SO(3)-equivariant flow-matching action head fed by typed invariant and equivariant conditions, plus a learned homography canonicalizer for oblique camera views. On LIBERO it lifts average success by 5.1 points over GR00T N1.5 and raises LIBERO-Goal success under scene rotation from 41.5% to 90.4%.

## Problem

- VLAs learn spatial competence purely from demonstrations with no architectural rotational-symmetry prior, so they stay reliable only within the scene poses their demonstrations cover.
- Spatial-reasoning VLAs such as SpatialVLA inject 3D position encodings and adaptive action grids, but nothing constrains the resulting policy to be rotation-equivariant.
- Equivariant generative policies like EquiBot and ET-SEED rely on 3D point-cloud inputs, and full-network equivariance is impractical for multi-billion-parameter pretrained RGB backbones, blocking direct VLA integration.

## Method

- ==Equivariant flow-matching action head==: each token splits into scalar (type-0) and vector (type-1) tracks, preserved by ==Vector Neuron== projections, equivariant RMS norm, rotation-invariant attention scores, and a parallel-component GELU FFN.
- ==Typed conditioning interface==: frozen VLM tokens serve as approximately invariant type-0 conditions, while third-person patch centers back-projected onto the tabletop become rotated type-1 reference points paired with each visual token.
- ==Learned canonicalizer==: a 0.09M-parameter CNN scores eight candidate gravity-axis rotations, a circular mean yields the scene angle, and a tabletop-plane homography warps the oblique image into canonical view; trained self-supervised.

## Results

- On LIBERO (three seeds), SAVLA beats a parameter-matched GR00T N1.5 on all four suites, averaging **91.6%** vs **86.5%**, with largest gains on Goal (**+5.9**) and Long (**+12.6**).
- Under ±30° scene rotation, mean success rises from **40.5%** to **81.8%** (Spatial) and **41.5%** to **90.4%** (Goal), beating re-rendered rotation augmentation (**74.5%**) on Goal without rotated demonstrations.
- Ablations confirm both components are needed: without canonicalization SAVLA drops to **46.0%**, and GR00T plus canonicalizer reaches only **63.5%**; the advantage grows to **+8.8** points at **10%** data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Equivariance need not reach the backbone: treating frozen, non-equivariant foundation-model features as invariant, plus typed geometric conditions, lets the action head enforce exact symmetry.
> - Canonicalization and architectural equivariance are complementary, not alternatives: normalize inputs where the network cannot be made equivariant, and build equivariance by construction where it can.
> - Approximate canonicalization shifts the bottleneck to image geometry: parallax and field-of-view errors from warping, not angle estimation, limit gains; symmetry priors also barely help long-horizon compounding errors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have become a central approach for language-conditioned robotic manipulation. These systems generally combine a pretrained vision-language model, which processes images and instructions, with an action-generation module that predicts robot commands. Early systems such as RT-1, RT-2, and OpenVLA generated discretized action tokens, whereas more recent approaches, including π0, SmolVLA, and GR00T, use diffusion or flow-matching action heads to generate continuous action sequences.

A persistent limitation of standard VLAs is their sensitivity to changes in object arrangement, camera viewpoint, and robot pose. Although visual observations and language instructions contain geometric information, conventional VLAs usually learn spatial transformations implicitly from demonstrations. They therefore tend to perform reliably only over the range of configurations represented in the training data. This is particularly problematic when a scene is rotated or viewed from a novel orientation.

The paper addresses this issue through geometric symmetry, specifically rotational equivariance. An equivariant policy should produce correspondingly rotated actions when the input scene is rotated. Previous work has incorporated symmetry into diffusion policies, point-cloud policies, and SE(3)-equivariant trajectory models. However, these methods often rely on 3D point clouds or require equivariant processing throughout the entire network. Such requirements are difficult to reconcile with large pretrained RGB vision-language backbones, which are generally not designed to be equivariant.

SAVLA occupies an intermediate position between these approaches. It retains a frozen, non-equivariant VLA backbone but adds symmetry to the action-generation stage. It also uses learned canonicalization to transform oblique-view images into a common reference frame. The central design principle is to treat the pretrained backbone’s features as approximately invariant after canonicalization, while explicitly representing geometric information through equivariant vector channels.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to improve the robustness and data efficiency of VLA policies under scene rotations without modifying or retraining the pretrained vision-language backbone. The authors focus on three related problems.

First, they seek to address the lack of an explicit rotational prior in ordinary VLAs. A policy trained on demonstrations with a limited range of object and robot orientations may fail when the same task is presented at a different scene angle. The model may recognize the objects and instruction semantically but still generate actions inappropriately because it has not learned the relevant geometric transformation.

Second, the authors aim to preserve the benefits of large pretrained vision-language models. Replacing the entire backbone with a fully equivariant architecture would be computationally expensive and would discard representations learned from large-scale vision-language pretraining. SAVLA therefore introduces an interface between a frozen semantic backbone and an equivariant action head.

Third, the work investigates whether architectural symmetry can reduce dependence on demonstration quantity. If the model is constrained to respond consistently to rotations by construction, it should not need separate demonstrations for every possible scene orientation. This could be relevant to robotics, where collecting high-quality demonstrations across many viewpoints and configurations is costly.

The paper evaluates SAVLA along three dimensions: standard task success on the LIBERO benchmark, generalization to unseen rotations, and performance when the demonstration budget is reduced. A smaller real-robot evaluation is also used to examine whether the method transfers beyond simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

SAVLA is built on GR00T N1.5. Its vision-language backbone remains frozen, while a new trainable flow-matching action head and a learned canonicalization module are added. At each control step, the policy receives a third-person RGB image, a wrist-camera image, the robot’s proprioceptive state, and a language instruction. It produces a chunk of future actions rather than a single action.

The action head uses flow matching. During training, a ground-truth action chunk is linearly interpolated with Gaussian noise at a randomly selected flow time. The model learns the velocity that transports the noise sample toward the demonstrated action. At inference, it starts from Gaussian noise and performs four Euler integration steps to generate an action chunk of horizon 16.

#### 3.2. Equivariant Typed Representations

The action head separates features into two transformation types:

- **Type-0 or invariant channels** contain scalar features that do not change under rotation.
- **Type-1 or equivariant channels** contain three-dimensional vectors that rotate with the scene.

The raw robot state is converted into a typed representation. The state includes end-effector position, orientation, and gripper joint positions. Position is centered relative to the workspace rotation center. Orientation is converted from axis-angle form into a rotation matrix, from which two columns are retained. The gravity direction is also included. This produces four vector components and two scalar components.

The action representation contains a three-dimensional position increment, a three-dimensional incremental rotation vector, and a scalar gripper command. It is therefore represented as two vector tracks and one scalar track.

Vector features are embedded using Vector Neuron operations, which mix feature channels while preserving the spatial dimensions and their rotational behavior. Scalar features use ordinary multilayer perceptrons.

#### 3.3. Symmetry-Preserving Action Head

Each action-head block contains normalization and modulation, self- and cross-attention, and a feed-forward network.

For scalar channels, the model uses LayerNorm and conventional modulation. For vector channels, it uses an equivariant RMS normalization based on vector magnitudes. Modulation changes vector magnitudes but not their directions, so it preserves equivariance.

The attention mechanism operates separately on scalar and vector tracks. Query, key, and value projections use ordinary linear operations for scalar features and Vector Neuron operations for vector features. Attention scores combine scalar inner products with vector inner products. Since inner products are invariant under rotations, the attention weights remain unchanged when inputs are rotated. Applying these shared invariant weights to vector values yields equivariant outputs.

The vector feed-forward network also avoids standard elementwise nonlinearities, because applying functions such as GELU independently to vector coordinates would generally break rotational equivariance. Instead, the model predicts an equivariant direction, decomposes each vector into parallel and perpendicular components relative to that direction, applies GELU only to the parallel scalar component, and leaves the perpendicular component unchanged.

Scalar and vector tracks interact through symmetry-preserving operations. Vector norms can be supplied to the scalar track as invariant information, while scalar coefficients can rescale vector features without changing their transformation behavior. Because each layer preserves these transformation types, the action head is designed to be exactly SO(3)-equivariant.

#### 3.4. Typed Conditioning Interface

The frozen vision-language model is not itself equivariant. SAVLA therefore constructs a typed conditioning interface for its outputs.

The third-person image is first canonicalized, with the intention that the resulting visual features are approximately invariant to scene rotations. Wrist-camera observations and language instructions are treated as invariant conditions. These features populate the scalar conditioning track.

The model also creates an equivariant geometric track using fixed reference points. The centers of third-person image patches are back-projected through the camera onto the tabletop plane. Each reference point is then rotated according to the angle predicted by the canonicalizer. Third-person visual tokens are paired with these rotated three-dimensional locations, while wrist and language tokens receive zero vector features. The action head can thus use semantic information from the VLM together with explicit geometric coordinates.

#### 3.5. Learned Canonicalization

Canonicalization is required because scene rotation about the gravity axis does not correspond to a simple two-dimensional image rotation under an oblique camera. Rotating an image around its center would introduce geometric misalignment due to perspective and camera elevation.

SAVLA uses a homography derived from the tabletop plane, camera intrinsics, camera pose, and table geometry. This homography maps an observation at a rotated scene pose back toward a canonical view. A small three-layer CNN with global average pooling scores eight discrete candidate rotation angles. A circular mean of the candidate probabilities produces a continuous angle estimate.

The canonicalizer is trained self-supervised using the original demonstrations. A demonstration image is treated as a canonical frame, synthetically rotated by a random angle using the homography, and then passed through the scorer. The training objective encourages consistency between the predicted angle for the original image and the predicted relative angle for the synthetic image. A separate scalar calibration gain is estimated from 12 simulator frames rendered at known angles.

At inference, the estimated angle is used in two ways: it warps the third-person image before it is processed by the frozen VLM, and it rotates the geometric reference points supplied to the equivariant action head. The paper’s end-to-end rotational guarantee is approximate and concerns gravity-axis rotations, corresponding to an SO(2) subgroup of SO(3).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Standard LIBERO Performance

SAVLA was compared with a parameter-matched GR00T N1.5 baseline using the same frozen backbone, demonstrations, and checkpoint-selection procedure. SAVLA had 103.3 million trainable parameters, compared with 100.8 million for the adjusted GR00T head.

Across the four LIBERO suites, SAVLA achieved an average success rate of **91.6%**, compared with **86.5%** for GR00T N1.5, a gain of **5.1 percentage points**. The suite-specific results were:

- LIBERO-Spatial: 96.7% versus 95.3%.
- LIBERO-Object: 92.9% versus 92.4%.
- LIBERO-Goal: 97.5% versus 91.6%.
- LIBERO-Long: 79.3% versus 66.7%.

The largest improvements occurred on Goal and Long tasks. Long tasks remain more difficult because errors accumulate over extended, multi-stage sequences.

#### 4.2. Rotation Generalization

The principal evaluation rotated the entire scene about the gravity axis across 13 angles from −30 to +30 degrees. Models were trained only on the original dataset.

SAVLA substantially improved mean success under these rotations:

- Spatial increased from 40.5% for GR00T N1.5 to 81.8%.
- Object increased from 26.0% to 53.9%.
- Goal increased from 41.5% to 90.4%.
- Long increased from 22.7% to 25.4%.

The smaller gain on Long indicates that rotational consistency does not eliminate failures caused by long-horizon execution and task complexity.

On LIBERO-Goal, rotation augmentation of GR00T improved performance, but remained below SAVLA. Warped-image augmentation reached 69.1% over the main rotation range and 24.0% on extrapolation angles. Re-rendered augmentation reached 74.5% and 59.2%, respectively, whereas SAVLA reached 90.4% and 64.5%.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Error Analysis

Both major components were necessary. Adding canonicalization to the standard GR00T action head produced 63.5% on LIBERO-Goal, while removing canonicalization from SAVLA reduced performance to 46.0%, compared with 90.4% for the complete model.

Oracle experiments showed that replacing the estimated angle with the ground-truth angle increased performance only slightly, from 90.4% to 90.9%. Supplying a true canonical view increased performance to 97.3%, including 96.8% at angles of at least 25 degrees. This suggests that image-warping inaccuracies, including parallax and field-of-view changes, are more important than angle-estimation errors.

#### 4.4. Data Efficiency and Real-Robot Evaluation

With 10% of the demonstrations, SAVLA achieved 57.1% average success compared with 48.3% for GR00T. With 25%, the results were 71.9% versus 66.1%. Thus, the performance advantage increased as the demonstration budget decreased.

On a SO-101 robot, SAVLA was evaluated on pick-and-place, block stacking, and block sorting. It achieved an average success rate of 71.7%, compared with 61.7% for GR00T. Under rotations in the pick-and-place task, the mean success rate was 57.1% for SAVLA and 47.9% for GR00T.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SAVLA demonstrates a way to introduce geometric structure into a VLA without retraining or redesigning its large pretrained vision-language backbone. Its typed interface separates semantic features from explicit geometric information, while the equivariant flow-matching head constrains action generation to transform consistently under rotation.

The results indicate potential benefits for reducing demonstration requirements and improving robustness to viewpoint and layout changes. The approach may also provide a general design pattern for combining frozen foundation models with structured downstream modules.

However, the scope of the claims is limited. The canonicalizer handles only rotations about the gravity axis, although the action head itself is SO(3)-equivariant. The experiments therefore do not establish robustness to arbitrary three-dimensional rotations, translations, scale changes, or substantial viewpoint changes. In addition, canonicalization depends on known camera and tabletop geometry, and image-warping errors become more significant at larger angles. The real-robot evaluation is also relatively small, with 20 episodes per task and three tasks.

Overall, the paper’s contribution is an architectural strategy for combining pretrained semantic representations with explicit rotational symmetry. Its findings support the use of equivariant action generation and canonicalized visual conditioning as complementary mechanisms for improving VLA robustness and data efficiency, while also identifying image geometry and broader three-dimensional generalization as remaining challenges.
