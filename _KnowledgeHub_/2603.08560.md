# CONTACT: CONtact-aware TACTile Learning for Robotic Disassembly

> [!summary] Summary
> This research systematically investigated the role of tactile sensing in robotic disassembly, comparing different tactile representations against vision-only approaches in both simulated and real-world tasks. It was found that a compact tactile force-field representation consistently improved disassembly success rates, particularly in contact-rich and visually challenging scenarios, whereas naive multimodal fusion of heterogeneous tactile inputs degraded performance.

## Problem

- Robotic disassembly tasks, crucial for recycling and remanufacturing, inherently involve complex contact-rich interactions, making them challenging for traditional manipulation systems.
- Vision-only policies often fail to capture subtle force interactions and critical contact dynamics, leading to robustness issues in scenarios with tight tolerances, changing resistance, or deformable components.
- A systematic comparison of different tactile observation representations (e.g., high-resolution images vs. compact force fields) and their effectiveness in structured robotic disassembly was lacking.

## Method

- Developed a ==structured suite of disassembly tasks==, including five rigid-body tasks in simulation and five real-world tasks with deformable components, progressively increasing in physical complexity.
- Integrated ==multimodal sensing==, combining front-view and wrist-mounted cameras with a ==GelSight R1.5 tactile sensor==, from which two distinct tactile representations (==TacRGB== and ==TacFF==, a tactile force field) were derived.
- Trained visuotactile policies using a ==Diffusion Policy== framework via supervised imitation learning, systematically comparing Vision Only, Vision + TacRGB, and Vision + TacFF configurations.

## Results

- **Vision + TacFF** consistently achieved the highest success rates in simulation, with its advantage increasing with geometric constraints (e.g., **86.5%** for loose socket vs. **44.3%** for tight socket compared to Vision Only's **81.7%** and **36.9%**).
- In real-world tasks, **TacFF** policies generally outperformed Vision Only and Vision + TacRGB, achieving **95.0%** success for a loose socket task (R1) vs. **80.0%** for Vision Only, and **55.0%** for a vertical clip task (R5) vs. **15.0%** for Vision Only.
- Under dim lighting conditions, tactile sensing acted as a stabilizing signal in contact-dominated tasks (R5), with Vision + TacFF maintaining **55.0%** success while Vision Only's performance sharply degraded from **15.0% to 0.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Tactile sensing's utility in robotic disassembly is task-dependent, becoming critical in scenarios with tight tolerances, asymmetric resistance, deformable components, or degraded visual information.
> - Compact force-field tactile representations (TacFF), which encode spatially distributed shear and normal forces, consistently outperform high-dimensional tactile imagery (TacRGB) and vision-only approaches in contact-dominated tasks.
> - Naive fusion of heterogeneous sensory inputs (e.g., combining TacRGB and TacFF directly) can degrade policy performance, suggesting a need for more sophisticated multimodal integration strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation encompasses a wide range of tasks, from simple pick-and-place operations to complex contact-rich interactions. Traditional pick-and-place tasks often rely on free-space motion and precise geometric positioning. However, tasks such as robotic disassembly, which are crucial for recycling, maintenance, and remanufacturing, inherently involve breaking existing contact constraints, overcoming frictional resistance, and disengaging interlocking geometries. These characteristics make disassembly fundamentally dependent on detecting force-dependent state transitions and modulating forces effectively.

Classical model-based approaches, exemplified by successes in structured insertion tasks like peg-in-hole assembly, depend on accurate modeling of geometry and contact conditions. In real-world disassembly, the variability in object geometry, wear conditions, and material properties often limits the robustness of such analytical methods.

Recent advancements in learning-based policies, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) and diffusion-based policies, have shown promise in structured manipulation tasks. RL has been applied to force-sensitive disassembly, enabling robots to learn complex contact interactions through trial and error. Diffusion-based policies, which model action generation as a denoising process, allow for flexible multi-modal conditioning and have been extended to disassembly scenarios involving compliant objects and language guidance.

Despite these advancements, many existing robotic manipulation systems primarily rely on visual perception. While high-capacity visual encoders and large-scale datasets have improved the capabilities of vision-only policies, visual observations alone may not capture subtle force interactions and critical contact transitions, especially in scenarios with tight tolerances, changing resistance, or deformable components. These unobserved contact dynamics can compromise the robustness of manipulation policies.

Tactile sensing has emerged as a complementary modality for contact-dominated manipulation, with recent work integrating tactile feedback into learning-based policies to enhance robustness. Various tactile observation representations exist, from high-resolution deformation images (e.g., GelSight RGB images, TacRGB) to compact force-field encodings derived from optical flow and depth reconstruction (e.g., TacFF). While these representations have been explored in general manipulation contexts, their comparative effectiveness in the specific domain of structured robotic disassembly, particularly under varying contact complexities and physical conditions, has remained an area requiring systematic investigation. This paper contributes to this gap by directly comparing the utility of different tactile representations against vision-only approaches within a unified framework for disassembly tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were to systematically investigate the role of tactile sensing in robotic disassembly and to evaluate the effectiveness of different tactile representations. The study aimed to address two central questions:

1. **When does tactile sensing significantly improve performance over vision alone in robotic disassembly tasks?**
 - **Motivation:** While vision is a dominant modality in robotics, its limitations in contact-rich or occluded scenarios are recognized. Disassembly inherently involves precise contact interactions, friction, and force-dependent state changes that might not be fully observable through vision. Understanding the specific conditions—such as increasing geometric constraints, asymmetric resistance, or the presence of deformable components—under which tactile feedback becomes crucial is essential for designing robust robotic disassembly systems.
2. **Which tactile representation is most effective for contact-rich disassembly?**
 - **Motivation:** Tactile sensors can provide diverse information, ranging from high-resolution deformation patterns (TacRGB) to structured representations of distributed forces (TacFF). Each representation encodes contact information differently, and their relative advantages for the specific challenges of disassembly, such as identifying slip, precise alignment under friction, or detecting material deformation, are not well-established. A systematic comparison is necessary to determine if high-dimensional, image-like tactile data or a more compact, structured force-field encoding offers superior performance in disengagement tasks.

To achieve these objectives, the researchers were motivated to develop a structured evaluation framework that spanned both simulated and real-world environments. This framework allowed for a systematic increase in task complexity, moving from simple loose extractions to scenarios involving tight tolerances, interlocking constraints, and deformable object interactions. By evaluating different sensing configurations (Vision Only, Vision + TacRGB, Vision + TacFF) under identical learning conditions, the study sought to provide direct empirical evidence regarding the task-dependent utility and optimal representation of tactile sensing in robotic disassembly. The long-term motivation includes enhancing robotic capabilities for applications like recycling, maintenance, and remanufacturing, where robust disassembly is a critical bottleneck.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a structured evaluation framework across both simulation and real-world settings to systematically compare the effectiveness of different sensing modalities for robotic disassembly. The core of the approach involved training visuotactile policies using a unified learning framework based on Diffusion Policy.

**A. Disassembly Task Suite Design:**
The study designed two sets of disassembly tasks with progressively increasing physical complexity to analyze sensing modality contributions under varying conditions.

- **Simulation Tasks (S1-S5):** Five rigid-body tasks were implemented in IsaacGym using TacSL for visuotactile rendering. These tasks focused on isolating structural aspects of disassembly under controlled physical assumptions (no deformation or material compliance modeling).
 - **S1 (Vertical Pull, Loose Socket):** A basic extraction task with generous clearance.
 - **S2 (Vertical Pull, Tight Socket):** Reduced tolerance, requiring more precise alignment and friction awareness.
 - **S3 (Loose Plug with Lid):** A multi-stage task involving disengaging a lid before extraction.
 - **S4 (Vertical Pull, Flat Barb):** Introduced directional resistance via a flat barb, requiring sliding before pulling.
 - **S5 (Vertical Pull, Spike Barb):** A spike-shaped barb demanding careful motion to avoid collision during extraction.
- **Real-World Tasks (R1-R5):** Five tasks were designed for a physical Franka Emika Panda manipulator, incorporating realistic friction variability and deformable components.
 - **R1 (Vertical Pull, Loose Socket) & R2 (Vertical Pull, Tight Socket):** Analogous to S1 and S2, but with real-world contact variability.
 - **R3 (Loose Plug with Lid):** Similar to S3, but with the actual object size.
 - **R4 (Vertical Push Tab):** Involved compressing a deformable tab prior to release, introducing compliance-dependent state transitions.
 - **R5 (Vertical Clip):** An elastic clip mechanism requiring controlled deformation and localized force application to disengage a hook.

**B. Multimodal Observations:**
The robotic system integrated visual and tactile sensing.
- **Visual Input:** Provided by a front-view camera (global context) and a wrist-mounted camera (local gripper observations). Both captured RGB images.
- **Tactile Sensing:** Obtained from a GelSight R1.5 sensor mounted on the end-effector finger. Two distinct representations were derived:
 - **TacRGB:** High-resolution RGB images representing surface deformation.
 - **TacFF (Tactile Force Field):** A compact, structured representation computed from optical flow and depth reconstruction. It consisted of a 10x14 grid encoding spatially distributed shear components (direction and magnitude as arrow length/direction) and normal force (color, green to red).
- **Robot State:** End-effector 3D position and 4D quaternion orientation.

All modalities were synchronized at 10 Hz. RGB images were resized to 240x320, TacRGB cropped to 240x160, and TacFF was a 10x14 grid with 3 channels (shear-x, shear-y, normal).

**C. Vision–Tactile Policy Learning:**
The policy learning framework was based on **Diffusion Policy**, trained via supervised imitation learning.
- **Observation History:** Policies conditioned on a two-step observation history `O_t = {O_{t-1}, O_t}`.
- **Feature Encoding:** Each image-like modality (front view, wrist view, TacRGB, TacFF) was encoded using a ResNet-18 backbone. End-effector pose history was appended to the concatenated visual and tactile features.
- **Sensing Configurations:** Separate policies were trained for three main sensing configurations:
 1. **Vision Only:** Uses `I_front`, `I_wrist`, `p_t`.
 2. **Vision + TacRGB:** Uses `I_front`, `I_wrist`, `I_tac`, `p_t`.
 3. **Vision + TacFF:** Uses `I_front`, `I_wrist`, `I_ff`, `p_t`.
- **Action Space:** A 5D relative command: 3D position increments (`∆x, ∆y, ∆z`), yaw rotation increment (`∆θ_z`), and gripper command (`g`).
- **Diffusion Model:** A 1D U-Net parameterized the denoising network to generate an action sequence with a horizon of H=16. During inference, DDIM sampling with K=10 denoising steps produced action chunks, with the first 8 actions executed before replanning.

**D. Experimental Setup and Evaluation:**
- **Simulation:** Object and socket positions were randomized within ±5 cm in x–y, and in-hand orientations perturbed by up to 10 degrees. Success was defined by full extraction above a height threshold. Success rates were averaged over the final 10 training epochs across three random seeds, with 50 distinct environment initializations per epoch (1500 rollouts/task).
- **Real-World:** Experiments used a Franka Emika Panda manipulator. 50 demonstrations per task were collected via teleoperation with randomized object poses. Policies were trained for 400 epochs. Each policy was evaluated over 20 independent trials.
- **Additional Experiments:**
 - **Dim Lighting:** Conducted for R1 and R5 (50 demos, 400 epochs training, 20 rollouts evaluation) to assess robustness under degraded visual perception.
 - **Combined Tactile Modalities:** An additional configuration (Vision + TacRGB + TacFF) was evaluated on R3 and R5 to investigate naive fusion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the role of tactile sensing and its representation in robotic disassembly, across both simulation and real-world environments.

**A. Simulation Results:**
- **Overall Performance and Geometric Constraints:** TacFF consistently achieved the highest success rates across all five simulated rigid-body tasks. The performance advantage of TacFF became more pronounced with increasing geometric constraints. For Task S1 (loose socket), Vision Only achieved 81.7%, while Vision + TacFF reached 86.5%, a marginal gain. However, for Task S2 (tight socket), Vision Only dropped to 36.9%, whereas Vision + TacFF significantly improved to 44.3%. This suggests that TacFF's low-dimensional normal and shear force cues assist in resolving contact ambiguities under tighter tolerances.
- **Asymmetric Resistance and Interlocking Constraints:** In tasks S4 and S5, involving flat and spike-shaped barb structures requiring specific sliding motions, TacFF effectively captured shear patterns, leading to higher success rates (78.8% for S4, 61.9% for S5) compared to Vision Only (74.6% for S4, 59.5% for S5). Notably, Vision + TacRGB slightly underperformed Vision Only in S5 (57.8% vs. 59.5%), indicating that high-resolution deformation images might introduce task-irrelevant variability when structured force cues are more critical for directional escape motions.

**B. Real-World Results:**
- **Success Rate:** Trends observed in simulation generally extended to physical hardware. TacFF consistently achieved the strongest performance across real-world tasks. For R1 (loose socket), Vision + TacFF achieved 95.0% success, compared to 80.0% for Vision Only. In more contact-sensitive and deformable scenarios like R4 (vertical push tab) and R5 (vertical clip), TacFF policies demonstrated 75.0% and 55.0% success, respectively, outperforming Vision Only (70.0% for R4, 15.0% for R5). TacRGB did not consistently improve performance over Vision Only; for instance, it achieved only 5.0% in R4 and 15.0% in R3, lower than Vision Only. This indicated the importance of structured force feedback over purely visual input, especially when visual cues are ambiguous or occluded by real-world contact uncertainties.
- **Execution Time:** TacFF policies generally exhibited longer average execution times for successful rollouts (e.g., 58.0 s for R5 vs. 28.3 s for Vision Only). This was attributed to TacFF providing fine-grained force feedback, enabling the policy to actively perform multiple corrective adjustments and explore contact states before successful disassembly. Vision-only and Vision + TacRGB policies, lacking precise force information, were more likely to terminate unsuccessfully when initial attempts failed, resulting in shorter average times for successful trials.

**C. Robustness and Modality Analysis:**
- **Dim Lighting Condition:** Experiments under dim lighting revealed task-dependent tactile contributions. For Task R1 (geometry-dominant), all configurations experienced a decrease in success rate, with Vision + TacFF dropping from 95.0% to 25.0%. In contrast, for Task R5 (contact-driven), Vision Only performance degraded sharply (from 15.0% to 0.0%), while Vision + TacRGB and Vision + TacFF remained comparatively stable. Vision + TacFF maintained its normal lighting performance of 55.0% under dim conditions. These results suggest that tactile sensing acts as a stabilizing signal in contact-dominated tasks, providing reliable information when visual signals are compromised.
- **Modality Variations (Naive Fusion):** Evaluation of a combined Vision + TacRGB + TacFF configuration on Tasks R3 and R5 showed a significant decrease in performance. Success rates dropped to 0.0% for R3 and 20.0% for R5, substantially lower than those achieved by Vision + TacFF alone (45.0% and 55.0%, respectively). This finding suggests that simple concatenation of heterogeneous sensory inputs does not inherently improve performance and can dilute task-relevant signals, preventing the policy from focusing effectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes several contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in the domain of disassembly.

Firstly, the research introduces a **structured set of disassembly task suites**, encompassing both rigid and deformable interactions with progressively increasing contact complexity in both simulation and real-world environments. This suite provides a standardized benchmark for evaluating the performance of robotic manipulation policies under conditions that range from simple geometric alignment to complex force-dependent state transitions, which is crucial for advancing research in contact-rich tasks.

Secondly, the study offers a **systematic comparison of visual and tactile representations** within a unified policy framework across diverse tasks. By directly contrasting Vision Only, Vision + TacRGB, and Vision + TacFF, the research provides empirical evidence on their relative effectiveness. The findings indicate that **compact force-field representations (TacFF) consistently outperform high-dimensional tactile imagery (TacRGB) and vision-only approaches** in contact-dominated and deformable disassembly scenarios. This highlights the importance of not just having tactile data, but having it represented in a structured and task-relevant manner.

Thirdly, the research identifies **task-dependent modality effects**. It demonstrates that while tactile sensing offers marginal gains in geometry-dominant tasks with sufficient visual cues, its contribution becomes critical in scenarios with tight tolerances, asymmetric resistance, or deformable components, and particularly when visual information is degraded (e.g., dim lighting). This insight is significant for guiding the design of perception systems, suggesting that selective and context-aware integration of modalities can yield more robust performance.

Finally, the finding that **naive multimodal fusion of heterogeneous tactile representations can degrade performance** (Vision + TacRGB + TacFF underperforming Vision + TacFF) underscores a critical aspect of multi-modal learning. It indicates that merely increasing observation dimensionality does not guarantee improved performance and points to the necessity of more principled and sophisticated multimodal integration strategies, such as modality-aware attention mechanisms or specialized encoders, to effectively leverage diverse sensory data without diluting task-relevant signals.

The potential impact of this work extends to enhancing the capabilities of robotic systems in practical applications such as **recycling, maintenance, and remanufacturing**. By improving the robustness and reliability of robotic disassembly, this research could contribute to greater automation in these industries, leading to increased efficiency, reduced waste, and more sustainable production cycles. Furthermore, these findings contribute to a deeper understanding of human-like manipulation capabilities for contact-rich tasks, informing the development of more dexterous and adaptable robots.
