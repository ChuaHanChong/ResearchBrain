# A Wearable Pneumatic Device for Continuous, Closed-Loop, Bidirectional Tactile Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> A wearable, untethered pneumatic haptic system in which the same TPU-coated nylon textile pouch both senses and displays normal force, each channel closing its own pressure loop with a dedicated proportional piezoelectric valve, pressure sensor, and microcontroller. Two devices pair wirelessly so gripper contact pressure is mirrored on the operator's fingertips, cutting applied pressure up to 23.1% during teleoperation.

## Problem

- Haptic displays for humans and tactile sensors for robots are engineered separately, so what the robot records never matches the modality the operator actually feels.
- Sensory-substitution cues such as vibration encode force in an unrelated channel, poorly representing continuous magnitude or compliance, raising cognitive load and degrading demonstration data.
- Compact pneumatic haptics stayed open-loop because compressors and valve banks are bulky, giving only transient pressure pulses and rapid leak-down instead of sustained regulated contact force.

## Method

- Per-channel ==closed loop==: each textile pouch pairs a ==proportional piezoelectric valve==, pressure sensor, and dedicated microcontroller running ==PI control==, scaling to twelve channels without a central bottleneck.
- Onboard CO2 cartridge or wall air plus die-cast ==TPU-coated ripstop nylon pouches== remove the compressor tether, letting one channel serve as sensor, actuator, or both.
- **Twin-system configuration** wirelessly mirrors pressure sensed at the gripper fingers onto fingertip pouches with a linear gain of four, using no intermediate modality encoding.

## Results

- Device holds regulated pressure for **30 minutes** unsupplied, reaches -3 dB force bandwidth of **34.2 Hz**, **76.2 N** peak force, **39 ms** settling, **64 ms** device-to-device latency.
- Across **25 participants**, force discrimination hit **92.2%** and **92.1%**; mediated stiffness matched the hand (**91.4%** vs **90.1%**), while teleoperated three-weight ordering fell to **53.8%** versus **100%**.
- Feedback cut applied pressure **19.2%**, **23.1%**, and **7.2%** across three pick-and-place views, sped PnP1 and PnP3 by **21.8%** and **25.6%**, and lowered mental demand **18.1%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Using one physical modality for both sensing and display removes the encode-decode gap, so operators interpret contact directly instead of learning an arbitrary substitution mapping.
> - Distributing valve, sensor, and controller per channel beats centralized regulation: local loops scale, stay wearable, and sustain force where open-loop pneumatics only pulse.
> - Signal fidelity alone is insufficient; perceptual accuracy collapses as concurrent motor control and memory load grow, so evaluate feedback under realistic task demands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The human sense of touch is characterized by its inherent bidirectionality, involving a continuous physical response to felt sensations. In the context of teleoperation, this two-way exchange is recognized for its potential to enhance performance by reducing task completion times, improving manipulation accuracy, and decreasing applied forces. However, practical implementations often separate these two directions: haptic displays are typically designed for human operators, while tactile sensors are developed for robots. This functional and often modal mismatch between what a robot senses and what an operator feels can constrain the effectiveness of teleoperation systems.

Current approaches to haptic feedback for operators broadly categorize into kinesthetic and cutaneous. Kinesthetic feedback, which conveys force to muscles and joints through world-grounded interfaces, often faces challenges in teleoperation due to stability issues with hard contacts, stiff environments, and communication latency. Cutaneous feedback, delivered directly to the skin, offers advantages such as wearability, freedom from workspace constraints, and intrinsic stability. Within cutaneous feedback, sensory substitution, where force is encoded into abstract cues like vibration or sound, is a common but often problematic approach. Such cues may increase cognitive load due to the need for operators to learn arbitrary mappings, and they can poorly encode continuous quantities like force magnitude or compliance, leading to noisy data for learning-based robotic systems. Local skin deformation feedback, which provides stimulation akin to real-world contact, is considered more suitable for dexterous manipulation requiring low-force interactions.

On the robot side, tactile sensing aims to measure contact attributes such as geometry, force, texture, and slip. Existing technologies include vision-based sensors that image elastomer deformation and dense electronic arrays. A reciprocal tension exists between spatial resolution and speed; high-resolution sensing generates large data streams, while faster sampling often necessitates coarser spatial resolution. Consequently, the tactile data stream is often simplified to a single scalar (e.g., normal force, slip) and mapped to a different modality for the operator, such as fingertip vibration, creating a modality mismatch.

Pneumatic systems offer a unique potential to address this mismatch because the same inflatable textile pouch can both display normal force through direct skin indentation and measure it via its internal pressure. This modality also naturally reproduces the growth of contact area with force, a key cue for softness discrimination. However, a significant historical challenge for pneumatics has been the bulkiness of external compressors and valves, confining closed-loop control to benchtop settings or leading to open-loop control in untethered devices, which limits stable, precise, and sustained feedback crucial for realistic perception and data collection.

This work addresses these limitations by developing a compact, wearable pneumatic device that unifies tactile sensing and haptic feedback within a single physical modality. It aims to overcome the control hurdles of prior pneumatic systems, offering a solution for continuous, closed-loop, bidirectional tactile interaction that supports both human-in-the-loop control and data collection for learning-based robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop and validate a wearable pneumatic haptic device capable of continuous, closed-loop, bidirectional tactile interaction at perceptually relevant force and temporal scales. This objective is motivated by several identified limitations in current haptic and teleoperation systems:

1. **Bridging the Sensing-Actuation Modality Gap:** Traditional teleoperation systems often utilize disparate modalities for robotic tactile sensing and human haptic feedback. The motivation was to overcome this mismatch by designing a system where the same physical mechanism—a textile pneumatic pouch—serves as both sensor and actuator. This direct mapping is intended to reduce information loss and cognitive load by providing a natural, interpretable form of feedback.
2. **Achieving Compact, Untethered Closed-Loop Pneumatic Control:** Prior pneumatic haptic devices either relied on bulky, tethered external compressors and valves for stable closed-loop control, or operated in an untethered but open-loop manner, leading to transient and unstable pressure outputs. A key motivation was to integrate proportional valves, pressure sensors, and microcontrollers directly into a compact, wearable device, enabling local closed-loop pressure regulation without external tethers. This would allow for sustained, stable skin indentation feedback crucial for realistic tactile perception.
3. **Enabling Real-Time Bidirectional Tactile Communication:** The researchers aimed to develop an architecture that supports wireless pairing of identical devices to transmit and reproduce tactile pressure signals in real time. This capability is motivated by the need for tight temporal coupling in bilateral and cooperative haptic interactions, including haptic mirroring in teleoperation, which is essential for tasks requiring fine force regulation and continuous contact information.
4. **Improving Teleoperation Performance and Reducing Cognitive Load:** A significant motivation was to demonstrate that high-fidelity, pressure-based fingertip feedback could enhance human performance in teleoperated manipulation tasks. This includes reducing applied forces to objects, decreasing task completion times, and lowering the operator's mental workload, particularly under visually constrained conditions. Such improvements are critical for applications like remote manipulation of delicate objects or surgical procedures.
5. **Providing a Foundation for Learning-Based Robotics:** The unification of tactile sensing and feedback in a single modality, coupled with the ability to record synchronized tactile pressure data, is motivated by the need for rich, consistent training data for imitation and diffusion learning in robotics. By capturing interpretable contact information alongside visual and proprioceptive data, the device aims to facilitate the development of more robust learned policies.
6. **Validating Perceptual Interpretability:** The work sought to confirm that the pressure-based fingertip feedback delivered by the device is perceptually interpretable, enabling human users to accurately discriminate fundamental tactile properties such as force, stiffness, and weight. This validation is critical for ensuring that the provided haptic cues are effectively perceived and utilized by operators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted approach involving device design, engineering characterization, and human-subjects studies to achieve its objectives.

#### 3.1. Device Design and Architecture

The core of the system is a wearable, untethered pneumatic device that integrates sensing and actuation capabilities within a single textile-based pouch. Key design choices include:
- **Modality Unification:** Each channel in a device consists of a textile-based pneumatic pouch that can function as a sensor, an actuator, or both. The internal pressure of the pouch serves as both the sensed and actuated variable. The pouches are fabricated from TPU-coated nylon using die casting.
- **Distributed Closed-Loop Control:** To overcome the limitations of prior pneumatic systems, each pouch is paired with a proportional piezoelectric valve, a pressure sensor (TE Connectivity MS583702BA), and a dedicated microcontroller (Nordic Semiconductor nRF5340). This distributed architecture allows for local, independent closed-loop pressure regulation for each channel.
- **Integrated Air Supply:** The valve and air supply (CO2 cartridge or wall air) are integrated onto the device body, removing the need for external compressors and tethers typically associated with closed-loop pneumatic haptics. The device can support up to twelve channels.
- **Wireless Communication:** A system microcontroller coordinates multiple channels and handles wireless communication via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE). This allows two identical devices to be wirelessly paired, enabling real-time transmission and reproduction of sensed pressure signals.

#### 3.2. System Software and Control

- **Local PI Control:** Each channel's microcontroller implements proportional-integral (PI) control for closed-loop pressure regulation. Pressure sensor readings are sampled continuously (approx. 500 Hz) to track desired pressure setpoints.
- **Asymmetric Valve Control:** Separate controllers are tuned for the inlet and exhaust piezo valve channels due to their asymmetric dynamic responses.
- **Anti-windup Mechanisms:** Four mechanisms—sign-change reset, integral clamping, soft leak decay, and setpoint change-based resets—are implemented to prevent integral wind-up.
- **Central Communication Layer:** A central communication layer manages setpoint updates and data logging, ensuring synchronization across channels and enabling wireless data exchange between paired devices.

#### 3.3. Device-Level Characterization

A series of engineering tests were conducted to quantify the device's performance:
- **Airtightness and Pressure Retention:** The device's ability to maintain pressure was evaluated by fully inflating actuators, disconnecting the external air supply, and monitoring pressure decay over time.
- **Flow Rate:** Inlet and outlet flow rates were estimated by measuring pressure changes in a fixed volume chamber.
- **Force Bandwidth:** The frequency-dependent force response of the textile actuator was characterized under blocked conditions by sweeping input frequencies from 1 to 100 Hz.
- **Maximum Output Force:** Peak force was measured under blocked conditions at maximum input pressure (2650 mbar).
- **Vertical Displacement under Load:** Actuator deformation was measured under varying pressures and applied loads using a vertical test stand with a digital sliding caliper.
- **Step Response:** The transient behavior was assessed by measuring rise and settling times in response to a step input (1050 mbar to 2000 mbar).
- **End-to-End Latency:** The delay between a pressure event sensed on one wirelessly connected device and the corresponding actuation response on a second device was measured.

#### 3.4. Human-Subjects Studies

User studies were conducted with 25 participants (19 for teleoperation tasks due to exclusions) to evaluate the perceptual interpretability of feedback and its impact on teleoperation performance. The protocol was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board.

- **Perception Tasks:** Five tasks were designed to assess discrimination of force, stiffness, and weight, progressing from direct haptic feedback alone to robot-mediated interaction:
 1. **Direct Force Discrimination:** Discriminating between 150, 300, and 450 mbar delivered directly to fingertips.
 2. **Direct Stiffness Discrimination:** Manual interaction with three soft cubes (0.630, 1.77, 3.35 N/mm).
 3. **Haptic-Mediated Stiffness Discrimination:** Robot gripper interacting with the same cubes, feedback conveyed via fingertip pouches.
 4. **Direct Weight Discrimination:** Manually lifting 150g, 300g, and 450g blocks.
 5. **Teleoperated Weight Discrimination:** Participants controlled a Kinova Gen3 7 DoF arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper (equipped with textile sensors) to lift weights, relying on haptic feedback from corresponding fingertip pouches (mapped with a linear scaling factor of 4).
- **Teleoperation System:** Robot position was controlled using wrist and finger tracking via a Microsoft HoloLens 2. Gripper opening was mapped to thumb-index finger distance after calibration.
- **Teleoperation Evaluation Tasks (Pick-and-Place):** Participants performed three pick-and-place (PnP) tasks using a hackey sack under different visual conditions (with and without haptic feedback):
 1. **PnP1 (Direct View):** Unobstructed view of the task.
 2. **PnP2 (Obstructed View):** Partially occluded view.
 3. **PnP3 (Camera View):** Relying solely on a wrist-mounted camera feed.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Quantitative:** Fingertip pressure signals, task completion time, gripper path length.
 - **Qualitative:** NASA Task Load Index (TLX) for perceived workload (Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, Frustration) and System Usability Scale (SUS) for perceived usability.
- **Statistical Analysis:** Paired t-tests were used to compare conditions with and without haptic feedback, consistent with prior work.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's findings are presented in two main categories: device-level characterization and human-subjects evaluation.

#### 4.1. Device-Level Characterization

- **Airtightness and Pressure Retention:** The device exhibited extended pressure retention, decreasing from 2650 mbar to 2300 mbar after 5 minutes and remaining above 1700 mbar after 16 minutes, fully depressurizing after 30 minutes. This contrasts with comparable compact pneumatic systems that showed rapid pressure loss within seconds.
- **Flow Rate:** Average inlet and outlet flow rates were 10.8 L/min and 13.03 L/min, respectively, which are four to five times higher than other reported pneumatic controller flow rates. These rates support rapid pressurization and depressurization.
- **Force Bandwidth:** The textile actuators maintained a -3 dB force response up to 34.2 ± 1.2 Hz, exceeding many other untethered pneumatic haptic systems (which often roll off below 15 Hz). This range is suitable for conveying dynamic tactile cues relevant to human perception (5 Hz to 50 Hz).
- **Maximum Output Force:** The device produced an average peak force of 76.2 ± 2.8 N, which is within the range for reliable normal-force perception and indentation-based tactile studies.
- **Vertical Displacement under Load:** Displacement increased non-linearly with pressure and inversely with external load. Even at the highest tested load, the actuator achieved displacements exceeding 5 mm.
- **Step Response:** The controller showed a rapid and well-damped response, with an average 10–90% rise time of 29 ms and a settling time of 39 ms.
- **End-to-End Latency:** The average sensing to actuation latency between two wirelessly connected devices was 64 ms. This value is below commonly cited perceptual thresholds (80–100 ms) for tactile feedback to be perceived as immediate.

#### 4.2. Human-Subjects Studies

- **Haptic-Enabled Discrimination:**
 - **Force Discrimination:** Participants achieved high accuracy in discriminating discrete force levels delivered directly by the device: 92.2% for 2-element and 92.1% for 3-element comparisons, indicating reliable perception of force magnitude.
 - **Stiffness Discrimination:** Performance was comparable between direct manual interaction and haptic-mediated teleoperation. For 2-element tasks, accuracy was 90.1% (by hand) and 91.4% (through device). For 3-element tasks, it was 96.7% (by hand) and 90.8% (through device), suggesting high fidelity in preserving stiffness information.
 - **Weight Discrimination:** Performance diverged between conditions, particularly for 3-element tasks. Accuracy was 95.7% (by hand) and 87.5% (teleoperation) for 2-element tasks. For 3-element tasks, it was 100% (by hand) but dropped to 53.8% (teleoperation). This reduction suggests that the added cognitive and motor complexity of teleoperated weight discrimination challenged perceptual encoding and recall.

- **Improved Teleoperation Performance and Reduced Mental Load:**
 - **Applied Pressure:** Across all three pick-and-place (PnP) tasks, participants consistently applied less pressure with haptic feedback: 19.2% reduction in PnP1, 23.1% in PnP2, and 7.2% in PnP3. This indicates gentler interaction with the object.
 - **Task Efficiency:** Task completion time improved in PnP1 (21.8% faster) and PnP3 (25.6% faster), with corresponding reductions in path length (14.6% and 28.9% shorter). Improvements in PnP2 were minimal. Speed gains in PnP1 and PnP3 were attributed to faster object transport, while grasp initiation speed remained unchanged.
 - **Mental Workload (NASA TLX):** TLX scores decreased across four of six subscales with haptic feedback. Mean Mental Demand decreased by 18.1% (from 47.6 to 39.0), and Frustration decreased by 14.4% (from 52.9 to 45.3), with the latter being statistically significant (p = 0.012, d_z = 0.65). Participants with longer task completion times showed more pronounced reductions in Mental Demand (34.3%), Performance (37.2%), and Frustration (29.7%).
 - **System Usability Scale (SUS):** Mean SUS scores increased from 61.1 ± 14.6 (No Haptics) to 64.9 ± 16.3 (Haptics), a 3.8-point improvement, though not statistically significant (p = 0.090, d_z = -0.41).

- **Exploratory Human Communication Task (Supplemental Information):** In a pilot study with five participants, location and number of active actuators were identified with 100% accuracy. Intensity classification across three levels achieved approximately 90% accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a wearable pneumatic device that offers continuous, closed-loop, bidirectional tactile interaction, addressing key limitations in the field of haptics and teleoperation. The significance of this work stems from its unified approach to tactile sensing and haptic display within a single, compact, and untethered platform.

The device's technical capabilities represent an advancement over prior pneumatic systems. Its ability to maintain regulated pressure for extended durations (30 minutes), achieve a high force bandwidth (34.2 Hz), deliver substantial peak force (76.2 N), and exhibit a rapid, well-damped step response (39 ms settling time) enables stable and continuous fingertip indentation at perceptually relevant scales. The low end-to-end latency of 64 ms further supports real-time bilateral interactions. These characteristics are crucial for applications requiring prolonged contact and fine force regulation, where transient or open-loop pneumatic feedback has historically been insufficient.

The human-subjects studies demonstrate that the device provides perceptually interpretable feedback. Participants consistently discriminated force and stiffness with high accuracy, often comparable to direct manual interaction. This suggests that direct, pressure-based indentation cues are effective for conveying continuous contact information and material properties, potentially requiring less cognitive processing than abstract vibrotactile feedback. While weight discrimination showed a performance decrease in complex teleoperated conditions, this highlights the interplay between signal fidelity and higher-level cognitive demands during active robot control, rather than a deficiency of the haptic signal itself.

The application of this device in teleoperated manipulation tasks yielded measurable performance improvements. Consistent reductions in applied force (up to 23.1%) and increases in task efficiency (up to 27.4% faster) were observed, particularly under conditions of visual constraint. The statistically significant decrease in subjective mental demand (18.1%) and frustration (14.4%) further indicates that direct haptic feedback can substantially lower cognitive effort for operators, making teleoperation tasks less taxing and potentially more sustainable. This suggests a shift from merely increasing data yield in teleoperation to also improving the quality and interpretability of individual demonstrations.

A notable impact lies in the device's potential to enhance data collection for learning-based robotic systems. By unifying sensing and actuation in the same modality, the twin-system configuration allows for synchronized recording of tactile pressure data during teleoperated manipulation, regardless of the feedback condition. This capability provides a foundation for treating tactile pressure as a primary modality in learning from demonstration, enabling the training of policies that integrate contact information alongside visual and proprioceptive data, potentially leading to more robust and dexterous robot behaviors.

While the study acknowledges limitations, such as occasional technical interruptions and participant conflation of robot and haptic system latency, the overall work establishes a practical foundation for bidirectional tactile communication. It opens avenues for future research in tactile interaction, teleoperation, and data-driven learning from touch by offering a platform that combines the benefits of soft pneumatic actuation with stable, closed-loop control and modality-matched bidirectional communication. The device's wearable, untethered nature expands the practical contexts in which such advanced tactile feedback can be deployed.
