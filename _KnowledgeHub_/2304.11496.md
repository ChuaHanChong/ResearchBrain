# AutoVRL: A High Fidelity Autonomous Ground Vehicle Simulator for Sim-to-Real Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> AutoVRL is an open-source high-fidelity autonomous ground vehicle simulator built on the Bullet physics engine, wrapping PyBullet with OpenAI Gym and Stable Baselines3 for sim-to-real deep RL. It ships GPS, IMU, ray-traced LiDAR and camera models, a digital twin of the XTENTH-CAR Ackermann vehicle, and five 3D environments. Trained search and racing policies transferred to hardware at 24.2% peak CPU.

## Problem

- Optimal deep RL policies for ground vehicle navigation need days or weeks of continuous training, making real-world-only learning prohibitively slow and expensive.
- Real-world training reinforces behavior through repeated collisions, risking physical vehicle damage and incurring costs that scale with task complexity.
- Prior MATLAB and Simulink reinforcement learning workflows are computationally demanding and poorly suited to real-time control of physical ground vehicles.

## Method

- Three-layer architecture: ==PyBullet== handles high-fidelity actions and observations, ==OpenAI Gym== mediates episode termination and API calls, ==Stable Baselines3== in PyTorch performs policy updates.
- Sensor suite implements GPS and IMU via PyBullet base-state queries, ==LiDAR== via a batched ==ray-tracing== model with derived hit-point Euclidean ranges, and camera via view and projection matrices.
- Actuation converts normalized policy outputs to clipped throttle and steering angle, adding drag-constant friction and per-joint acceleration integration for higher-fidelity vehicle dynamics.

## Results

- Soft Actor-Critic search agent trained **50,000,000** steps over **9.5** days in the 20m outdoor map, with moving-average return rising quickly then fluctuating around **3.9**.
- The search policy transferred zero-shot to an unseen 50m urban environment, producing collision-free trajectories exploring shaded regions beneath buildings and parked vehicles.
- PPO racing agent trained **10,000,000** steps over **1.4** days reached return **4.6**; on-vehicle Jetson AGX Orin used **24.2%** CPU and **17.0%** memory with vision enabled.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Matching software stacks between simulator and robot, here Python on Ubuntu, lets sim-to-real transfer reduce to swapping the physics bindings for a hardware interface.
> - Inference cost and training cost decouple sharply: a policy needing days of GPU training runs under 10 MB at a fraction of embedded compute.
> - Reward shaping dominates behavior quality here; collision-free but non-time-optimal racing lines show safety penalties can suppress the aggressive maneuvers optimality requires.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous ground vehicles (AGVs) capable of navigating complex and unstructured environments is a significant area of research. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising technique for achieving cognitive AGV navigation, particularly for tasks that require decision-making based on raw sensor data without reliance on a-priori maps or Global Positioning Systems (GPS). This capability is crucial for applications in hazardous, dynamically changing, or information-poor environments, such as disaster zones, subterranean exploration, or extraterrestrial planetary missions.

However, a primary impediment to the real-world implementation of DRL for AGVs is the substantial training time required. Learning an optimal DRL policy can extend over days or weeks for complex tasks, often involving numerous interactions with the environment, including collisions. Conducting such extensive training in the real world is economically prohibitive, time-consuming, and carries risks of physical damage to the robotic hardware.

This challenge highlights the critical need for effective "sim-to-real" transfer, where DRL policies trained in a simulated environment can be reliably deployed on physical AGVs. Bridging this simulation-to-reality gap necessitates simulators that offer high physical accuracy, realistic sensor models, and a software architecture compatible with real-world robotic systems. While various proprietary and open-source simulation tools exist—such as Bullet, Gazebo, MuJoCo, and Unreal Engine—researchers continue to seek platforms specifically tailored for high-fidelity sim-to-real DRL in AGV applications, emphasizing ease of use, extensibility, and direct compatibility with common robotic hardware interfaces.

AutoVRL addresses this specific gap by presenting an open-source, high-fidelity simulator built upon established physics engines and DRL frameworks. It positions itself as a tool designed to enable cost-effective and safe learning of DRL policies by replicating real-world AGV behaviors and sensory inputs in a virtual environment. The work builds upon the understanding that previous DRL investigation tools, such as those relying on MATLAB, can be computationally demanding and less optimal for real-time control, prompting a shift towards more efficient, Python-based platforms compatible with Ubuntu and standard robotic operating systems. By focusing on a compatible software architecture (Ubuntu with Python interface, facilitating ROS integration), AutoVRL aims to streamline the transfer of learned policies from simulation to physical hardware, thereby advancing the practical deployment of DRL in AGV robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and present AutoVRL, an open-source, high-fidelity simulator specifically designed to facilitate sim-to-real Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) research and development for Autonomous Ground Vehicle (AGV) applications. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

Firstly, a significant motivation stems from the inherent challenges of DRL training for AGVs. Developing an optimal DRL policy typically requires extensive training durations, spanning days or weeks for complex tasks. This process often involves frequent exploratory actions, including collisions with the environment, which are essential for reinforcing positive application-specific behaviors. Performing such training directly with real-world AGVs is financially expensive, resource-intensive, time-consuming, and poses a risk of damaging valuable hardware. The simulator aims to mitigate these drawbacks by providing a safe and cost-effective virtual environment for iterative policy learning.

Secondly, the researchers are motivated by the necessity for cognitive AGV navigation in environments where traditional localization and mapping techniques are unreliable or unavailable. These include information-poor or dynamically altering scenarios such as rescue operations in natural disaster zones, mapping unexplored subterranean environments, and extraterrestrial planetary exploration. In such contexts, AGVs must operate solely based on raw sensor data, without dependence on a-priori maps or GPS. DRL offers a solution for such intelligent decision-making, and AutoVRL is intended to serve as a platform to train and evaluate DRL agents for these critical applications.

Thirdly, effectively bridging the "simulation-to-real-world gap" is a core motivation. For DRL policies learned in simulation to be successfully deployed on physical AGVs, the simulator must accurately mimic real-world physics, sensor characteristics, and actuator responses. AutoVRL aims to achieve this by employing a high-fidelity physics engine (Bullet) and providing realistic sensor implementations and AGV models, thereby enabling the learning of policies that are robust and transferable to hardware.

Fourthly, the project is motivated by the desire to provide an open-source, accessible, and extensible platform for the research community. By releasing AutoVRL as open-source, the authors intend to lower the barrier to entry for DRL research in AGVs, allowing other researchers to readily utilize, customize, and expand the simulator with new AGV models, environments, and DRL algorithms. This fosters collaborative development and accelerates advancements in the field.

Finally, the design of AutoVRL is motivated by the need for a software architecture that facilitates straightforward sim-to-real transfer. By utilizing Python bindings (PyBullet), OpenAI Gym, and Stable Baselines3 within an Ubuntu environment, the simulator's framework closely mirrors the software stacks commonly found on real-world AGVs (e.g., using ROS for hardware interfaces). This architectural consistency is intended to simplify the deployment of trained policies onto physical robots by minimizing modifications required for the real-world hardware interface.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AutoVRL is designed as an open-source, high-fidelity simulator for sim-to-real AGV DRL research, built upon a modular architecture comprising three core components: PyBullet, OpenAI Gym, and Stable Baselines3 (SB3).

**Architecture and Data Flow:**
The foundation of AutoVRL is the **Bullet physics engine**, accessed via its **PyBullet** Python bindings. This component handles the high-fidelity simulation of rigid-body dynamics, executing actions and generating sensor observations. The **OpenAI Gym** library provides a standardized Application Programming Interface (API) that acts as an intermediary, enabling communication between DRL algorithms and the simulation environment. **Stable Baselines3 (SB3)**, a collection of state-of-the-art DRL algorithms built on the PyTorch Machine Learning framework, is used to implement and train the DRL agents. The modular design allows for SB3 to be replaced by alternative DRL libraries or custom algorithms developed in PyTorch or TensorFlow.

The data flow within AutoVRL is structured as follows: At each time step, the DRL algorithm (from SB3) strategizes goal-specific actions based on the reward function. These actions are then communicated to the PyBullet simulation environment via the OpenAI Gym API. PyBullet executes these actions, updating the simulated AGV's state. The updated observations from the physics engine are relayed back to Gym, which then verifies episode termination conditions. This information, including observations, rewards, and termination status, is transmitted to the SB3 DRL algorithm for learning and policy updates.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer Mechanism:**
A key aspect of AutoVRL's methodology is its approach to sim-to-real transfer. The policies learned within the simulator are designed to be applied to a real-world AGV by maintaining an identical software architecture. This involves replacing the PyBullet functions responsible for action control inputs and sensor observations with the AGV’s hardware interface, such as the widely used Robot Operating System (ROS) framework. The simulator’s operation on Ubuntu with a Python interface is intended to ensure compatibility with common AGV software environments, simplifying the transition from virtual to physical hardware.

**AGV and Environment Models:**
High-fidelity AGV and environment models are generated from 3D CAD data using Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) files. These XML-based files contain physical descriptions of sub-components, including dimensions, joint types, colors, and base positions. AutoVRL includes a digital twin of the XTENTH-CAR, a 1/10th scale Ackermann steered vehicle, which shares hardware and software similarities with the full-sized X-CAR experimental vehicle, facilitating DRL research for both all-terrain and on-road autonomous vehicles.
The simulator provides five pre-configured environments for training and evaluation: two outdoor environments (20m x 20m and 50m x 50m) with objects like trees and boulders, two urban environments (20m x 20m and 50m x 50m) with buildings and passenger vehicles, and an oval race track. The use of URDF files allows for easy extensibility, enabling users to enlarge existing environments, add new objects, or generate application-specific scenarios (e.g., indoor or subterranean) from open-source or custom CAD models.

**Sensor Implementations:**
AutoVRL is equipped with implementations of key AGV sensors:
- **GPS**: Global position (Cartesian x, y, z) and orientation (quaternion, converted to Euler angles roll, pitch, yaw) are extracted using PyBullet's `getBasePositionAndOrientation` and `getEulerFromQuaternion` functions.
- **IMU**: Linear and angular velocities (ẋ, ẏ, ż and ϕ̇, θ̇, ψ̇) in Cartesian global coordinates are acquired using `getBaseVelocity`. Linear and angular accelerations are then computed from this velocity information and the sampling time.
- **LiDAR**: Simulated using ray tracing. The origin coordinates of the rays are calculated based on the LiDAR's position relative to the AGV's body frame and the AGV's yaw. Endpoints of the rays are generated based on a maximum ray length and an angular segmentation. The `rayTestBatch` function obtains hit positions for each ray, from which the Euclidean distance to detected obstacles is determined.
- **Camera**: Images are rendered using PyBullet’s OpenGL GPU visualizer and CPU renderer. The `computeViewMatrix` and `computeProjectionMatrixFOV` functions specify the synthetic camera, and `getCameraImage` generates RGB images, a depth buffer, and a segmentation mask buffer with unique object IDs.

**Actuation:**
DRL algorithm outputs, normalized in the range [-1, 1], are converted into vehicle control inputs: throttle (T in) and steering angle (δ in [δ_min, δ_max]). These control inputs are applied to the simulated AGV using the `setJointMotorControlArray` PyBullet function, with the number of affected joints depending on the AGV model (e.g., two for steering, four for wheel speed in an Ackermann steered vehicle). To enhance fidelity, resistive forces are modeled. Friction is computed based on drag constants and the joint speed from the previous time step. Joint acceleration is determined from the throttle input, a throttle constant, and the resistive force, which is then used to update the current joint speed.

**DRL Training and Evaluation:**
The effectiveness of AutoVRL was evaluated through two distinct applications:
1. **Exploration and Search**: An AGV was trained to explore shaded regions with poor aerial visibility to detect subjects of interest. The **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)** algorithm, an off-policy DRL algorithm, was used. A custom reward function (`R_search`) was formulated to encourage obstacle avoidance, promote exploration near obstacles, and prevent repetitive motion. Training was conducted for 50,000,000 steps over 9.5 days in a 20m x 20m outdoor environment, and the learned policy was then evaluated in a 50m x 50m urban environment.
2. **Autonomous Racing**: An AGV was trained for autonomous racing on an oval track. The **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm, an on-policy DRL algorithm, was employed. A conservative reward function (`R_racing`) was designed to encourage safe, time-optimal trajectories. Training was performed for 10,000,000 steps over 1.4 days.

Training for both applications was performed on an Intel Core i9 13900KF CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. For real-world performance assessment, policies trained in simulation were implemented on a physical XTENTH-CAR equipped with an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin System on Module (SOM), utilizing an AutoVRL ROS driver to apply control signals based on real-world sensor data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research details the successful development and evaluation of AutoVRL, demonstrating its capabilities as a high-fidelity simulator for sim-to-real DRL in AGV applications.

**Simulator Functionality and Extensibility:**
AutoVRL successfully integrates the Bullet physics engine via PyBullet, OpenAI Gym, and Stable Baselines3 (SB3) to provide a functional and customizable environment for DRL training. The simulator includes a digital twin of the XTENTH-CAR AGV and five diverse environments (outdoor, urban, oval racetrack). The use of URDF files facilitates extensibility, enabling the straightforward addition of new AGV models and modification or expansion of existing environments. This extensibility was demonstrated through the evaluation of learned policies in environments differing from their training environments.

**Exploration and Search Application Results:**
For the exploration and search task, an AGV was trained using the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm with a custom reward function designed to promote exploration in obstacle-dense terrains and avoid collisions.
- **Learning Curve**: During training in the 20m x 20m outdoor environment, the 100,000 moving average return initially increased rapidly, then fluctuated around a value of 3.9 over 50,000,000 training steps (approximately 9.5 days). The gradual increase in the minimum average return suggests potential for further policy improvement with prolonged training.
- **Policy Performance (Simulation)**: Post-training, the AGV successfully strategized collision-free trajectories to locate subjects of interest underneath objects such as trees and boulders, which are typically unobservable by aerial surveillance. This was achieved solely using raw sensor data, without any prior map knowledge.
- **Policy Robustness and Extensibility**: When the trained agent was evaluated in a new 50m x 50m urban environment, which presented different map characteristics and objects (buildings, passenger vehicles), it demonstrated robustness. The agent was able to navigate efficiently and without collisions to explore shaded regions beneath urban structures and vehicles, indicating the policy's transferability across varied simulated environments. The authors suggest that longer training, tuned hyperparameters, and refined reward functions could lead to more efficient learning and complex, indefinite exploration trajectories.

**Autonomous Racing Application Results:**
For the autonomous racing application, the AGV was trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with a reward function aimed at promoting safe, time-optimal trajectories.
- **Learning Curve**: The 100,000 moving average return showed a rapid increase during the first 600,000 training steps, reaching a value of 4.6. Training continued for a total of 10,000,000 steps over 1.4 days.
- **Policy Performance (Simulation)**: The resulting trajectory at the end of training was collision-free. However, the agent was observed to consistently traverse the longer outside curve of the corners rather than optimizing for a time-optimal path that would involve switching lanes. This indicates that while safe navigation was achieved, further refinement of the reward function would be necessary to learn truly time-optimal racing strategies. The results, nonetheless, demonstrate AutoVRL's versatility across different AGV application types.

**Real-World Performance (Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
To assess sim-to-real transfer and real-time computational performance, the policies learned in simulation were deployed on a physical XTENTH-CAR equipped with an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin System on Module (SOM). An AutoVRL ROS driver facilitated the use of real-world sensor data to select actions based on the trained policy and apply control signals to the AGV's actuators in real-time.
- **Computational Efficiency**: The findings indicate that while DRL training can be computationally intensive, the real-time execution of a trained DRL policy is significantly less demanding.
- **Resource Usage**: The XTENTH-CAR's embedded computer utilized average CPU and memory percentages as follows:
 - Actuator Control, LiDAR Point Cloud Map (PCM), and DRL for navigation: 7.6% CPU and 12.6% Memory.
 - Actuator Control, PCM, Computer Vision (using Mask R-CNN), and DRL for navigation: 24.2% CPU and 17.0% Memory.
- **Policy Size and Transferability**: The trained policy itself was noted to be under 10 MB in size. The policies were observed to function seamlessly in both the physics engine (simulation) and on real-world hardware, affirming the simulator's capability to bridge the simulation-to-real-world gap and the practical viability of deploying these policies on embedded systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AutoVRL simulator represents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous ground vehicles (AGVs) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) research, offering several important implications and potential impacts.

Firstly, **enabling and accelerating DRL research for AGVs** is a primary significance. DRL policy development for AGVs often requires extensive training, which is costly, time-consuming, and potentially damaging in real-world scenarios. AutoVRL provides a high-fidelity, safe, and cost-effective virtual environment where DRL agents can undergo prolonged training without incurring physical damage or operational expenses. This allows researchers to iterate rapidly on algorithm design, reward function formulation, and hyperparameter tuning, thereby accelerating the pace of innovation in AGV DRL.

Secondly, AutoVRL directly addresses a major challenge in robotics: **bridging the simulation-to-real-world gap**. By incorporating a high-fidelity physics engine (Bullet), realistic sensor models (GPS, IMU, LiDAR, camera), accurate AGV digital twins (XTENTH-CAR), and a compatible software architecture (Ubuntu, Python, OpenAI Gym, Stable Baselines3, and an ROS-compatible interface), the simulator is designed to produce policies that are directly transferable to physical hardware. The demonstrated successful transfer of policies to the physical XTENTH-CAR underscores its efficacy in this regard, making DRL a more practical solution for real-world AGV applications.

Thirdly, the **open-source nature and extensibility** of AutoVRL are highly significant. By making the source code and installation guide publicly available, the authors contribute to the broader scientific community. This fosters transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative development. Researchers can readily utilize, customize, and extend the simulator with new AGV models, diverse environments, and novel DRL algorithms, promoting innovation and reducing duplicated effort across institutions.

Fourthly, the simulator's versatility, demonstrated through its application to distinct tasks like **exploration/search and autonomous racing**, highlights its broad utility. This indicates that AutoVRL can serve as a foundational platform for a wide array of AGV applications, from navigation in unstructured disaster environments to high-performance control tasks, without necessitating a complete redesign of the simulation infrastructure for each new problem.

Finally, the findings regarding the **computational efficiency of deployed DRL policies** on embedded hardware (NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin) are impactful. While training is resource-intensive, the ability of trained policies (under 10 MB) to execute with low CPU and memory usage in real-time on an AGV is crucial for practical deployment. This demonstrates the feasibility of integrating advanced DRL capabilities into resource-constrained robotic systems, expanding the potential for autonomous functionality in real-world operational settings. This paves the way for the deployment of DRL-driven AGVs in critical applications such as search and rescue, surveillance, and hazardous environment exploration, where reliance on pre-mapped environments or external positioning systems is not feasible. The simulator also lays a foundation for future research in areas like domain randomization, robust policy learning, and multi-robot collaboration within diverse simulated scenarios.
