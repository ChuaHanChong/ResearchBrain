# Strict Subgoal Execution: Reliable Long-Horizon Planning in Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Strict Subgoal Execution (SSE) is a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework that enables reliable long-horizon planning in sparse-reward goal-conditioned tasks. The method consistently achieves higher success rates and better sample efficiency than existing baselines, especially in complex multi-objective environments, by employing Frontier Experience Replay, decoupled exploration, and failure-aware path refinement.

## Problem

- Existing hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) methods often struggle with unreliable subgoal execution, where low-level policies fail to reach proposed intermediate goals.
- Conventional hindsight experience replay (HER) in HRL can misinform high-level policies, leading to inefficient planning with infeasible subgoals and diffuse credit assignment.
- Long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks pose significant challenges for exploration and require robust planning to achieve distant goals effectively.

## Method

- Introduced **Frontier Experience Replay** (FER) to strictly distinguish between successful, partial-success, and failed high-level subgoal executions, providing precise and consistent feedback.
- Developed a decoupled exploration strategy that combines an exploitation-focused policy with a dedicated novelty-seeking policy to ensure comprehensive coverage of the goal space.
- Integrated a failure-aware path refinement mechanism that dynamically adjusts graph edge costs based on observed low-level failure statistics, guiding high-level planning away from high-risk regions.

## Results

- SSE consistently surpassed all baseline HRL and graph-based methods in success rate and sample efficiency across nine diverse long-horizon tasks.
- The framework successfully solved complex multi-objective tasks, such as KeyChest and ReacherWallDoubleGoal, which proved challenging or unsolvable for existing methods.
- Ablation studies confirmed the vital contributions of Frontier Experience Replay, the decoupled exploration policy, and failure-aware path refinement to SSE's superior performance and efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly characterizing subgoal reachability through FER is critical for stable high-level policy training and reliable long-horizon planning, preventing the selection of infeasible subgoals.
> - A decoupled exploration strategy is essential to balance the conservatism from strict subgoal execution with comprehensive coverage of the goal space, facilitating the discovery of novel paths.
> - Incorporating failure statistics into graph planning significantly improves path reliability and overall task success by guiding the agent to avoid unstable or high-risk regions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved notable successes across various domains. However, standard RL approaches encounter challenges when adapting to new objectives without extensive retraining, particularly in tasks characterized by long horizons and sparse rewards. Goal-Conditioned RL (GCRL) was developed to enable agents to pursue diverse, specified goals by explicitly conditioning policies on target states. Despite this advancement, GCRL often struggles with exploration in sparse-reward, long-horizon environments due to the difficulty of obtaining positive feedback for distant goals.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) represents a significant paradigm in addressing long-horizon goal-conditioned tasks. HRL decomposes complex control problems into a high-level policy that proposes intermediate subgoals or skills, and a low-level policy that executes primitive actions to achieve these subgoals. This decomposition can facilitate learning by breaking down a difficult overall goal into a sequence of more attainable intermediate objectives. However, a persistent challenge in HRL is the degradation of performance when proposed subgoals are not reliably reachable by the low-level policy.

Graph-based HRL methods emerged to mitigate this issue by constructing a graph over the goal space. In these methods, nodes typically represent states or regions, and edges encode the feasibility or cost of transitioning between them. This graph structure supports subgoal selection through shortest-path planning algorithms, guiding the high-level policy towards more feasible sequences of subgoals. While these methods improve navigation and planning, scalability remains a concern, especially when the final goal is significantly distant from the agent's current state, as this may still necessitate many high-level decisions and lead to unstable training.

A common technique for improving sample efficiency in sparse-reward GCRL and HRL is goal relabeling, exemplified by Hindsight Experience Replay (HER). HER transforms unsuccessful trajectories into useful training signals by reinterpreting achieved states as if they were the intended goals. While beneficial for the low-level policy, the paper identifies that conventional hindsight relabeling can introduce issues at the high level in graph-based HRL. Specifically, if a subgoal is unreachable, HER might relabel intermediate visited states as successes, leading the high-level policy to repeatedly select ineffective or infeasible subgoals. This can result in unnecessarily long high-level trajectories and diffuse credit assignment, hindering stable and efficient learning in complex tasks. Some recent graph-based GCRL methods have attempted to simplify the problem by removing explicit hierarchical structures, directly training a low-level policy to reach the final goal. However, this simplification can reduce flexibility in environments requiring reasoning about multiple sequential goals or heterogeneous reward signals.

The present work, Strict Subgoal Execution (SSE), is positioned within this landscape to address the limitations of existing graph-based HRL, particularly regarding reliable subgoal execution and efficient high-level planning. SSE aims to explicitly characterize the reachability boundary of subgoals, thereby ensuring that the high-level policy selects only reliably achievable subgoals. It seeks to overcome the issues associated with conventional hindsight relabeling in HRL by introducing a novel experience replay mechanism and integrating exploration and path refinement strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework that achieves reliable long-horizon planning in goal-conditioned tasks, particularly those characterized by distant goals and sparse rewards.

The motivation for SSE stems from identified limitations in existing HRL and graph-based GCRL approaches:

1. **Subgoal Infeasibility and Inefficient High-Level Planning**: A core problem highlighted is that in many HRL frameworks, the low-level policy may fail to reach a proposed subgoal due to factors such as limited skill, environmental obstacles, or excessive distance. Traditional hindsight relabeling techniques, such as HER, mitigate sparse rewards by treating any achieved state along a failed trajectory as a successfully reached subgoal. While this provides dense rewards for low-level policy training, it can misinform the high-level policy. The high-level policy might consequently select subgoals that are repeatedly infeasible for the low-level policy to complete within its allotted horizon. This leads to the high-level policy proposing subgoals that it "believes" are reachable (due to relabeling), but which consistently result in low-level execution failure, creating inefficient, elongated high-level trajectories and hindering stable credit assignment.

2. **Unstable Training and Credit Assignment**: When the high-level policy repeatedly selects subgoals that the low-level policy cannot reliably execute, the resulting transitions can be inconsistent, making it difficult for the high-level policy to learn which subgoals lead to overall task success. Even if the agent eventually reaches the final goal, the credit for success is spread over many inefficient high-level steps, preventing effective reinforcement of earlier, critical decisions. This instability is exacerbated in long-horizon tasks where the final reward is sparse and delayed.

3. **Conservative Exploration and Goal Space Coverage**: While efforts to ensure subgoal feasibility might lead to more conservative high-level planning, this can inadvertently reduce the exploration of novel or challenging regions of the goal space. Agents might learn to avoid areas associated with past low-level failures, even if those areas are critical for reaching the ultimate goal. This can lead to suboptimal policies or an inability to solve tasks requiring traversal through difficult regions.

4. **Suboptimal Path Planning**: Graph-based methods typically use shortest-path algorithms (like Dijkstra's) based on learned or estimated edge costs. However, these costs may not fully capture practical failure cases such as collisions or the agent getting stuck. Following the "shortest" path could still lead the low-level policy into high-risk, failure-prone regions, undermining subgoal reliability and overall task success.

To address these motivations, SSE aims to:
- **Enforce Strict Subgoal Execution**: Update the high-level policy with positive returns only upon confirmed low-level success in reaching the assigned subgoal, thereby providing precise and consistent feedback.
- **Delineate Reachability Frontiers**: Distinguish between successful, partial-success, and failure transitions at the high-level to explicitly map out which subgoals are reliably achievable and where execution typically breaks down.
- **Promote Decoupled Exploration**: Introduce a dedicated exploration policy to systematically cover underexplored regions of the goal space, counteracting potential conservatism from strict subgoal execution.
- **Refine Path Planning with Failure Awareness**: Adjust graph edge costs based on observed low-level failure statistics, guiding the high-level planner to avoid unstable or high-risk transitions.
- **Improve Overall Efficiency and Success Rate**: Ultimately, by achieving the above, SSE seeks to solve a broader range of complex long-horizon tasks with higher success rates and improved sample efficiency compared to existing methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Strict Subgoal Execution (SSE) framework is a graph-based Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) approach designed to improve reliability and efficiency in long-horizon goal-conditioned tasks. Its methodology is centered on three main components: Frontier Experience Replay, Decoupled Exploration, and Failure-Aware Path Refinement, all operating within a structured graph over the goal space.

#### 3.1. Frontier Experience Replay (FER)

FER is a central mechanism in SSE that redefines how high-level transitions are recorded and used for policy updates. Unlike conventional hindsight relabeling, which often treats achieved states along a failed trajectory as successful goals, FER explicitly characterizes the outcome of a high-level subgoal attempt:
- **Success Transition**: If the low-level policy successfully reaches the assigned subgoal $\tilde{g}_t$ within a predefined threshold $\lambda$ (i.e., $\| \phi(s_{t'}) - \tilde{g}_t \| < \lambda$), a success transition $(s_t, g, \tilde{g}_t, \sum_{j=t}^{t'-1} r_j, s_{t'})$ is stored. Here, $s_{t'}$ is the state upon subgoal completion, and $\sum r_j$ is the cumulative reward. The episode then continues.
- **Stop-on-Failure Transition**: If the low-level policy fails to reach the subgoal within the threshold ($\| \phi(s_{t'}) - \tilde{g}_t \| \ge \lambda$), a failure transition $(s_t, g, \tilde{g}_t, 0, s_T)$ is stored. The return is zero, and the episode is immediately terminated, effectively penalizing the high-level policy for proposing unreachable subgoals and preventing future returns from being obtained from that path. $s_T$ represents a terminal state.
- **Partial-Success Transition**: In case of failure, if any intermediate waypoint ($wp_{final}$) along the planned path was reliably reached, a partial-success transition $(s_t, g, wp_{final}, \sum_{j=t}^{t_{wp}-1} r_j, s_{t_{wp}})$ is stored. This transition credits the high-level policy for the reliably executed portion of the path, localizing how far progress extended.
These three types of transitions in FER delineate the reachability frontier, provide consistent high-level training signals by strictly enforcing subgoal completion, and identify unreliable subgoals to be avoided.

#### 3.2. Decoupled Exploration for Goal Space Coverage

To counteract the potential conservatism introduced by strict subgoal execution and to ensure broad goal space coverage, SSE employs a decoupled high-level control scheme:
- **High-Level Policy ($\pi_h$)**: This policy is primarily responsible for exploitation and is trained on the FER buffer. It uses an $\epsilon$-greedy strategy, selecting the greedy subgoal $\tilde{g}_{max,t}$ (from a Q-network trained to maximize return) with probability $1-\epsilon$, or a uniformly sampled random subgoal $\tilde{g}_{rand}$ from the entire goal space $G$ with probability $\epsilon$. This allows $\pi_h$ to target diverse points regardless of the agent's current location.
- **Exploration Policy ($\pi_{exp}$)**: A complementary policy dedicated to promoting coverage of underexplored regions. $\pi_{exp}$ samples subgoals from three options with equal probability (1/3 each): the final goal $g$, the greedy subgoal $\tilde{g}_{max,t}$, and a novel subgoal $\tilde{g}_{novel}$ from an identified novel region $V_n$.
 - **Novel Region ($V_n$) Definition**:
 - **Grid-based (SSE-Grid)**: $V_n$ is defined as the grid cell $C_m^G$ with the minimum visitation count $N(C_m^G)$, promoting visits to the least explored areas of the discretized goal space.
 - **Model-based (SSE-Model)**: $V_n$ consists of nodes $v$ where a prediction network $f_\zeta$ (trained to predict a fixed target network $f_{\zeta_{targ}}$) exhibits high prediction error, consistent with Random Network Distillation (RND) principles.
- **Exploration Schedule**: The agent samples from $\pi_{exp}$ early in training (initial ratio 1:0) and gradually mixes it with $\pi_h$ using a ratio $\eta:(1-\eta)$, where $\eta$ controls the exploration strength and decays over time. Both $\pi_h$ and $\pi_{exp}$ operate under the FER mechanism.

#### 3.3. Failure-Aware Path Refinement

Graph-based planning relies on shortest paths, but these can lead to failure in regions prone to collisions or getting stuck. SSE introduces a failure-aware path refinement strategy to adjust graph edge costs:
- **Refined Edge Distance ($\tilde{d}$)**: For an edge from node $v_1$ to $v_2$, the distance is adjusted as $\tilde{d}(v_1 \rightarrow v_2) = d(v_1 \rightarrow v_2) \times \max(1, c_d \cdot \rho_f(v_2))$. Here, $d$ is the original edge distance (derived from low-level Q-values and Euclidean distance), $c_d > 1$ is a scaling factor, and $\rho_f(v_2)$ is the failure ratio for the target node $v_2$.
- **Failure Ratio ($\rho_f$) Definition**:
 - **Grid-based**: $\rho_f^{grid}(v_2) = N_{fail}(C_m^G) / N(C_m^G)$, where $v_2$ falls into cell $C_m^G$, $N_{fail}$ is the number of failures, and $N$ is the total visits in that cell.
 - **Model-based**: $\rho_f^{model}(v_2) = F_\xi(v_2)$, where $F_\xi$ is a failure predictor network trained with a binary cross-entropy loss to estimate the probability of failure at node $v_2$.
This refinement encourages Dijkstra's algorithm to plan paths that avoid frequently failing regions, enhancing subgoal reliability. This mechanism is activated only after a specified number of successful visits to ensure sufficient low-level policy competence.

#### 3.4. Graph Construction and RL Training

- **Landmark Node Set (V)**: SSE primarily uses a grid-based approach for constructing landmark nodes. The goal space $G$ is partitioned into cells of size $d_G$, and landmark nodes are placed at these grid intersections, enabling systematic exploration.
- **Edge Set (E) and Distance (d)**: The raw edge distance $d(v_1 \rightarrow v_2)$ is defined as a hybrid of the low-level value function $Q_l$ (representing steps required) and Euclidean distance. Specifically, $d(v_1 \rightarrow v_2) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \log_{\gamma_l}(1 + (1-\gamma_l)Q_l(v_1, v_2, \pi_l)) + \log_{\gamma_l}(1 + (1-\gamma_l)d_E(v_1, v_2)) \right]$. This combines the stability of Euclidean distance with the accuracy of learned traversal costs.
- **RL Training**: Both high-level and low-level policies and value functions are trained using the TD3 algorithm, an off-policy RL method. The low-level policy $\pi_l$ receives a reward of 0 for reaching a waypoint and -1 otherwise. The high-level Q-function is updated using the cumulative external rewards from FER. Different discount factors are used: $\gamma_l = 0.99$ for low-level (typical) and $\gamma_h = 0.4$ for high-level (to encourage shorter paths). An early termination condition is applied if the agent exhibits no movement for 500 steps to improve sample efficiency.

In summary, SSE integrates a robust experience replay, a balanced exploration strategy, and adaptive path planning to ensure that high-level planning is both reliable and efficient in long-horizon goal-conditioned tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Strict Subgoal Execution (SSE) was conducted across nine challenging long-horizon tasks, including five AntMaze environments, two KeyChest tasks, and two Reacher tasks. These environments tested navigation, bottleneck traversal, and sequential multi-objective reasoning capabilities. The evaluation compared two variants of SSE – SSE (Grid) and SSE (Model) – against a range of hierarchical RL methods (HIRO, HRAC, DHRL, HIGL) and recent graph-based approaches (BEAG, NGTE, PIG).

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Efficiency

- **Consistent Superiority**: Both SSE (Grid) and SSE (Model) consistently outperformed all baseline methods in terms of both success rate and sample efficiency across all benchmark environments. This indicates the robustness and generalizability of the proposed framework.
- **Faster Convergence**: SSE variants typically converged more quickly to high success rates, even in relatively simpler environments like U-maze and $\pi$-maze, compared to baselines that showed reasonable performance.
- **Effectiveness in Complex Tasks**: In complex multi-objective tasks such as KeyChest and ReacherWallDoubleGoal, which require agents to reason about intermediate objectives, most baseline methods largely failed. In contrast, SSE demonstrated the ability to successfully solve these tasks, attributing its performance to strict subgoal completion and efficient high-level decision-making.

#### 4.2. Impact of Key Components

- **Frontier Experience Replay (FER)**: Ablation studies revealed that removing FER (i.e., using a standard replay buffer) or replacing strict subgoal execution with conventional HER led to complete failure in complex tasks like AntDoubleKeyChest. This finding underscores the critical importance of FER's explicit success, stop-on-failure, and partial-success signals for training a reliable high-level policy and for avoiding infeasible subgoals.
- **Decoupled Exploration Policy ($\pi_{exp}$)**: Disabling the decoupled exploration policy (SSE without $\pi_{exp}$) consistently impaired goal-space coverage and overall learning performance across environments. This confirms that actively promoting visits to underexplored regions is necessary to counteract potential conservatism from strict subgoal execution.
- **Failure-Aware Path Refinement**: Removing path refinement (SSE without PR) degraded performance, particularly in bottleneck environments where navigating unstable regions is crucial. The mechanism effectively steered the planner away from failure-prone paths towards safer alternatives, as qualitatively demonstrated in a bottleneck scenario.
- **Graph Construction**: While SSE (Grid) generally converged faster due to lower overhead, SSE (Model), designed for scalability in higher-dimensional goal spaces, also achieved strong results. An ablation study comparing grid-based landmarks with Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) showed that SSE with FPS eventually succeeded, albeit less efficiently, indicating that the core framework is effective regardless of the specific landmark selection strategy.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Trajectory Analysis

- **Multi-Stage Task Completion**: Trajectory analyses in tasks like AntDoubleKeyChest illustrated SSE's progression from broad initial exploration to sequentially reasoning over multiple objectives. The agent learned to reliably collect multiple keys and then navigate to the final goal in a minimal number of high-level steps, demonstrating effective sequential decision-making without an explicit curriculum.
- **Bottleneck Navigation**: In bottleneck environments, SSE initially struggled but, with training, successfully leveraged failure-aware refinement to guide the high-level policy through narrow passages to the goal, avoiding areas with high failure rates.

#### 4.4. Computational Efficiency

- **Faster Convergence & Lower Per-Episode Time**: Comparative analysis on computational complexity showed that SSE achieved faster convergence and lower average per-episode computation time compared to relevant baselines like NGTE and BEAG. This efficiency is attributed to the early termination of episodes upon subgoal failure, which prevents long, unproductive trajectories and minimizes unnecessary computation.

#### 4.5. Hyperparameter Sensitivity

- Ablation studies on key hyperparameters such as the path refinement scaling factor ($c_d$), grid resolution ($d_G$), and exploration ratio ($\eta$) indicated that SSE's performance is robust across a relatively wide range of values for these parameters, requiring minimal tuning.
- The high-level discount factor ($\gamma_h = 0.4$) was found to be optimal. A lower $\gamma_h$ sharpens credit assignment, making the high-level Q-function sensitive to temporal inefficiencies and encouraging more direct, high-probability transitions, which aligns with SSE's principle of strict, single-step subgoal reachability.

In conclusion, the main findings demonstrate that SSE effectively addresses the challenges of unreliable subgoal execution and inefficient high-level planning in long-horizon, sparse-reward environments. Its novel components contribute synergistically to achieve superior performance, sample efficiency, and task generalizability across diverse and complex benchmarks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Strict Subgoal Execution (SSE) framework represents a notable advancement in the field of hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) for long-horizon, goal-conditioned tasks, particularly those with sparse rewards. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Reliability in Hierarchical Planning**: A fundamental contribution of SSE is its mechanism for ensuring reliable subgoal execution. By introducing Frontier Experience Replay (FER), SSE explicitly differentiates between successful, partial-success, and true failure outcomes for high-level subgoal attempts. This strict enforcement of subgoal completion provides the high-level policy with precise, unambiguous feedback, preventing it from repeatedly proposing infeasible subgoals. This reliability is critical for complex tasks where incorrect high-level decisions can lead to cascading failures and unstable learning.

- **Improved Efficiency and Sample Economy**: SSE significantly enhances sample efficiency by eliminating long, unproductive trajectories. The immediate termination of episodes upon high-level subgoal failure, combined with the clear credit assignment of FER, ensures that computational resources are not wasted on unrecoverable paths. This capability leads to faster convergence and reduced overall training time, as evidenced by the computational complexity analysis. Such efficiency is vital for applying RL to real-world problems where data collection can be costly and time-consuming.

- **Robust Exploration Strategy**: The decoupled exploration policy (π_exp) addresses the challenge of balancing exploitation with comprehensive goal-space coverage. By actively targeting underexplored regions, π_exp ensures that the agent discovers novel pathways and capabilities, counteracting any potential conservatism induced by strict subgoal execution. This dual-policy approach promotes a more robust and complete understanding of the environment's reachability graph.

- **Adaptive and Safer Path Planning**: The failure-aware path refinement mechanism contributes to safer and more effective navigation. By dynamically adjusting graph edge costs based on observed low-level failure statistics, SSE's planner can steer away from problematic regions (e.g., bottlenecks, collision-prone areas) even if they appear to be the "shortest" path. This adaptive planning capability is crucial for deploying agents in environments with complex physics or intricate layouts.

- **Versatility Across Complex Tasks**: The experimental results demonstrate SSE's effectiveness across a diverse range of challenging environments, including those with multiple bottlenecks, sequential multi-objectives (like the KeyChest tasks), and complex contact dynamics (AntPush, AntFall). The ability to tackle tasks requiring sequential reasoning without an explicit curriculum highlights its generalizability and potential for solving complex real-world robotics and control problems.

- **Foundation for Future Research**: SSE provides a robust framework that can serve as a foundation for further advancements in HRL. Its principles of strict subgoal execution, frontier delineation, and adaptive planning could be integrated into more sophisticated hierarchical architectures, potentially enabling agents to learn even longer and more abstract temporal hierarchies. The model-based variant also indicates a path towards scaling these methods to higher-dimensional state and goal spaces, where grid-based approaches become infeasible.

In summary, SSE addresses core limitations in current HRL methodologies, offering a more reliable, efficient, and robust approach to long-horizon planning. Its practical implications are significant for developing autonomous agents capable of performing complex, multi-stage tasks in challenging environments with sparse rewards, reducing the need for extensive human intervention or curriculum engineering.
