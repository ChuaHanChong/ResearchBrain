# Memory Limitations of Prompt Tuning in Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> This research provides the first formal proof for the empirically observed memory degradation in transformers with extended contexts, demonstrating that prompt tuning's memorization capacity scales at most linearly with prompt length and is subject to an intrinsic upper bound regardless of context size. The work offers a theoretical justification for why large language models struggle to retain information from very long prompts.

## Problem

- Lack of a formal theoretical explanation for the empirically observed performance degradation in transformers when handling extended contexts and very long prompts.
- Absence of a rigorous understanding of prompt tuning's inherent memory limitations within arbitrary (not synthetically constructed) transformer architectures.
- Unclear whether transformers have fundamental architectural limitations regarding the amount of information that can be effectively transferred or memorized via pre-prompts.

## Method

- Employs a purely theoretical and mathematical approach, leveraging concepts from metric space theory, information theory, and ==Lipschitz continuity== of neural networks.
- Utilizes the ==mean-field generalization== of transformers and ==q-Wasserstein distance== to analyze prompts of arbitrary length and model sequences as empirical probability measures.
- Applies ==covering and packing numbers== to quantify and compare the total target output space with the limited output space accessible through prompt tuning.

## Results

- The maximum number of memorizable input/output pairs (k) scales at most **linearly** with prompt length (k ∈ O(m_p/m)), with the proportion of accessible output sequences decreasing **exponentially** for k exceeding this linear bound.
- Transformers exhibit an intrinsic memory limitation for prompt tuning, where beyond a certain threshold of information, the proportion of accessible output distributions decreases **exponentially**, independent of the prompt length.
- For single-layer transformers, prompt tuning restricts accessible outputs to a severely limited subspace, making it impossible to even approximately memorize certain target input/output pairs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prompt tuning's ability to facilitate memorization is fundamentally constrained by the transformer's Lipschitz continuity and the finite capacity of the prompt space to encode new information.
> - There is an intrinsic upper bound on the total diversity or complexity of information that can be encoded and retrieved through prompt tuning, which is independent of the context or prompt length.
> - For shallow architectures like single-layer transformers, prompt tuning severely restricts the accessible output space, rendering many desired input/output pairs fundamentally unreachable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Transformers have revolutionized machine learning, achieving state-of-the-art results across various domains, notably in natural language processing and computer vision. Their success largely stems from their ability to adapt pre-trained models to downstream tasks. Traditionally, this involved "full fine-tuning," where all model parameters are updated. However, with the advent of massive Large Language Models (LLMs), full fine-tuning has become prohibitively costly, leading to the rise of parameter-efficient alternatives like prompt tuning.

Prompt tuning involves prepending a small set of learnable parameters (soft prompts) or carefully crafted human-readable text (prompt engineering) to the input. Despite its simplicity and minimal parameter updates, prompt tuning often matches or exceeds the performance of full fine-tuning, especially in settings like in-context learning (ICL), where demonstrations are provided within the prompt itself.

The theoretical understanding of prompt tuning, however, has been relatively limited. Prior theoretical work has primarily focused on its "universal approximation properties." Studies by Wang et al. [2023a], Petrov et al. [2024a], Hu et al., and Nakada et al. have shown that prompt tuning, under certain conditions, can enable a transformer to approximate any function with sufficient accuracy. A key limitation of these universality results is their reliance on *synthetically constructed* transformers, whose weights are specifically designed to facilitate prompt tuning, which may not accurately reflect the behavior of the large, pre-trained models used in practice.

Separately, the general "memorization capability" of transformers has been studied (e.g., Kim et al., Mahdavi et al., Kajitsuka and Sato), demonstrating that transformers can indeed memorize input sequences with sufficient architectural depth or parameters. However, these works typically focus on the transformer's inherent memorization capacity as a whole, rather than the specific limitations imposed by prompt-based adaptation.

This paper critically addresses a different, but increasingly pressing, aspect: the *memorization capability of prompt tuning* in *arbitrary* (i.e., not specially constructed) transformers. Recent empirical studies have consistently observed that even advanced LLMs struggle to memorize information presented in very long prompts, often exhibiting performance degradation or "lost in the middle" phenomena (Hsieh et al., Levy et al., Laban et al., Li et al. [2024a], Liu et al., Jin et al.). This empirical gap lacked a formal theoretical explanation. This work positions itself as the first to provide a rigorous, mathematical justification for this observed limitation. By focusing on general transformers and formalizing the memory constraints of prompt tuning, it shifts the theoretical discourse from "what *can* transformers approximate" to "what are their *fundamental limits* when adapted via prompts." Furthermore, it explicitly considers masked self-attention, which is crucial for understanding decoder-only LLMs like GPT, a detail often overlooked in prior theoretical analyses.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to advance the theoretical understanding of prompt tuning by rigorously analyzing its inherent memorization capabilities within the transformer architecture. The authors identify a significant gap: while empirical observations highlight performance degradation in transformers with extended contexts, and existing theoretical work largely focuses on universal approximation properties, a formal understanding of prompt tuning's *memory limitations* is absent.

The paper aims to answer two fundamental questions:
1. **Can the pre-prompt length be shortened while retaining information to improve the memorization ability of transformers?** This questions the efficiency of information encoding within prompts.
2. **Do transformers have inherent limitations as to the amount of information a pre-prompt can transfer?** This probes the existence of fundamental architectural constraints on prompt tuning.

The motivation for addressing these questions is multi-faceted:
- **Formalizing Empirical Observations:** The primary motivation is to provide the "first formal proof" for the empirically observed "performance degradation in transformers with extended contexts" and the general struggle of LLMs to memorize information from long prompts. This bridges the gap between practical observation and theoretical understanding.
- **Gaining Fundamental Understanding:** The research seeks to offer a "fundamental understanding of the intrinsic limitations of transformer architectures," particularly concerning their ability to handle and retain information from long sequences when adapted via prompt tuning.
- **Informing Prompt Engineering and Design:** By characterizing these limitations, the work intends to guide the development of more effective prompt tuning strategies and prompt engineering practices, helping practitioners understand the theoretical bounds of what can be achieved with current architectures.
- **Differentiating from Universal Approximation:** The authors explicitly state their departure from prior work on universal approximation. While transformers *can* approximate functions, this paper investigates the practical, quantifiable limits of *how much new information* they can reliably internalize and reproduce through prompt tuning, especially in the context of memorizing specific input/output pairs.
- **Understanding Asymptotic Gains:** The work also seeks to complement existing analyses on the expressive differences between soft prompt optimization and prompt engineering, particularly regarding their asymptotic gains in memorization capacity.

In essence, the paper aims to mathematically demonstrate that transformers possess an inherent, limited memory capacity when exclusively leveraging prompt tuning, and that this limitation is a fundamental property of the architecture, rather than just an artifact of current model sizes or training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research adopts a purely theoretical and mathematical approach to analyze the memorization capabilities of prompt tuning in transformers. The methodology primarily relies on concepts from metric space theory, information theory, and the Lipschitz properties of neural networks.

**1. Transformer Architecture Definition:**
The paper begins by defining a standard transformer architecture as a stack of self-attention and MLP layers.
- **Self-Attention Layer:** Defined with multiple heads, projecting query, key, and value tokens. The normalization factor is absorbed into the weights for simplicity.
- **Transformer Layer:** Combines self-attention with a feed-forward MLP, including residual connections.
- **Full Transformer:** A composition of multiple transformer layers.
- **Positional Encodings:** Acknowledged but omitted for clarity, with the justification that they can be absorbed into the input sequence without altering the core analysis structure.
- **Masked Self-Attention:** Importantly, the analysis explicitly extends to masked self-attention, which is critical for decoder-only LLMs. This is handled by modifying the input space to include positional timestamps and using a "position-aware distance" (d_m) in the mean-field regime, maintaining the Lipschitz property.

**2. Lipschitz Continuity:**
A cornerstone of the analysis is the Lipschitz continuity of transformers.
- A function is L-Lipschitz if the distance between outputs is bounded by L times the distance between inputs.
- The paper leverages existing results (Kim et al., Castin et al., Geshkovski et al.) on the Lipschitz constants of self-attention and mean-field self-attention, noting that these constants generally depend on the input radius (inputs are restricted to a compact ball) and can grow with sequence length in the finite-token regime.

**3. Mean-Field Transformers:**
To analyze prompts of *arbitrary length* and overcome the sequence-length dependency of Lipschitz constants in the finite-token regime, the authors utilize the "mean-field generalization" of transformers.
- This framework treats sequences of tokens as empirical probability measures. Due to the permutation equivariance of transformers, they can be viewed as mappings between probability measures.
- The mean-field self-attention and transformer layers are defined as pushforwards of these probability measures.
- The **q-Wasserstein Distance** (Santambrogio) is used to measure distances between probability distributions, crucial for quantifying "ε-distinct output distributions."

**4. Covering and Packing Numbers:**
These concepts from high-dimensional probability and metric geometry (Vershynin) are central to quantifying information capacity.
- **ε-Covering Number (N):** The minimum number of balls of radius ε needed to cover a set.
- **ε-Packing Number (M):** The maximum number of ε-separated points (or disjoint balls of radius ε/2) that can be placed in a set.
- These numbers provide bounds on the "size" or "diversity" of a space. The general idea is to compare the maximum number of distinct target outputs a transformer *could* produce (C_out) with the maximum number of distinct outputs it *can* produce by varying the prompt (C_in), given its Lipschitz constant.

**5. Proof Strategy for Main Theorems (Theorems 4.7 & 4.10):**
The proofs for the main memorization capacity results follow a three-step outline:
- **Step 1: Quantify Target Output Space (C_out):** Using covering numbers, the authors determine the maximum number of *ε-distinct output sequences* (for bounded length prompts) or *ε-distinct output distributions* (for arbitrary length prompts using mean-field) that exist in the target output space. This represents the total "information" that could potentially be memorized.
- **Step 2: Quantify Accessible Output Space (C_in):** The pre-prompt space (P ∈ R^(d x m_p)) is also finite-dimensional and bounded. By discretizing this space and applying the Lipschitz property of the transformer, they determine how many *ε-accessible output sequences/distributions* can be produced by varying the pre-prompt. That is, if two prompts are close, their outputs are also close (bounded by L). This limits the number of *distinct* outputs that can be generated by a fixed set of prompt parameters.
- **Step 3: Comparison and Proportion:** If the number of *possible* distinct outputs (C_out) far exceeds the number of *accessible* distinct outputs (C_in), it implies that a large proportion of target outputs cannot be memorized. The ratio C_in / C_out provides this proportion, which the proofs show decreases exponentially with the number of input/output pairs (k). This exponential decrease signifies the rapid degradation of memorization.

**6. Analysis of Single-Layer Transformers (Theorems 5.7 & 5.8):**
- The paper extends previous work by Wang et al. [2023a], which showed limitations of single-layer transformers under strong assumptions. This work *removes* most of these strong assumptions (e.g., full-rank weights, distinct intermediate representations), retaining only a mild condition on the MLP's spectral norm (often met in practice).
- The approach involves decomposing the self-attention mechanism to analyze how prompt tuning modifies the input. For a single layer, the attention output can be seen as modifying the last vector of inputs along a specific axis.
- By demonstrating that the number of independent constraints required for memorizing multiple input/output pairs quickly exceeds the effective "degrees of freedom" offered by prompt tuning, they prove that the accessible output space is severely restricted (akin to a hyperplane).
- This is further generalized to *approximate memorization*, showing that even with a relaxed error tolerance, a significant portion of desired outputs remains unreachable. This utilizes geometric arguments about high-dimensional orthogonal vectors.

Overall, the methodology is characterized by a rigorous mathematical foundation, employing advanced tools from functional analysis and information theory to derive fundamental limitations that are applicable to a broad class of transformer architectures, including those with masked attention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents two principal theoretical contributions regarding the memory limitations of prompt tuning in transformers, along with refined analysis for single-layer architectures.

**1. Linear Scaling of Memorized Information with Prompt Length (Theorem 4.7):**
- **Finding:** The amount of information a transformer can reliably learn and memorize through prompt tuning cannot scale faster than linearly with the prompt length. Specifically, the maximal number *k* of input/output pairs of length *m* that a transformer can memorize via a prompt of length *m_p* scales as *k* ∈ O(m_p/m).
- **Elaboration:** For a given transformer and precision (ε), if the number of input/output pairs *k* exceeds a certain threshold (which is linearly dependent on *m_p* and inversely on *m*), the proportion of desired output sequences that are "ε-accessible" (i.e., can be approximated within error ε by *any* prompt of length *m_p*) decreases exponentially with *k*.
- **Significance:** This quantifies the rate at which memorization capacity diminishes with increasing data. It implies that simply making prompts longer does not lead to a proportionally unbounded increase in the number of distinct items a transformer can reliably memorize.
- **Scope:** This result holds for both unmasked and masked self-attention mechanisms.

**2. Intrinsic Memory Limitations Independent of Prompt Size (Theorem 4.10):**
- **Finding:** Transformers inherently have a limited memory when using prompt tuning, constraining the total amount of information they can retain, *regardless of the context size or prompt length*. This provides the "first formal proof" of the empirically observed phenomenon of performance degradation in transformers with extended contexts.
- **Elaboration:** Using the mean-field generalization of transformers, which allows analysis for arbitrary prompt lengths, the paper demonstrates that beyond a certain threshold *k*, the proportion of ε-accessible output *distributions* decreases exponentially. This threshold *k* depends on the transformer's Lipschitz constant, embedding radius, embedding dimension, and the target precision, but crucially, it does not depend on the specific prompt length (m_p), only the characteristics of the transformer itself and the target items.
- **Significance:** This is a more profound limitation, suggesting that even if a prompt is infinitely long, there is an upper bound on the *total diversity* or *complexity* of information that can be encoded and retrieved through prompt tuning. It provides a fundamental theoretical justification for why LLMs "get lost in the middle" or struggle with very long contexts, irrespective of architectural support for longer sequences.
- **Scope:** This result also holds for both unmasked and masked self-attention.

**3. Limitations of Prompt Tuning on Single-Layer Transformers (Theorems 5.7 & 5.8):**
- **Weakening Assumptions:** The paper significantly generalizes previous results on single-layer transformers (Wang et al. [2023a]) by removing restrictive assumptions (e.g., full-rank weights, distinctness of intermediate representations) and retaining only a mild condition on the MLP's spectral norm, which is often met in practice.
- **Restricted Expressivity (Theorem 5.7):** For a 1-layer transformer with multiple heads, the space of outputs accessible through prompt tuning for certain input configurations is severely limited. It is a subspace (or "almost a hyperplane") of the full output space, with a significantly reduced dimension. This means that many target output pairs (X, Y) are fundamentally unreachable through prompt tuning.
- **No Approximate Memorization (Theorem 5.8):** This limitation extends even when the goal is relaxed to *approximating* the desired outputs. The paper proves that for a single-layer transformer, it is impossible to even approximate certain output pairs within a specified error margin *ε*, regardless of the prompt.
- **Significance:** These findings underscore the severe architectural constraints at shallow depths. They suggest that for complex tasks requiring memorization, multiple layers are indispensable, and even then, prompt tuning alone might not unlock the full expressive power of the network.

In summary, the core findings are that prompt tuning's memorization capacity is fundamentally limited, showing at best linear scaling with prompt length and, more critically, an inherent upper bound on retrievable information regardless of context size. These limitations are attributed to the underlying architecture's Lipschitz continuity and the finite capacity of the prompt space to encode new information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper makes significant theoretical contributions that have profound implications for the understanding, design, and practical application of transformers and Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly concerning prompt tuning and in-context learning.

**1. Fundamental Theoretical Justification for Empirical Phenomena:**
The most critical impact is providing the *first formal proof* for the widely observed "performance degradation in transformers with extended contexts" (the "lost in the middle" problem) and the difficulty LLMs face in reliably memorizing information from very long prompts. This moves the understanding from an empirical observation to a fundamental, theoretically grounded limitation of the transformer architecture when adapted via prompt tuning. This is crucial for building a more complete scientific understanding of these powerful models.

**2. Guiding Prompt Engineering and In-Context Learning (ICL):**
The findings directly inform prompt engineering strategies. The result that memorization scales at most linearly with prompt length (Theorem 4.7) implies diminishing returns for simply increasing prompt length. There's a quantifiable limit to how much information can be reliably encoded and retrieved from examples within a prompt. Practitioners may need to consider alternative strategies when dealing with vast amounts of in-context data, rather than assuming that longer prompts automatically lead to better memorization. The paper suggests that for a fixed number of input/output pairs, there might be an optimal prompt length, and exceeding it could lead to exponential degradation in the proportion of memorized information.

**3. Architectural Implications for Future Model Design:**
The intrinsic memory limitation (Theorem 4.10), independent of prompt size, suggests that simply scaling up context windows or model parameters might not fundamentally overcome the constraints of prompt tuning for memorization-heavy tasks. This implies that for tasks requiring truly extensive in-context memory, novel architectural modifications beyond the current attention mechanism, or hybrid approaches combining prompt tuning with external memory systems, might be necessary. It shifts the focus from merely "how to make context longer" to "how to make context *more effectively usable* for memorization."

**4. Redefining "Memory" in Transformers:**
The work contributes to a deeper theoretical understanding of what "memory" entails in the context of frozen pre-trained transformers adapted by prompts. It highlights that prompt tuning primarily modulates the input context to a fixed function, rather than fundamentally altering the model's internal memory capacity as full fine-tuning might. This distinction is crucial for understanding the true capabilities and limitations of various adaptation paradigms.

**5. Advancing Theoretical Foundations of Parameter-Efficient Learning:**
By rigorously analyzing prompt tuning, the paper contributes to the broader theoretical understanding of parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods. It complements existing work on universal approximation by adding a crucial layer of "capacity limitations." This balanced perspective is essential for developing robust and reliable AI systems. The comparison with Petrov et al. [2024b] further refines our understanding of the asymptotic gains of soft prompt optimization over prompt engineering, indicating that these gains might be more limited (linear) in general settings than previously suggested for specific constructions.

**6. Importance of Masked Self-Attention Analysis:**
The inclusion of masked self-attention in the analysis ensures that the findings are applicable to a significant class of modern LLMs (decoder-only models like GPT), which are primarily used for in-context learning. This broadens the relevance and impact of the theoretical results on current state-of-the-art models.

In conclusion, this research provides a vital theoretical anchor for understanding why current LLMs struggle with long contexts. Its findings serve as a cautionary tale against unbounded expectations from prompt tuning, emphasizing that inherent architectural limitations can impede memorization despite vast model sizes. This theoretical framework is poised to guide future research into more robust and memory-efficient adaptation strategies for transformers, potentially leading to new architectural designs or hybrid approaches that can overcome these identified fundamental bounds.
