# Multi-Agent Robotic Control with Onboard Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research presents a Multi-Agent System (MAS) architecture for robotic control, deploying compact Vision-Language Models (VLMs) entirely on onboard hardware. The system autonomously performs diverse industrial tasks in a simulated warehouse, validating a cost-efficient and robust alternative to cloud-dependent solutions.

## Problem

- Existing VLM/VLA-based robotic control solutions require substantial computing power and continuous network connectivity, leading to high costs, latency, and network dependency.
- VLA models often lack explainability, which hinders their safe and ethical deployment in critical real-world industrial applications.
- Smaller language models struggle with context retention and effective generalization when tasked with long-horizon planning and diverse objectives.

## Method

- Developed a ==Multi-Agent System (MAS)== architecture, using the RAI framework, designed to run entirely on the robot's onboard hardware with compact Vision-Language Models (3B parameters).
- Implemented a supervisory **"Megamind"** agent with a ==two-state self-feedback loop== (planning and analysis) to manage task delegation and recovery, mitigating context retention issues for smaller LLMs.
- Utilized specialized agents for continuous vision-based monitoring (Inspection and Safety) and low-level robot control (MoveIt and Nav2), enhancing modularity and task-specific performance.

## Results

- The MAS successfully executed all **five** predefined industrial task categories: safety inspection, warehouse maintenance, object search, package quality verification, and human request response.
- Fine-tuning the **3B**-parameter VLM for package quality inspection increased box condition annotation accuracy from **76.7%** to **91.5%** and improved the F1-score from **0.755** to **0.915**.
- The system achieved real-time operation on an AMD Ryzen™ AI mini PC within a hardware-in-the-loop simulated industrial warehouse, demonstrating practical onboard deployment feasibility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is feasible to deploy complex multi-agent robotic control with compact VLMs on onboard edge hardware, offering a real-time, cost-efficient, and network-independent alternative to cloud solutions.
> - A supervisory orchestration agent effectively manages long-horizon tasks, overcoming context limitations of smaller LLMs by externalizing planning, delegation, and recovery processes.
> - Domain-specific fine-tuning of compact VLMs, using simulation-derived annotated datasets, yields substantial accuracy improvements for specialized robotic inspection tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has increasingly leveraged Vision Language Models (VLMs) and Vision Language Action (VLA) models for control tasks. These models allow robots to interpret visual information and natural language commands to perform complex actions. However, their deployment in practical robotic systems faces several significant challenges.

Current VLM-based solutions often necessitate substantial computing power and reliable network connectivity, typically relying on cloud or powerful edge infrastructure. This can lead to increased operational costs, latency, and vulnerability to network interruptions. VLA models, while capable, frequently suffer from a lack of explainability, which is a critical concern for safety and ethical deployment in real-world scenarios, particularly in industrial settings. Existing approaches to enhance VLA interpretability, such as analyzing hidden layers, using embodied chain-of-thought for intervention, or applying constraint functions, have shown limitations in their generalization capabilities, especially when confronted with out-of-distribution data. Some proposals suggest restricting VLA deployment to "safe" robot types, but this limits their broader applicability.

This research positions itself as an attempt to address these limitations by proposing a Multi-Agent System (MAS) architecture. Unlike many VLM/VLA deployments that offload computation, this work focuses on a fully onboard solution running on the robot's hardware. By deploying specialized agents and utilizing compact VLMs, the authors aim to mitigate the reliance on external compute, enhance explainability through modularity, improve generalization through task specialization, and ensure real-time responsiveness. This approach seeks to provide a cost-efficient and robust alternative to cloud-dependent or high-compute edge solutions, making advanced robotic capabilities more accessible and practical for dynamic industrial environments, particularly for small- to medium-sized enterprises. The work also contributes by providing an open-source platform for further research into agentic systems, aligning with the growing interest in embodied AI frameworks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged drawbacks of existing VLM and VLA models in robotic control. Specifically, the authors aimed to overcome:
- **High Compute Requirements:** VLM-based solutions often demand significant computational resources, leading to high energy consumption and reliance on powerful, often offboard, hardware.
- **Network Dependency:** Many current implementations require continuous network connectivity to external compute platforms (cloud or powerful edge servers), introducing latency and potential points of failure.
- **Lack of Explainability:** VLA models, while powerful, can operate as "black boxes," making it difficult to understand their decision-making processes, which is problematic for safety-critical applications.
- **Limited Generalization:** Existing VLM/VLA solutions often struggle to generalize effectively to new or out-of-distribution data, limiting their adaptability to dynamic environments.
- **Context Retention Issues:** Smaller language models, when tasked with long-horizon planning across diverse objectives, tend to lose crucial contextual information due to limited context windows.

To address these motivations, the research established several key objectives:
1. **Develop a Multi-Agent System (MAS) Architecture:** The central objective was to design and implement a MAS capable of controlling a multi-purpose autonomous mobile manipulator. This architecture was intended to run entirely on the robot's onboard hardware, eliminating external compute dependencies.
2. **Ensure Real-Time Response and Operation:** The system was designed to operate efficiently and respond in real-time within dynamic industrial environments, such as a warehouse.
3. **Demonstrate Versatile Task Fulfillment:** The MAS was developed to autonomously perform a diverse set of five specific task categories, requiring varied contextual understanding and actions:
 - **T1: Safety Inspection:** Continuous monitoring for regulatory compliance.
 - **T2: Warehouse Maintenance:** Addressing issues like misaligned packages.
 - **T3: Searching for Objects:** Locating specific items or anomalies.
 - **T4: Package Quality Verification:** Assessing the condition of packages.
 - **T5: Responding to Human Requests:** Interacting with human operators via natural language commands.
 The diverse nature of these tasks specifically motivated the MAS approach, as a single, smaller VLM would face significant context scaling issues.
4. **Validate Feasibility with Compact VLMs:** The project aimed to demonstrate that compact Vision-Language Models (ranging from 3 to 20 billion parameters) could be effectively utilized within the onboard MAS, reducing hardware costs and energy consumption.
5. **Mitigate Context Retention with Orchestration:** A specific objective was to introduce a supervisory agent ("Megamind") to manage task planning and execution, thereby mitigating the inherent context retention challenges of smaller LLMs during long-horizon operations.
6. **Provide an Open-Source Development Platform:** An additional objective was to release the simulation environment, including a configurable warehouse and robot platform, as open-source. This aims to provide a standardized, integrated platform (with ROS 2 and RAI) for further research and development in robotic agentic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers developed a Multi-Agent System (MAS) architecture, implemented using the RAI framework, to control a simulated multipurpose autonomous mobile manipulator. The core principle was to deploy the entire system, including VLM inference, on onboard hardware.

**Hardware and Simulation Environment:**
The system was validated in a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) simulation of an industrial warehouse environment. The simulated robot platform was an RB-KAIROS+ mobile manipulator. All VLM inference for the agents was performed on an AMD Ryzen™ AI mini PC, configured with 128 GB unified RAM (96 GB allocated to the GPU), demonstrating the feasibility of edge computing. The simulation environment, including the configurable warehouse, packages, and robot platform, was released as open source under the Apache 2.0 license, integrated with ROS 2 and RAI.

**Overall Architecture:**
The MAS architecture (depicted in Figure 1 of the paper) comprises a supervisory planning agent, specialized vision-based inspection and safety agents, and low-level control agents for navigation and manipulation. User interaction occurs via a Human-Robot Interface (HRI) providing a graphical display, live video feed, and status updates.

**Vision Language Model (VLM):**
A compact 3B-parameter VLM, LFM2-VL-3B, was shared by the Megamind, Inspection, and Safety agents.
- **Fine-tuning:** Initial evaluation revealed that the base VLM struggled with package quality inspection (Task T4). To enhance performance, the model was fine-tuned. A simulation-derived dataset was created, where each image was annotated with three attributes: `anomaly` (True/False), `box_condition` (good/damaged), and `safety_hazard` (True/False). A larger teacher model, Qwen3-256B-A22B, was then conditioned on these annotations to generate synthetic natural-language descriptions. The LFM2-VL-3B VLM was subsequently fine-tuned for one epoch on this dataset.

**Specialized Agents:**
- **Megamind Agent (Supervisory):** This agent addresses the context retention limitations of compact LLMs in long-horizon planning. It operates as a two-state self-feedback loop:
 1. **Planning State:** Selects the next task based on the current robot state and task queue, then delegates it to the appropriate sub-agent.
 2. **Analysis State:** After a sub-agent returns its output, Megamind determines if the task was successful. If recovery is needed, it generates corrective steps and appends them to the task queue before returning to the planning state. This explicit control loop externalizes key aspects of task management.
- **Inspection Agent:** This agent continuously monitors the front-facing camera feed for warehouse anomalies relevant to warehouse maintenance and object search (T2, T3). Its workflow involves two stages:
 1. The VLM audits each image, generating a concise natural-language description and flagging up to three potential issues.
 2. Flagged issues are categorized as `box` (graspable boxes in the aisle), `trash` (graspable litter), `other` (non-manipulable obstacles to be reported), or `nothing`. For graspable objects, 3D pose information is obtained from simulation ground truth. The agent outputs a structured JSON object containing the issue type, pose, and description, which is then passed to the Megamind agent for action selection.
- **Safety Agent:** This agent performs continuous regulatory-compliance monitoring using the front-facing camera feed (T1, T4). It employs a three-stage pipeline:
 1. **Vision Stage:** The fine-tuned VLM describes the scene and identifies up to three potential safety hazards, providing a structured observation with an explicit list of anomalies. The model is instructed to report only hazards supported by clear visual evidence.
 2. **Retrieval Stage:** For each identified hazard, the agent queries a vector database constructed offline from relevant sections of OSHA 29 CFR 1910 regulations. The database is indexed using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B. Retrieved candidates are re-ranked using Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B, retaining only documents above a relevance threshold (maximum three excerpts per hazard).
 3. **Reasoning Stage:** The VLM receives the original image, scene description, and retrieved regulatory excerpts. It then determines if the hazard constitutes a violation, outputting applicable regulation identifiers, a supporting excerpt, a severity level (LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH), and a brief rationale. To optimize inference on edge hardware, hazards are processed independently, and consecutive frames with high visual similarity (measured by Structural Similarity Index Measure) are skipped.
- **MoveIt and Nav2 Agents (Low-level Control):** These agents provide foundational robotic control. RAI tools interface with the ROS 2-based MoveIt 2 (for manipulator control) and Nav2 (for mobile platform navigation) stacks. They execute commands issued by Megamind and report status updates through the HRI.

**Demonstration and Interaction:**
The system was demonstrated across three nodes: an O3DE simulation host, the HIL compute node (AMD Ryzen™ AI mini PC running the agent stack), and an HRI display. Attendees could interact with the simulated robot by issuing natural language commands via the Qt-based HRI, covering tasks like package relocation, anomaly inspection, misaligned package correction, and safety compliance reporting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research successfully demonstrated the capabilities of a comprehensive Multi-Agent System (MAS) running entirely on onboard compute, controlling a mobile manipulator in a simulated industrial warehouse environment.

**Feasibility of Onboard MAS:**
The core finding is the successful validation of a fully onboard MAS architecture as a viable and cost-efficient alternative to cloud-dependent deployments. The system, utilizing compact VLMs (3B parameters), proved capable of real-time operation within a dynamic industrial setting using an AMD Ryzen™ AI mini PC for inference. This demonstrates that significant computing power and network connectivity are not always necessary for advanced robotic control.

**Task Fulfillment and Versatility:**
The MAS successfully fulfilled all five predefined task categories:
- **Safety inspection (T1)**
- **Warehouse maintenance (T2)**
- **Searching for objects (T3)**
- **Package quality verification (T4)**
- **Responding to human requests (T5)**
This demonstrated the system's ability to handle a diverse and extensive set of objectives, which would typically pose challenges for a single, smaller VLM due to context window limitations.

**Impact of VLM Fine-tuning:**
Fine-tuning the LFM2-VL-3B VLM specifically for package quality inspection (Task T4) yielded substantial improvements in performance:
- The **box condition annotation accuracy** increased from 76.7% to 91.5%.
- The **F1-score** for this task improved from 0.755 to 0.915.
- Analysis of specific metrics showed a significant increase in **true negatives** (from 54% to 95.5%) and a substantial decrease in **false positives** (from 46% to 4.5%), indicating better reliability in correctly identifying good packages and reducing erroneous flagging.

**Effectiveness of the Megamind Agent:**
The novel "Megamind" orchestration agent effectively mitigated the context retention issues inherent to long-horizon planning when using smaller LLMs. Its two-state self-feedback loop (planning and analysis) successfully externalized task progress, delegation, and recovery, enabling the compact VLMs to manage multi-step robotic tasks that require maintaining context over extended periods.

**Hardware-in-the-Loop Validation:**
The use of hardware-in-the-loop simulation with an AMD Ryzen™ AI mini PC provided concrete evidence that small, locally hosted VLMs can be practically employed for real-time robot control, moving beyond purely theoretical or cloud-dependent validations.

**Cost-Efficiency and Potential for Real-World Transfer:**
The elimination of dependence on external compute platforms presents a cost-efficient solution, reducing both initial hardware investments and ongoing operational costs (e.g., electricity, cloud subscriptions). The demonstrated capabilities and robust performance in simulation suggest strong potential for transfer to real-world physical deployments, particularly for small- to medium-sized enterprises.

**Open-Source Contribution:**
The researchers released the simulation environment as open source under the Apache 2.0 license. This provides a readily available platform integrated with ROS 2 and RAI, facilitating further academic and industrial research into agentic systems for mobile manipulators.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by demonstrating a practical and scalable approach to deploying advanced AI capabilities on robotic platforms. The findings hold several significant implications and potential impacts:

**Advancement in Onboard Edge AI Robotics:** The work provides evidence for the viability of deploying complex multi-agent systems with compact Vision-Language Models entirely on onboard hardware. This reduces the traditional reliance on cloud infrastructure or high-end external compute, which is a bottleneck for many robotic applications. This advancement can accelerate the adoption of edge AI in robotics, making intelligent automation more accessible.

**Cost Reduction and Accessibility for SMEs:** By eliminating the need for continuous network connectivity and substantial offboard computing resources, the proposed architecture offers a cost-efficient solution. This reduction in both initial capital expenditure (for powerful external hardware) and ongoing operational costs (e.g., cloud service fees, energy consumption for data transfer) is particularly impactful for small- to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). It lowers the barrier to entry for adopting flexible robotics, enabling them to integrate advanced automation solutions into their operations.

**Enhanced Robustness and Real-Time Operation:** An onboard system inherently reduces latency associated with network communication, enabling real-time responsiveness critical for dynamic industrial environments and safety-sensitive tasks. Furthermore, independence from network connectivity increases the system's robustness against communication failures, a crucial factor for reliable autonomous operations.

**Improved Generalization and Adaptability:** The multi-agent system (MAS) approach, with specialized agents for different tasks, addresses limitations in generalization often seen in monolithic VLM/VLA models. By delegating specific competencies to distinct agents, the system can more effectively handle a diverse range of objectives. The "Megamind" orchestrator further enhances this by managing long-horizon planning and context retention, allowing compact models to perform complex, multi-step tasks that would otherwise overwhelm them.

**Foundation for Certifiable and Interpretable Control:** The modular nature of the MAS, built upon established robotic stacks like ROS 2, MoveIt 2, and Nav2, provides a framework that can support certifiable and potentially more interpretable control. The explicit division of labor among agents and the structured decision-making loop of Megamind offer a clearer pathway to understanding system behavior compared to end-to-end, less transparent models. This directly addresses the explainability challenges faced by many VLA models.

**Acceleration of Research and Development:** The open-sourcing of the simulation environment provides a valuable resource for the broader research community. This readily available platform, integrated with standard robotic frameworks, can foster further experimentation, benchmarking, and development of agentic systems for mobile manipulators, potentially leading to more rapid advancements in the field of embodied AI.

**Potential for Real-World Transfer:** The validation performed in a hardware-in-the-loop simulation, which mimics real-world hardware constraints and dynamic environments, suggests a strong potential for the successful transfer of this architecture to physical robot deployments. This moves the research beyond theoretical possibilities towards practical application in industrial settings.
