# RhinoVLA Technical Report

> [!summary] Summary
> RhinoVLA is a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed for real-time deployment on edge hardware, addressing computational bottlenecks and enabling cross-robot generalization. The system achieves an end-to-end inference frequency of 11.69 Hz on the Huixi R1 SoC, surpassing the 10 Hz real-time requirement, while maintaining competitive task performance with an average 90.0% success rate on the LIBERO benchmark.

## Problem

- Existing VLA models are computationally demanding, exceeding the capabilities of onboard edge hardware for real-time (10 Hz) closed-loop control.
- The number of visual and context tokens in VLM backbones (e.g., from MLP projection operators) is a significant bottleneck for inference latency on edge platforms.
- Integrating heterogeneous robot datasets with disparate camera layouts, sensing modalities, and action definitions makes learning a unified policy challenging for cross-robot generalization.

## Method

- Developed a token-efficient VLA architecture using a 2.13B Qwen3-VL backbone with 64 merged visual tokens and a 0.40B Action Expert to reduce computational load.
- Implemented algorithm-system co-design for the Huixi R1 edge SoC, incorporating hardware-aware compilation, mixed-precision (W8A16) execution, and parallel visual encoding.
- Introduced a cross-robot learning framework using a View Registry, a unified 72D physical state-action slot space with binary masks, and robot-instance LoRA modules for embodiment-specific adaptation.

## Results

- Achieved an end-to-end inference frequency of 11.69 Hz on the Huixi R1 SoC, demonstrating real-time capability and a 2x speedup over an unoptimized baseline.
- Demonstrated competitive task performance, with an average 90.0% success rate on the LIBERO benchmark (e.g., 82.4% on LIBERO-Long, outperforming "0 by 9.4 points) and effective real-robot performance on diverse tasks across three platforms.
- Confirmed the effectiveness of robot-instance LoRA modules in adapting to embodiment variations, showing consistent improvements in masked flow-matching loss and Mean Absolute Error for held-out action prediction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The count of visual and context tokens, rather than solely model size, is a critical bottleneck for achieving real-time inference speeds of VLA models on edge devices.
> - Algorithm-hardware co-design, including specialized compilation and mixed-precision techniques, is essential to translate theoretical hardware throughput into practical real-time performance for complex VLA models.
> - Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) modules can effectively provide robot-specific residual corrections for a shared Action Expert, enabling efficient cross-robot generalization and adaptation across diverse embodiments with a unified policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which aim to develop general-purpose robot policies by integrating visual perception, natural language understanding, and continuous action generation. Recent VLA models, such as `RT-1`, `RT-2`, `π0`, `π0.5`, and `GR00T N1`, have demonstrated capabilities in robotic manipulation and embodied decision-making. These models often leverage large, pre-trained vision-language backbones and complex action generation modules, leading to enhanced policy capabilities.

However, a significant challenge in this landscape is the real-time deployment of these sophisticated VLA models on resource-constrained edge hardware. Existing VLA systems frequently encounter high computational and memory demands, particularly from the VLM backbones and iterative action generation processes. This makes it difficult to achieve the high inference speeds (e.g., 10 Hz for closed-loop control) required for practical robotic applications.

RhinoVLA addresses this deployment bottleneck through an algorithm-system co-design approach. It differentiates itself by focusing on token efficiency within the VLM backbone and optimizing execution on a purpose-built edge System-on-Chip (SoC). Furthermore, the work tackles the heterogeneity inherent in robot datasets, a problem that complicates the training of unified policies across diverse robot embodiments. By introducing a standardized interface for multi-robot observations and actions, RhinoVLA aims to enable more scalable and adaptable VLA training, contributing to the development of generalist robot policies that can operate effectively in real-world, varied environments. This positions RhinoVLA as a contribution to the practical, deployable aspects of VLA research, bridging the gap between high-capability models and real-time edge performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the RhinoVLA project are twofold: first, to enable real-time deployment of VLA models for robotic manipulation on edge hardware, specifically targeting a 10 Hz closed-loop inference rate; and second, to facilitate cross-robot learning by establishing a unified interface for heterogeneous robot observations and action schemas.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from two core challenges identified in the current VLA research landscape.

### 3. Real-time Deployment Latency on Edge Hardware

- **Computational Burden:** Current VLA models, while powerful, are computationally intensive. Analysis of existing models like `π0.5` on edge platforms (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin) reveals that the VLM backbone and Action Expert collectively account for over 90% of the end-to-end inference latency. This high computational cost prevents these models from meeting the 10 Hz real-time closed-loop control target necessary for responsive robotic operation.
- **Token-Scaling Bottleneck:** A deeper operator-level breakdown of the VLM component, particularly in `π0.5`, indicated that MLP projection operators are the dominant source of latency, contributing approximately 74.7% of VLM latency. The computational cost of these GEMM-dominated operations scales linearly with the number of input tokens (visual and context tokens) when model dimensions are fixed. This implies that reducing the number of input tokens can significantly alleviate the computational load.
- **Hardware Limitations:** Existing edge platforms, such as the Jetson AGX Orin, often reach or exceed their effective compute limits even at moderate control frequencies (e.g., 5 Hz) for current VLA models. This highlights the need for either more powerful edge hardware or more efficient model architectures, or a combination of both.

Motivated by these observations, the project aims to co-design the RhinoVLA model with the Huixi R1 edge SoC. The goal is to develop a VLM backbone that is token-efficient while retaining strong multimodal reasoning capabilities, and to optimize the VLA system's execution specifically for the R1's high computational headroom.

### 4. Heterogeneity in Cross-Robot Training Data

- **Lack of Unified Interface:** Robot datasets are inherently diverse, varying in camera layouts, viewing orders, sensing modalities, action definitions, end effectors, and low-level control conventions. Directly pooling such heterogeneous data for training a shared policy poses a significant challenge. For instance, the same image index might correspond to different camera roles across datasets, or the same action dimension might represent different physical quantities, leading to an ill-defined learning problem.
- **Impediments to Generalization:** The absence of a unified input-output interface hinders the ability of VLA models to learn shared visuomotor structures across different robot embodiments. This necessitates robot-specific policy development or complex data preprocessing, increasing development costs and limiting the generalizability of learned policies.

To address this, RhinoVLA aims to introduce a unified interface and a unified pre-training strategy. This involves mechanisms to explicitly align heterogeneous robot observations and action schemas, allowing for the development of a single policy that can be adapted to various robotic platforms efficiently.

In summary, the motivation is to overcome critical practical barriers to VLA deployment: computational inefficiency on edge hardware and the challenges of learning from diverse robot data, thereby advancing VLA models towards more general-purpose and real-world applicability.

### 5. Methodology and Approach

RhinoVLA employs an algorithm-system co-design methodology, integrating architectural choices for model efficiency with hardware-aware optimizations for deployment on the Huixi R1 edge SoC, alongside a unified framework for cross-robot training.

#### 5.1. Algorithm Design: Token Efficiency and Unified Interface

- **VLA Architecture Decomposition:** RhinoVLA adopts a two-module structure, similar to `π0.5`, consisting of a visual-language backbone for perception and instruction understanding, and an Action Expert for continuous action generation via flow matching.
- **Token-Efficient VLM Backbone:** To reduce the VLM's computational burden, RhinoVLA utilizes a 2.13B-parameter `Qwen3-VL` model as its visual-language backbone. This choice is motivated by `Qwen3-VL`'s ability to represent a 256x256 image with approximately 64 merged visual tokens, a four-fold reduction compared to the 256 tokens used by `PaliGemma-224` for a 224x224 image in `π0.5`. This directly targets the identified MLP GEMM bottleneck by reducing the number of input tokens.
- **Action Expert Redesign:** The Action Expert comprises 0.40B parameters, comparable in scale to `π0.5`'s expert. It is redesigned with Qwen-compatible transformer components and conditions on the last 18 layers of the `Qwen3-VL` KV cache, current 72D robot state, state/action masks, a noisy action chunk, flow-matching time, and the robot-instance index. It predicts a flow velocity over a unified 72D action slot space.
- **Unified Cross-Robot Training Framework:** To address data heterogeneity, RhinoVLA integrates three mechanisms:
 - **View Registry:** This component explicitly tags each image with its camera role and modality (e.g., `[head|rgb]`, `[left_wrist|depth]`) during preprocessing. This decouples camera identity from dataset-specific image order, enabling consistent interpretation of multi-view observations across diverse robots.
 - **Unified 72D Physical State-Action Slot Space with Binary Masks:** A fixed 72-dimensional vector space is defined, where each slot has a consistent physical meaning across all datasets (e.g., arm joint angles, gripper closed ratio, base velocities). Binary state and action masks are used to indicate which slots are active or valid for a given robot and sample, preventing spurious supervision for physically absent or irrelevant degrees of freedom. This provides a shared semantic contract for robot state and action.
 - **Robot-Instance LoRA Adapters:** To capture embodiment-specific residual variations (e.g., calibration, joint limits) without introducing robot-specific output heads, low-rank adaptation (LoRA) modules are inserted into the feed-forward networks of the Action Expert layers. A shared Action Expert learns common visuomotor structure, while individual LoRAs, selected by an `instance_id`, learn small, robot-specific corrections. This design ensures a unified deployment graph, sparse adapter activation, and low-cost extension to new robots.

#### 5.2. Training Strategy

- **Data Standardization:** Heterogeneous robot demonstration data from sources like Open X-Embodiment and AgiBotWorld are converted to conform to the View Registry, 72D physical slot space, and masking conventions.
- **Pre-training:** The `Qwen3-VL` backbone is frozen. A VLM LoRA (to adapt the VLM to robot views), the shared Action Expert, and the robot-instance LoRAs are jointly optimized. Training batches are sampled using a power-law balancing rule across mixed robot datasets. A masked flow-matching loss is applied, supervising only active action slots. Regularization is applied to the adapter residuals to ensure the instance LoRAs focus on corrections rather than learning the full policy.
- **Post-training and Real-Robot Transfer:** For adaptation to specific real-robot tasks, the visual-language encoder (including the VLM LoRA) and most Action Expert parameters are frozen. Only the target robot's instance LoRA is updated using a small amount of task-specific data, leveraging the residual adaptation mechanism for efficient transfer.

#### 5.3. System Optimization: Efficient Deployment on Huixi R1

RhinoVLA is optimized for real-time execution on the Huixi R1, a 7nm edge SoC with 500 TOPS INT8 compute:

- **Hardware-Aware Compilation:**
 - **Operator-Level Optimization:** FlashAttention-style tiling is adapted for R1's software-managed on-chip scratchpad memory (SPM), optimizing Attention kernels by keeping Q/K/V tiles and intermediate computations on-chip to reduce off-chip memory traffic.
 - **Graph-Level Optimization:** Aggressive operator fusion is applied within VLA Transformer blocks, combining adjacent operations (e.g., normalization, linear projection, activation) into larger execution units to minimize intermediate tensor materialization in DDR and improve energy efficiency.
 - **Runtime-Level Optimization:** Fine-grained operator task scheduling is implemented to flexibly assign heterogeneous operators to compute resources based on their demand and memory characteristics, thereby improving overall chip utilization.
- **Mixed-Precision Deployment:**
 - **W8A16 Quantization:** Weights are quantized to INT8 while activations remain in FP16 (W8A16) to reduce memory bandwidth and storage costs, as W8A8 quantization showed noticeable accuracy degradation.
 - **Custom W8A16 GEMM Kernel:** A specialized kernel for the R1 architecture fuses weight loading, dequantization, and matrix multiplication into a single pipeline. This avoids the overhead of conventional dequantization and leverages R1's multi-core parallelism and memory-channel-aware weight layout for improved efficiency (1.69x speedup observed for the `up_proj` operator).
- **Parallel Visual Encoding:** Instead of sequentially processing multiple camera views (e.g., three image streams), images are batched and processed in parallel by the vision encoder. This increases the effective workload size, improving data reuse and parallel occupancy on R1, reducing the latency for three input images from 34.52 ms to 24.31 ms.

These combined algorithmic and system-level optimizations aim to achieve the target real-time inference speed while maintaining policy performance and facilitating cross-robot generalization.

### 6. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RhinoVLA encompasses training behavior, action prediction accuracy, simulation performance, real-robot deployment, and inference efficiency.

#### 6.1. Model Evaluation

- **Pre-training Stability:** Diagnostics of the masked flow-matching loss during unified pre-training showed stable convergence. Distinct magnitudes and decay rates were observed for per-instance losses on AgiBot G1 and G2, suggesting that the robot-instance LoRA modules effectively handle embodiment-specific variations.
- **Instance LoRA Effectiveness:**
 - **Embodiment Residuals:** A diagnostic comparing LoRA-residual similarity with action-mask Hamming distances demonstrated a correlation: robot pairs with more similar active action supports generally exhibited more similar LoRA residuals. This indicated that the adapters capture embodiment-dependent corrections rather than just dataset identities.
 - **Improved Action Prediction:** An ablation study showed that the inclusion of instance LoRA resulted in small but consistent improvements in masked flow-matching loss (0.0191 vs. 0.0192), arm-joint MAE (0.0440 vs. 0.0446), yaw-rate MAE (0.0194 vs. 0.0195), and gripper MAE (0.1056 vs. 0.1064) on held-out validation data, with negligible impact on base-velocity MAE. This supports the hypothesis that instance LoRA is beneficial for capturing variations in arm and gripper control.
- **Simulation Evaluation (LIBERO Benchmark):**
 - RhinoVLA achieved an average success rate of 94.1% across the LIBERO-Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites using a single, jointly trained checkpoint.
 - This performance surpassed several direct policy baselines (e.g., Diffusion Policy, Octo, MDT) and other VLA models (e.g., OpenVLA, CoT-VLA, π0-FAST, π0).
 - A notable improvement was observed on the challenging LIBERO-Long suite, where RhinoVLA reached 90.4% success, outperforming π0 and π0-FAST by 17.4 and 30.2 percentage points, respectively.
 - Ablation studies indicated that robot pretraining improved the average success rate from 90.0% to 91.8%, and the proposed View Registry contributed an additional 2.3 percentage points, leading to the final 94.1%. While slightly lower than π0.5's 96.9%, the results demonstrate competitive policy performance from a compact VLM backbone.
- **Real-Robot Evaluation:**
 - RhinoVLA was adapted and evaluated on three commercial robot platforms (Galbot G1, AgiBot G2, AgiBot G1) with distinct controllable structures across various manipulation tasks (pick-and-place, bimanual towel-folding).
 - **Galbot G1 (unseen embodiment):** Achieved 100% success rate for "Red bag to the far bin" (matching π0.5), 40% for "Black fan to the middle bin," and 20% for "White foam to the near bin" in unseen settings.
 - **AgiBot G2 (long-horizon pick-and-place):** Demonstrated a 58% success rate in the seen setting and 24% in the unseen setting, surpassing π0.5 by 6 percentage points in the unseen scenario (18%).
 - **AgiBot G1 (bimanual towel-folding):** Achieved 67% success rate in the seen setting and 43% in the unseen setting, indicating robustness in deformable object manipulation.

#### 6.2. Inference Efficiency on Huixi R1

- **End-to-End Performance:** Through algorithm-system co-optimization, RhinoVLA achieved an end-to-end inference frequency of 11.69 Hz on the Huixi R1 SoC, successfully meeting the 10 Hz real-time closed-loop control target.
- **Cumulative Gains from Optimizations:**
 - The unoptimized baseline ran at 1.09 Hz.
 - Compilation optimization improved it to 3.02 Hz.
 - Mixed-precision (W8A16) deployment further increased it to 5.84 Hz.
 - Parallel visual encoding brought the final frequency to 11.69 Hz.
- **Latency Breakdown:** The optimized pipeline's total latency was 85.54 ms. The Vision Encoder for three views accounted for 24.31 ms (28.4%), the VLM Backbone for 20.78 ms (24.3%), and the Action Expert for 36.71 ms (42.9%).

These results collectively indicate that RhinoVLA successfully addresses both the real-time deployment and cross-robot generalization challenges, providing a VLA system with competitive policy performance and efficient edge inference.

### 7. Significance and Potential Impact

The RhinoVLA project presents several significant contributions and has potential impacts across robotic manipulation, VLA model development, and edge AI deployment.

**1. Enabling Real-Time VLA on Edge Hardware:** The most immediate significance is the successful demonstration of a VLA model achieving real-time closed-loop control (11.69 Hz) on a dedicated edge SoC (Huixi R1). This addresses a critical bottleneck that has hindered the practical deployment of capable VLA models on robots, which typically require high-frequency control loops for responsiveness and safety. This achievement suggests a pathway for integrating advanced VLA capabilities into a wider range of autonomous robotic systems operating under power and size constraints.

**2. Validation of Algorithm-System Co-Design:** The project exemplifies the effectiveness of an algorithm-system co-design approach. By specifically tailoring the VLA model architecture (e.g., token-efficient VLM) and its software optimizations (e.g., hardware-aware compilation, mixed-precision, parallel encoding) to the characteristics of the target edge hardware, significant performance gains were realized. This methodology could influence the future development of AI accelerators and specialized models for embodied intelligence, emphasizing the synergy between software and hardware design.

**3. Advancement in Cross-Robot Learning and Generalization:** The introduction of a unified interface comprising the View Registry, a 72D physical state-action slot space with binary masks, and robot-instance LoRA adapters represents a methodical approach to handling the heterogeneity of robot platforms. This framework facilitates the training of shared policies that can generalize across diverse embodiments while allowing for low-cost, embodiment-specific adaptation. This can accelerate data collection efforts by enabling the pooling of data from various robots and reduces the engineering effort required to adapt policies to new robotic systems.

**4. Contribution to Open-Source Robotics:** The commitment to open-sourcing RhinoVLA (https://github.com/HuixiAI/RhinoVLA) has the potential to foster broader research and development in the VLA and robotics communities. An accessible, efficient, and generalizable VLA framework can serve as a baseline or a building block for future work on robot policy learning, real-world deployment, and hardware acceleration.

**5. Future Trajectories for VLA Development:** The work also lays out clear directions for future research, such as enabling full data collection, training, and deployment on the same edge SoC, and integrating with reinforcement learning pipelines for online policy improvement. These planned extensions point towards a more autonomous and self-improving robotic intelligence ecosystem, where robots can continuously learn and adapt in their deployed environments.

In summary, RhinoVLA contributes to overcoming key practical barriers for VLA models, making them more amenable to real-world deployment. Its methodologies for efficiency and generalization hold implications for the design of future VLA models, robotic systems, and the underlying AI hardware.
