# KerJEPA: Kernel Discrepancies for Euclidean Self-Supervised Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> KERJEPA introduces a generalized framework for Euclidean self-supervised learning, building on LeJEPA by employing various kernel discrepancies for regularization. It reveals that LeJEPA's slicing implicitly yields heavy-tailed, dimension-dependent kernels and empirically demonstrates that analytically derived discrepancies improve training stability and convergence, with KSD leveraging non-Gaussian priors effectively and an IMQ kernel achieving 91.90% accuracy on ImageNette.

## Problem

- Existing Euclidean self-supervised learning (SSL) methods, like LeJEPA, suffer from high estimation variance and computational costs due to stochastic projections in their sliced Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) regularization.
- These methods offer limited flexibility, being constrained to specific Gaussian kernels and priors, which restricts the exploration of diverse feature space geometries.
- Approximations involved in integral calculations for regularization introduce additional hyperparameters and potential biases.

## Method

- The KERJEPA framework generalizes regularization in ==Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)== by employing a flexible family of ==kernel discrepancies==, including MMD and Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD).
- Closed-form analytical expressions are derived for 'infinitely' sliced MMD and KSD, eliminating the ==Monte Carlo variance== associated with stochastic projections.
- ==KSD== is introduced as a novel regularizer that utilizes only the score function of the prior distribution, enabling the use of a wider range of non-Gaussian priors (e.g., Laplace) and alternative kernels like Inverse Multiquadric (IMQ).

## Results

- Various KERJEPA variants achieved performance comparable to or superior to LeJEPA on ImageNette, with an unsliced KSD using an IMQ kernel and Gaussian prior reaching the highest top-1 accuracy of **91.90%** (compared to LeJEPA's **91.13%**).
- Analytically sliced MMD and KSD variants consistently exhibited improved training stability and faster convergence rates across different projector dimensions due to the elimination of Monte Carlo variance.
- Unsliced KSD demonstrated its effectiveness with non-Gaussian priors (e.g., Laplace), outperforming finite-sliced MMD in these scenarios by directly leveraging the prior's score function.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LeJEPA's SIGReg regularizer is theoretically equivalent to an MMD using a specific heavy-tailed, dimension-dependent kernel, demonstrating that finite slicing implicitly induces a unique feature space geometry.
> - The variance from finite Monte Carlo slicing significantly impacts training stability and convergence, especially in higher embedding dimensions, which is consistently alleviated by using analytically derived discrepancies.
> - KSD offers a powerful and flexible approach for enforcing diverse target prior distributions on embeddings, particularly for non-Gaussian priors, without requiring approximations from integral estimation or target sampling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the rapidly evolving field of **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)**, particularly focusing on **Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)** for learning useful, general representations from unlabeled data. SSL aims to overcome the bottleneck of labeled data by having models learn intrinsic data structures, which is crucial for building powerful foundation models.

Historically, many successful SSL methods, such as SimCLR and MoCo (contrastive methods), or BYOL and Barlow Twins (non-contrastive methods), have focused on learning representations constrained to a **unit hypersphere**. This constraint helps stabilize training, bound gradients, and implicitly encourages a uniform distribution of features over the sphere, maximizing information entropy.

However, a recent shift in the SSL landscape, spearheaded by algorithms like **VICReg** and notably **LeJEPA**, advocates for learning **Euclidean representations** (i.e., moving "off the hypersphere"). LeJEPA, a direct predecessor to KerJEPA, argued that an isotropic Gaussian distribution over embeddings is optimal for downstream regression and classification tasks, especially with weight decay, a geometry unattainable with hyperspherical representations. LeJEPA achieves this by introducing a regularizer called SIGReg, which essentially approximates a Sliced Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a Gaussian prior and Gaussian kernel, applied to randomly sampled univariate projections of high-dimensional embeddings.

The current paper, KerJEPA, directly addresses key limitations inherent in LeJEPA and similar sliced MMD approaches:
1. **Estimation Variance and Computational Cost:** LeJEPA's reliance on stochastic projections for the Epps-Pulley test introduces gradient noise, especially in high-dimensional settings, requiring an increased number of projections, which undermines computational efficiency.
2. **Rigidity in Kernel and Prior Choice:** LeJEPA's approach is specialized to Gaussian priors and a fixed Gaussian kernel, limiting exploration of alternative regularizers, non-Gaussian priors, or heavy-tailed kernels that might offer superior geometric properties for diverse downstream tasks.
3. **Approximation Bias:** The integral approximations via quadrature in LeJEPA introduce additional hyperparameters and potential bias.

KerJEPA builds upon the foundation of kernel methods, specifically Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD), which are powerful tools for comparing probability distributions by embedding them into Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS). It aims to generalize the regularization component of LeJEPA, drawing from the rich body of work on kernel-based discrepancies and their ability to capture full distributional differences. By tackling these issues, KerJEPA seeks to provide a more flexible, robust, and theoretically grounded framework for Euclidean self-supervised learning, pushing the boundaries of how representations are regularized and understood.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the KerJEPA framework are multifaceted, aiming to address the limitations of existing Euclidean SSL methods, particularly LeJEPA, and to expand the algorithmic design space for practitioners:

1. **Generalize Euclidean Self-Supervised Learning Regularization:** The overarching goal is to introduce a flexible family of self-supervised learning algorithms, KerJEPAs, that can accommodate a wider array of kernel-based regularizers beyond the specific instance employed by LeJEPA (i.e., the sliced Epps-Pulley test, which approximates a sliced MMD with a Gaussian kernel and Gaussian prior).
2. **Expand the Space of Viable Kernels and Priors:** A core motivation is to allow practitioners to use various kernels (e.g., Gaussian, Inverse Multiquadric (IMQ) with heavy tails) and diverse prior distributions (e.g., Gaussian, Laplace, Student-t). This flexibility enables tailoring the regularization to specific geometric properties desired for downstream tasks, moving beyond the rigidity of fixed Gaussian kernels and priors.
3. **Improve Training Stability and Design Flexibility:** Address the tension between estimation variance and computational cost inherent in LeJEPA's stochastic slicing approach. By developing alternative KerJEPAs, the authors aim to achieve improved training stability and greater flexibility in algorithm design, especially in high-dimensional embedding regimes.
4. **Deeply Study Computational and Statistical Tradeoffs:** Provide a comprehensive analysis of the computational and statistical implications of different regularization strategies. This includes investigating the impact of slicing (finite vs. analytic/infinite slices), minibatch size, and embedding dimension on training dynamics and performance.
5. **Unveil a Family of Discrepancies for Regularization:** Introduce and investigate Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD) as a novel and powerful regularizer in the SSL context. KSD offers the advantage of eliminating the need for target sampling by relying solely on the score function of the prior, making it particularly suitable for arbitrary prior distributions.
6. **Derive Analytical Forms for Sliced Discrepancies:** A significant objective is to compute the closed-form high-dimensional limits of sliced MMDs and KSDs. This theoretical contribution allows for regularization without the Monte Carlo variance associated with finite random projections, offering more stable and potentially faster convergence.
7. **Provide Guidelines for Informed Algorithm Design:** Based on theoretical insights and empirical analysis, offer practical guidelines for navigating the complex tradeoffs in high-dimensional distributional regularization for SSL, enabling practitioners to make informed decisions about algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning.

In essence, KerJEPA aims to transform LeJEPA from a specific, albeit successful, algorithm into a broader, more robust framework, providing a "toolbox" of regularizers that can be adapted to various SSL scenarios and computational environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of KerJEPA revolves around generalizing the regularization component of Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) using a flexible family of kernel discrepancies. The paper follows a rigorous theoretical and empirical approach:

1. **Formalizing the SSL Objective:** The work starts by defining a general batch-level self-supervised objective function: $L(\theta) = \frac{1}{n|P_l|} \sum_{l=1}^n \sum_{(i,j)\in P_l} L_{align}(f_\theta(v_i^l), f_\theta(v_j^l)) + \lambda\Omega(Z; Q)$. Here, $L_{align}$ minimizes the distance between embeddings of positive pairs (e.g., MSE), and $\Omega(Z; Q)$ is the kernel-based regularization term, enforcing structure on the batch of embeddings $Z$ towards an isotropic prior $Q$.

2. **Kernel Discrepancies as Regularizers:**
- **Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD):** The paper re-establishes MMD as a fundamental measure for comparing distributions $P$ and $Q$ via their kernel mean embeddings in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). It explicitly connects LeJEPA's SIGReg (based on the Epps-Pulley test) to a specific MMD with a Gaussian kernel and prior, thus framing SIGReg as an instance of KerJEPA. This connection lays the groundwork for generalizing beyond LeJEPA's specific formulation.
- **Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD):** KSD is introduced as a novel and powerful alternative. Unlike MMD, KSD only requires the score function (gradient of the log-density) of the target prior $Q$, eliminating the need for sampling from $Q$ or computing normalization constants. This makes KSD particularly advantageous for a broader family of non-Gaussian priors (e.g., Laplace, Student-t). The paper provides the definition of the Stein kernel and its spectral representation, highlighting its theoretical advantages.

3. **Addressing Slicing and Its Tradeoffs:**
- **Slicing as a Computational Strategy:** The concept of Sliced Integral Probability Metrics (SIPMs) is explained, leveraging the Cramér-Wold theorem to compare high-dimensional distributions by averaging discrepancies over one-dimensional random projections. This traditionally reduces computational complexity from quadratic to linear in sample size (per slice).
- **Analytic Sliced Discrepancies:** A core theoretical contribution is the derivation of closed-form expressions for "infinitely" sliced MMD and KSD for an isotropic Gaussian prior.
 - For MMD, SIGReg is proven equivalent to an MMD with a specific heavy-tailed, dimension-dependent kernel characterized by the Kummer Confluent Hypergeometric function ($_1F_1$). This means that finite slicing implicitly induces a heavier-tailed similarity measure.
 - For KSD, a similar closed-form is derived for SKSD (Sliced KSD) with a Gaussian kernel and isotropic Gaussian prior, also involving Kummer functions.
- **Unbiased Estimators without Slicing:** The paper introduces unbiased U-statistics for these newly derived analytic sliced discrepancies (MMD and KSD). While these estimators eliminate the Monte Carlo variance of finite slicing, they incur a quadratic computational cost and require evaluations of the complex Kummer function, which can be approximated by Inverse Multiquadric (IMQ) kernels for high dimensions ($d \ge 20$).

4. **Experimental Setup and Ablation:**
- **Dataset:** ImageNette, a smaller version of ImageNet, allowing for faster experimentation.
- **Architecture:** ViT-s/8 (Vision Transformer small with patch size 8) as the backbone, combined with a projector head.
- **Training Details:** Single 80GB-A100 GPU, AdamW optimizer, BF16 precision, 800 epochs, cosine learning rate schedule, batch size 256, 4 views per image.
- **Hyperparameters:** Projector output dimension of 128. For finite-sliced methods, 1024 slices and 21 quadrature points. Standard augmentation pipeline (random crops, color jitter, blur, grayscale, solarization).
- **Ablation Studies:** Extensive grid search over kernel bandwidth and regularization weight. Performance is measured by top-1 accuracy using an online instance-normalized linear probe.
- **Comparative Analysis:** Experiments compare LeJEPA (finite sliced MMD with Gaussian kernel/prior) against various KerJEPA variants:
 - Unsliced MMD (Gaussian kernel, Gaussian prior)
 - Analytic Sliced MMD (Gaussian kernel, Gaussian prior)
 - Finite Sliced MMD (Gaussian kernel, Laplace prior)
 - Unsliced KSD (Gaussian/IMQ kernels, Gaussian/Laplace priors)
 - Analytic Sliced KSD (Gaussian kernel, Gaussian prior)
 - Finite Sliced KSD (Gaussian kernel, Gaussian/Laplace priors)
- **Impact of Slicing and Dimension:** Dedicated experiments investigate how the number of slices (16, 128, 1024 vs. infinite analytic) and embedding dimensions (16, 128, 1024) affect test accuracy and training stability across different training horizons (100, 300, 800 epochs).

This systematic approach, combining theoretical derivations with comprehensive empirical validation, allows KerJEPA to establish a generalized framework and provide concrete insights into the behavior and tradeoffs of different kernel-based regularization strategies in Euclidean SSL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key theoretical derivations and empirical findings that reshape the understanding of Euclidean self-supervised learning regularization:

1. **Theoretical Equivalence of SIGReg and MMD:**
- The paper formally proves that LeJEPA's SIGReg regularizer, previously understood as an Epps-Pulley test on random projections, is mathematically equivalent to an MMD with a specific, complex kernel ($\kappa_d$) (Theorem 7). This kernel, expressed using the Kummer Confluent Hypergeometric function ($_1F_1$), exhibits **polynomially decaying tails** (heavy-tailed) unlike the exponentially decaying Gaussian kernel, and is **dimension-dependent**. This finding is crucial as it reveals the implicit heavy-tailed geometry induced by the slicing operation in LeJEPA.

2. **Unbiased Estimators Without Slicing (Analytic Sliced Discrepancies):**
- For the equivalent MMD (representing SIGReg), the authors derive an unbiased U-statistic estimator ($\hat{\text{MMD-Gauss}}_{\kappa_d}$) that entirely avoids Monte Carlo sampling of slice directions (Theorem 8). This eliminates the variance introduced by stochastic slicing.
- Similarly, a closed-form, unbiased U-statistic estimator is derived for the Sliced Kernel Stein Discrepancy (SKSD) with a Gaussian prior (Theorem 9).
- **Tradeoff:** While these analytic methods offer exact computation (no Monte Carlo variance) and unbiased estimation (for MMD, up to practical approximations of $F_1$), they incur a **quadratic computational cost** ($O(n^2)$) due to pairwise kernel evaluations, contrasting with the linear cost ($O(n)$) of finite sliced methods. Moreover, computing the $F_1$ function can be expensive, though it can be approximated by simpler Inverse Multiquadric (IMQ) kernels for high dimensions ($d \ge 20$).

3. **Empirical Performance of KerJEPA Variants (Table 1):**
- **General Viability:** Across the board, various MMD and KSD KerJEPA variants perform similarly to LeJEPA on ImageNette, demonstrating the utility and robustness of the generalized framework.
- **KSD Advantage for Non-Gaussian Priors:** When targeting non-Gaussian priors like Laplace, the unsliced KSD consistently outperforms the finite-sliced MMD, which relies on quadrature approximations. This validates KSD's theoretical advantage in directly leveraging the score function of arbitrary priors without approximation errors from integral estimation or target sampling.
- **IMQ Kernel Performance:** Unsliced KSD with an IMQ kernel and Gaussian prior achieves the highest accuracy (91.90% vs. LeJEPA's 91.13%), suggesting that the heavy-tailed nature of the IMQ kernel can be beneficial for downstream performance. This aligns with the theoretical insight that slicing implicitly induces heavy tails.

4. **Impact of Finite Slicing (Figure 2 and Appendix I):**
- **Variance and Convergence:** Experiments consistently show that the variance introduced by finite Monte Carlo slicing significantly increases with the embedding dimension. Insufficient slicing (fewer slices) leads to slower convergence rates and introduces instability in the early phases of training, particularly in higher dimensions.
- **Stability of Analytic Slicing:** The analytically sliced variations of both MMD and KSD (representing infinite slices) consistently exhibit superior stability and faster convergence, irrespective of the projector output dimension. This confirms that the variance from finite slicing is a real practical issue, and its elimination leads to more robust training.
- **Dimension Invariance:** The choice of projector output dimension itself does not inherently impact performance when the slicing variance is mitigated (i.e., with analytically sliced methods).

In summary, the main findings highlight that LeJEPA implicitly uses a heavy-tailed kernel, that KerJEPA successfully generalizes the regularization to a wider array of kernels and priors using both MMD and KSD, and that the variance from finite slicing is a critical factor impacting training stability and convergence, especially in high dimensions. Analytically derived (or high-dimension approximated) discrepancies offer a path to more stable and efficient Euclidean SSL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The KerJEPA framework and its associated findings have several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of self-supervised learning:

1. **Unified and Flexible Framework for Euclidean SSL:** KerJEPA provides a foundational and generalized framework that unifies various kernel-based regularization strategies for learning Euclidean representations. This shifts the paradigm from ad-hoc regularization techniques to a principled "toolbox" where practitioners can select specific kernel, prior, and discrepancy combinations tailored to their data, architectural choices, and downstream objectives. This significantly expands the algorithmic design space for SSL.

2. **Deeper Theoretical Understanding of Regularization:** The explicit connection drawn between LeJEPA's SIGReg and a specific MMD with a heavy-tailed, dimension-dependent kernel is a profound theoretical insight. It reveals the implicit geometry imposed by the slicing operation, highlighting that popular methods implicitly encourage heavy-tailed similarity measures. This understanding can guide future theoretical advancements and the deliberate design of kernels with desired properties.

3. **Improved Training Stability and Convergence:** The finding that variance from finite Monte Carlo slicing significantly impacts training stability and convergence, especially in high dimensions, is a critical practical contribution. The derivation of analytic sliced discrepancies (or their high-dimension IMQ approximations) provides a pathway to more stable and potentially faster training by eliminating this variance. This can lead to more reliable and efficient development of SSL models.

4. **Versatility of Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD):** The introduction and validation of KSD in the SSL context is highly impactful. KSD's ability to match arbitrary priors via their score functions, without requiring samples from the prior or complex integral approximations (as often needed by MMD for non-Gaussian priors), makes it a powerful and flexible tool. This opens avenues for exploring a much broader range of target geometries for latent embeddings, potentially leading to representations better suited for specific tasks.

5. **Informed Computational Tradeoffs:** The detailed analysis of computational tradeoffs between linear-time finite slicing (with variance) and quadratic-time analytic slicing (without variance) provides essential guidance for practitioners. It prompts a re-evaluation of when the theoretical linear scaling of slicing is truly beneficial, particularly in light of batch size, dimension, and training stability. This awareness enables more judicious resource allocation and hyperparameter tuning in large-scale SSL training.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** The paper inspires several critical future research directions. It prompts further investigation into the optimal choice of kernels and priors for different data modalities and downstream tasks. It also encourages exploring alternative approximations for complex functions like Kummer's, the use of Random Fourier Features for reducing quadratic complexity, and the application of these robust regularization techniques to larger datasets and more complex transfer learning scenarios (e.g., detection, segmentation). The observation that prior choice had negligible impact in some experiments also raises questions about the direct impact of target geometry versus the favorable learning dynamics induced by Euclidean gradients.

In conclusion, KerJEPA makes significant strides in formalizing, generalizing, and improving Euclidean self-supervised learning. By offering a principled framework, revealing hidden geometric properties, and providing tools for more stable training, it empowers researchers and practitioners to design more effective and robust SSL algorithms, pushing closer to the goal of building truly generalizable foundation models.
