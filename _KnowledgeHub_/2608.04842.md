# RORA: Realistic Object Reconstruction with Articulation

> [!summary] Summary
> RORA reconstructs simulation-ready articulated 3D assets from a single static video input by integrating photorealistic 3D Gaussian Splatting with mesh-based geometry for physical interaction, outperforming state-of-the-art methods in kinematic accuracy and visual quality for complex articulations.

## Problem

- The 'sim-to-real gap' in robot learning struggles with accurately representing and interacting with articulated objects in photorealistic simulations.
- Existing motion-based methods require extensive, multi-state observations of object movement, which is impractical and time-consuming.
- Learning-based articulation methods are dependent on their training data and often fail when encountering novel or 'out-of-distribution' real-world objects.

## Method

- **RORA** is a semi-automated, ==human-in-the-loop (HIL) pipeline== that reconstructs articulated objects from a single static video input.
- Generates a ==hybrid representation== combining ==3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)== for photorealistic rendering and mesh-based geometry for physical interaction.
- Features a geometry-driven ==Automatic Joint Suggestion Algorithm== and user-guided refinement to define joint axes and types, then exports to a simulation-ready URDF format.

## Results

- Outperformed state-of-the-art baselines (Articulate-Anything, ScrewSplat) on the PartNet-Mobility benchmark in geometry metrics (angular and positional error), especially for complex multi-joint and chained linkage categories.
- Achieved superior visual quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS) across all categories, ensuring visually consistent rendering even in unseen articulated configurations.
- Demonstrated comparable total runtime (**7 min 23 sec** average) to fully automated baselines, with active human interaction consuming only **42 seconds**, and successfully deployed assets in robotic simulation platforms like NVIDIA Isaac Sim for manipulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A geometry-driven approach with minimal human input can robustly identify complex kinematic structures, including multi-joint and chained linkages, from static visual data.
> - Integrating photorealistic 3DGS rendering with physically interactive mesh geometry within a single articulated asset is crucial for effective sim-to-real transfer.
> - A HIL workflow that automates initial steps and guides human feedback for articulation can achieve high accuracy and efficiency, overcoming limitations of purely automated or manual methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the broader research landscape addressing the "sim-to-real gap" in robot learning. This gap refers to the disparity between models or policies trained in simulated environments and their performance when transferred to real-world robotic systems. A common strategy to mitigate this gap involves creating increasingly realistic simulations that accurately replicate real-world environments. Recent advancements in visually realistic scene representation, such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), have shown promise in enhancing photorealism within simulations, thereby improving sim-to-real transfer success rates and data efficiency for robot learning.

However, a significant challenge remains in accurately representing *articulated objects*—objects with movable parts—within these photorealistic simulations. Many real-world objects possess internal kinematic structures (joints), and merely rendering them as static entities is insufficient for training robots to interact with them effectively. Existing methods for reconstructing articulated objects fall into two main categories: motion-based and learning-based (often utilizing Vision-Language Models, VLMs).

Motion-based approaches typically infer joint properties by observing an object's movement across multiple configurations. While capable of modeling joints, these methods often require extensive, multi-state observations, where users must manually capture the object in various joint states. This process can be impractical for real-to-sim workflows, particularly for complex objects with multiple or chained joints where movement dependencies are intricate. Failures often occur on multi-joint or hierarchically linked objects where one joint's trajectory affects others.

Learning-based methods, including those leveraging VLMs, aim to automate articulation by drawing upon semantic knowledge from trained datasets. These approaches often formulate actor-critic loops or use open-vocabulary part segmentation with visual clues to generate joint parameters. However, their primary limitation is dependence on their training data and underlying model libraries. Consequently, real-world objects that are "out-of-distribution" relative to these datasets can lead to failures in link placement and joint allocation.

RORA addresses these limitations by proposing an end-to-end pipeline that reconstructs simulation-ready articulated assets from a single static video input. It aims to overcome the data capture complexity of motion-based methods and the dataset dependency of learning-based approaches through a geometry-driven, human-in-the-loop (HIL) process. This approach seeks to provide a practical, accurate, and efficient solution for generating articulated digital twins of arbitrary real-world objects for use in robotic simulation, directly integrating photorealistic 3DGS rendering with physically interactive mesh geometry.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop and validate an end-to-end pipeline capable of reconstructing simulation-ready, articulated 3D assets from a single static video input of a real object. This reconstruction aims to produce a hybrid representation combining photorealistic 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for rendering and mesh-based geometry for physical interaction within simulation environments.

The motivation for this research is multi-faceted:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** In data-driven robot learning, reducing the sim-to-real gap is crucial for achieving robust policies and enabling zero-shot transfer from simulation to reality. Replicating real-world environments with high visual fidelity and accurate physical properties in simulation is an effective strategy. While 3DGS has demonstrated success in photorealistic scene rendering to enhance sim-to-real transfer, the ability to incorporate object articulation has been a persistent challenge.
- **Addressing the Need for Articulated Assets:** Many real-world manipulation tasks involve objects with internal kinematics (e.g., doors, drawers, tools with handles). Current sim-to-real pipelines largely focus on static object representations, which restricts the scope of learnable robotic behaviors. A framework that accurately models articulation is essential for training robots to interact with a broader range of objects.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Articulation Methods:**
 - **Data Acquisition Complexity:** Prior motion-based articulation methods require capturing objects in multiple articulated states or during dynamic movement. This process is time-consuming, requires specialized setups, and is particularly challenging for objects with complex kinematic structures (e.g., multi-joint or chained linkages), often leading to low success rates. The motivation here is to simplify the scanning process to a single static video.
 - **Accuracy and Robustness:** Both motion-based and learning-based methods frequently exhibit low accuracy when dealing with complex kinematic structures. Learning-based approaches, while automated, are often constrained by their training datasets, leading to failures or inaccuracies when encountering novel or out-of-distribution real-world objects. The paper aims for a method that is robust across various object types and complexities, reducing dependence on large, specific datasets.
 - **Operational Bottlenecks:** Manual creation of articulated assets is labor-intensive and slow. While automated methods exist, their reliability on complex real-world objects is limited. The motivation is to provide a semi-automated, human-in-the-loop (HIL) process that offers efficiency comparable to fully automated methods while maintaining high accuracy through guided user feedback.
- **Facilitating Robot Learning and Manipulation Research:** By generating simulation-ready assets that can be directly imported into platforms like NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Unreal Engine, RORA aims to expedite data collection and environment setup. This would allow researchers to focus more on robot learning algorithms rather than on asset creation, ultimately advancing research in dexterous manipulation and humanoid robotics.
- **Community Contribution:** The authors intend to open-source their code and establish an asset storage platform to foster collaboration and distribution of reconstructed assets within the robotics community.

In summary, the core motivation is to provide a practical, accurate, and efficient solution for translating static video captures of real objects into fully articulated, photorealistic digital models suitable for advanced robotic simulation and learning, thereby streamlining the creation of realistic interactive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RORA proposes a semi-automated, human-in-the-loop (HIL) pipeline that reconstructs articulated objects from a single static video input. The framework exports a hybrid representation combining 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for photorealistic rendering and mesh-based geometry for physical interaction. The methodology consists of six sequential stages: Initial Reconstruction, Refinement, Decomposition, Segmentation, Articulation, and Export.

#### 3.1. Initial Reconstruction Stage

The pipeline begins by generating an initial 3DGS representation and a corresponding mesh from a single video using the GS2Mesh pipeline. GS2Mesh optimizes 3DGS to produce stereo images, from which a mesh is generated using the marching cubes algorithm based on depth information. To isolate the target object from its background, Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) is employed to generate filtering masks. Gaussians whose projections fall within the SAM2 mask for a number of views exceeding a configurable visibility threshold are identified as belonging to the object.

#### 3.2. Refinement Stage

The initially reconstructed mesh often contains imperfections such as holes (e.g., at the bottom due to occlusion) and floating fragments. This stage transforms the incomplete mesh into a watertight surface, a prerequisite for subsequent geometric analysis. The refinement process includes:
1. **Spatial Alignment:** The plane normal of any bottom hole is computed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The mesh is then reoriented to an upright position by aligning this normal with the global z-axis and moving its center to the global origin.
2. **Geometry Cleaning:** A series of steps are applied:
 - Filtering out small, isolated fragments.
 - Reconstructing the surface using Screened Poisson Reconstruction.
 - Removing vertices below the ground plane (z < 0).
 - Reducing face count with Quadric Edge Collapse Decimation.
 - Applying Isotropic Remeshing for uniform triangle quality.
 - Flattening the bottom region and filling large ground-contact holes.
 - Performing global hole-filling to ensure watertightness.
 - Restoring vertex properties from the original mesh.

#### 3.3. Decomposition Stage

The watertight mesh is decomposed into multiple convex fragments. This stage operates under the assumption that movable subparts often manifest as geometrically extruded components. Approximate Convex Decomposition (ACD) is used to divide the mesh into non-overlapping convex fragments that collectively cover the entire object.

#### 3.4. Segmentation Stage (Human-in-the-Loop)

This stage addresses the limitations of fully automated segmentation models, which can struggle with part-level semantics when subparts share similar visual appearances. A lightweight human-in-the-loop (HIL) process is introduced:
- A user interactively assigns group numbers to the decomposed convex fragments, combining them into semantically meaningful subparts.
- Vertices of the original mesh are mapped to their corresponding fragment groups; any uncovered vertices are assigned to the nearest group based on L2 distance.
- 3D Gaussian points are then bound to these defined mesh subparts using a k-nearest-neighbors (KNN) voting strategy. Each Gaussian identifies its k-closest mesh vertices and adopts the majority part ID, establishing a unified part segmentation for both mesh and 3DGS representations.

#### 3.5. Articulation Stage (Human-in-the-Loop - Automatic Joint Suggestion Algorithm)

After part segmentation, an Automatic Joint Suggestion Algorithm proposes joint candidates to minimize manual effort. This systematic workflow involves four key steps:

1. **Boundary Detection:** For each segmented subpart *i*, a set of boundary cycles *C_i* is extracted. These cycles consist of vertices belonging to subpart *i* and adjacent to another subpart *j*. An induced subgraph is constructed, and vertices with a degree less than two are pruned. Depth-First Search (DFS) enumerates candidate cycles, and a greedy selection strategy retains unique cycles based on edge length and a uniqueness threshold.
2. **Axis Estimation:** For each boundary cycle *c* in *C_i*, a unidirectional connection *u_a* = (*o_a*, *n_a*, *x_a*) is formed:
 - *o_a*: Joint origin, defined as the centroid of cycle vertices.
 - *n_a*: Normal vector, derived from single-plane estimation. A linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) is fitted to the local geometry around the cycle, with its normalized normal vector defining *n*.
 - *x_a*: Intersection vector, derived from multi-plane estimation for corner geometries. An Expectation-Maximization (EM) framework coordinates two hyperplanes to identify an intersection line, representing *x*.
3. **Bidirectional Fusion:** Since boundaries are detected independently per subpart, unidirectional connections between subpart *i* and *j* are paired. Each paired connection is merged into a bidirectional connection *B* = (*o*, *n*, *x*, *p*), where *o*, *n*, and *x* are averaged from the paired unidirectional connections. The cross-product vector *p* = *n* × *x* is computed, and these three vectors (*n*, *x*, *p*) serve as primary candidate axes for joint suggestion.
4. **Joint Suggestion (Heuristics for Multi-Boundary Cases):** For complex topologies where a single subpart pair (*i*, *j*) has multiple boundary connections, additional joint candidates are generated using geometric heuristics:
 - **Two Connections:** An additional vector is suggested at the midpoint of the two connection origins, with its axis oriented along the line connecting them.
 - **Three or More Connections:** The joint origin is placed at the arithmetic mean of all connection origins, and its axis is aligned with the average of their normal vectors.

#### 3.6. Export Stage (Human-in-the-Loop)

In the final stage, the estimated joints and hybrid representation are converted into a simulation-ready asset. An interactive Graphical User Interface (GUI) presents the suggested joint candidates to the user, allowing them to:
- Select the active joint axis.
- Assign the joint type (fixed, revolute, or prismatic).
- Adjust minor spatial deviations of the joint placement.
After user confirmation, the system automatically generates a Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) file, which encodes the articulated kinematics. Concurrently, the 3D Gaussian Splats are bound to their corresponding mesh subparts and registered to their respective kinematic links. This enables photorealistic rendering of the articulated asset within physics simulation environments while maintaining physical interaction capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of the RORA framework was evaluated through quantitative benchmarks on a synthetic dataset and qualitative demonstrations with real-world objects, followed by deployment in robotic simulation platforms.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Evaluation on PartNet-Mobility Benchmark

The pipeline was benchmarked against two state-of-the-art articulation baselines: Articulate-Anything (AA), a VLM-prior-based method, and ScrewSplat, a motion-based method leveraging screw theory with 3D Gaussians. The evaluation used the PartNet-Mobility dataset, categorized by kinematic complexity: Category I (Single Joint, 6 items), Category II (Multiple Joints, 6 items), and Category III (Chained Linkage, 4 items).
Notably, RORA utilized only 48 multi-view images of a *single static configuration*, whereas AA required a 15-second continuous motion video, and ScrewSplat needed 240 multi-view images across 5 discrete articulated states.

**1. Geometry Metrics:** RORA demonstrated superior performance across all categories compared to both baselines.
- **Category I (Simple):** All methods showed acceptable performance, but RORA achieved the lowest angular error (0.220°) and positional error (0.005m), with zero type errors.
- **Category II (Multiple Joints):** RORA maintained low errors (Type: 0.167, Angle: 1.425°, Pos: 0.066m), while AA and ScrewSplat showed significantly higher errors (e.g., ScrewSplat: Type: 2.000, Angle: 39.953°, Pos: 0.549m).
- **Category III (Chained Linkage):** This category, involving hierarchically dependent joints, posed the greatest challenge. RORA successfully reconstructed most physical joints with minimal errors (Type: 0.000, Angle: 0.849°, Pos: 0.028m). In contrast, both AA and ScrewSplat exhibited catastrophic failures (e.g., AA: Type: 1.500, Angle: 22.545°, Pos: 1.367m; ScrewSplat: Type: 2.000, Angle: 62.820°, Pos: 0.636m), often failing to isolate cascading dependencies.

**2. Visual Metrics:** RORA consistently achieved the best performance across all visual metrics: Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS).
- RORA showed the highest PSNR, SSIM, and lowest LPIPS across all categories (e.g., Cat. III: PSNR: 29.606 dB, SSIM: 0.983, LPIPS: 0.017).
- The performance degradation in baselines was attributed to failed joint estimations. AA often retrieved mismatched meshes, leading to visual discrepancies when joints were moved. ScrewSplat produced high-quality renderings in zero configuration but struggled to capture kinematic structures accurately, resulting in lower scores in complex categories. RORA's accurate segmentation and articulation, coupled with 3DGS rendering, yielded visually consistent results even in unseen configurations.

**3. Runtime Comparison:** Despite incorporating a human-in-the-loop process, RORA's total runtime was comparable to fully automated baselines.
- RORA averaged 7 minutes 23 seconds, including initial GS2Mesh generation, user interaction, and system processing.
- Crucially, active human interaction accounted for only 42 seconds due to the suggestion-based approach.
- Articulate-Anything averaged 11 minutes 35 seconds, with significant latency from actor-critic reasoning loops. ScrewSplat averaged 6 minutes 56 seconds.
This demonstrates that RORA achieves high precision without incurring a substantial time penalty typically associated with HIL workflows.

#### 4.2. Real-World Asset Generation and Simulated Robot Manipulation

RORA successfully reconstructed real-world objects, preserving fine visual elements such as text labels and detailed surface textures. The segmented 3DGS representation and URDF meshes accurately replicated real-world kinematic constraints, enabling realistic movements across diverse joint configurations.
- **Qualitative Comparison:** While Articulate-Anything often failed on real-world objects due to out-of-dataset issues and VLM limitations on co-moving joints, and ScrewSplat required precise camera trajectories, RORA proved robust with standard camera devices and complex articulation topologies.
- **Simulation Deployment:** Reconstructed assets were deployed in two distinct simulation platforms to confirm their practical utility:
 - **HERMES Simulator (Unreal Engine 5.3):** Assets were used for dexterous teleoperation with an Inspire Hand via the VIST hand tracking interface, allowing operators to collect demonstration data.
 - **NVIDIA Isaac Sim (Fourior GR-1 platform):** Bimanual robotic hands were controlled in real-time using Meta Quest controllers, enabling natural interaction with objects.
These deployments demonstrate RORA's ability to bridge the domain gap by providing realistic, articulated assets for advanced robotic applications, such as data collection and teleoperation in simulated environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RORA framework represents a significant advancement in the field of real-to-sim object reconstruction, particularly for articulated objects in robotic applications. Its key significance lies in offering an end-to-end pipeline that addresses several long-standing challenges in creating simulation-ready assets.

One of the primary impacts is the **simplification of the data acquisition process**. By successfully reconstructing complex articulated objects from a *single static video input*, RORA eliminates the need for capturing dynamic motions or multiple articulated states. This dramatically reduces the time, effort, and specialized equipment previously required for data collection, making the real-to-sim pipeline more accessible and practical for a wider range of users and scenarios. This reduced operational bottleneck can accelerate the iteration cycle for roboticists and researchers.

The framework's ability to **accurately handle complex kinematic structures**, including multi-joint objects and chained linkages, is a substantial contribution. Previous methods frequently struggled with these complexities, leading to inaccurate joint estimations or failures. RORA's geometry-based Automatic Joint Suggestion Algorithm, combined with human-in-the-loop refinement, provides a robust mechanism for recovering these intricate kinematics, which are prevalent in real-world objects.

The generation of **simulation-ready assets with a hybrid representation** (3DGS for photorealism and meshes for physical interaction) directly addresses the demands of modern robot learning. The photorealistic rendering capabilities of 3DGS minimize visual discrepancies between simulation and reality, a critical factor for successful sim-to-real transfer. Concurrently, the integrated URDF-mesh structures ensure stable and accurate physical interactions within physics engines, allowing for realistic manipulation tasks. This combination supports robust policy optimization and zero-shot transfer for robotic systems.

RORA has the potential to **significantly accelerate data collection and environment setup for robot simulation**. By automating much of the articulated asset creation process, it alleviates the bottleneck of manual asset generation, allowing researchers to rapidly populate simulated environments with high-fidelity, interactive objects. This can lead to more efficient training of robot manipulation policies, particularly for dexterous hands and humanoid robots, by providing diverse and realistic interaction scenarios.

Furthermore, the commitment to **open-sourcing the framework and establishing an asset sharing platform** will foster community collaboration and accelerate the advancement of robotics research. Researchers will be able to share custom, high-fidelity articulated assets, building a collective repository that can benefit various studies in manipulation and interaction.

While the framework has limitations, such as potential difficulties with non-extruded or very small subparts and inherent challenges of Gaussian Splatting with reflective/transparent surfaces, its core contributions pave the way for more efficient and accurate creation of interactive virtual environments. Ultimately, RORA is expected to improve the success rate for articulated object manipulation after sim-to-real transfer, serving as a foundational tool for the next generation of robot learning systems.
