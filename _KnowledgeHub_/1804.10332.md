# Sim-to-Real: Learning Agile Locomotion For Quadruped Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Agile galloping and trotting gaits for quadruped robots were successfully learned in simulation by Google Brain, X, and DeepMind and directly transferred to a real Minitaur robot without real-world fine-tuning. The learned policies achieved speeds of up to 1.18 m/s and demonstrated up to 35% greater energy efficiency compared to handcrafted alternatives.

## Problem

- Traditional methods for designing agile locomotion controllers are labor-intensive, require extensive expert knowledge, and often yield brittle results.
- The "reality gap" between simulation and real-world performance prevents direct deployment of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) policies, particularly for dynamic locomotion tasks.
- Learning controllable locomotion policies that can also be effectively transferred to physical systems remains challenging due to safety concerns and difficulty in automated real-robot training.

## Method

- Improved ==simulation fidelity== in ==PyBullet== by using a precisely measured Minitaur URDF, a custom ==non-linear actuator model==, and realistic ==latency handling== to accurately mirror real-world dynamics.
- Employed ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== to learn robust controllers by extensively ==randomizing physical parameters== (e.g., mass, friction, motor strength, latency) and applying ==random external perturbations== during training.
- Designed a ==compact observation space== (4D IMU) to reduce overfitting to simulation specifics and enhance real-world transfer, while a ==hybrid policy architecture== allows for user-guided gait references.

## Results

- Achieved direct sim-to-real transfer of agile galloping (**1.18 m/s**) and trotting (**0.60 m/s**) gaits on a real Minitaur robot, without any real-world fine-tuning.
- Learned galloping reduced mechanical power consumption by **35%** and learned trotting by **23%** compared to expertly handcrafted gaits from the robot's manufacturer.
- Policies trained with the combined strategies (improved simulation fidelity, dynamics randomization, compact observation space) consistently enabled robust and stable locomotion on the physical Minitaur.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A multi-pronged approach combining high-fidelity simulation (especially accurate actuator models and latency) with robust policy learning (dynamics randomization, compact observation space) is crucial for successful sim-to-real transfer of agile locomotion.
> - High-fidelity simulation and robust training methods are independently necessary; neither alone is sufficient for effective transfer, highlighting their synergistic role.
> - Deep reinforcement learning can discover more energy-efficient control policies than traditional handcrafted methods for dynamic robot locomotion, as evidenced by significant power consumption reductions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of agile locomotion for quadruped robots has long been a complex and challenging problem in robotics, involving the intricate control of under-actuated systems performing dynamic, balance-critical movements. Traditionally, designing such controllers has relied on extensive domain expertise and laborious manual tuning, often yielding brittle results.

The advent of **deep reinforcement learning (DRL)** has revolutionized the field, offering the promise of learning complex behaviors from scratch with minimal human intervention. DRL algorithms have demonstrated impressive capabilities in simulated environments, solving tasks that were previously intractable. However, a significant hurdle, famously known as the **"reality gap,"** persists: policies trained in simulation often perform poorly when deployed on real physical robots. This discrepancy arises from unmodeled dynamics, inaccurate simulation parameters, and numerical errors, which are particularly amplified in highly dynamic locomotion tasks involving frequent contact changes.

While direct learning on physical systems has been successful for some manipulation tasks (e.g., grasping), it is largely impractical for locomotion due to the difficulties of automated experiment resets, continuous data collection, and the risk of damaging expensive hardware during falls. Consequently, learning in simulation remains the preferred, safer, faster, and cheaper approach for locomotion.

This paper directly addresses the "reality gap" challenge in the context of agile quadrupedal locomotion. It positions itself at the forefront of research aiming to achieve successful **sim-to-real transfer** for complex, dynamic robotic behaviors. Previous work on bridging the reality gap has explored various avenues, including:
- **System Identification:** Accurately measuring and incorporating physical parameters into simulation models.
- **Enhanced Simulation Fidelity:** Improving the realism of simulators by modeling uncaptured dynamics or physical phenomena.
- **Robust Controller Learning:** Training policies that are less sensitive to model discrepancies through techniques like injecting noise, applying perturbations, or randomizing dynamic parameters (domain randomization).
- **Domain Adaptation/Fine-tuning:** Training a policy in simulation and then adapting or fine-tuning it on real-world data, often requiring some amount of real-robot interaction.

The authors distinguish their work by aiming for **direct sim-to-real transfer without additional real-world training or fine-tuning**. They combine advancements in simulation fidelity (e.g., accurate actuator models, latency modeling) with robust policy learning techniques (e.g., dynamics randomization, compact observation spaces). This comprehensive approach seeks to narrow the reality gap sufficiently to enable seamless deployment of simulated policies, representing a significant step towards truly autonomous and agile robotic locomotion from learned policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **automate the design of agile locomotion for quadruped robots by leveraging deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques.** The authors aim to enable robots to learn complex, dynamic gaits from simple reward signals and transfer these learned skills to real hardware without further training.

The motivation behind this work stems from two primary challenges:

1. **Complexity of Agile Locomotion Design:** As noted, traditional methods for designing agile locomotion controllers are labor-intensive, requiring extensive expert knowledge and tedious manual tuning. Automating this process through DRL could drastically reduce development time and potentially discover more optimal or energy-efficient gaits.
2. **The Reality Gap in DRL Robotics:** Despite significant progress in DRL, most successful applications have been confined to simulation environments. Policies trained in simulation often fail to perform adequately on real robots due to discrepancies between the simulated and real physical systems. For locomotion tasks, learning directly on physical robots is impractical due to safety concerns (potential damage), high costs, and difficulty in automated data collection and experiment resets. Therefore, bridging this "reality gap" is paramount for DRL to have a meaningful impact on real-world robotics.

Specifically, the paper identifies two core challenges they address:
- **Learning Controllable Locomotion Policies:** While DRL can learn from scratch, in robotics, users often desire some level of control or guidance over the learned behavior (e.g., specifying a particular gait style). The objective is to provide a "full spectrum of controllability," allowing users to guide the learning process with open-loop references or let the system learn entirely on its own.
- **Transferring Policies to the Physical System:** This is the direct attack on the reality gap. The objective is to improve the simulation model and train robust controllers such that policies learned purely in simulation can be successfully and reliably deployed on real quadruped robots.

Ultimately, the motivation is to make the development of agile, energy-efficient, and robust quadrupedal locomotion accessible and automated, moving DRL from a simulation curiosity to a practical tool for real-world robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a comprehensive system for learning and transferring agile quadruped locomotion policies from simulation to reality. Their methodology combines advancements in physics simulation fidelity with techniques for learning robust control policies.

#### 3.1. Robot Platform and Physics Simulation

- **Robot:** The **Minitaur** from Ghost Robotics, an 8-actuator direct-drive quadruped. It uses position control for its motors and is equipped with motor encoders and an IMU.
- **Hardware Augmentation:** An **Nvidia Jetson TX2** is integrated to perform neural network inference for the learned policy, communicating with the Minitaur's on-board STM32 microcontroller. The control loop operates at approximately 150-200Hz.
- **Simulator:** **PyBullet**, a Python module built on the Bullet Physics Engine, is used to simulate the Minitaur's dynamics.

#### 3.2. Learning Locomotion Controllers

- **Problem Formulation:** Locomotion control is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP).
- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an on-policy, stable, and parallelizable algorithm, is used.
- **Observation Space:** A compact observation space is chosen for robustness, including the robot's base roll, pitch, angular velocities (from IMU), and eight motor angles. Crucially, yaw (prone to drift) and noisy motor velocities are excluded.
- **Action Space:** Actions are desired leg poses decomposed into "swing" and "extension" components in "leg space," then mapped to motor angles. This design avoids self-collisions and creates a more convex, easier-to-learn action space compared to raw motor angles.
- **Reward Function:** A simple function combining positive rewards for forward running speed (`(p_n - p_{n-1}) · d`) and penalties for energy consumption (`wΔt|τ_n · ˙q_n|`). This encourages fast, energy-efficient locomotion.
- **Policy Representation (Controllability):** A hybrid policy `a(t, o) = ā(t) + π(o)` is introduced:
 - `ā(t)`: An open-loop, periodic reference signal provided by the user to specify the desired gait style (e.g., trotting, galloping rhythm).
 - `π(o)`: A feedback component represented by a neural network, learned by PPO, which adjusts leg poses based on observations to maintain balance, speed, and efficiency.
 - This allows for a spectrum of control, from fully learned policies (by setting `ā(t)=0`) to user-guided gaits (by providing `ā(t)` and limiting `π(o)`'s output range).

#### 3.3. Narrowing the Reality Gap

This is a core contribution, employing two main strategies:

- **A. Improving Simulation Fidelity:**
 - **Accurate URDF:** The Minitaur was disassembled to precisely measure dimensions, mass, and center of mass for each link, building an accurate Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) file. Motor frictions were also measured.
 - **Faithful Actuator Model:** Bullet's default position control was replaced with a custom model based on ideal DC motor dynamics (torque, current, back EMF, resistance). Critically, it incorporated **non-linear torque saturation** (a piece-wise linear function) observed in real motors and ensured the PD servo in simulation used *current* motor states, mirroring the real hardware. This was validated by matching simulated and real motor trajectories.
 - **Latency Handling:** To account for time delays between command and sensor feedback, the simulator was modified to keep a history of observations and interpolate them based on measured latencies for the microcontroller (3ms) and Jetson TX2 (15-19ms).

- **B. Learning Robust Controllers:**
 - **Dynamics Randomization:** At the beginning of each training episode, various physical parameters (mass, motor friction, inertia, motor strength, control step, latency, battery voltage, contact friction, IMU bias, IMU noise) were randomly sampled from defined ranges and used in the simulation. This forces the policy to learn to perform well across a variety of dynamics, increasing robustness.
 - **Random Perturbations:** During training, random forces were periodically applied to the Minitaur's base (e.g., every 200 steps) to knock it off balance, compelling the policy to learn recovery behaviors.
 - **Compact Observation Space:** The authors intentionally used a minimal observation space (e.g., 4D IMU information instead of 12D including motor angles). This reduces the risk of the policy overfitting to specific, potentially inaccurate, details of the simulated environment, thereby improving transferability to the real world where observation distributions might differ.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the system focused on learning two agile locomotion gaits: galloping and trotting, with subsequent deployment on the real Minitaur robot without any real-world fine-tuning.

#### 4.1. Locomotion Tasks Performance

- **Galloping (Learned from Scratch):** With the improved simulation (accurate actuator model, latency handling), an agile galloping gait emerged automatically, demonstrating the effectiveness of learning from scratch with appropriate environmental fidelity.
 - Speed: Approximately 1.34 m/s in simulation and 1.18 m/s on the real robot (2.18 body lengths per second).
 - Consistency: The majority of training runs converged to galloping, while some other gaits (trotting, pacing) also emerged.
- **Trotting (Learned with User Reference):** By providing an open-loop sine wave reference signal for leg swing and extension, the system learned a stable trotting gait. The feedback controller learned to maintain balance and refine the movement.
 - Speed: Approximately 0.50 m/s in simulation and 0.60 m/s on the real robot (1.11 body lengths per second).
 - Transferability: Initial mixed results were improved by further reducing the observation space, leading to stable, comparable movements in both simulation and reality.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Handcrafted Gaits

- The learned gaits were compared against expertly handcrafted gaits from Ghost Robotics (the Minitaur's manufacturer).
- **Speed:** The learned gaits achieved comparable running speeds to the handcrafted ones.
- **Energy Efficiency:** A significant finding was the superior energy efficiency of the learned gaits:
 - Galloping: 35% reduction in average mechanical power consumption.
 - Trotting: 23% reduction in average mechanical power consumption. This highlights the potential of DRL to discover more optimal and energy-efficient control policies than manual tuning.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Reality Gap Narrowing Techniques

The authors conducted comprehensive evaluations on the impact of each technique for bridging the reality gap, using the trotting gait as a test case, with multiple training runs and real-robot deployments to ensure statistical confidence. The "reality gap" was quantitatively measured as the difference in expected return (speed minus energy penalty) between simulation and real-world performance.

- **Improving Simulation Fidelity (Actuator Model and Latency):**
 - **Crucial for Transfer:** Policies trained with the baseline simulation (without the improved actuator model and latency handling) performed poorly on the real robot, exhibiting a large reality gap, even with random perturbations.
 - **Essential Role:** Policies trained with the *improved* simulation (accurate actuator model + latency handling) combined with random perturbations showed comparable performance in simulation and on the real robot, demonstrating the necessity of high-fidelity simulation. Neither the accurate actuator model nor latency simulation alone was sufficient for successful transfer; both were required.
- **Learning Robust Controllers (Dynamics Randomization and Perturbations):**
 - **Robustness vs. Optimality Trade-off:** Randomization increased the robustness of controllers across varying environments (lower standard deviation in performance across different test parameters) but sometimes led to slightly lower peak performance (lower mean return), indicating a trade-off where policies learn to be more conservative.
 - **Improved Transferability:** Regardless of observation space choice, randomization consistently helped narrow the reality gap and improved real-world performance.
- **Compact Observation Space:**
 - **Impact on Transfer:** A larger observation space (12D, including motor angles) led to higher performance in simulation, suggesting easier learning by leveraging more information. However, these policies performed *worse* on the real robot, resulting in a *wider* reality gap. This was attributed to overfitting to simulation details and mismatches in observation distributions between sim and real.
 - **Optimal for Transfer:** A smaller, more compact observation space (4D IMU only) resulted in slightly lower simulation performance but significantly *better* real-world transfer and a *narrower* reality gap, as it reduces the potential for overfitting.
- **Combined Effectiveness:** The most successful results in transferring policies to the real robot were achieved by combining all three strategies: **improved simulation fidelity + dynamics randomization/perturbations + compact observation space.** This combination allowed all tested controllers to consistently trot and maintain balance on the physical Minitaur.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant stride in the field of robotic locomotion and deep reinforcement learning, particularly concerning the challenging problem of bridging the "reality gap." Its contributions and potential impacts are multifaceted:

1. **Direct Sim-to-Real Transfer for Agile Locomotion:** The paper conclusively demonstrates that complex, agile locomotion policies (trotting and galloping) can be learned entirely in simulation and then directly deployed on a real quadruped robot without any further real-world fine-tuning. This is a crucial breakthrough, as it alleviates the considerable practical barriers (cost, safety, data collection difficulty) associated with training dynamic behaviors on physical hardware.

2. **Comprehensive Approach to Reality Gap Mitigation:** The study provides a thorough empirical evaluation of multiple techniques for narrowing the reality gap. It highlights that no single solution is sufficient and that a multi-pronged approach – combining precise system identification, high-fidelity simulation modeling (especially for actuators and latency), robust policy learning via dynamics randomization and perturbations, and a carefully designed compact observation space – is critical for successful transfer in dynamic tasks. The finding that accurate actuator models and latency simulation are independently vital for transfer is particularly impactful for hardware-in-the-loop simulation development.

3. **Automation and Optimization of Locomotion Design:** By automating the process of learning locomotion from simple reward signals, the system significantly reduces the need for extensive human expertise and tedious manual tuning. The demonstration of learned gaits being notably more energy-efficient than meticulously handcrafted ones underscores the potential of DRL to discover novel, more optimal, and robust control strategies that might be difficult for humans to design. This paves the way for more efficient and performant robotic systems.

4. **Practical Controllability in Learned Policies:** The proposed hybrid policy architecture, which combines an open-loop user reference with a learned feedback component, offers a practical solution for robotic applications where some degree of human guidance or control over the learned behavior is desired. This allows users to specify gait styles while DRL handles the complex balance and efficiency optimization.

5. **Open-Sourcing and Reproducibility:** The use of open-source tools like PyBullet and the commitment to open-sourcing the simulated Minitaur environments contribute to the reproducibility and accessibility of this research, fostering further development in the community.

Looking forward, this work opens several exciting avenues for future research. The authors themselves point towards developing policies that can dynamically change running speed and direction, crucial for real-world navigation. Moreover, integrating visual sensory input to handle complex terrain structures represents a logical next step, moving beyond flat ground and enabling robots to navigate diverse and unstructured environments. This paper solidifies the foundation for future generations of agile, intelligent, and autonomous legged robots operating effectively in the physical world.
