# Beyond Placement and Articulation: Usage-Driven Code Scenes for Embodied Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> RoomWright is an agentic framework that generates indoor 3D scenes entirely as executable code for embodied interaction. It infers objects from intended activities, compiles multi-part behaviors into trigger-condition-effect rules over object state, and orients small manipulands using modeling-time part annotations. It yields more interactive objects than code-based baselines and loads into Isaac Sim and OmniGibson for robot manipulation.

## Problem

- Existing code-based scene generators such as SceneCode and Code-as-Room focus on visual construction and layout, leaving how a scene is actually used, and the functional relations among objects, unmodeled.
- Prior methods pick objects sequentially in a fixed spatial order or from local visual judgments, so object composition lacks any systematic link to intended activities, and needed items or controls go missing.
- Interactive scene generation stops at per-part articulation such as hinges and slides, so causal multi-part or multi-object behaviors, like a switch turning on a lamp, cannot be represented.

## Method

- ==Usage-driven object reasoning== treats each major floor anchor as a task centre, admits only ==necessity-gated one-hop companion objects==, then exhaustively induces ==interaction edges== (source, target, method, state change) into a ==symbolic scene state==.
- A code agent compiles each interaction edge into a ==trigger-condition-effect rule== over declared object states, executed by one ==generic runtime== shared across all assets and bound to named parts.
- Annotation-informed ==usage-guided orientation== fuses ==part-level semantic annotations== with rendered views so a VLM predicts hand and arm anchors, and their displacement gives each manipuland a use-facing direction.

## Results

- On SceneEval over **28** room prompts, RoomWright leads on Accessibility (**65.6** vs **63.1** best baseline), ObjCount (**57.0** vs **48.8**), and ObjAttr (**64.6** vs **56.7**), and on Collision-free (**95.9** vs **95.0**), with mixed results on other metrics.
- Interactive content is far richer: **16.4** interactive objects and **13.2** articulated objects per scene, against **4.4** for SceneCode and **2.4** for SceneSmith. Only RoomWright yields **6.4** complex non-hinge interactions and **3.6** multi-part coordination groups.
- Ablations show removing usage-driven reasoning drops contextually necessary objects and accessible controls, and removing usage-guided orientation leaves keyboards and mice misoriented. Scenes run in Isaac Sim, where a gripper closes doors and a button lights a display.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Let the language model handle semantic and task reasoning, and leave coordinates, orientation and physics to deterministic geometry and simulation modules, which avoids errors from pixel-level judgment.
> - Semantic annotations recorded during code-based asset modeling give downstream reasoning cues that rendered pixels lack, such as the small or occluded features that determine object orientation.
> - Representing behaviors as declarative trigger-condition-effect rules on a shared runtime keeps interactions inspectable and editable, and lets coordinated multi-object behavior emerge from composing simple edges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of indoor scene synthesis is critical for developing and testing embodied artificial intelligence (AI), robotic manipulation systems, and simulation-based policy learning. Traditionally, scene generation methods have focused on creating visually realistic environments by generating or retrieving individual 3D assets and then arranging them. However, these conventional approaches often result in static geometric representations that lack explicit semantic structure, modifiability, or extensibility.

Recent advancements have leveraged the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) to introduce a new paradigm: representing entire 3D scenes as executable code. This code-based representation offers advantages such as flexibility, preservation of modeling procedures, semantic structures, and construction histories, enabling verification, test-time editing, and iterative extension without starting from scratch. Prior works such as SceneCode and Code-as-Room have explored this direction, focusing on generating procedural assets, refining object placement, and assigning articulation attributes (e.g., doors opening or closing).

RoomWright addresses a perceived limitation in these existing code-based methods. While previous work has advanced visual construction and object-level articulation, it has largely overlooked the functional usage of scenes and the complex, causal interactions that involve multiple parts or multiple objects. These interactions, such as a switch controlling a lamp or a knob adjusting a fan's speed, are essential for creating environments that accurately reflect real-world functionality for embodied agents. Furthermore, determining the correct orientation for manipulable objects, especially when interaction features are visually subtle or ambiguous, remains a challenge for purely visual systems.

This work contributes to the landscape by moving beyond static geometry and isolated object articulation. It aims to generate scenes where object selection, placement, orientation, and interaction are explicitly driven by intended usage, thereby creating more functionally coherent and interactive environments suitable for advanced embodied AI tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, named RoomWright, that generates 3D indoor scenes represented entirely as code, with a focus on functional usage and complex interactions for embodied AI. The motivation stems from several limitations identified in previous scene generation methods:

1. **Limited Functional Modeling:** Existing scene generation methods, including recent code-based approaches, predominantly focus on visual plausibility and object-level articulation (e.g., a single door opening). They often fail to model the functional usage of a scene as a whole, meaning they do not explicitly consider how objects are intended to be used by an agent or how their presence and arrangement contribute to specific activities. This results in scenes that might appear visually coherent but lack functional depth required for meaningful embodied interaction. The motivation here is to enable agents to reason about and perform tasks that require understanding the "why" behind an object's presence and its role in a broader activity.

2. **Absence of Multi-Part and Cross-Object Interactions:** Current interactive scene generation often stops at specifying how individual parts of an object can move (articulation). However, real-world environments frequently involve causal interactions that span multiple parts within an object or even multiple distinct objects. Examples include pressing a button to turn on a lamp, turning a dial to change a fan's speed, or placing a kettle on a base to activate it. Such complex, coordinated behaviors cannot be adequately represented by isolated articulation parameters. The motivation is to imbue generated scenes with rich, causally linked interaction rules that mimic real-world functional dependencies, thereby enabling more complex and realistic agent behaviors.

3. **Ambiguity in Manipuland Orientation:** Determining the correct orientation for manipulable objects (manipulands) is challenging. Visual cues alone can be insufficient because critical interaction features might be small, occluded, or visually similar to other parts. Additionally, the "front" direction of an object is often usage-dependent rather than solely geometrically defined. Incorrect orientations can hinder an embodied agent's ability to effectively interact with objects. The motivation is to provide a more robust method for orienting manipulable objects, moving beyond pixel-based inference by incorporating semantic annotations and usage context.

In summary, RoomWright's key objectives are to produce editable, simulation-ready 3D environments that go beyond simple visual construction and individual object articulation by:
- Modeling scenes based on intended functional usage and task-centric object reasoning.
- Enabling complex multi-part and cross-object interactions through a code-based rule system.
- Addressing the challenge of manipuland orientation by leveraging semantic annotations and usage-guided directions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoomWright is an agentic, usage-driven framework designed to generate interactive 3D scenes represented as code from natural language room descriptions. Its methodology is structured into three primary stages: Scene Planning, Simulation-ready Asset Generation, and Layout Generation.

**Overall Architecture (Figure 2):**
The framework begins with a natural-language prompt describing a room. This input is processed through the three stages, producing a fully instantiated, interactive, and physically executable 3D scene. The output includes a set of simulation-ready articulated assets, a metric layout with object poses, and an interaction script detailing controller-target relationships and their effects. These outputs are designed to be directly loadable into physics simulators like NVIDIA Isaac Sim or OmniGibson.

**Stage 1: Scene Planning**
This initial stage focuses on inferring the functional and structural components of the scene.
1. **Room Shell Planning:** The architectural layout of the room, including its shape, floor, and wall configuration, is first planned.
2. **Usage-Driven Object Reasoning (Algorithm 1):** This is a core component that expands an initial set of explicitly named objects into a complete, operable scene state `S = (O, F, Gx)`. This state comprises an object set `O`, a support forest `F` (recording which object rests on another and on what surface), and an interaction graph `Gx` (specifying how each object is operated). This reasoning process is hierarchical and usage-driven, employing two main phases:
 - **Task-Centric Expansion:** Each major floor-standing object (anchor) is treated as a "task center." The system identifies the human task associated with this anchor (e.g., a dining table centers dining activity). It then radially infers "companion objects" necessary for that task (e.g., chairs, tableware for a dining table) within a user's reach. This expansion is necessity-gated, meaning only objects essential to the task are admitted, extending `O` and `F`.
 - **Interaction Induction:** For every object in `O`, the system exhaustively induces its interaction edges `e = (s, t, m, ϕ)`. These edges link controllers, targets, and named parts using a closed method vocabulary, capturing every affordance a user could actuate. This results in the interaction graph `Gx`.

**Stage 2: Simulation-ready Asset Generation**
This stage translates the symbolic scene state into executable, physical assets.
1. **Articulated Code Generation & Part Annotation:** Articraft is used as a backend for generating articulated object code. During this process, part-level annotations `A = {(ℓi, pi)}` are extracted, recording the semantic label `ℓi` and local 3D position `pi` for each part `i`. These annotations are preserved to aid downstream reasoning.
2. **Physical Property Generation:** Each asset is compiled into addressable links with collision geometry and dynamic properties. Hinged and sliding parts are converted into revolute and prismatic joints, with inferred origins, axes, and limits. Physical properties such as mass `mℓ`, center of mass `cℓ`, inertia `Iℓ`, friction `μℓ`, and simplified convex collision geometry `Cℓ` are estimated for each link `ℓ`.
3. **Code-Based Multi-part Interaction:** Each edge `e` from the interaction graph `Gx` is compiled into a trigger–condition–effect rule over object states. The trigger is derived from the interaction method `m`, conditions from prerequisites, and the effect `ϕ` applies the resulting state or visual property update. This system supports coordinated motion across multiple parts and causal dependencies between objects (e.g., a switch controlling a lamp). These specifications are executed by a generic runtime shared across all assets within the OmniGibson environment.
4. **PBR Material & Texture Generation:** FLUX.2 generates tileable albedo maps for architectural surfaces. Objects retain part-level semantic colors, and PBR metalness and roughness are assigned based on material class to ensure consistent appearance.

**Stage 3: Layout Generation**
This stage optimizes the placement of objects within the room.
1. **Symbolic Layout Grounding:** A symbolic layout program, derived from user reference frames (support relations, user-viewpoint spatial relations, and usage-guided orientations), is grounded into collision-free poses by a geometric solver.
2. **Vision-in-the-Loop Critic and Iterative Refinement:** A VLM-based critic evaluates the provisional scene based on rendered observations against layout guidance. It assesses visual realism, functional consistency, spatial organization, and completeness. The critic then invokes scene-editing operations to adjust object placement, orientation, and inter-object relations.
3. **Physics-Simulation Validation:** Each revision is validated by physics simulation tools, which resolve geometric conflicts and reject modifications that introduce collisions, instability, or physical violations. This iterative process refines the layout while maintaining high-level organization.
4. **Annotation-Informed Usage-Guided Orientation:** For manipulable objects, the VLM, informed by the preserved part-level semantic annotations `A` and a small set of rendered views, predicts an intrinsic orientation frame. To resolve orientation ambiguity, the VLM also predicts a "hand anchor" `h` (user contact point) and an "arm anchor" `a` (approach side of the body). The displacement `duse = h - a` provides a geometric cue for the object's use-facing direction `fuse`, which guides its placement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of RoomWright was evaluated through extensive experiments comparing it with existing code-based and agentic scene generation methods, using both automatic metrics and qualitative assessments.

**Baselines:** The method was compared against SceneCode (Wang et al. 2026), Code-as-Room (Yang et al. 2026b), and SceneSmith (Pfaff et al. 2026). SceneCode and Code-as-Room represent code-based scene generation, while SceneSmith is an agentic, retrieval-based framework.

**Input Text Descriptions:** A curated set of 28 room-level prompts, categorized into three difficulty levels (short, medium, long/detailed), was used to evaluate diverse room types and functional requirements.

**Automatic Evaluation (SceneEval Metrics - Table 1):**
RoomWright achieved competitive or superior performance across several scene-level metrics compared to baselines:
- **Collision-free (95.9%)**, **In-bound (99.7%)**, and **Opening-clear (95.7%)** indicated robust geometric plausibility, suggesting effective collision avoidance, object containment within room bounds, and unobstructed openings.
- **Navigability (97.3%)** was high, demonstrating that the generated scenes provide ample walkable space for agents.
- **Support (53.6%)** and **Accessibility (65.6%)** metrics, judged by a VLM and geometric verification, showed that a majority of objects were correctly supported and reachable on their functional sides.
- **Object Count (57.0%)**, **Object Attribute (64.6%)**, **Object-Object Relationship (22.4%)**, and **Object-Architecture Relationship (40.6%)** evaluated prompt fidelity. RoomWright generally performed better in satisfying object-level attributes and counts compared to other code-based methods, though object relationship satisfaction was still an area with room for improvement across all methods.

**Interactive Content Evaluation (Table 2):**
RoomWright demonstrated a substantial advancement in generating interactive content:
- **Interactive Objects (IO):** RoomWright generated an average of 16.4 interactive objects per scene, significantly higher than SceneCode (4.4) and SceneSmith (2.4). Code-as-Room did not provide this attribute.
- **Articulated Objects (AO):** It produced an average of 13.2 articulated objects, also higher than SceneCode (4.4) and SceneSmith (2.4).
- **Complex Non-Hinge Interactions (CHI):** RoomWright uniquely supported 6.4 complex non-hinge interactions per scene, a capability not provided by any baseline. These interactions extend beyond simple articulation, reflecting causal dependencies.
- **Multi-Part Coordination (MPC):** It also uniquely generated 3.6 multi-part coordination groups, enabling coordinated interactions involving multiple parts or objects.

**Qualitative Comparisons (Figure 3):**
Qualitative results indicated that RoomWright produced layouts with improved plausibility and achieved realistic visual quality. Unlike SceneSmith which relies on asset retrieval, RoomWright's entirely code-based approach maintained interpretability and editability. Baselines like SceneCode and Code-as-Room exhibited limitations in holistic scene-level planning and physical guarantees, leading to lower visual quality or accumulation of errors.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Usage-Driven Object Reasoning (Figure 5):** The ablation study demonstrated that the usage-driven reasoning chain was crucial for generating a richer and more functionally plausible object composition. Scenes generated without this component lacked contextually necessary objects and accessible interaction controls, similar to the limitations observed in prior methods that rely on sequential object-by-object planning.
- **Usage-Guided Orientation (Figure 6):** This study highlighted the effectiveness of using annotation-informed usage-guided orientation. RoomWright was able to correctly orient and arrange manipulable objects like keyboards and mice, leveraging part-level semantic annotations from the modeling stage. In contrast, methods relying solely on visual information or lacking this orientation guidance often placed manipulands incorrectly.

**Robot Interaction (Figure 4):**
The generated scenes were successfully imported into Isaac Sim, demonstrating their physical executability. Robots could manipulate objects, such as closing a revolute door, and interact with causal elements, like pressing a button to activate a display, confirming the functional realism of the interactive content.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RoomWright represents an advancement in 3D scene synthesis for embodied AI and robotics, moving beyond static visual representations to functionally interactive and editable environments. The significance and potential impact of this work are evident in several areas:

1. **Enhanced Embodied AI Training and Evaluation:** By explicitly modeling functional usage, multi-part interactions, and usage-guided object orientations, RoomWright generates scenes that are more representative of real-world complexity. This capability provides richer, more realistic, and causally coherent environments for training and evaluating embodied AI agents. Agents can learn not just to navigate or manipulate individual objects, but to understand and execute tasks that involve complex sequences of interactions and dependencies across multiple entities. This could lead to more generalizable and capable AI systems.

2. **Simulation-Ready Environments:** The framework's output consists of simulation-ready articulated assets, metric layouts, and executable interaction scripts. This direct compatibility with physics simulators like OmniGibson streamlines the process of deploying generated scenes for robotic policy learning and physically realistic simulations. The ability to programmatically generate such complex, interactive environments reduces the manual effort traditionally required for creating diverse simulation datasets.

3. **Code-Based Editability and Extensibility:** Representing entire scenes as executable code offers inherent advantages. The generated scenes are inspectable, editable, and iteratively extensible without needing to rebuild from scratch. This flexibility is crucial for researchers and developers who need to rapidly iterate on scene designs, introduce new object types, modify interaction rules, or adapt environments to specific task requirements. The parametric editing capabilities demonstrated for individual assets (e.g., varying cabinet designs from a single template) further amplify this impact, enabling efficient creation of diverse object instances.

4. **Addressing Key Challenges in Scene Generation:** RoomWright directly tackles previously identified limitations in scene generation. Its usage-driven object reasoning provides a principled approach to populating scenes with functionally relevant objects, addressing the issue of arbitrary object placement. The framework for multi-part and cross-object interactions fills a gap by enabling complex causal behaviors essential for realistic manipulation tasks. Furthermore, the annotation-informed usage-guided orientation method offers a robust solution to the ambiguous problem of orienting manipulable objects, enhancing agent interaction success rates.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions, including more complex task-conditioned scene generation, learning to infer even richer interaction topologies, and developing agents that can dynamically modify or repair scene functionality through code. The structured, semantic representation of scenes could also facilitate language-guided scene manipulation and inverse graphics tasks at a higher level of abstraction.

In conclusion, RoomWright's approach to integrating functional usage and complex interactions into code-based 3D scene generation offers a significant step towards creating more intelligent, interactive, and truly embodied AI environments.
