# Learning Physical Interaction: A Survey of Tactile- and Force-aware Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of tactile- and force-aware robot learning that proposes TF-ART, a taxonomy mapping 53 recent methods onto a shared four-phase pipeline: multimodal fusion and representation learning, primary action generation, contact-aware action refinement, and robot-end compliant control. It characterizes where force and tactile signals enter each stage, and surveys the sensors, contact-rich tasks, and hardware interfaces used.

## Problem

- Prior tactile surveys organize the field by sensor hardware, transduction mechanisms, or perception representations, and stop short of how learned policies actually consume contact signals.
- Robot-learning and contact-rich manipulation reviews treat policy architectures and compliance control separately, so no unified view of the policy-to-control pipeline exists.
- Existing reviews register force and tactile inputs as extra sensor channels, ignoring where in a multi-stage system each signal enters and for what purpose.

## Method

- **TF-ART** taxonomy maps every method onto one information flow: Phase 0 fusion, Phase 1 primary action policy, Phase 2 ==contact-aware refinement==, Phase 3 robot-end control.
- Phase 0 groups ==representation learning== into ==auxiliary reconstruction==, ==masked autoencoding==, ==VQ-based reconstruction== and ==contrastive alignment==, and fusion into ==FiLM injection==, ==attention conditioning==, ==mixture-of-experts== and ==gated filtering==.
- Phase 2 formalizes ==discriminative modality injection==, routing tactile, force, proprioception and local vision past the slow primary policy into a fast refinement stage feeding ==PD==, ==impedance==, ==admittance== or ==hybrid force-position== controllers.

## Results

- Corpus of **53** papers published between 2024 and April 2026 covers **21** task types but is heavily skewed: peg-in-hole insertion in **26** papers, surface wiping **18**, pick-and-place **13**.
- Sensor adoption is tactile **27 of 53 (51%)**, wrist force/torque **18 of 53 (34%)**, joint torque **9 of 53 (17%)**; only **4** methods pair a force-domain sensor with a tactile sensor.
- Only **12 of 53** methods implement an explicit Phase-2 refinement stage; impedance control leads compliant execution with **10** methods, while **13** still fall back on plain position control.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Where a contact signal enters the pipeline matters more than whether it is present; phase placement sets latency, precision, and coupling to the controller.
> - Splitting policies across phases resolves the tension between slow semantic reasoning and millisecond contact response, since small refinement heads can run asynchronously at higher frequency.
> - Force and tactile encoders stay tied to specific sensors and embodiments, so no shared pretrained backbone exists and cross-platform transfer remains unverified.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of embodied intelligence has experienced growth driven by advancements in multimodal sensing, large-scale reasoning models, and generative motion planning. Current approaches frequently combine visual perception with language instructions for object recognition and scene understanding, which are effective for general manipulation tasks. However, the authors identify a shift in critical information when robots transition from free-space motion to physical interaction. In contact-rich scenarios, visual observations may be occluded, object states can change due to deformation, and successful execution often depends on precise contact location, stability, and interaction force. This necessitates the integration of force and tactile sensing, which provide essential feedback complementary to vision and proprioception.

While the utility of force and tactile information has been explored from both learning-based and control-oriented perspectives, existing literature has not systematically reviewed force- and tactile-aware robot learning from a unified perspective. Prior surveys have focused on specific aspects such as tactile sensing technologies, visuo-haptic perception, general robot learning, or contact manipulation, often covering only subsets of components like representation learning, multimodal fusion, policy architecture, action refinement, or robot-end control.

This survey addresses this identified gap by proposing TF-ART (Tactile/Force-Aware Robot learning Taxonomy), which provides a unified framework for multimodal and multi-phase robot learning systems. TF-ART maps individual methods onto a common information and control flow, characterizing how observations are organized, heterogeneous sensory inputs are encoded and fused, actions are generated and refined across multiple phases, and learned policies connect to reactive robot control. By integrating both algorithmic and practical perspectives, the work aims to offer a comprehensive understanding of the current state of force- and tactile-aware robot learning, distinguishing itself from previous reviews by its focus on the complete multimodal policy-control pipeline as its primary organizing dimension.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this survey is to provide a comprehensive and unified overview of recent advancements in force- and tactile-aware robot learning. This objective is motivated by several factors concerning the current state of robot manipulation and the limitations of existing research analyses:

- **Necessity of Physical Interaction:** Physically grounded robot intelligence requires robots to perceive, reason about, and regulate their interactions with the physical world. This capability is particularly critical in contact-sensitive manipulation, where successful task execution depends on force regulation and adaptive control, beyond just visual perception and motion generation.
- **Limitations of Vision-Centric Approaches:** While vision and language inputs provide rich semantic and geometric cues for general manipulation, their effectiveness diminishes under contact. During physical interaction, objects may be occluded, deform, or require precise force application, situations where force and tactile feedback become essential for task success and safety.
- **Emergence of Multimodal Integration:** Recent robot learning methods have progressed by integrating diverse sensory modalities, including force, tactile, vision, language, and proprioception, into learned manipulation policies. This raises questions about how force and tactile signals complement other modalities, how their representations can be leveraged, and how such integration guides contact-aware action generation.
- **Rise of Multi-Phase Architectures:** An increasing number of systems adopt multi-phase architectures, combining high-level policies with action-refinement modules and low-level controllers. This design addresses the challenge of bridging semantic task understanding (which benefits from large vision-language-based policies) with the low-latency, high-precision requirements of local, force-sensitive physical execution.
- **Gap in Existing Surveys:** Despite these advances, prior surveys have not explicitly reviewed force- and tactile-aware robot learning from a unified perspective that jointly captures both multimodal sensing and multi-phase system design. This fragmented view hinders a holistic understanding of the field's progress and challenges.

To address these motivations, the survey outlines specific contributions:

- **Proposing TF-ART:** The survey introduces TF-ART, a unified hierarchical taxonomy, to summarize and categorize recent methodologies in force- and tactile-aware robot learning. This framework aims to provide a comprehensive view of the field by mapping diverse methodological pipelines onto corresponding modules.
- **Analyzing Multimodal Integration:** The work examines how force and tactile signals are incorporated into multimodal robot policies, discussing representation, alignment, fusion, and their roles in primary action generation. It highlights the mechanisms, benefits, limitations, and task-dependent suitability of these multimodal designs.
- **Investigating Multi-Phase Pipelines:** The survey analyzes how force and tactile feedback reshape robot learning pipelines beyond single-stage action prediction. This includes discussions on phase-specific sensing, discriminative modality injection, action refinement, and low-level control, demonstrating how contact feedback is used for correction, real-time responsiveness, and stable physical interaction.
- **Discussing Practical Factors:** The authors consider practical and implementation-level factors, such as sensor types, sensor models, robot hardware capabilities, and representative contact-rich manipulation tasks, integrating both algorithmic and engineering perspectives.

The overarching motivation is to consolidate knowledge, clarify the interdependencies between different system components, and identify future research directions to advance generalizable physical interaction intelligence in robotics.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The authors systematically review the literature on force- and tactile-aware robot learning by proposing and employing TF-ART (Tactile/Force-Aware Robot learning Taxonomy). This taxonomy organizes robot learning systems into a unified hierarchical architecture comprising several phases and modules, enabling a structured analysis of how different approaches handle multimodal sensing, representation, action generation, refinement, and control.

**Literature Search and Selection:**
The survey defines specific criteria for inclusion:
- **Tactile-aware:** Methods using contact measurements from camera-based tactile sensors, tactile pads/arrays, or robotic skins.
- **Force-aware:** Methods using or explicitly estimating force-related information from wrist/end-effector force/torque sensors, joint-torque sensors, or proprioceptive signals.
- **Usage Requirement:** Tactile or force information must be used as a policy input, training signal, or explicitly predicted as an intermediate representation or output within the learned action-generation process.
- **Robot Learning:** Data-driven methods that generate robot actions or control targets. This excludes studies limited to sensor design, calibration, contact/force estimation, or representation learning without downstream action generation, as well as classical controllers lacking a learned action component.
- **Multi-phase Design:** This is an analysis category, not an inclusion requirement, referring to explicit separation between primary action generation and subsequent contact-aware refinement or force/compliance control stages.
Papers were identified through searches on Google Scholar, arXiv, and IEEE Xplore, supplemented by citation tracing, focusing on work published from 2024 through April 2026 for the core TF-ART corpus, which ultimately comprised 53 papers.

**TF-ART Framework (Figure 2):**
The TF-ART framework is structured into sequential phases and auxiliary modules:

- **Phase 0: Multimodal Fusion and Representation Learning:** This initial phase defines the sensory and representational foundation.
 - **Observation Space (a):** Categories of sensory measurements used as model inputs.
 - **Tactile (T):** Includes static contact/pressure sensing, active exploration of shape/material, dynamic contact events, and tactile signal transmission. Measurement formats include visual-tactile images, tactile matrices, and contact points.
 - **Force/Torque (F):** Includes six-axis force/torque sensing, joint torque sensing, and sensorless external-load estimation. Measurement formats are end-effector wrench, joint torque, and tactile-based force estimates.
 - **Non-contact Modalities:** Language (L) (human instructions), Vision (V) (RGB, depth images, point clouds), Proprioceptive/Environmental State (S) (EEF state, joint angle, object pose), and other potential modalities (audio, task indicator).
 - **Input Encoding and Modality Fusion (b):** Methods for representing and integrating diverse sensory inputs.
 - **Multimodal Representation Learning (c):** Auxiliary objectives to learn informative and aligned representations. These include Auxiliary Reconstruction (AR) for preserving modality-specific information, Masked Autoencoding (MAE) for reconstructing missing parts, Vector Quantization (VQ)-based Reconstruction (VQR) for compact representations, and Multimodal Contrastive Alignment (MCA) for learning shared latent spaces. Task-relevant supervision (e.g., gaze regions, affordances) is also noted.
 - **Multimodal Fusion (b continued):** Strategies for integrating representations. These comprise FiLM injection (feature-wise scaling/shifting), Attention conditioning (token-level interaction), Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) (dynamic routing to specialized networks), and Gated Filtering (dynamically controlling modality contributions). Potential future directions like denoising-stage modality composition, balanced multimodal fusion, and foresight modality imagination are also discussed.

- **Phase 1: Primary Action Generation (d):** The core decision-making module, mapping fused observations to initial actions.
 - **Architecture:** Categorized into Regression-based Policies (deterministic mapping), Reinforcement Learning (RL) (trial-and-error optimization), Diffusion/Flow Matching (DP) (generative denoising), Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) (simultaneous sequence generation), and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (grounding VLM representations into actions).
 - **Outputs (Intermediate Modalities):** Includes actions (end-effector, joint space, delta actions, per-step, or chunked), reference force signals, control stiffness parameters, and other auxiliary predictions (skill-switching triggers, task-progress indicators, affordance maps, future latent predictions).
 - **Sensory Prediction (e):** Models that estimate or forecast sensory observations (force, state, tactile) from other modalities to enhance anticipatory perception or compensate for missing inputs.

- **Phase 2: Modality-Discriminative Action Refinement (g):** A subsequent stage that refines initial actions using additional or auxiliary modalities, addressing the latency-precision trade-off.
 - **Discriminative Modality Injection:** Raw sensory modalities (tactile, force/torque, proprioception, localized vision) and fusion-phase inferred features can bypass Phase 1 and feed directly into Phase 2. Intermediate predictions from Phase 1 (action chunks, force commands) also serve as inputs.
 - **Policy Architecture:** Adapted versions of RL, ACT, DP, regression, and model-based/algorithmic methods are employed for refinement.
 - **Output:** Refined actions (often incremental or residual) or force commands, which are then passed to low-level control.
 - **Objectives:** To leverage auxiliary modalities for fine-grained adjustments, operate at higher frequencies, and process exclusive modality information better suited to specific task phases.

- **Phase 3: Robot-End Control (h):** The final layer that connects learned models to robot hardware for stable and compliant physical execution.
 - **Control Theory Preliminaries:** Brief introduction to robot dynamics, Cartesian mass-spring-damper model, and Jacobian transformations.
 - **Control Methods:** Categorized as PD control (position tracking), Impedance Control (enforcing dynamic relationship between motion and force), Admittance Control (mapping external force to desired motion), and Hybrid Force/Position Control (combining position and force control in orthogonal directions).
 - **Implementations:** Discussion on how controllers are implemented, identifying control-demanding modalities (fusion-forwarded, Phase 1 outputs, Phase 2 outputs), robot model selection (e.g., Franka for impedance, Flexiv for hybrid F/P), and end-effector types (gripper, dexterous hand, custom tools).
 - **Practical Considerations:** Emphasizes that robot hardware dictates control strategy and that proper tuning of compliant controllers is crucial for safety and performance.

This methodical breakdown allows the survey to systematically analyze a diverse corpus of research, highlighting common practices, emerging trends, and areas requiring further investigation in force- and tactile-aware robot learning.

### 4. Main findings and results

The survey's analysis of 53 papers using the TF-ART framework reveals several observations and trends within force- and tactile-aware robot learning:

- **Dominance of Tactile Sensing, but Limited Multimodal Combination:** Tactile sensors are employed in 27 out of 53 surveyed methods (51%), demonstrating their importance. Wrist-mounted force/torque sensors are used in 18 papers (34%), and joint torque sensors in 9 papers (17%). However, only 4 methods combine a force-domain sensor with a tactile sensor. This indicates a prevalent focus on individual contact modalities rather than systematic integration of their complementary information (global load from force, local contact geometry from tactile).
- **Varied Multimodal Fusion Strategies:** The survey identifies several approaches for multimodal fusion and representation learning, including Auxiliary Reconstruction (AR), Masked Autoencoding (MAE), Vector Quantization (VQ)-based Reconstruction (VQR), and Multimodal Contrastive Alignment (MCA). Fusion mechanisms range from FiLM injection and Attention Conditioning to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) and Gated Filtering. The choice of fusion often reflects attempts to manage modality dominance or phase-dependent relevance.
- **Diverse Primary Policy Architectures:** Five main architectures are identified for primary action generation (Phase 1): regression-based policies, reinforcement learning (RL), diffusion/flow matching policies (DP), Action Chunking Transformer (ACT), and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Regression and VLA models are frequently observed, while DP and ACT address the need for modeling multimodal action distributions and temporal consistency.
- **Outputs Beyond Actions:** Learned policies increasingly predict not only robot actions but also intermediate modalities such as reference force and control stiffness parameters. These outputs are crucial for interfacing with low-level compliant controllers, enabling more nuanced and adaptive physical interactions. Sensory prediction modules are also used to estimate or forecast force, state, and tactile observations, improving anticipatory perception and handling missing data.
- **Adoption of Multi-Phase Architectures for Refinement:** A significant number of methods utilize multi-phase architectures (Phase 2) to refine actions generated by the primary policy. This hierarchical design allows higher-level models to focus on semantic reasoning while lower-level modules handle rapid, contact-sensitive adjustments using force and tactile feedback. Discriminative modality injection is common in Phase 2, where specific high-frequency modalities bypass the initial policy stage for direct, real-time refinement.
- **Reliance on Compliant Control:** Low-level robot-end control (Phase 3) is a critical component for achieving compliant manipulation. PD control is the most basic, while Impedance, Admittance, and Hybrid Force/Position control are widely used for force regulation and stable physical interaction. The choice of controller often depends on the robot platform's native capabilities (e.g., Franka's impedance control, Flexiv Rizon's hybrid force/position control). Learned policies are increasingly designed to output parameters (e.g., reference force, control stiffness) that directly inform these compliant controllers.
- **Concentration on Specific Contact-Rich Tasks:** The analysis of task distribution (across 21 tasks) reveals a strong concentration on precision-contact tasks such as peg-in-hole insertion (26 papers), deformable/surface-contact tasks like wiping (18 papers), and household tasks such as pick-and-place (13 papers). Dynamic multi-contact manipulation (e.g., mobile catching, in-hand manipulation) and long-horizon household tasks beyond pick-and-place remain comparatively underexplored. This indicates a current research focus on localized contact-refinement problems over complex, dynamic, or highly generalized interaction scenarios.
- **Hardware Dependence:** The efficacy and choice of control strategies are heavily influenced by robot hardware and its native control interfaces. Platforms offering built-in impedance or hybrid force/position control simplify the integration of learned compliant behaviors, reducing the burden on learning algorithms to model complex physical interactions from scratch.

In summary, the survey highlights a growing recognition of the importance of force and tactile sensing in robot learning, the emergence of multi-phase architectures to balance semantic reasoning with reactive control, and a strong reliance on established compliant control methods. However, it also points to limitations in multimodal sensor integration, the generalization capabilities across diverse tasks and hardware, and the need for more sophisticated simulation and evaluation protocols.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The "Learning Physical Interaction: A Survey of Tactile- and Force-aware Robot Learning" paper offers several significant contributions and has the potential to impact the field in various ways:

- **Structured Understanding of a Complex Field:** The primary significance of this survey lies in its development of TF-ART. This unified hierarchical taxonomy provides a structured framework to categorize and understand the diverse methodologies, challenges, and advancements in force- and tactile-aware robot learning. By mapping individual research efforts onto a common pipeline, the survey brings coherence to a previously fragmented area, making it easier for researchers to identify commonalities, differences, and gaps.
- **Identification of Key Research Gaps:** The detailed analysis through TF-ART explicitly identifies critical areas where current research is lacking. These include the limited systematic integration of force and tactile sensing, the challenge of cross-sensor and cross-embodiment generalization, the need for a generalizable phase-encoding framework in multi-phase policies, and the insufficiency of current evaluation protocols for generalizable physical interaction.
- **Guidance for Future Research Directions:** By pinpointing these gaps, the survey provides a roadmap for future research. It suggests developing systematic methods for jointly encoding and fusing force and tactile observations, formalizing the division of labor in hierarchical policies, integrating learned policies with robot control more deeply by predicting control-relevant parameters, and creating standardized benchmarks for diverse contact-rich tasks.
- **Advancing Multimodal Robot Intelligence:** The emphasis on integrating force and tactile sensing with other modalities like vision and language is crucial for developing more intelligent and capable robots. The survey's insights into representation learning and fusion strategies can lead to more robust perception systems that better interpret physical interactions.
- **Enhancing Robot Autonomy and Safety:** The focus on compliant control and action refinement highlights the importance of safe and adaptive physical interaction. By enabling robots to perceive, reason about, and regulate forces, the research can lead to robots that are safer to operate in human environments, more robust to uncertainties, and capable of performing delicate manipulation tasks without damage.
- **Informing Hardware and Software Co-design:** The discussion on the tight coupling between learned policies and robot platform control interfaces underscores the need for hardware-aware learning. This can inform future robot designs, encouraging the development of platforms with native compliant control capabilities and adaptable sensor configurations that better support advanced learning algorithms. The concept of treating mechanical compliance as a design variable also suggests a paradigm shift towards joint optimization of robot morphology and control.
- **Catalyzing Data and Simulation Improvements:** The identified bottlenecks in simulating tactile and force data highlight the need for more accurate physics-based simulators and large-scale, diverse real-world datasets. This could spur development in sim-to-real transfer techniques and data generation, crucial for scaling foundation models in robotics.
- **Pathway to Generalizable Physical Concepts:** Ultimately, the survey's long-term vision—moving beyond task-specific motion patterns towards acquiring transferable physical concepts like contact, pressure, and friction—points to a foundational shift in robot learning. This could lead to robots that possess a deeper understanding of the physical world, enabling unprecedented levels of adaptability and intelligence in real-world environments.

By synthesizing the current state and outlining critical future directions, this survey serves as a valuable resource for researchers and practitioners, accelerating progress towards robots capable of truly intelligent and robust physical interaction.
