# Learning Force Control for Legged Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A whole-body RL policy for a quadruped with a mounted arm that tracks commanded end-effector forces without any force-torque sensor, using only joint encoders and IMU. Training injects a simulated spring-damper contact field so the gripper experiences forces, and a concurrent state estimator infers gripper force and position. Force tracking error stays under 10 N across 0-70 N, enabling compliance, gravity compensation, and impedance control.

## Problem

- Prior sim-to-real RL loco-manipulation policies achieve forceful interaction only implicitly, giving no command interface to set contact force or end-effector compliance.
- Task-specific forceful RL controllers, like fall recovery or ball dribbling, optimize force for one task and cannot be repurposed or quantitatively evaluated for force accuracy.
- Classical force control relies on wrist force-torque sensors or model-based whole-body optimization, and kinesthetic teaching was considered impractical on high-degree-of-freedom legged systems.

## Method

- A single policy trained with ==PPO== tracks end-effector force, end-effector position, and base velocity, switched by a ==binary mode flag== resampled twice per episode.
- A simulated ==soft-contact force field== applies a ==spring-damper== load to the gripper about a body-frame setpoint, so the policy experiences realistic reaction forces instead of free-space reaching.
- A concurrent ==state-estimation module== predicts privileged quantities, body velocity, gripper position, and external gripper force, from a 30-step observation history and feeds the actor.

## Results

- Simulated force tracking error averages about **5 N** at low targets and stays below **10 N** across the full **0-70 N** range; estimation error is consistently larger.
- Real dynamometer trials at **five** gripper poses show **5-10 N** tracking error matching simulation, and whole-body coordination pulls **90 N** versus the arm's **36 N** rated payload.
- Whole-body position mode enlarges reachable workspace volume by **59 percent**, with mean position error of **4.6**, **4.8**, and **5.5 cm** on the x, y, and z axes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Injecting a simulated compliant contact field during training is what makes force commands learnable; free-space reaching gives no reaction force to regulate against.
> - Force tracking stayed accurate even though the learned force estimator overshot in reality, suggesting the policy largely ignores its own estimated force feedback.
> - Proprioception alone supports useful force control, but roughly 5 cm and 5 N accuracy caps its use for precision tasks, and compliance degrades past 4 kg payloads.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of contact forces during interaction is a fundamental and critical capability for many robotic tasks, spanning both locomotion and manipulation. While reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved remarkable success in various contact-rich problems—such as agile legged locomotion, dexterous manipulation, and drone flight—current RL methods often achieve forceful interactions *implicitly*. This means that while robots might execute tasks requiring force (e.g., running, kicking, fall recovery), the underlying policies do not provide a *direct* or *explicit* mechanism for regulating interaction forces. This lack of explicit force control limits a user's ability to precisely set the robot's compliance, which is crucial for balancing safety, robustness, and task effectiveness.

Classical control theory has long addressed force and impedance control, developing techniques like impedance control and hybrid force-velocity control. These methods often rely on closed-loop feedback from force-torque sensors or model-based estimations, which can be precise but also demand accurate models and specialized hardware. In the context of legged manipulators, model-based whole-body control approaches have also shown success in coordinating dynamic and compliant behaviors, including accurate contact force control through the end-effector. However, these approaches typically rely on complex inverse dynamics models and trajectory optimization.

The present work bridges a significant gap by proposing a method for training RL policies to achieve *direct force control* in legged manipulators *without requiring access to force-torque sensors*. This is a crucial distinction, as it allows robots to exhibit sophisticated force-interaction capabilities using only proprioceptive sensing (joint encoders and IMU), which is a common and often more robust sensor suite for legged robots. Previous RL efforts in loco-manipulation have either focused on end-effector position/locomotion velocity control without explicit force regulation, or optimized forceful interaction for a single, specific task, making it difficult to repurpose these controllers for general force application.

By enabling direct, explicit force control through RL, this research empowers legged manipulators with greater versatility, safety, and adaptability. It moves beyond implicit force generation towards a user-commandable compliance, aligning with future needs for human-robot interaction, kinesthetic teaching, and robust interaction in unknown environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a method for training reinforcement learning policies that can achieve **direct force control** at the end effector of a legged manipulator. A critical constraint and distinguishing factor is that this force control must be achieved **without requiring access to force sensing** (e.g., wrist force-torque sensors). Instead, the system relies solely on proprioceptive sensing.

Building upon this core objective, the motivations and desired capabilities are multifaceted:

- **Enabling Compliant Whole-Body Manipulation:** The learned force control is intended to facilitate compliant interaction, allowing the robot to gracefully adapt to environmental contact, absorb unexpected disturbances, and perform tasks that require variable stiffness.
- **Gravity Compensation and Impedance Control:** The ability to directly command forces should intrinsically enable capabilities like gravity compensation, where the robot can hold an object in place without actively fighting gravity, and impedance control, where the end effector tracks a position while exhibiting controllable stiffness.
- **Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction and Teleoperation:** A major motivation is to make teleoperation of complex legged manipulators more intuitive for humans. By allowing the teleoperator to command only the manipulator's desired position and force, the robot's body should automatically adjust through the learned whole-body policy to achieve these commands, reducing the cognitive load on the operator.
- **Facilitating Kinesthetic Demonstration:** Compliant behavior and explicit force control are crucial for kinesthetic teaching, where a human physically guides the robot to demonstrate a task. The ability for the robot to comply with human guidance and estimate exerted forces makes this teaching method viable for high-dimensional systems like legged manipulators.
- **Optimizing Whole-Body Posture for Force Application:** The policy should learn to coordinate the entire robot's body and legs to apply large forces efficiently and effectively across an expanded workspace, beyond what a fixed-base arm could achieve.
- **Paving the Way for Versatile Legged Robots:** Ultimately, the work aims to unlock a wider variety of loco-manipulation tasks for legged robots, making them more adaptable and robust in diverse, real-world scenarios.

The authors explicitly state their key contribution as providing "the first deployment of learned whole-body force control in legged manipulators," underscoring the novelty and significance of their practical implementation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around **sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL)**, leveraging a whole-body control platform consisting of a quadruped robot with a mounted arm.

**Robot Platform and Hardware:**
- **Quadruped:** Unitree B1, a 55 kg robot with 12 electric actuators (each with a joint position encoder) and an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) for orientation.
- **Arm:** Unitree Z1, a 6-degree-of-freedom (DoF) arm weighing 5.3 kg, mounted on the B1's base.
- **Control:** An onboard NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX computer runs the control policy at 50 Hz.
- **Teleoperation:** Oculus Meta Quest 2 or Apple Vision Pro headsets are used, mapping controller movements/gestures to end-effector position commands, and joysticks/buttons for base velocity, force application, and gripper control.

**Simulation Environment:**
- Policy training is conducted in **Isaac Gym**, a high-performance GPU-based physics simulator known for its ability to run massively parallel environments.

**Policy Design and Learning:**
- **Unified Policy:** A single policy is trained to simultaneously handle locomotion, whole-body end-effector position regulation, and end-effector force regulation.
- **Action Space:** A 17-dimensional action space (a_t ∈ R^17) directly controls the position targets for a proportional-derivative (PD) controller in each of the robot's 12 leg joints and the first 5 arm joints.
- **Observation Space (o_t):** Critically, the policy relies *only* on proprioceptive sensing, including:
 - Gravity vector (projected in body frame).
 - Feet clock timings (for gait synchronization).
 - Joint positions and velocities (for all 17 controlled joints).
 - Previous actions.
 - Observation history (H=30 timesteps) and history of task-associated commands are concatenated as input. **No direct force-torque sensor data is used.**
- **Command Inputs (c_cmd):** The policy receives four types of commands from the teleoperator:
 - Desired end-effector force vector (F_cmd ∈ R^3), specified in Cartesian world coordinates (range ±70 N per axis).
 - Desired end-effector position (p_cmd_ee ∈ R^3), expressed in spherical coordinates relative to the body frame.
 - Desired linear (x, y) and angular (yaw) velocities for the base (v_cmd_b).
 - A binary flag (C_f) to switch between position tracking mode (C_f=0) and force tracking mode (C_f=1). During training, this input is randomly resampled within episodes to alternate between modes.

**Training Setup for Force Control (Crucial Aspect):**
- To enable the robot to learn how its actions result in applied forces, the training setup in simulation includes an **external force field**. This field models soft contact, applying force to the gripper based on its displacement from a position setpoint using a **spring-damper model** (F_e = K_ext_p(x_s - x_g) + K_ext_d(ẋ_s - ẋ_g)).
- The setpoint (x_s) moves with the robot and is initialized at the gripper's initial position when switching to force tracking mode, ensuring the robot experiences varied forces across its workspace.
- Force targets (F_cmd) are repeatedly sampled, linearly interpolated from zero to the target, held, and then interpolated back to zero. A 20% chance of a zero-force target ensures practice of fully compliant behavior.

**Training Setup for Position Control:**
- This mode supports locomotion, grasping, and generates diverse initial poses for force mode. It re-implements elements from prior work, adapting them for the robot's specifications and for alternating with force training. End-effector commands are body-centric.

**Reward Function and Optimization:**
- **Reward:** A composite reward function (r_b_v + r_g_x + r_g_f)e^(r_l) combines:
 - Locomotion task (r_b_v): Rewards body velocity tracking and a smooth trot gait using a contact schedule.
 - Gripper force control (r_g_f): Rewards tracking F_cmd (active only when C_f=1).
 - Gripper position control (r_g_x): Rewards tracking p_cmd_ee (active only when C_f=0).
 - Safety and smoothness (r_l): Penalties for collisions, joint limits, high joint velocities/accelerations, and action smoothness.
- **Policy Architecture:** Consists of an actor network, a critic network, and a state estimation module.
 - **Privileged State:** During simulation, "privileged" information (robot's body velocity, gripper position in body frame, external force on gripper) is available.
 - **State Estimation Module:** Predicts an estimate (ê_t) of the privileged state from the observation history. This is trained concurrently with a supervised loss.
 - **Actor Network:** Takes observation history and the *estimated* privileged state (ê_t) as input to output actions.
 - **Critic Network:** Takes observation history and the *true* privileged state (e_t) as input.
- **Optimization:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used, with training conducted across 4096 parallel environments. Episodes terminate if the gripper collides or the body height drops too low.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings, demonstrating the efficacy of the proposed RL approach for whole-body force control in legged manipulators.

**A. Force Control Policy Performance:**
- **Simulated Force Tracking:** For z-axis force control, the mean absolute tracking error was approximately 5 N for low force targets and remained below 10 N across the entire training range (0-70 N). The estimation error (difference between estimated and actual force) was generally equal to or greater than the tracking error, suggesting some limitations in observability.
- **Real-World Force Tracking:** On the physical Unitree B1 robot, connecting the end effector to a dynamometer revealed a tracking error of 5-10 N across the full training range (0-70 N) for five different gripper positions. This performance is notably similar to simulation. Interestingly, the estimated force in the real world tended to overshoot, indicating a moderate sim-to-real gap in force *estimation*. However, the force *tracking* performance remained proficient, suggesting the policy's robustness even with estimation discrepancies.
- **Whole-Body Force Application:** The policy successfully coordinated the robot's body and legs to apply substantial forces. The robot achieved a pulling force of **90 N**, significantly exceeding the arm's rated payload of 36 N. This demonstrates the benefit of learned whole-body coordination for forceful interaction. Force tracking was also maintained across a large portion of the expanded workspace, though errors increased near the edges.

**B. Impedance Control:**
- The learned force control policy was successfully utilized to implement an impedance controller *without additional training*. By feeding back the estimated gripper position and velocity (from the learned state estimation module) into the force command (F_cmd = -K_imp_p(x - x_des) - K_imp_d ẋ), the robot could track a target position while complying to external forces like a spring-mass-damper system. This was qualitatively demonstrated by teleoperating the robot to grasp and drag a chair.

**C. Compliance and Kinesthetic Demonstration:**
- **Compliant End Effector State (Gravity Compensation):** When commanded to track zero force (C_f=1, F_cmd=0), the robot entered a fully compliant mode. In this state, the body and arm coordinated to drive gripper force application to zero in all three axes, allowing the gripper to remain suspended against gravity (gravity compensation). This compliant behavior proved robust against unexpected disturbances (e.g., bumping into a wall) and enhanced safety around humans.
- **Kinesthetic Teaching:** Compliance directly facilitates kinesthetic teaching, where a human operator physically guides the robot. A proof-of-concept demonstration involved kinesthetically manipulating the robot to insert a drill into its charging station.
- **Compliant Manipulation of Heavy Objects:** By modulating the vertical force command, the compliant mode could be extended to handle payloads. For payloads up to 4 kg, matching the vertical force command to the object's weight restored gravity compensation and compliance in other axes. At 6 kg, compliance was reduced, indicating performance limits.

**D. Walking, Reaching, and Grasping (Position Control):**
- **Expanded Workspace:** The whole-body position control policy significantly enlarged the manipulation workspace. Compared to a fixed-base arm, the whole-body policy **increased the workspace volume by 59%**, highlighting the benefits of coordinating the arm and legs.
- **Position Tracking Accuracy:** In simulation (standing in place), the mean position error was 4.6 cm (x), 4.8 cm (y), and 5.5 cm (z) across 1000 sampled end-effector positions.
- **Real-World Position Tracking:** On hardware, teleoperated arm trajectories showed average positional errors of 4.42 cm (x), 5.37 cm (y), and 6.86 cm (z). These values were very similar to simulation, indicating a minimal sim-to-real gap for position tracking.
- **Qualitative Tasks:** Successful teleoperation of the position mode was demonstrated for tasks like door opening and water pouring.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in robot learning for complex physical interaction tasks, particularly for legged manipulators. Its primary significance lies in the **first successful deployment of learned whole-body force control in legged manipulators without requiring external force-torque sensors**. This achievement has several profound implications:

- **Expanded Capabilities for Legged Robots:** By enabling direct, explicit force control and compliance, the work unlocks a new spectrum of tasks for legged robots. These robots can now not only navigate complex terrains and manipulate objects but also interact with their environment in a nuanced, forceful, and adaptive manner, similar to how humans use their bodies. This includes tasks requiring pulling, pushing, lifting, and maintaining specific contact forces.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** The intuitive teleoperation interface, where a human commands the end-effector and the robot autonomously adjusts its whole body, drastically simplifies control. More importantly, the learned compliance makes robots safer to operate around humans and more amenable to human guidance (e.g., kinesthetic teaching). This could accelerate the deployment of advanced robots in collaborative settings.
- **Robustness and Adaptability:** Compliant behaviors inherently make robots more robust to unexpected disturbances, environmental uncertainties, and unknown contact geometries. The ability to modulate compliance means robots can balance safety with the need for forceful interaction, dynamically adapting their stiffness to the task at hand.
- **Reduced Hardware Requirements:** Achieving sophisticated force control without dedicated force-torque sensors is a crucial practical advantage. It simplifies robot design, reduces cost, and makes the system more robust to sensor failures, relying on readily available proprioceptive data. This makes advanced force control accessible to a wider range of robot platforms.
- **Foundation for Future Autonomous Behaviors:** The established teleoperation framework for forceful loco-manipulation provides a rich source for data collection. This paves the way for future work in imitation learning, where robots could learn to accomplish compliant and forceful behaviors autonomously from human demonstrations, without explicit programming.
- **Advancement in Sim-to-Real RL:** The demonstration of successful sim-to-real transfer for both position and force control (despite a sim-to-real gap in force *estimation*, the *tracking* remains robust) further validates the power of massively parallel RL simulation environments like Isaac Gym for developing real-world robot skills.

While acknowledging limitations such as the tracking accuracy (around 5 cm for position and 5 N for force commands) potentially being insufficient for highly precise tasks, this work nonetheless establishes a robust and generalizable foundation for compliant and forceful interaction. It is a vital step towards more versatile, adaptable, and safe legged robots capable of performing complex tasks in human environments.
