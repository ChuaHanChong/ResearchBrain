# AnimateAnything: Consistent and Controllable Animation for Video Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> A unified framework named AnimateAnything processes diverse control signals, including arrow-based motion annotations, camera movements, and reference videos, by converting them into a consistent optical flow representation to guide video generation. The approach achieved superior video quality and control alignment, demonstrating the lowest LPIPS (0.135), FID (48.11), and FVD (380) on WebVid while maintaining high subject consistency and motion smoothness.

## Problem

- Current controllable video generation methods often struggle to integrate diverse control signals (e.g., camera movements, object motions, text prompts) simultaneously without conflicts or inconsistencies.
- Text-to-video models lack the precision for detailed camera trajectories or specific object manipulation, while image-guided methods are limited to minor movements and do not handle large-scale dynamics well.
- Combining multiple control signals or introducing large-scale motions frequently leads to temporal instability, flickering, and incoherent pixel motion in generated videos.

## Method

- A two-stage pipeline, consisting of Unified Flow Generation and Video Generation, converts various control signals into a consistent motion representation.
- The Unified Flow Generation stage transforms diverse visual inputs (arrow annotations, camera poses, reference videos) into dense, unified optical flows using a specialized Flow Generation Model and a Camera Reference Model.
- A Frequency Stabilization module operates in the frequency domain on attention weights within the Diffusion Transformer (==DiT==) to mitigate flickering and enhance temporal consistency during video generation.

## Results

- Achieved superior quantitative performance in image-to-video generation, demonstrating the lowest LPIPS (0.135), FID (48.11), and FVD (380) on WebVid, and highest Subject Consistency (0.928) and Motion Smoothness (0.991).
- Outperformed existing methods in camera control, consistently achieving lower translation and rotation errors (e.g., T-Err 0.041, R-Err 0.159 on basic trajectories) compared to CameraCtrl and MotionCtrl.
- Produced more stable and consistent videos for user arrow annotations and reference video guidance, effectively mitigating flickering and preserving appearance details, as confirmed by ablation studies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Converting disparate control signals (camera, object motion, reference video) into a unified, frame-by-frame optical flow representation effectively resolves conflicts and inconsistencies during multimodal input integration.
> - Optical flow serves as a robust intermediate representation that can precisely guide both local object dynamics and global camera trajectories for comprehensive video control.
> - Leveraging frequency-domain analysis and modifications within the attention mechanism significantly enhances temporal coherence and mitigates flickering, particularly in videos with large-scale motion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of video generation has seen significant advancements, particularly with the emergence of large-scale models like Sora. A prominent direction within this field is controllable video generation, which aims to provide users with specific means to dictate aspects of the generated video, such as camera trajectories and object movements. This capability is crucial for expanding the applicability of video generation to areas like film production and virtual reality.

Existing approaches to controllable video generation exhibit several limitations. Text-to-video (T2V) methods, such as MotionCtrl and CameraCtrl, primarily rely on text prompts, which often lack the precision needed for detailed manipulation of specific visual elements or complex motion paths. Image-guided methods, like Motion-I2V and MOFA-Video, offer more detailed visual cues through user-annotated trajectories or sparse motion hints. However, these methods often face challenges in handling large-scale camera movements or struggle to integrate diverse control signals simultaneously. Some approaches attempt to train individual control signals (e.g., using LoRA modules) and then combine them, but this often necessitates careful parameter tuning and can lead to flickering or temporal instability in the output video. Furthermore, methods that leverage optical flow as an intermediate representation, like MOFA-Video and Motion-I2V, are typically effective for local object motion but often fall short when addressing global camera motion signals.

AnimateAnything addresses these gaps by proposing a unified framework that can simultaneously process and integrate various dynamic control signals—including arrow-based motion annotations, camera movements, and reference videos—into a consistent motion representation. By converting all control information into frame-by-frame optical flows, the work seeks to provide a more comprehensive and robust solution for precise and temporally coherent controllable video generation, moving beyond the limitations of individual control modalities or simplified integration strategies. The paper positions its approach as a step towards resolving issues of incoherent pixel motion and temporal flickering that arise when combining disparate control signals in existing methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of AnimateAnything is to develop a unified controllable video generation approach that facilitates precise and consistent video manipulation across a diverse range of conditions. These conditions include user-defined camera trajectories, text prompts, and motion annotations.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges observed in the current landscape of controllable video generation:

- **Lack of Unified Control for Diverse Signals:** Existing methods often struggle to simultaneously handle different modalities of control signals, such as camera movements, object motions, and general scene descriptions. Integrating these disparate signals directly into a single video generation model is complex, often leading to conflicts or inconsistencies in the generated output. The authors hypothesize that converting all control information into a common, frame-by-frame pixel movement representation (optical flow) could overcome this challenge.
- **Imprecision of Text-Only Control:** While text-to-video models have advanced significantly, text descriptions alone are often insufficient for conveying specific, detailed instructions regarding camera paths or precise object movements. This limits their utility in applications requiring fine-grained control.
- **Limitations of Image-Guided Control:** Current image-based guidance methods, while offering more detail than text, are often restricted to minor object movements or small-scale camera shifts. They typically lack the ability to manipulate large-scale camera trajectories or handle complex, dynamic scene changes comprehensively.
- **Temporal Instability and Flickering:** When multiple control signals are combined, or when large-scale motions are introduced, existing video generation methods are prone to generating flickering effects or incoherent pixel motion. This instability significantly degrades the quality and realism of the output videos. The authors aim to mitigate this by ensuring consistency in the frequency domain.
- **High Complexity of Large-Scale Motion:** Generating videos with complex camera and object movements is inherently challenging. Existing models often fail to maintain global consistency and temporal coherence under such dynamic conditions.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve:
1. The conversion of all visual control signals (e.g., arrow-based motion annotations, camera movements, reference videos) into a unified optical flow representation.
2. The effective utilization of this unified optical flow to guide a video generation model, synchronizing with text controls to produce high-quality videos.
3. The development of a mechanism to enhance temporal coherence and reduce flickering, particularly when dealing with large-scale motion.

By pursuing these objectives, AnimateAnything aims to provide a more robust, flexible, and consistent framework for generating dynamic and controllable videos, making them more applicable for professional creative workflows.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AnimateAnything employs a two-stage pipeline designed for multi-condition joint control, which comprises Unified Flow Generation and Video Generation, supplemented by a Frequency Stabilization module.

#### 3.1. Stage 1: Unified Flow Generation

This stage focuses on converting various visual control signals into a unified optical flow representation. The process categorizes injection modules into explicit and implicit methods, both involving the reference image. The output optical flow is compressed into a latent space by a Flow Variational Autoencoder (VAE) for efficiency.

- **Explicit Injection:** This module handles control signals that can be directly converted into sparse optical flow for specific frames or pixels.
 - **Arrow-based Motion Annotation:** Users label desired object or environmental movements on a reference image. These trajectories are converted into a sparse point set `M`, which then generates a point-wise sparse motion flow `F_s` using bicubic interpolation, similar to MOFA-Video. The method also incorporates CMP for enhancing sparse optical flows.
 - **Generalization:** The framework is designed to accept other visual control signals convertible to sparse optical flow, such as audio, videos, or object landmarks, through extraction or generation methods.
 - **Flow Generation Model (FGM):** A classical latent diffusion model, FGM, transforms these initial sparse optical flows into dense optical flows.

- **Implicit Injection:** This module incorporates control signals that are challenging to convert directly to pixel-level optical flow, primarily camera trajectories.
 - **Camera Reference Model (CRM):** The CRM, initialized with a pre-trained image generation network (SD1.5 U-Net architecture), implicitly embeds camera trajectories into the FGM's denoising process.
 - **Plücker Embeddings:** Camera poses (extrinsic and intrinsic parameters `R`, `t`, `K_f`) are represented using Plücker embeddings for each pixel, forming a 6-dimensional vector `p_f,h,w` that captures both position and orientation.
 - **Trajectory Encoder:** A specialized encoder, structurally similar to CameraCtrl's camera encoder but with improved architecture (self-attention replacing temporal attention after 2D ResNet blocks), processes the Plücker embeddings to output multi-scale trajectory features.
 - **Camera Motion Attention:** CRM integrates these camera features with reference image latents. Image latents serve as queries, while camera features act as keys and values in a cross-attention mechanism.
 - **Reference Attention:** The multi-scale reference features from CRM then guide the FGM's generation of dense optical flow through a reference attention layer, mirroring AnimateAnyone's progressive condition injection design.

Through this combined explicit and implicit injection, the system aims to resolve incoherent pixel motion caused by different control signals, generating unified, dense optical flow. Unimatch is used to extract ground truth optical flow from training videos.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Video Generation

This stage utilizes the unified dense optical flow from Stage 1 to guide the final video generation, aligning with reference image semantics and user annotations.

- **Base Framework:** The video generation model inherits from the CogVideoX framework.
- **Flow Encoder:** The generated unified optical flows are compressed using a flow encoder. This encoder comprises four symmetrically arranged stages with interleaved ResNet blocks, performing 2x downsampling and upsampling, achieving a 4x temporal and 8x8 spatial compression, similar to a 3D VAE encoder. This results in flow latents `z_f`.
- **Vision Transformer (ViT) Block Integration:** A single basic ViT block queries video latents `z_v` from the flow latents `z_f`. In this attention mechanism, `z_v` forms the query (Q), while `z_f` serves as both key (K) and value (V), facilitating the integration of flow information into the video latent space.
- **Text Encoding:** Text prompts are processed via a Google Research T5 model, generating text embeddings.
- **Full Attention Transformer Block:** The output of the ViT block is concatenated with the text embeddings before being fed into a full-attention transformer block, which is part of the DiT (Diffusion Transformer) architecture.
- **Training Strategy:** Only the optical flow encoder, the input transformer block, and the video smoothing module (described below) are trained in this stage, keeping other parameters fixed to reduce training complexity. Noise is applied to the optical flow during training to enhance robustness.

#### 3.3. Frequency Stabilization Module

To address flickering and instability caused by large-scale motion and independent noise addition in different frames, a frequency-based stabilization module is proposed.

- **Information Encoding Perspective:** Flickering is viewed as feature misalignment between frames, stemming from independent noise in video diffusion models.
- **Frequency Domain Analysis:** Frequency-domain features are leveraged to reveal essential video-level information and suppress flickering.
- **Adaptive Modification:** The module modifies the attention mechanism within the DiT blocks.
 - **FFT Application:** Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is applied to the weight matrices (Q, K, V) of the attention mechanism to obtain their spectral features.
 - **Parameterized Weight Matrix:** These spectral features are then multiplied by a trainable parameterized weight matrix `W`.
 - **Inverse FFT:** An inverse FFT (InvFFT) restores the modified features back to the temporal domain.
 - **Dot-Product Attention:** This process ensures consistency of scene features along the temporal direction before computing dot-product attention.

#### 3.4. Training Strategy

- **Computational Resources:** Experiments were conducted using 8x NVIDIA Tesla A800 80G GPUs.
- **Datasets:**
 - **Stage 1 (Optical Flow Generation):** Primarily Real10K and DL3DV10K datasets.
 - **Stage 2 (Video Generation):** WebVid10M and OpenVid datasets, known for their size and diversity.
- **Camera Trajectory Data Augmentation:** Addressing the scarcity of dynamic video data with camera pose information:
 - Initial training on Real10K.
 - Identification of approximately 10,000 videos from OpenVid with relatively fixed camera positions by evaluating global optical flow magnitude.
 - Subsequent training on these selected dynamic videos, assuming fixed camera viewpoint at the origin.
- **Independent Training:** Both stages can be trained independently, with their connection established only during the inference process.
- **Robustness Enhancement:** Noise is applied to the optical flow input during the training of the second stage to improve the video generation model's learning capability and robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AnimateAnything involved both quantitative metrics and qualitative comparisons against state-of-the-art methods across various controllable video generation tasks.

#### 4.1. Image-to-Video Generation Ability

For general image-to-video (I2V) generation, the method was evaluated on the WebVid and OpenVid datasets using image-level (LPIPS, PSNR, SSIM, FID) and video-level (FVD) quality metrics. Additionally, VBench metrics (Subject Consistency (SubC), Motion Smoothness (MoS), Aesthetic Quality (AesQ)) were used.

- **Quantitative Performance:** AnimateAnything demonstrated superior performance across nearly all metrics compared to Motion-I2V, MOFA-Video, DynamiCrafter, CogVideoX, PyramidFlow, and OpenSora.
 - On WebVid, it achieved the lowest LPIPS (0.135), FID (48.11), and FVD (380), and the highest PSNR (25.22) and SSIM (0.810).
 - On OpenVid, it similarly showed the lowest LPIPS (0.113), FID (33.12), and FVD (322), and the highest PSNR (25.04) and SSIM (0.836).
- **Consistency and Smoothness:** In terms of VBench metrics, AnimateAnything achieved the highest SubC (0.928 on WebVid, 0.971 on OpenVid), MoS (0.991 on WebVid, 0.993 on OpenVid), and AesQ (0.474 on WebVid, 0.600 on OpenVid), indicating better subject consistency, smoother motion, and higher aesthetic quality compared to baselines.
- **Specific Strengths:** The authors noted that the method particularly excels in generating videos with human and animal motions, attributed to its optical flow guidance.

#### 4.2. Control Signals Driven I2V Generation

The efficacy of controlling specific aspects of video generation was evaluated for camera trajectory, user arrow annotation, and reference video guidance.

- **Camera Trajectory:**
 - **Metrics:** Camera alignment was measured by the distance between predicted and ground-truth camera trajectories, using rotation error (R-Err) and translation error (T-Err). Camera trajectories were estimated using three Structure-from-Motion (SfM) methods: ParticleSfM, VggSfM, and DUSt3R, to eliminate scale differences.
 - **Comparison:** AnimateAnything was compared with CameraCtrl and MotionCtrl on both "basic" and "difficult" trajectories from the Real10K dataset.
 - **Results:** AnimateAnything consistently achieved lower translation and rotation errors across all SfM methods and trajectory types. For instance, on basic trajectories with DUSt3R, it yielded T-Err of 0.041 and R-Err of 0.159, outperforming CameraCtrl (0.090, 0.300) and MotionCtrl (0.057, 0.233). Similar improvements were observed for difficult trajectories and other SfM methods.

- **User Arrow Annotation:**
 - **Qualitative Assessment:** The method demonstrated the ability to convert user-defined drags into optical flows for guiding video generation.
 - **Comparison:** Compared to MOFA-Video, DragAnything, and Motion-I2V, AnimateAnything generated more stable and consistent videos for specific user drags, maintaining appearance details of the reference object.

- **Reference Video:**
 - **Motion Transfer:** When the optical flow and given images were well-aligned (e.g., replacing/stylizing a subject in the first frame), AnimateAnything produced significant motion alignment without requiring skeleton or facial keypoint extraction, while maintaining optimal subject consistency. This contrasted with Motion-I2V (lacking sensitivity to wide motions) and MotionClone/MOFA-Video (prone to style inconsistency and artifacts).
 - **Generalization (Facial Replacement):** The method showed robustness even when the image and unified optical flow were not perfectly aligned (e.g., extracting dense optical flow from a different facial motion video for facial replacement). Stage 2 could tolerate some misalignments while still generating consistent expressions and lip motions, indicating greater flexibility.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study and Analysis

Ablation studies on the Real10K dataset confirmed the contribution of key components.

- **Unified Optical Flow vs. Direct Camera Embedding:** A setup where multi-frame camera encoding was added directly to latent variables (without optical flow conversion) showed significantly worse visual quality (e.g., LPIPS 0.401, FVD 346) and trajectory alignment (T-Err 0.551, R-Err 0.048) compared to the full model (LPIPS 0.142, FVD 168, T-Err 0.354, R-Err 0.047). This highlighted the superiority of the unified optical flow representation.
- **Unified Optical Flow vs. ControlNet-Like Integration:** Replicating the first half of FGM blocks as a reference network and adding its output to corresponding original blocks (a ControlNet-like approach) also yielded inferior results (LPIPS 0.400, FVD 356, T-Err 0.737, R-Err 0.050), reinforcing the effectiveness of the proposed unified flow generation and integration strategy.
- **Impact of Frequency Stabilization (FS):** Removing the Frequency Stabilization module ("w/o FS") resulted in a significant drop in visual quality (LPIPS 0.241, FVD 311) compared to the full model (LPIPS 0.142, FVD 168), indicating its crucial role in maintaining temporal stability and suppressing flickering.
- **Impact of Noise Application to Optical Flow:** Not applying noise to the globally estimated optical flow before feeding it into the Flow Encoder ("w/o noise") led to reduced robustness, reflected in higher FVD (474) and LPIPS (0.228) compared to the full model.

The ablation studies collectively demonstrate that the two-stage pipeline, the unified optical flow representation, and the frequency stabilization module are essential for achieving the reported performance in terms of both visual quality and adherence to control signals.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AnimateAnything framework represents a notable advancement in controllable video generation, primarily through its innovative two-stage pipeline and the unification of diverse control signals via optical flow.

The **unification of disparate control signals** (camera trajectories, user motion annotations, reference videos) into a single, frame-by-frame optical flow representation is a significant contribution. This approach simplifies the complex task of integrating multimodal inputs, addressing a long-standing challenge where previous methods either lacked precision, were limited to specific control types, or suffered from inconsistencies when combining signals. By converting all motion information into a universal pixel-level movement prior, AnimateAnything offers a more coherent and less conflict-prone guidance mechanism for video generation models.

The **introduction of a frequency-based stabilization module** is also critical. Large-scale motions and the inherent noise in diffusion models often lead to temporal flickering in generated videos. By operating in the frequency domain, this module effectively enhances temporal coherence and mitigates instability, resulting in higher quality and more perceptually pleasing video outputs. This addresses a practical limitation that hinders the real-world applicability of dynamic video generation.

The experimental results demonstrate **superior performance** across quantitative metrics for video quality and control signal alignment, as well as qualitative advantages in terms of consistency and stability over existing state-of-the-art methods. This indicates that AnimateAnything can generate clearer, more stable, and precisely controlled videos.

The **robustness and generalization capabilities** of the method, particularly its ability to handle misalignments between input images and optical flows in tasks like facial replacement, suggest a broader utility beyond strictly aligned conditions. This flexibility can make the system more adaptable to diverse real-world scenarios and less demanding on perfectly curated input data.

The potential impact of AnimateAnything is substantial, particularly in fields requiring precise video customization:

- **Film Production and Animation:** Animators and filmmakers could leverage this technology for rapid prototyping of complex scenes, precise character animation, and dynamic camera movements, reducing manual effort and iteration cycles.
- **Virtual Reality and Gaming:** Content creators could generate highly controllable and dynamic environments or character animations for immersive experiences, where precise motion and consistent visuals are paramount.
- **Creative Industries:** Artists and designers could utilize the tool for generating unique visual content with granular control over motion, opening new avenues for creative expression.
- **Research and Development:** The two-stage architecture and the optical flow-based unification strategy could serve as a foundational approach for future research into multimodal conditional video generation, inspiring further innovations in control mechanisms and temporal consistency.

In summary, AnimateAnything pushes the boundaries of controllable video generation by offering a unified, robust, and temporally stable solution for animating arbitrary characters and scenes under diverse user-defined conditions, positioning it as a valuable tool for both academic research and practical applications.
