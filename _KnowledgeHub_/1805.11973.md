# MolGAN: An implicit generative model for small molecular graphs

> [!summary] Summary
> MolGAN introduces the first Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) for directly generating molecular graphs, sidestepping the limitations of string-based representations and computationally expensive likelihood-based graph models. It integrates a reinforcement learning objective, specifically DDPG, to guide generation towards molecules with desired chemical properties, achieving high validity and superior property optimization compared to previous methods.

## Problem

- Existing SMILES-based generative models for molecules struggle with syntactic ambiguity, multiple valid string representations for one molecule, and often produce chemically invalid compounds.
- Likelihood-based graph generative models face computational challenges due to graph permutation invariance, requiring expensive graph matching or imposing restrictive node ordering heuristics.
- Generating molecules with specific, desired chemical properties (e.g., druglikeliness, solubility) remains challenging, requiring explicit mechanisms to optimize for non-differentiable metrics.

## Method

- **MolGAN** employs an ==Improved Wasserstein GAN (WGAN-GP)== framework to implicitly generate molecular graphs directly, avoiding explicit likelihood computations and handling permutation invariance.
- The generator uses an ==MLP== to non-sequentially predict entire graph structures (atom types and bond types), with ==Gumbel-Softmax straight-through gradients== for discrete sampling.
- A discriminator and a separate reward network, both built using ==Relational Graph Convolutional Networks (R-GCNs)== and permutation-invariant readouts, evaluate generated graphs and approximate property-based rewards, respectively.
- ==Reinforcement learning==, specifically a ==DDPG==-like objective, is integrated into the generator's training to optimize for external, non-differentiable chemical properties, with invalid molecules receiving zero reward.

## Results

- MolGAN achieved significantly higher validity (**>99%**) and better scores for optimized chemical properties (e.g., druglikeliness, solubility) compared to SMILES-based GANs like ORGAN.
- Training time was at least **~5 times faster** than sequential generation models like ORGAN, attributed to MolGAN's non-sequential, one-shot graph prediction generator.
- The model consistently exhibited high novelty (e.g., **97%** of unique molecules were unseen in the training data) but suffered from mode collapse, leading to low uniqueness scores (around **2%** in RL-optimized settings).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - GANs can effectively generate discrete, structured graph data directly, offering an implicit, likelihood-free approach that inherently addresses the permutation invariance problem.
> - Integrating a reinforcement learning objective (DDPG) with a GAN enables the generation of molecules optimized for specific chemical properties, leading to higher property scores and implicitly improving validity.
> - While the WGAN objective provides a diverse base, the model is susceptible to mode collapse, resulting in low uniqueness of generated molecules despite achieving high novelty among the unique samples.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of novel chemical compounds with desired properties, particularly in *de novo* drug design, is a highly challenging and computationally expensive task. The vast, discrete, and high-dimensional nature of chemical space makes traditional search methods inefficient. Deep generative models have emerged as a promising approach to navigate this space more effectively.

Prior to MolGAN, the landscape of deep generative models for molecular design was primarily divided into two main categories:

1. **SMILES-based Generative Models:** Many early and contemporary works (e.g., Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2016; Kusner et al., 2017; Guimaraes et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2018) relied on the Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) representation. SMILES encodes molecules as strings, allowing the use of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for generation. While effective, SMILES strings have inherent disadvantages:
 - **Syntactic and Order Ambiguity:** RNNs must learn both the grammatical rules of SMILES and handle the fact that a single molecule can have multiple valid SMILES representations, consuming model capacity.
 - **Generalizability:** This approach is specific to molecular strings and not readily applicable to generic graph generation.
 - **Validity Issues:** Small errors in string generation can lead to syntactically or chemically invalid compounds. Grammar VAEs (Kusner et al., 2017) attempted to mitigate this by imposing grammatical constraints.

2. **Likelihood-based Graph Generative Models:** A more recent and direct approach involves generating molecules directly as graph-structured data (e.g., Kipf & Welling, 2016; Johnson, 2017; Li et al., 2018b; Simonovsky & Komodakis, 2018; You et al., 2018). These methods, often based on Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), operate in the intrinsic graph space, thereby avoiding the overhead of string representations. However, they face their own set of challenges:
 - **Permutation Invariance:** Graphs are inherently permutation-invariant (the order of nodes in a matrix representation does not change the graph itself). Likelihood-based methods, when working with matrix representations, often require expensive graph matching procedures or the evaluation of likelihoods over all possible node orderings, which becomes computationally prohibitive for even small graphs.
 - **Complexity:** Sequential graph generation models can be complex to train and often restricted to small sizes.

MolGAN introduces a novel approach by applying Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to directly generate graph-structured data, specifically molecular graphs. This positions it as the *first* work to use GANs for direct molecular graph generation, distinguishing it from both SMILES-based GANs like ORGAN (Guimaraes et al., 2017) and likelihood-based graph VAEs. By using an *implicit, likelihood-free* GAN framework, MolGAN inherently sidesteps the need for explicit likelihood computation and expensive graph matching procedures, offering a distinct advantage in tackling the permutation invariance problem. The integration of reinforcement learning (RL) further allows the model to optimize for specific chemical properties, aligning with the ultimate goal of *de novo* drug design.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of MolGAN are multifaceted, addressing the limitations of existing molecular generation techniques:

1. **Direct Generation of Molecular Graphs:** A core objective is to generate molecules directly in their graph representation rather than relying on string-based (SMILES) representations. This aims to eliminate the need for models to learn extraneous syntactic rules and to handle order ambiguity, freeing up model capacity for learning chemical structures.
2. **Circumventing Likelihood-based Challenges:** MolGAN seeks to overcome the computational hurdles associated with likelihood-based graph generative models. Specifically, it aims to avoid:
 - Expensive graph matching procedures required to evaluate the likelihood of generated graphs, which are necessary due to the permutation invariance of graphs.
 - The need for fixed or randomly chosen node ordering heuristics that simplify graph representation but may introduce biases or limitations.
 This is achieved by employing an *implicit, likelihood-free* generative model, namely a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN).
3. **Optimization Towards Desired Chemical Properties:** Beyond simply generating valid molecules, a crucial motivation is to enable the generation of molecules with specific, pre-defined chemical properties (e.g., druglikeliness, solubility, synthesizability). This is vital for practical applications like *de novo* drug discovery, where target properties are paramount. MolGAN integrates a reinforcement learning (RL) objective to achieve this, allowing optimization towards non-differentiable metrics provided by external chemical software.
4. **Computational Efficiency:** The authors explicitly aim for a computationally efficient generative model. By designing a generator that predicts the entire graph at once (non-sequentially), rather than atom-by-atom or bond-by-bond, MolGAN intends to be faster and easier to optimize compared to sequential graph generation models, albeit with a limitation on maximum graph size.
5. **Permutation Invariance in Discriminator/Reward Network:** To correctly process graph data, the discriminator and reward network must be invariant to permutations of node order. The objective is to build these networks using graph neural networks (specifically Relational-GCNs) that naturally incorporate this property.

In essence, MolGAN is motivated by the desire to build a robust, efficient, and property-guided generative model for molecular graphs that leverages the strengths of GANs and reinforcement learning to overcome the inherent challenges of graph-structured data and the limitations of previous molecular representation methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MolGAN's architecture is built around three core components: a generator ($G_\theta$), a discriminator ($D_\phi$), and a reward network ($\hat{R}_\psi$). Its methodological novelty lies in adapting the GAN framework to operate directly on graph-structured molecular data and integrating a reinforcement learning objective for property optimization.

#### 3.1. Molecular Representation

Molecules are represented as undirected graphs $G=(V, E)$. Each node $v_i \in V$ (atom) is associated with a one-hot feature vector $x_i$ indicating its type. Each edge $(v_i, v_j) \in E$ (bond) is associated with a one-hot vector indicating its type. For a graph with $N$ nodes, this translates to a node feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T}$ (where $T$ is the number of atom types) and an adjacency tensor $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N \times Y}$ (where $Y$ is the number of bond types).

#### 3.2. Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) Framework

MolGAN employs an implicit, likelihood-free GAN (Goodfellow et al., 2014) to generate graphs. This choice inherently avoids the need for explicit likelihood computation and the complex graph matching procedures required by likelihood-based methods to handle permutation invariance.
To enhance stability and prevent issues like mode collapse, MolGAN utilizes the **Improved Wasserstein GAN (WGAN-GP)** (Gulrajani et al., 2017). WGAN-GP minimizes the Earth Mover's (Wasserstein-1) distance between the generated and real data distributions, offering a more stable training objective than original GANs, particularly with the added gradient penalty to enforce Lipschitz continuity.

#### 3.3. Generator ($G_\theta$)

The generator takes a low-dimensional vector $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ sampled from a standard normal distribution and maps it to a graph. For simplicity and computational efficiency, the generator is a simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that directly predicts the entire graph structure at once, rather than sequentially. This limits generation to a pre-chosen maximum number of nodes ($N=9$ in experiments for QM9 dataset). The MLP outputs two continuous, dense objects:
- $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T}$ representing atom type probabilities for each node.
- $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N \times Y}$ representing bond type probabilities for each pair of nodes.

Since graph data is inherently discrete, the continuous outputs $X$ and $A$ need to be converted into discrete, sparse representations $\tilde{X}$ and $\tilde{A}$ via **categorical sampling**. To enable gradient-based training through this non-differentiable sampling process, three variations are explored:
1. **Continuous Forward Pass:** Using the continuous $X$ and $A$ directly in the forward pass to $D_\phi$ and $\hat{R}_\psi$.
2. **Gumbel Noise:** Adding Gumbel noise to $X$ and $A$ before passing them to the discriminator/reward network, making the generation stochastic while maintaining continuous values.
3. **Straight-Through Gumbel-Softmax:** Using a sample from a categorical distribution (derived from $X$ and $A$) during the forward pass, but leveraging the continuous relaxed values (from the Gumbel-Softmax distribution, Jang et al., 2017; Maddison et al., 2017) for the backward pass gradients.

#### 3.4. Discriminator ($D_\phi$) and Reward Network ($\hat{R}_\psi$)

Both the discriminator and the reward network receive a graph (represented by $\tilde{X}$ and $\tilde{A}$) as input and output a scalar value. They share the same underlying architecture but have separate parameters.
- **Graph Convolutional Layers:** These networks are built upon **Relational Graph Convolutional Networks (R-GCNs)** (Schlichtkrull et al., 2017). R-GCNs are suitable for graphs with multiple edge types (like different bond types) and inherently handle permutation invariance by aggregating information from neighbors. Each layer propagates node features $h_i^{(l)}$ based on an aggregation of features from neighbors, weighted by bond types and self-connections.
- **Graph-Level Readout:** After several R-GCN layers, node embeddings are aggregated into a single graph-level representation vector $h_G$ using a permutation-invariant attention-like mechanism (Li et al., 2016).
- **Scalar Output:** This graph-level vector is then passed through an MLP to produce a scalar output. For the discriminator, this output determines if the graph is real or fake. For the reward network, it predicts the approximate reward score for the molecule. The reward network uses a sigmoid activation on its output to scale scores between 0 and 1.

#### 3.5. Reinforcement Learning (RL) Objective

To optimize the generation of molecules with specific chemical properties, MolGAN integrates a reinforcement learning objective. Unlike previous works that used REINFORCE for sequence generation, MolGAN employs a simplified version of **Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)** (Lillicrap et al., 2016).
- The GAN generator $G_\theta$ acts as the deterministic policy, taking a latent vector $z$ and outputting a molecular graph (action).
- The reward network $\hat{R}_\psi$ learns to approximate the immediate reward function for a generated graph, based on real rewards provided by external chemical software (e.g., RDKit). This network is trained via a mean squared error loss.
- The generator is then trained to maximize this predicted reward $\hat{R}_\psi(G)$ through backpropagation, as $\hat{R}_\psi(G)$ is differentiable. Invalid molecular graphs are assigned a zero reward.

#### 3.6. Combined Training Objective

The generator's total loss is a linear combination of the WGAN loss and the RL loss:
$L(\theta) = \lambda \cdot L_{WGAN}(\theta) + (1 - \lambda) \cdot L_{RL}(\theta)$, where $\lambda \in$ is a hyperparameter balancing the two objectives.
The discriminator is trained solely with the WGAN objective. The reward network is trained to predict external chemical property scores. Notably, the reward network is pre-trained for an initial phase before its loss is incorporated into the generator's objective, preventing early divergence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

MolGAN was evaluated on the QM9 chemical database (133,885 organic compounds up to 9 heavy atoms) using metrics such as validity, novelty, uniqueness, and specific chemical properties (druglikeliness, solubility, synthesizability).

#### 4.1. Effect of $\lambda$ Hyperparameter

- **Trade-off between Objectives:** The $\lambda$ parameter, controlling the balance between the WGAN and RL objectives, showed a clear trend: lower $\lambda$ (more emphasis on RL) led to higher validity and higher scores for the optimized property (solubility in this experiment).
- **Implicit Validity Optimization:** Since invalid molecules receive a zero reward, the RL component implicitly optimizes for valid compounds.
- **Pretraining Importance:** Experiments with $\lambda=0$ (RL-only in the second half) indicated that pretraining with the WGAN objective is fundamental. Without it, the model collapses, producing very few diverse samples, suggesting that the GAN acts as a regularizer towards diversity.
- **Mode Collapse Susceptibility:** Even with pretraining, the "unique" score remained consistently low (around 2%), regardless of $\lambda$, indicating that MolGAN is susceptible to mode collapse. Early stopping based on the unique score was implemented as a countermeasure.

#### 4.2. Objectives Optimization and Comparison with ORGAN

MolGAN was compared against ORGAN (Guimaraes et al., 2017), a SMILES-based sequential GAN with an RL objective, on optimizing druglikeliness, synthesizability, solubility, and a combined "all objectives" score.

- **Superior Validity and Property Scores:** In all single-metric optimization settings (druglikeliness, synthesizability, solubility), MolGAN achieved significantly higher validity scores (typically >99% vs. 85-97% for ORGAN) and better scores for the optimized chemical properties. This was attributed to directly optimizing molecular graphs and using DDPG, which provides a more effective gradient than REINFORCE for high-dimensional action spaces.
- **Faster Training Time:** MolGAN demonstrated at least **∼5 times faster training time** compared to ORGAN. This is a significant advantage, attributed to its non-sequential, one-shot graph prediction generator, contrasting with ORGAN's character-by-character SMILES generation.
- **Mode Collapse Persistence:** The low uniqueness scores (∼2%) persisted across all objectives, confirming MolGAN's susceptibility to mode collapse, unlike ORGAN which showed much higher uniqueness (e.g., 69.4% for druglikeliness, 97.2% for all objectives). The authors suggested that ORGAN's ability to generate larger molecules and its explicit penalty for non-unique outputs might contribute to its higher diversity.
- **Dataset Size Impact:** Training MolGAN on the full QM9 dataset (vs. a 5k subset) for fewer epochs further improved results across almost all scores, indicating that observing more diverse samples aids learning.
- **Novelty:** Despite mode collapse resulting in low uniqueness, MolGAN achieved high novelty (e.g., 97% for solubility objective), meaning the *unique* molecules it generated were mostly unseen in the training dataset.

#### 4.3. Comparison with VAE Baselines

MolGAN was compared to VAE-based generative models (CharacterVAE, GrammarVAE, GraphVAE) on the full QM9 dataset without optimizing specific chemical properties (only implicit validity).

- **Significantly Higher Validity:** MolGAN achieved a validity score of 98.1%, substantially outperforming CharacterVAE (10.3%), GrammarVAE (60.2%), and various GraphVAE configurations (55.7-81.0%).
- **High Novelty:** MolGAN also demonstrated high novelty (94.2%), comparable to CharacterVAE (90.0%) and higher than GrammarVAE (80.9%) and GraphVAE (61.0-75.8%). Furthermore, 97% of MolGAN's unique molecules were novel, confirming it was not merely memorizing.
- **Mode Collapse vs. Uniqueness:** While MolGAN's uniqueness (10.4%) was higher than in the RL-optimized experiments, it was still lower than some VAE baselines like CharacterVAE (67.5%) and GraphVAE (76.0%), reaffirming its mode collapse issue. However, it was comparable to GrammarVAE (9.3%). The authors reiterate that VAEs do not explicitly optimize for validity or novelty, but their reconstruction objective might indirectly lead to some diversity.

#### 4.4. Discretization Method

The Gumbel-Softmax straight-through gradient approach (variation iii) was generally found to be the best-performing discretization method in the experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MolGAN represents a significant advancement in the field of deep generative models for molecular design, offering several key contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Pioneering GAN for Direct Molecular Graph Generation:** This work is the first to successfully apply Generative Adversarial Networks to directly generate molecular graphs. This approach intrinsically addresses the challenges of permutation invariance and avoids the computationally expensive likelihood computations or node ordering heuristics that plague likelihood-based graph generative models.
2. **Overcoming SMILES Representation Limitations:** By operating directly in the graph space, MolGAN bypasses the inherent disadvantages of string-based (SMILES) representations, such as learning syntactic rules, managing order ambiguity, and dealing with potentially invalid outputs due to minor string errors. This leads to higher validity of generated compounds.
3. **Effective Property Optimization:** The integration of a reinforcement learning objective, specifically DDPG, allows MolGAN to effectively guide the generation process towards molecules with specific, desired chemical properties (druglikeliness, solubility, synthesizability). This is crucial for practical applications in *de novo* drug discovery and materials science, where functional criteria are paramount. MolGAN's superior performance in this regard compared to previous methods like ORGAN is highly impactful.
4. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** The non-sequential, one-shot generation of graphs by MolGAN's generator results in significantly faster training times (at least 5x faster than comparable sequential models like ORGAN). This efficiency can accelerate the iterative design-build-test cycle in chemical discovery.
5. **High Validity and Novelty:** MolGAN consistently achieves very high validity scores (close to 100%) and impressive novelty, ensuring that the generated compounds are chemically sound and often previously unseen. This makes the generated molecular space more valuable for exploration.
6. **New Benchmark for GAN Stability:** The acknowledged limitation of mode collapse, while a challenge, also positions MolGAN as a valuable testbed for developing and evaluating new techniques for improving GAN stability and diversity in the context of structured data generation. Insights gained from addressing this issue in MolGAN could have broader implications for GAN research beyond molecular graphs.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Drug Discovery:** By generating chemically valid and property-optimized molecular candidates much faster, MolGAN can significantly accelerate the initial stages of drug discovery, reducing the time and cost associated with identifying promising lead compounds.
- **Materials Design:** The methodology can be extended beyond small molecules to design novel materials with specific desired properties by generating new crystal structures or polymers represented as graphs.
- **Broader Graph Generation:** The principles demonstrated by MolGAN could be applied to other domains requiring the generation of structured graph data, such as neural network architectures, social networks, or protein structures.

While the susceptibility to mode collapse is a recognized limitation, the overall framework provides a robust foundation for future research. Addressing this issue, perhaps through improved reward functions or alternative generative architectures (e.g., recurrent graph-based models), will further amplify MolGAN's impact.
