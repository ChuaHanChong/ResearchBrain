# Elucidating the SNR-t Bias of Diffusion Probabilistic Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers characterized a "Signal-to-Noise Ratio-timestep (SNR-t) bias" in Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DPMs), where denoising samples consistently exhibit lower SNR than ideal, leading to inaccurate noise predictions. A training-free differential correction method, Differential Correction in Wavelet domain (DCW), was developed to address this, achieving substantial FID score reductions, such as a 42.6% decrease for IDDPM on CIFAR-10 with 20 sampling steps.

## Problem

- Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DPMs) suffer from inherent biases that degrade the quality of generated outputs.
- A newly identified "Signal-to-Noise Ratio-timestep (SNR-t) bias" causes a misalignment between the SNR of denoising samples and their expected timesteps during the inference phase.
- This misalignment leads to inaccurate noise predictions by the DPM network, particularly an overestimation of noise in reverse-denoising samples, due to accumulated prediction and discretization errors.

## Method

- The researchers characterized the ==SNR-t bias== empirically and theoretically, demonstrating that reverse-denoising samples consistently possess a lower ==Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)== than ideal forward samples at corresponding timesteps.
- A ==training-free differential correction== method was introduced, which applies a dynamic differential signal to guide biased samples toward ideal ones during each denoising step without incurring additional ==Neural Function Evaluations==.
- This correction was enhanced in the ==wavelet domain== (**DCW**) by decomposing samples into ==frequency subbands== and applying ==independently weighted differential corrections==, aligning with the coarse-to-fine denoising process of DPMs.

## Results

- The DCW method reduced the FID score for IDDPM on CIFAR-10 by **42.6%** (from **13.19** to **7.57**) for **20-step** generation and by **25%** (from **5.55** to **4.16**) for **50-step** generation, demonstrating improved generation quality.
- DCW consistently outperformed recent exposure bias correction methods and further enhanced the performance of already bias-corrected models (e.g., **47.1%** FID reduction for EDM with **13 NFE**) across various DPM architectures and sampling strategies.
- Qualitative assessments revealed that DCW mitigated visual artifacts such as over-smoothing and overexposure, yielding images with improved coherence, semantic fidelity, and clearer details, particularly with a small number of sampling steps, while incurring negligible computational overhead (**0.08%** to **0.47%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - DPM networks exhibit sensitivity to a mismatch between input sample SNR and its associated timestep, leading to inaccurate noise predictions.
> - The reverse-denoising process in DPMs inherently produces samples with a lower Signal-to-Noise Ratio than ideal forward-perturbed samples at the same timestep.
> - Applying differential correction in the wavelet domain, with dynamic weighting for different frequency components, is an effective and computationally efficient strategy to mitigate this bias and improve generation quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DPMs) have demonstrated considerable performance across various generative tasks, including image, audio, and video generation, becoming a prominent class of generative models. Despite their successes, DPMs are known to exhibit certain limitations, often manifesting as biases that can degrade generation quality. Previous research has identified and addressed "exposure bias," which refers to discrepancies between training samples and inference samples.

This work introduces and characterizes a distinct phenomenon termed "Signal-to-Noise Ratio-timestep (SNR-t) bias." This bias is presented as a more fundamental issue than exposure bias, potentially underlying it. By identifying this specific misalignment between the SNR of denoising samples and their corresponding timesteps during inference, the paper contributes to a deeper understanding of DPM failure modes. The proposed mitigation strategy, a training-free differential correction method, aims to improve the fidelity and stability of DPMs, positioning this work within the ongoing effort to enhance the robustness and performance of generative models. The method's ability to complement existing bias-correction techniques further situates it as a contribution to the general improvement of DPM inference.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to identify, analyze, and mitigate a newly observed phenomenon in Diffusion Probabilistic Models referred to as the Signal-to-Noise Ratio-timestep (SNR-t) bias. The authors posit that this bias significantly impairs the generation quality of DPMs by causing a misalignment between the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of denoising samples and their corresponding timesteps during the inference phase.

The motivation for this work stems from an observed discrepancy: during the training of DPMs, the SNR of a perturbed sample is deterministically coupled with its timestep. However, the authors note that this correspondence is disrupted during inference due to the cumulative nature of prediction errors from the neural network and discretization errors from numerical solvers. This disruption leads to an accumulation of errors, which, in turn, degrades the quality of generated samples.

Specifically, the key objectives include:
1. **Empirical and Theoretical Elucidation:** To provide comprehensive empirical evidence and a robust theoretical analysis to substantiate the existence and mechanisms of the SNR-t bias. This involves demonstrating how mismatched SNR and timesteps affect network predictions and how the SNR of reverse-denoising samples deviates from ideal forward samples.
2. **Bias Mitigation:** To propose a straightforward yet effective method for mitigating the identified SNR-t bias. A significant motivation is to develop a solution that does not require additional training or fine-tuning of the diffusion models, thereby ensuring negligible computational overhead and broad applicability.
3. **Frequency-Aware Correction:** To enhance the correction mechanism by recognizing the coarse-to-fine denoising characteristic of DPMs, where low-frequency components are reconstructed before high-frequency details. This motivates decomposing samples into frequency components and applying targeted corrections.
4. **Generality and Compatibility:** To develop a "plug-and-play" method that can be readily integrated into various existing DPM architectures and sampling strategies, including those already designed to mitigate other biases like exposure bias, and to demonstrate its efficacy across different datasets and resolutions.

The overarching motivation is to improve the generation quality and robustness of DPMs by addressing a fundamental source of error that has not been explicitly characterized or comprehensively tackled by prior research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves a multi-pronged approach encompassing empirical observation, theoretical derivation, and the development of a novel correction algorithm.

#### 3.1. Characterization of SNR-t Bias

The authors initiated their investigation by conducting empirical experiments to demonstrate the SNR-t bias:
- **Key Finding 1 (Network Prediction Inaccuracy):** An experiment was designed using an ADM model on CIFAR-10. The neural network `ϵ_θ(·, s)` was fixed to a specific timestep `s`, and samples `x_t` (generated with varying actual timesteps `t` from the forward process, thus varying SNR) were fed into it. The `ℓ2` norm of the network's output, `||ϵ_θ(x_t, s)||^2`, was computed. Results indicated that when `x_t` had a lower SNR (i.e., `t > s`), the network overestimated noise predictions. Conversely, when `x_t` had a higher SNR (i.e., `t < s`), predictions were underestimated. This demonstrated the network's sensitivity to SNR-timestep misalignment.
- **Key Finding 2 (Lower SNR in Reverse Process):** This experiment compared the `ℓ2` norm of noise predictions from forward-perturbed samples `x_t` (`||ϵ_θ(x_t, t)||^2`) with those from reverse-denoised samples `x̂_t` (`||ϵ_θ(x̂_t, t)||^2`) at the same timestep `t`. It was consistently observed that `||ϵ_θ(x̂_t, t)||^2` was larger than `||ϵ_θ(x_t, t)||^2`. Combined with Key Finding 1, this led to the conclusion that reverse-denoising samples `x̂_t` generally maintain a lower SNR than their corresponding forward samples `x_t` at the same timestep, leading to overestimated predictions.

For theoretical justification, the authors introduced:
- **Assumption 5.1:** This assumption models the reconstructed sample `x₀_θ(x_t, t)` as `γ_t x₀ + ϕ_t ϵ_t`, where `0 < γ_t ≤ 1` and `ϕ_t` is a scalar coefficient. This formulation suggests that reconstruction operations inherently involve information loss (`γ_t < 1`), distinguishing it from prior assumptions that posited `γ_t = 1`. This assumption is supported by references to Tweedie's formula and the L2-norm loss function, indicating that DPMs predict the posterior mean, and by an empirical experiment showing that `E[||x₀_θ||^2]` is consistently less than `E[||x₀||^2]`.
- **Theorem 5.1:** Based on Assumption 5.1 and the DPM reverse process equations, an analytical form for the SNR of the biased denoising sample `x̂_t` was derived. This derivation formally demonstrates that the actual SNR of `x̂_t` in the reverse process is consistently lower than the ideal SNR of `x_t` from the forward process, thereby providing a theoretical underpinning for the observed SNR-t bias.

#### 3.2. Differential Correction in Pixel Space

To mitigate the bias, the authors proposed a dynamic differential correction method. The core idea is to leverage the implicit gradient information within the denoising process to guide biased samples `x̂_t` towards the ideal perturbed samples `x_t`.
- The differential signal `x̂_(t-1) - x₀_θ(x̂_t, t)` is identified as containing directional information pointing towards `x_(t-1)`.
- This differential signal is integrated into each denoising step: `x̂_(t-1) = x̂_(t-1) + λ_t (x̂_(t-1) - x₀_θ(x̂_t, t))`, where `λ_t` is a scalar guidance factor.
- This correction is applied *after* the neural network has completed its prediction for `x₀_θ(x̂_t, t)`, specifically to the denoised sample `x̂_(t-1)`. This strategy ensures that no additional Neural Function Evaluations (NFEs) are incurred.

#### 3.3. Differential Correction in Wavelet Domain (DCW)

To further enhance the correction effect, the method was extended to the wavelet domain, based on two motivations:
- DPMs reconstruct images in a coarse-to-fine manner, focusing on low-frequency contours initially and then high-frequency details.
- Performing correction in the time-frequency domain helps reduce noise interference.
- **Process:** At each denoising step, Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) is applied to `x̂_t` and `x₀_θ(x̂_t, t)` to decompose them into four frequency subbands (`ll`, `lh`, `hl`, `hh`).
- Differential correction is then applied independently to each frequency subband: `x̂^f_(t-1) = x̂^f_(t-1) + λ^f_t (x̂^f_(t-1) - x₀^f_θ(x̂_t, t))`, where `f` denotes the frequency component.
- Finally, the inverse DWT (iDWT) reconstructs the corrected sample `x̃_(t-1)` back into pixel space.
- **Dynamic Weighting:** The adjustment coefficients `λ^f_t` are dynamically weighted based on the reverse process variance `σ_t`. For low-frequency components, `λ^l_t = λ_l ⋅ σ_t` is used. For high-frequency components, `λ^h_t = (1 - λ_h)σ_t` is applied. Since `σ_t` generally decreases during denoising, both low and high-frequency correction coefficients scale down over time. This design aims to align correction intensity with the denoising stage, prioritizing low-frequency correction in early stages and adapting for high-frequency details later.

The proposed method is designed to be training-free and plug-and-play, integrating seamlessly into existing DPM pipelines with minimal computational overhead.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several main findings and results through extensive experimentation:

#### 4.1. Elucidation of SNR-t Bias

- **Empirical Evidence:** The experiments in Section 4 demonstrated that DPM networks produce significantly inaccurate predictions when input samples have SNRs that mismatch their assigned timesteps (Key Finding 1). Specifically, samples with lower SNR lead to overestimated noise predictions, while those with higher SNR result in underestimated noise predictions. Furthermore, reverse denoising samples were consistently found to exhibit lower SNR compared to their corresponding forward samples at the same timestep (Key Finding 2), which consequently leads to persistently overestimated noise predictions during inference.
- **Theoretical Proof:** The theoretical analysis, including Assumption 5.1 regarding information loss in reconstruction and Theorem 5.1 deriving the analytical form of SNR for biased denoising samples, provided a robust theoretical basis for the empirical observations. It formally established that the actual SNR of predicted samples in the reverse process is consistently lower than that of ideal forward samples, confirming the existence of the SNR-t bias.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness and Generality of DCW

- **Performance Improvement on Classic DPMs:** The Differential Correction in Wavelet domain (DCW) method significantly improved the generation quality of various classic DPMs, including IDDPM, ADM, and ADM-IP, across different datasets (CIFAR-10, CelebA 64x64, ImageNet 128x128, LSUN Bedroom 256x256) and sampling steps (20 and 50). For instance, DCW reduced the FID score for IDDPM on CIFAR-10 by 42.6% (from 13.19 to 7.57) for 20-step generation and by 25% (from 5.55 to 4.16) for 50-step generation. Similar improvements were observed for other models and datasets.
- **Superiority Over Recent Bias-Correction Methods:** DCW consistently outperformed recent methods addressing exposure bias, such as DPM-AE and DPM-AT, when applied to A-DPM and ADM models on CIFAR-10. For example, on A-DPM with 10 DDIM steps, DCW reduced FID from 14.40 to 9.36, while DPM-AE only achieved 13.98.
- **Enhancement of Existing Bias-Corrected Models:** DCW further improved the performance of models already designed to mitigate exposure bias, including ADM-IP, ADM-ES, and DPM-FR. This was demonstrated across stochastic sampling (A-DPM, NPR-DM) and deterministic sampling (EDM, PFGM++) on CIFAR-10. For EDM with 13 NFE, DCW reduced FID by 47.1% (from 10.66 to 5.67). When integrated into EDM-ES, DCW still achieved a 7.0% FID reduction, and for PFGM-FR, it yielded a 6.6% FID reduction, showcasing its ability to provide additional gains beyond other bias-correction techniques. Experiments on DiT with ImageNet 256x256 also showed similar improvements.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Improvements

- Qualitative comparisons using FLUX and Qwen-Image models revealed that DCW effectively mitigated visual artifacts such as over-smoothing and overexposure, especially with a small number of sampling steps (e.g., 10 steps). The images generated with DCW exhibited more coherent scene structures, improved semantic fidelity, and clearer details, leading to enhanced aesthetic quality and visual appeal.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies and Efficiency

- **Effect of Wavelet Domain:** An ablation study confirmed the benefits of performing differential correction in the wavelet domain. Correcting both high and low-frequency components (DCW) achieved superior results compared to correction only in pixel space (DC) or only in high (DH) or low (DL) frequency components. This supports the utility of frequency-aware correction.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The method demonstrated robustness to variations in its hyperparameters `λ_l` and `λ_h`, with optimal values being identifiable through a relatively simple two-stage search process.
- **Computational Overhead:** DCW incurred negligible computational overhead. For various generation tasks across different resolutions (CelebA 64x64, ImageNet 128x128, LSUN Bedroom 256x256), the additional time overhead introduced by DCW ranged from approximately 0.08% to 0.47%, indicating its efficiency and practicality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of generative modeling, particularly DPMs, in several significant ways:

1. **Fundamental Bias Identification:** The identification and comprehensive characterization of the SNR-t bias represent a novel understanding of DPM limitations. By demonstrating that this bias is distinct from, and potentially more fundamental than, previously studied "exposure bias," the work provides a deeper insight into the underlying mechanisms that degrade generative quality in DPMs. This elucidation can guide future research into DPM design and optimization.

2. **Effective and Efficient Mitigation Strategy:** The proposed Differential Correction in Wavelet domain (DCW) method offers a practical solution to address the identified bias. Its design as a training-free and plug-and-play approach makes it highly versatile and easily implementable across a wide array of existing DPMs without requiring extensive retraining or fine-tuning. The demonstrated capability to improve generation quality across various models, datasets, and resolutions attests to its robustness and generalizability.

3. **Enhanced Generative Performance:** The significant reductions in FID scores and improvements in qualitative results (e.g., mitigating over-smoothing and overexposure, enhancing visual fidelity) indicate that DCW can lead to the production of higher-quality and more visually appealing synthetic data. This enhancement is particularly relevant in scenarios where high-fidelity generation is critical, such as in image synthesis, content creation, and data augmentation.

4. **Compatibility and Complementarity:** The ability of DCW to not only improve baseline DPMs but also further enhance the performance of models already designed to correct other biases (like ADM-IP, ADM-ES, DPM-FR) highlights its complementary nature. This suggests that DCW can be integrated into advanced generative pipelines to achieve cumulative benefits, leading to more robust and accurate generative processes.

5. **Low Computational Cost:** The negligible computational overhead associated with DCW is a crucial advantage. This ensures that the improvements in generation quality do not come at the expense of increased inference time, making the method suitable for real-world applications where efficiency is a key concern.

In summary, this work provides both a novel theoretical understanding of a DPM limitation and a practical, efficient, and broadly applicable method for its mitigation. Its impact is expected to be observed in the development of more stable, higher-fidelity, and robust generative models, contributing to the advancement of artificial intelligence applications that rely on high-quality synthetic data.
