# ViTL: Temporal Logic-Guided Zero-Shot Natural Language Navigation via Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The ViTL framework from Lehigh University enables zero-shot robot navigation for long-horizon natural language commands containing complex temporal and logical constraints in unknown environments. It achieves a 95.3% task completion rate for multi-target tasks, showing a 15.31% reduction in path length compared to LLM-based planners, and demonstrates competitive performance for single-target ObjectNav with a 54.1% SR and 30.9 SPL.

## Problem

- Existing zero-shot ObjectNav methods are limited to single-target navigation and cannot execute multi-step, temporally constrained tasks from natural language.
- Prior multi-step navigation approaches often require task-specific fine-tuning, assume known environments, or rely on repeated LLM queries that lack formal guarantees for task completion.
- A gap exists in combining VLM-guided zero-shot exploration in unknown continuous environments with formal temporal logic planning, which typically assumes known or discrete state spaces.

## Method

- The **ViTL** framework integrates a task-level planner that compiles natural language commands into ==Linear Temporal Logic (LTL)== and then into a ==Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA)== offline.
- A ==dynamic target selection== mechanism uses a ==weighted shortest-path search== on the DFA to opportunistically choose the next sub-task based on detected objects and environmental observations, while guaranteeing temporal constraint satisfaction.
- A navigation-level executor guides the robot using ==frontier-based exploration== and a novel ==directional score== method, which queries a ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)== for spatially-varying navigation cues and updates ==multi-channel value maps== for all potential targets.

## Results

- ViTL achieved a **95.3%** task completion rate for multi-target, temporally constrained tasks, significantly outperforming an LLM-based planner (**88.3%** with GPT-5.2) due to robust constraint satisfaction.
- The framework demonstrated a **15.31%** reduction in path length compared to the LLM-based planner for mutually completed tasks, indicating more efficient planning and fewer exploration attempts.
- The single-target navigation executor achieved competitive performance on the HM3D dataset, with a **54.1%** Success Rate (SR) and **30.9** Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), rivaling state-of-the-art zero-shot ObjectNav methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Translating natural language commands into LTL and then a DFA provides a formal, provably correct mechanism for ensuring temporal and logical constraint satisfaction in complex multi-step navigation, outperforming incremental LLM-based planning.
> - Dynamically weighting DFA edges based on environmental discoveries allows for efficient, opportunistic replanning and sub-task reordering, leading to shorter overall path lengths.
> - Leveraging VLMs to generate 'directional scores' for distinct frontier candidates offers more granular and effective guidance for zero-shot exploration compared to direction-agnostic scene scores, enhancing single-target navigation performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the problem of object-goal navigation (ObjectNav) for autonomous mobile robots, specifically focusing on the challenges of enabling robots to execute long-horizon, multi-step tasks specified through natural language commands in unknown environments.

Current advancements in zero-shot ObjectNav have largely leveraged pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) to guide exploration and identify single target objects without task-specific training. Methods such as CoW, ESC, L3MVN, VLFM, WMNav, GAMap, and SG-Nav-GPT have demonstrated capabilities in navigating to a single semantic category. These approaches typically involve exploring frontiers until an object detector or VLM identifies the target, often relying on score maps derived from VLM outputs to prioritize exploration directions.

However, a significant limitation of these existing zero-shot systems is their confinement to single-target navigation. Real-world applications frequently involve complex commands that require navigating to multiple targets in a specific, temporally constrained order, or with logical choices (e.g., "Clean either the chair or the couch, then turn on the tv."). Previous attempts at multi-step task execution from natural language, such as InstructNav, often involve repeated LLM queries at each decision point, lack formal modeling of temporal constraints, and provide no guarantees on task completion. Other approaches, like NavGPT-2 and SayPlan, may require task-specific fine-tuning or assume known environments, which limits their applicability in zero-shot, unexplored settings.

Separately, the field of formal methods in robotics has explored translating natural language commands into formal temporal logic specifications, such as Linear Temporal Logic (LTL). Works like Lang2LTL, LTLCodeGen, and NL2TL focus on accurate translation. Once LTL formulas are obtained, systems like SELP and NL2HLTL2PLAN use temporal logic to guide robot execution, often by constructing automata. However, these applications typically assume known environments with pre-defined landmark locations or operate on discrete state spaces with probabilistic label priors, which differs from navigation in continuous, visually observed, and completely unknown environments.

ViTL positions itself by bridging these two areas: combining the zero-shot exploration capabilities of VLM-guided navigation with the formal guarantees and structured planning provided by temporal logic and automata. It aims to develop a system that can interpret complex natural language commands, satisfy their temporal and logical constraints, and perform efficient navigation in unseen, continuous environments, a gap not fully addressed by existing zero-shot or temporal-logic-based systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a zero-shot navigation framework that enables robots to execute long-horizon natural language commands containing complex temporal and logical constraints within previously unknown environments. This overarching goal is motivated by the observation that real-world robotic applications demand capabilities beyond single-target navigation, requiring robots to perform sequences of actions and make choices based on environmental discoveries.

The specific motivations and objectives can be detailed as follows:

1. **Address the limitations of single-target zero-shot navigation:** Existing zero-shot ObjectNav methods are effective for finding a single object but cannot interpret or execute commands that involve multiple targets, sequencing, disjunctions (e.g., "either A or B"), or other temporal dependencies. The work aims to extend these capabilities to more complex, multi-objective tasks.

2. **Enable extraction of implicit temporal and logical constraints from natural language:** Natural language commands often contain implicit temporal orderings and logical relationships between sub-tasks. A key objective is to accurately extract these constraints and formalize them for reliable robot execution. This includes converting commands like "Clean either the chair or the couch, then turn on the tv" into a computable, unambiguous representation.

3. **Develop a high-level task planner for dynamic sub-task selection and replanning:** In unknown environments, the optimal sequence of sub-tasks cannot be predetermined. The system needs a planner that can continuously integrate new environmental observations (e.g., detecting new objects), dynamically select the most efficient next sub-task, and ensure that all temporal and logical constraints are consistently met, even as the environment unfolds. This planner must support opportunistic reordering of sub-tasks to minimize travel distance.

4. **Improve the efficiency and accuracy of zero-shot single-target navigation:** While the primary focus is multi-target tasks, the authors also seek to enhance the underlying single-target navigation executor. This involves developing a method that can more effectively leverage VLMs to guide exploration, providing spatially-aware navigation cues rather than direction-agnostic scores.

5. **Provide formal guarantees for task completion and constraint satisfaction:** Unlike purely LLM-based planners that can suffer from reasoning errors, temporal constraint violations, or premature termination, the goal is to implement a system that offers provable guarantees regarding the satisfaction of temporal constraints and the logical completion of the task.

6. **Minimize runtime computational overhead and potential for cumulative errors:** The authors motivate the use of a single, offline LLM call for task compilation, as opposed to repeated runtime queries, to reduce latency and avoid the accumulation of reasoning errors inherent in iterative LLM prompting.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more sophisticated, robust, and formally grounded zero-shot navigation capabilities in autonomous robots, moving beyond simple single-target localization to address the complexities of real-world, long-horizon tasks described in natural language.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ViTL is presented as a zero-shot navigation framework designed to execute long-horizon natural language commands with temporal constraints. Its methodology integrates a high-level task planner with a low-level navigation executor, addressing the problem at two distinct but interconnected levels.

#### 3.1. A. Task-Level Planner

The task-level planner is responsible for interpreting natural language commands, formalizing temporal constraints, dynamically selecting targets, and replanning based on environmental observations.

1. **From Natural Language to Automaton:**
 - **LTL Formulation:** Natural language commands are first compiled into Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) formulas. An LLM is prompted once offline to extract target categories and temporal constraints, producing an LTL formula. For example, "Reach either a chair or a couch, then reach a tv" translates to `φ = ♢((chair ∨ couch) ∧ ♢ tv)`. This single LLM call is intended to reduce runtime latency and reasoning errors compared to repeated LLM queries.
 - **DFA Compilation:** The generated LTL formula is then converted into a Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA) using standard tools (e.g., the `lomap` library). The DFA, represented as a graph, formally encodes all valid execution orderings of sub-tasks. Each state in the DFA represents a stage in task progression, and transitions are triggered upon reaching a specific target object. The graph structure allows for principled sub-task decomposition, with branching paths representing choices and path length indicating remaining sub-tasks.

2. **Dynamic Target Selection and Replanning:**
 - **Environmental State Tracking:** The planner maintains three sets to track the robot's knowledge: `D` (detected objects with known locations but not yet reached), `R` (reached objects), and `B` (targets the navigator failed to reach).
 - **Weighted DFA Search:** The DFA's edges, which correspond to reaching specific target objects, are assigned dynamic weights. These weights reflect the robot's current knowledge: `w_det` (very low weight) for detected targets, `w_fail` (very high weight) for failed targets, and `1.0` for undetected targets.
 - **Shortest Path Selection:** At each decision point, the planner computes the shortest weighted path through the DFA from the current state to an accepting state. The target associated with the first edge of this shortest path is selected as the next navigation target. This dynamic weighting allows the system to opportunistically switch to more easily accessible targets if they are detected, thereby minimizing overall travel distance and adapting to new information.
 - **Replanning Triggers:** Replanning is initiated whenever `D`, `R`, or `B` changes (e.g., a new object is detected, a target is reached, or navigation to a target fails).
 - **Infeasibility Detection:** The system identifies task infeasibility if the compiled DFA has no path from the initial state to an accepting state (contradictory command) or if the shortest path requires traversing a target that has been marked as failed (`∑ w(e) ≥ w_fail`).

3. **Multi-Channel Value Maps:**
 - To support multi-target execution and prevent loss of exploration effort when switching targets, the system maintains a separate value map channel `V^(j)` for each target category `c_j` specified in the natural language command.
 - All these channels are updated in parallel at every timestep by the navigation-level executor. When the task-level planner selects a target `c_k`, it activates the corresponding channel `V^(k)` for the navigation executor to use.

#### 3.2. B. Navigation-Level Executor

The navigation-level executor guides the robot to a specific target `c_k` using frontier-based exploration, informed by a novel "directional score" method.

1. **Frontier-Based Exploration:**
 - The system constructs a top-down occupancy map using depth observations and odometry.
 - Frontiers (boundaries between explored and unexplored regions) are extracted from this map, and their midpoints serve as 2D waypoints.

2. **Directional Score:**
 - Unlike prior VLM-based approaches that yield a single, direction-agnostic score for the entire field of view, ViTL introduces directional scores.
 - Frontiers are projected onto the current RGB image and assigned unique letter labels (e.g., A, B, C). An additional "explore elsewhere" option is included for calibration.
 - A Vision-Language Model (VLM, specifically LLaVA-NeXT-34B) is prompted with a multiple-choice question: "Among directions (A, B, C), or explore elsewhere (D), which should I choose to find a {target}? Answer with ONLY a single letter." The VLM's next-token logits for the option letters are used to compute a probability distribution `P(f_i | I_t, c_k)` for each frontier.
 - The VLM is also queried for an overall "scene relevance score" (e.g., "Does this view show paths or doorways that could lead to a {target}? Answer Yes or No."), yielding `S_curr`.
 - The final current value for each frontier is a blend: `V_curr(f_i) = S_curr × P(f_i | I_t, c_k)`.

3. **Smooth Value Map Construction:**
 - **Temporal Fusion:** Each frontier's score is fused with historical observations at its map location. A scene score map `S` records the `S_curr` from the last observation. New and old values are combined using scene-weighted averaging, where observations from more informative viewpoints (higher `S_curr`) contribute more to the fused value `˜V(f_i)`.
 - **Spatial Smoothing:** The discrete `˜V(f_i)` values are interpolated to create a dense, smoothly varying gradient across the entire Field-of-View (FOV) cone. This is achieved by computing bearing angles for each frontier, inserting virtual "edge frontiers" at FOV boundaries, and performing linear interpolation for every pixel within the cone.
 - The smoothed values are written into the corresponding channel of the multi-channel value map `V`.

4. **Frontier Selection and Low-Level Navigation:**
 - The agent selects the next waypoint `f*` by finding the frontier whose surrounding pixels (within a radius `r`) have the highest score in the active value map channel `V^(k)`.
 - This selected frontier `f*` serves as a goal for a pre-trained depth-based PointNav policy, which translates the relative goal position into low-level robot actions (e.g., `move_forward`, `turn_left`, `turn_right`).

This continuous cycle of map update, frontier scoring, selection, and navigation repeats until the target object is detected, reached, or the task fails.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of ViTL was conducted on the Habitat simulator using the Habitat-Matterport 3D (HM3D) dataset, specifically the ObjectNav v2 validation split (2000 episodes across 20 scenes). The agent's performance was measured using Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL). The system utilized LLaVA-NeXT-34B as the VLM, YOLOv7-E6E for object detection, and a pre-trained depth-based PointNav policy for low-level control. The `lomap` library compiled LTL formulas into DFAs.

#### 4.1. A. Navigation-Level Executor (Single-Target ObjectNav)

The initial set of experiments focused on evaluating ViTL's navigation-level executor in the context of single-target ObjectNav, a common benchmark for zero-shot navigation systems.

- ViTL (using LLaVA-NeXT-34B) achieved a Success Rate (SR) of 54.1% and an SPL of 30.9.
- These results were competitive with or surpassed several state-of-the-art zero-shot ObjectNav methods, including WMNav (Gemini 1.5 Flash: 53.5% SR, 30.7 SPL; Gemini 1.5 Pro: 58.1% SR, 31.2 SPL), GAMap (53.1% SR, 26.0 SPL), SG-Nav-GPT (54.0% SR, 24.9 SPL), VLFM (52.4% SR, 30.8 SPL), and InstructNav (GPT4 + GPT4V: 56.0% SR, 22.5 SPL; GPT4 + LLaVA 34B: 50.0% SR, 19.4 SPL).
- The findings suggest that the proposed directional score method, which generates spatially-varying navigation value maps, contributes to improved navigation reliability. This performance was achieved using an open-source VLM (LLaVA-NeXT-34B) without task-specific fine-tuning, competing effectively against methods leveraging larger, closed-source models.

#### 4.2. B. Task-Level Planner (Multi-Target, Temporally Constrained Tasks)

To evaluate the task-level planner, a benchmark of 2000 natural language navigation tasks with ground-truth LTL formulas was created. These tasks involved up to six targets with complex temporal constraints. Since no direct zero-shot baseline existed for such complex tasks, ViTL was compared against an LLM-based planner (LLM-planner) that used GPT-5.2 to generate the next target at each decision point. The low-level navigation executor was probabilistically modeled, with `p_obs` representing the probability of finding the intended target during exploration.

- **Task Completion Rate:**
 - ViTL's NL-to-LTL translation achieved 95.3% accuracy (1906 out of 2000 tasks). Given a correct LTL translation, ViTL's task-level planner achieved 100% task completion across all `p_obs` values (0.1 to 1.0) because task execution reduces to a guaranteed graph search on the DFA. Therefore, ViTL's overall reported success rate was capped by its translation accuracy at 95.3%.
 - The LLM-planner, which performs incremental temporal reasoning, achieved an 88.3% completion rate with GPT-5.2. Its failure rate (approximately 11%) remained relatively constant across `p_obs` values, indicating that failures were primarily due to reasoning errors, temporal constraint violations, or premature termination, rather than navigation challenges.

- **Exploration Attempts:**
 - ViTL consistently required fewer exploration attempts across all `p_obs` values compared to the LLM-planner. This suggests that ViTL's DFA-guided dynamic replanning leads to more efficient task execution by optimizing target selection.

- **Case Studies:** Two specific examples illustrated the LLM-planner's limitations:
 - **Case 1 (Temporal Constraint Violation):** For a complex task with multiple disjunctive and negative temporal constraints, the LLM-planner violated a constraint by reaching a target (tv) before a prerequisite (bed), leading to task failure despite visiting multiple objects. ViTL correctly identified and followed a valid path, satisfying all constraints.
 - **Case 2 (Premature Termination):** In another task, the LLM-planner falsely declared task completion despite remaining unsatisfied, while ViTL correctly identified and executed the shortest satisfying path. These cases demonstrated that the DFA in ViTL effectively enforces constraint satisfaction and prevents logical errors.

#### 4.3. C. Full System Evaluation

An end-to-end evaluation was performed to quantify the impact of planning-level advantages on actual navigation paths. Two HM3D scenes were selected where the single-target navigator achieved a high success rate (approximately 90%) to minimize noise from low-level navigation failures.

- On 50 mutually completed episodes (where both ViTL and LLM-planner successfully completed the task), ViTL achieved an average path length of 25.45 m, compared to 30.05 m for the LLM-planner.
- This represented a 15.31% reduction in path length for ViTL, confirming that its more efficient planning and fewer exploration attempts translate into tangible savings in navigation distance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ViTL framework represents an advancement in embodied AI by enabling robots to perform long-horizon natural language navigation tasks with complex temporal and logical constraints in unknown environments. This work addresses a notable gap in existing zero-shot navigation systems, which have largely been limited to single-target objectives.

The significance of ViTL stems from several key contributions:

1. **Enabling Complex Task Execution:** ViTL is designed to handle natural language commands that specify not only multiple targets but also intricate temporal relationships (e.g., sequencing, disjunctions, "until" conditions). This capability extends the utility of zero-shot navigation to more realistic and complex scenarios relevant for service robotics, exploration, and other autonomous agent applications.

2. **Formal Guarantees and Robustness:** By leveraging Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and Deterministic Finite Automata (DFA), ViTL introduces a formal mechanism for task planning. This approach provides provable guarantees for temporal constraint satisfaction, which contrasts with the observed limitations of purely LLM-based planners that can suffer from reasoning errors, constraint violations, or premature termination. The DFA-based execution enhances the robustness and reliability of multi-step task completion.

3. **Efficient and Dynamic Replanning:** The dynamic weighting of DFA edges and the use of shortest path search enable the robot to adapt its execution order in real time based on new environmental observations (e.g., detecting an object earlier than expected). This opportunistic replanning mechanism can lead to more efficient task completion by minimizing exploration efforts and overall travel distance. The maintenance of multi-channel value maps ensures that spatial knowledge for all potential targets is accumulated in parallel, preventing redundant re-exploration when the planner switches targets.

4. **Improved Zero-Shot Navigation Performance:** The introduction of the directional score method at the navigation level enhances the accuracy and efficiency of single-target navigation. By querying VLMs for spatially-varying scores across different frontier directions, the system gains more granular and informative guidance than previous direction-agnostic approaches, contributing to better local navigation decisions.

5. **Reduced LLM Dependency at Runtime:** ViTL utilizes a Large Language Model for a single, offline translation of natural language to LTL, rather than repeated runtime queries for every decision. This design choice contributes to lower latency and mitigates the risk of cumulative reasoning errors that can arise in iterative LLM prompting, making the system more efficient and reliable in dynamic environments.

The potential impact of this research is substantial for the field of robotics and embodied AI. It provides a foundation for developing more intelligent and autonomous robots capable of understanding and executing high-level human instructions for complex, long-horizon tasks in previously unknown spaces. Future work could explore extending ViTL to incorporate richer sub-task semantics beyond mere object reaching, such as manipulation or interaction, further broadening the scope of robot capabilities.
