# The Surprising Effectiveness of Linear Models for Whole-Body Model-Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University and the Florida Institute for Human and Machine Cognition demonstrate that a single time-invariant linear dynamics model within a convex Model Predictive Control framework can effectively achieve dynamic locomotion, disturbance rejection, and navigation on both quadrupedal and hydraulic humanoid robots, enabling high control rates and robust performance. This approach bypasses the computational demands of full nonlinear model predictive control, showing its capability for fundamental legged locomotion tasks.

## Problem

- Legged robots exhibit underactuated, non-smooth, and highly nonlinear dynamics, posing challenges for robust control.
- Traditional Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) is computationally expensive, requiring the online solution of large, non-convex optimal control problems.
- NMPC approaches often lack convergence guarantees and are difficult to tune and formally analyze due to their complexity.

## Method

- A ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework linearizes the robot's whole-body dynamics about a fixed standing pose into a single time-invariant affine linear state-space model, discretized for control.
- Contact conditions are handled by pre-specifying contact modes, transforming non-convex constraints into convex equality or inequality constraints.
- The resulting convex Quadratic Program (QP) allows for offline pre-factorization of its Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) system, reducing online computation to matrix-vector products and back-substitutions.

## Results

- The Unitree Go2 quadruped successfully demonstrated disturbance rejection from pushes, navigated to goal positions from substantial initial errors (up to 90-degree yaw), and stepped onto a 24 cm high box, handling significant joint deviations.
- The IHMC Nadia hydraulic humanoid performed stable walking in-place for over a minute and robust forward walking with stride lengths up to 30 cm across multiple trials, indicating dynamic motion capabilities.
- Online QP solutions using OSQP required only **0.10** 	 **0.02** ms for the Nadia humanoid's KKT system after offline factorization, facilitating closed-loop control rates of 500 Hz for the quadruped and 333 Hz for the humanoid.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single time-invariant linear dynamics model can be sufficiently effective for many fundamental legged locomotion tasks, obviating the need for complex online nonlinear dynamics reasoning.
> - The convexity of the MPC formulation, combined with offline pre-factorization of the KKT system, enables real-time control rates of hundreds of Hertz on complex robots.
> - Adopting a linear, convex MPC framework enhances solution reliability, predictability, and analytical tractability compared to non-convex NMPC.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged locomotion for robots operating in human environments presents significant control challenges due to the underactuated nature, non-smooth dynamics, and inherent nonlinearities of these systems. Historically, early successes in legged locomotion leveraged simplified models, such as the spring-loaded inverted pendulum (SLIP) and the zero-moment point (ZMP) concept, to construct controllers. These methods yielded simple, often closed-form control laws that were amenable to stability analysis, demonstrating capabilities like stable running on rough terrain with open-loop control. However, these simplified approaches often struggle with generalization and have limited capacity to account for limb inertias, complex constraints, or external disturbances.

To overcome these limitations, the field has increasingly adopted optimization-based methods, particularly Model Predictive Control (MPC). MPC formulates and solves a receding-horizon optimal control problem, incorporating robot dynamics, constraints, and cost functions. Modern MPC approaches, particularly those dealing with full nonlinear dynamics, known as Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC), have enabled impressive agile and robust behaviors in complex robots, exemplified by notable achievements in humanoid and quadrupedal locomotion. However, NMPC is computationally intensive, frequently requiring the online solution of large, non-convex optimization problems, often through Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP). SQP involves repeatedly linearizing dynamics and constraints and solving Quadratic Programs (QPs), which can be computationally expensive due to the need for solving large linear systems and the challenges of parallelization. Furthermore, NMPC problems can be non-convex, lacking convergence guarantees and requiring substantial domain expertise for practical implementation.

Given these computational and analytical challenges, researchers have explored avenues to reduce the complexity of online MPC. This includes leveraging the observation that subproblems in MPC do not change significantly between solves when dynamics are slow relative to the control rate, or that QPs can yield closed-form linear feedback policies under constant active constraint sets. Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) controllers and linearized dynamics models have also shown surprising capabilities in various complex control tasks.

This work contributes to the debate by specifically exploring the effectiveness of a **single time-invariant linear dynamics model within a convex MPC framework** for performing basic locomotion tasks on complex legged robots. It directly addresses the question of whether complex nonlinear dynamics reasoning is *always* necessary for foundational locomotion capabilities. By demonstrating robust performance with a significantly simplified model, the paper positions itself as a challenge to the prevailing trend of increasing model complexity in exchange for computational cost and analytical intractability. It bridges the gap between the simplicity and analyzability of early models and the robustness of modern optimization-based control, suggesting that a middle ground might exist for a range of practical tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate the extent to which a simplified linear dynamics model, within a convex Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework, can achieve effective locomotion on complex legged robots. The authors specifically aim to determine if online reasoning about full nonlinear dynamics is an indispensable requirement for basic locomotion tasks, or if a significantly less computationally intensive approach can suffice.

The key motivations driving this research are rooted in the limitations of current advanced legged locomotion control methods:

1. **Computational Cost:** Full Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) and learning-based methods, while powerful, are computationally expensive. NMPC often requires solving large, non-convex optimal control problems online, typically involving iterative linearization and solving Quadratic Programs (QPs). This process is resource-intensive and can be a bottleneck for achieving high control rates on real-time systems. The authors aim to develop a control approach that significantly reduces online computational load.
2. **Difficulty in Tuning and Analysis:** Nonlinear and non-convex optimization problems are often challenging to tune effectively for real-world robotic systems. Their non-convex nature also means there are no guarantees of finding a global optimum, and their stability and robustness properties can be difficult to analyze formally. The researchers are motivated to explore a formulation that offers greater analytical tractability and potentially simpler tuning.
3. **Necessity of Nonlinear Reasoning:** The fundamental question motivating this work is: "When do locomotion controllers require reasoning about nonlinearities?" The authors hypothesize that for many practical locomotion tasks that do not involve extreme joint angles or body orientations, a convex MPC formulation using a single time-invariant linear dynamics model might be sufficient. They seek to test this hypothesis empirically on diverse robot platforms.
4. **Desirable Control Properties:** Based on their hypothesis, the authors aim to develop an MPC formulation with the following specific properties:
 - **Convexity:** To ensure a unique solution can be found in polynomial time, thereby improving reliability and predictability.
 - **Linearity:** To eliminate the need for computationally expensive online nonlinear dynamics and kinematics evaluations, which typically involve complex trigonometric functions and matrix exponentials.
 - **Matrix-inverse-free Operation:** To avoid costly online matrix inversions or factorizations, reducing online computation primarily to matrix-vector products and clamp operations. This leverages the fact that for a fixed dynamics and constraint structure, the KKT system can be pre-factorized offline.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to provide an alternative, more computationally efficient, and potentially more robust control paradigm for a subset of legged locomotion challenges, thereby expanding the applicability and accessibility of advanced robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented involves developing and testing a Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulation that relies on a single time-invariant linear approximation of the robot's whole-body dynamics. This approach aims to simplify the online optimization problem while retaining sufficient control authority for basic locomotion tasks.

**A. Dynamics Model:**
The robot's dynamics are modeled using a floating-base representation. The configuration `q` includes the base position, body-to-world quaternion, and joint angles. The velocity vector `v` comprises linear and angular velocities of the base (in the body frame) and joint velocities. A kinematic relationship `q̇ = E(q)v` connects `q̇` and `v`. The continuous-time manipulator equations `M(q)v̇ + C(q,v) = Bτ + J(q)ᵀλ` describe the robot's dynamics, where `M(q)` is the mass matrix, `C(q,v)` is the dynamics bias, `B` maps actuator torques `τ` to generalized forces, and `J(q)ᵀλ` represents generalized forces from contact forces `λ`. For robots with low-level PD motor controllers (e.g., Unitree Go2), the dynamics equation is augmented to include these effects: `Mv̇ + C + K_p q + K_d q̇ = Bτ`, where `K_p` and `K_d` are diagonal gain matrices.

**B. Dynamics Linearization:**
To achieve a linear MPC formulation, the continuous-time state-space model derived from the robot's dynamics is linearized about a chosen operating point. This linearization uses an axis-angle rotation parameterization for state error `∆x = e(x, x₀)`, where `x₀` is the linearization point for state, `u₀` for control, and `λ₀` for contact forces. The linearization is performed around a single, time-invariant standing pose. The linearized dynamics are then discretized using backward Euler integration, resulting in an affine linear dynamics model: `A+ ∆x_k+1 + A∆x_k + B∆u_k = d_k`, where `∆u_k = [∆τ_k; ∆λ_k]`.

**C. Contact Modeling:**
Instead of directly solving non-convex complementarity constraints inherent in classical contact modeling, the approach adopts a hybrid strategy. It pre-specifies whether contact occurs at each time step (contact mode). This allows for the conversion of contact conditions into convex equality or inequality constraints:
- **No contact:** `ϕ(q) ≥ 0, n = 0` (distance non-negative, normal force zero).
- **Contact:** `ϕ(q) = 0, n ≥ 0` (distance zero, normal force non-negative).
For contact points, the constraint enforces that the x and y positions at adjacent knot points are equal, allowing the MPC to determine foot placement. Assuming the ground is at `z=0`, this is expressed as `c(q_k+1) − diag([1 1 0])c(q_k) = 0`, which linearizes to `J∆q_k+1 − diag([1 1 0])J∆q_k = 0`. For non-contact, the constraints on contact point motion are set to be very loose. For the hydraulic humanoid with patch contacts, four contact points at the foot corners are modeled, and a small regularizer (10⁻⁵) is added to contact force constraints to address potential rank deficiencies.

**D. Reference Tracking and MPC Formulation (Linear Time-Invariant MPC):**
The controller tracks offline-generated whole-body gait references (`x̂`, `û`) for state, control, and contact forces. These references are also defined relative to the chosen linearization point. The core of the MPC is formulated as a Quadratic Program (QP):
`min_{x, u, λ} Σ_{k=1}^{N-1} (1/2) [(x_k - x̂_k)ᵀ Q (x_k - x̂_k) + (u_k - û_k)ᵀ R (u_k - û_k)] + (1/2) (x_N - x̂_N)ᵀ Q_f (x_N - x̂_N)`
subject to:
- `x₁ = x_{i.c.}` (initial state constraint)
- `A+ x_{k+1} + A x_k + B u_k = d_k` (linearized dynamics constraint)
- `l_c ≤ J x_{k+1} ≤ h_c` (contact mode constraints, including friction cone and position/velocity)
- `l_f ≤ u_k ≤ h_f` (force and torque constraints)

This QP is convex because the dynamics and all constraints are linear, and the cost function is quadratic and positive semi-definite. A crucial aspect of this formulation is that the resulting cost Hessian `H` and constraint Jacobian `D` are fixed. This allows for offline pre-factorization or pre-inversion of the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) system, an `O(n³)` operation. Consequently, online, the QP solver (OSQP, using QDLDL) only needs to perform computationally cheaper `O(n²)` back-substitutions or matrix-vector products to obtain a solution. The reference and contact mode changes only modify the cost gradient `g` and constraint bounds `l` and `h`, without requiring re-factorization.

**E. Experimental Setup:**
The MPC formulation was tested on two distinct robot platforms:
1. **Unitree Go2 Quadruped:** A relatively lightweight robot (15.7 kg) with 12 joints. Each foot was modeled as a single contact point. The system had 36 states, 12 actuator torques, and 24 contact forces. A planning horizon of 0.2 seconds with a 0.01-second timestep was used, achieving a closed-loop control rate of approximately 500 Hz.
2. **IHMC Nadia Hydraulic Humanoid:** A more challenging system with hydraulic linear actuators, complex linkage mechanisms (e.g., knee), large leg inertias (21 kg per leg), and patch contacts. The lower body was controlled (12 revolute joints, 1 spine yaw joint), resulting in 38 states, 13 actuator torques, and 24 contact forces (four contact points per foot). A 0.2-second horizon with 0.01-second timestep was used, running 20 QP iterations for each solve at a 333 Hz closed-loop control rate.

Offline reference motions were planned using the Pinocchio rigid body dynamics library and the trajectory optimization library Aligator. The QP was solved online using OSQP.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed linear time-invariant MPC approach on both a quadrupedal robot and a hydraulic humanoid, presenting quantitative and qualitative results across various locomotion tasks.

**A. Computational Performance:**
A key finding related to the methodology's efficiency was the measured timing for the OSQP solver (using QDLDL) on the Nadia humanoid's KKT system (a 3,504 x 3,504, 99.3% sparse matrix). Offline factorization of this system took 5.9 ± 0.8 ms, while an online back-solve operation required only 0.10 ± 0.02 ms. This indicates a significant computational advantage over Nonlinear MPC (NMPC), which would necessitate online evaluation of nonlinear dynamics, Jacobian computations, and potentially multiple factorization steps or line-searches, making the linear approach substantially faster for online execution.

**B. Quadruped Experiments (Unitree Go2):**
Experiments on the Unitree Go2 quadruped utilized a walking-in-place reference without a separate high-level planner or contact heuristics.
1. **Disturbance Rejection:** The controller demonstrated the ability to recover from moderate pushes (from front and side) while the robot was stepping in place. This included exhibiting footstep recovery behaviors. However, the system showed limitations with large or sustained perturbations, which led to controller failure due to infeasible QPs (contact constraints could not be satisfied), suggesting a need for a higher-level planner for such extreme conditions.
2. **Goal Navigation:** The linear controller was able to navigate the quadruped to a goal position using only an open-loop walking-in-place reference. The robot successfully drove its body position and orientation errors to zero, even when initialized with substantial yaw errors (up to 90 degrees) and displacements (from 0.1 m to 1.7 m) from the linearization pose. This capability arose purely from the MPC planning footsteps without assistance from a dedicated footstep planner.
3. **Stepping onto a Box:** The controller successfully managed stepping onto a 24 cm high box. This task involved significant deviations from the linearization pose, including up to 10 degrees in body pitch, 11 degrees in hip joints, 29 degrees in thigh joints, and 60 degrees in calf joints. The controller handled the change in contact height by updating the contact constraint.

**C. Hydraulic Humanoid Experiments (IHMC Nadia):**
The Nadia humanoid presented a more complex control challenge due to its hydraulic actuators, linkage mechanisms, large leg inertias, patch contacts, and a notable sim-to-real gap.
1. **Stable Walking:** The MPC controller enabled the Nadia humanoid to stably walk in-place for over a minute, demonstrating robust foundational locomotion without noticeable instabilities.
2. **Forward Walking:** The robot successfully performed forward walking with both short (17 cm) and long (30 cm) stride lengths. Six trials were conducted for each forward walking reference, with no reported failures over eight steps per trial.
3. **Dynamic Motion Capability:** The reference trajectories for forward walking were not quasi-static, as indicated by the center of mass frequently being outside the support polygon of the stance foot during each step. This suggests the controller's ability to manage dynamic motions.
4. **Tracking Accuracy:** For the 30 cm stride length, the maximum final tracking error for the center of mass across all six runs was measured at 3.6 cm, indicating reasonable accuracy in tracking the desired gait despite the system's complexities and model simplifications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of legged robot locomotion by demonstrating the surprising effectiveness of a simplified control paradigm. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Re-evaluation of Model Complexity:** The work directly challenges the prevailing assumption that complex, full nonlinear dynamics models are always necessary for basic and robust legged locomotion. By achieving dynamic walking, disturbance rejection, and navigation on both a quadruped and a hydraulic humanoid using a single, time-invariant linear dynamics approximation, the paper suggests that a substantial portion of legged locomotion tasks may not require the computational overhead of NMPC. This could lead to a re-evaluation of the required model fidelity for various robotic applications.

2. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency and Real-time Performance:** The core achievement of this method is its computational efficiency. By formulating the MPC problem as a convex Quadratic Program (QP) with a fixed Hessian and constraint Jacobian, the most computationally intensive steps (matrix factorization) can be performed offline. This drastically reduces the online computation to O(n²) operations (matrix-vector products and back-substitutions), enabling significantly faster control rates (500 Hz for quadruped, 333 Hz for humanoid) compared to NMPC. This efficiency is critical for deploying advanced locomotion controllers on embedded systems or for high-frequency control applications where computational resources are limited.

3. **Improved Robustness and Analyzability:** The convex nature of the MPC problem guarantees a unique solution in polynomial time, which enhances the reliability and predictability of the controller compared to non-convex NMPC problems that may converge to local optima or fail to converge. While the paper notes limitations in extreme scenarios, the inherent mathematical properties of convexity contribute to a more robust optimization process. Furthermore, linear models are generally easier to analyze for stability and performance guarantees, potentially simplifying controller design and validation.

4. **Accessibility and Ease of Implementation:** The described approach avoids the complexities associated with online nonlinear dynamics evaluations, Jacobian computations, and intricate tuning procedures often required for NMPC. This simplification could make advanced model-predictive control techniques more accessible to a broader range of researchers and engineers, lowering the barrier to entry for developing capable legged robot controllers.

5. **Foundation for Hybrid Control Architectures:** While the linear model has limitations (e.g., inability to re-plan contacts or handle extreme kinematic configurations), its computational speed makes it an excellent candidate for serving as a fast, reactive "inner loop" in a hierarchical or hybrid control architecture. A higher-level, slower planner (e.g., learning-based or heuristic-based) could handle contact re-planning, gait adaptation, or task-specific objectives, while the linear MPC provides robust, high-frequency execution. This integrated approach could leverage the strengths of both simplified and complex models.

6. **Insights into System Structure:** The success on complex systems like the hydraulic Nadia humanoid, with its significant limb inertia and complex kinematics, suggests that the underlying control landscape of these robots might possess more structure or linearity than conventionally assumed, at least within typical operating ranges. This provides valuable insights into the fundamental properties of dynamic legged locomotion.

In conclusion, this research demonstrates that a thoughtfully simplified linear model within an MPC framework can yield surprisingly effective and computationally efficient locomotion control. Its impact lies in offering a practical, robust, and fast control solution for many common tasks, potentially influencing the design of future robotic control systems and inspiring new hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of both simple and complex models.
