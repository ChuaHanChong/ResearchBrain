# Towards High-DoF Dexterous Manipulation through VLA Post-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> A four-stage post-training pipeline adapting the π0.5 VLA to a bimanual robot with two 20-DoF hands. A frozen temporal Transformer VAE compresses each hand's action chunk to 9 latent dimensions, shared across supervised fine-tuning, human-gated DAgger with buffered rollback and 2-second command blending, and TD3 latent residual RL on the frozen policy. All five real-world marker tasks reach 100% success over 20 trials.

## Problem

- Open-source VLAs such as π0.5 expose a fixed 32-dimensional action interface that cannot natively carry absolute commands for a bimanual platform with two 20-DoF dexterous hands.
- Human-gated DAgger under absolute hand commands suffers gesture mismatch at takeover, causing command discontinuities and polluting corrections with realignment motion; prior fixes like HandITL abandon absolute commands via relative retargeting.
- Real-world residual RL in raw joint space perturbs dozens of joints independently, making exploration sample-inefficient and uncoordinated, while contact-rich dexterous dynamics resist accurate simulation.

## Method

- **Temporal hand-action codec**: a frozen per-hand chunk-level ==Transformer VAE== compresses each 20-DoF hand to 9 latents, so π0.5 is fine-tuned in a 32-D latent space with an added ==decoded-hand-action loss==.
- **Buffered-rollback DAgger**: a 5-second ==rolling state buffer== lets the operator roll back to a pre-failure state, an interface aligns operator and robot hand poses, and a 2-second ==linear blend== smooths the command switch.
- **Latent residual RL**: chunk-level ==TD3== adds a bounded, zero-initialized residual to the frozen VLA's latent output, conditioned on an ==RL-token readout==, with ==Brownian hand exploration==, residual and smoothness penalties, and critic warm-up.

## Results

- At nine latents per hand, the chunk Transformer codec reconstructs at **0.275°** per-joint error versus **1.066°** for frame-wise PCA, and latent imitation shows no consistent success loss versus raw 54-D joint-space actions.
- DAgger beats demonstration-only training at matched **250** and **300** episode budgets, yet Transfer plateaus at **70%**; RL rollouts cost **1.2** worker-hours per 100 episodes versus **3.9** for DAgger.
- Within **3,000** real transitions, latent residual RL lifts Transfer from **70%** to **100%** and Rotate from **50%** to **100%**, whereas matched raw joint-space residual RL ends at **80%** and **50%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Residual RL is a late-stage polisher: it gave little gain when the frozen base policy succeeded under about 20%, but helped consistently once base success exceeded roughly 50%.
> - The action space shapes exploration: a learned synergy-like latent turns uncoordinated joint perturbations into coherent corrections, and temporally correlated noise outperforms white noise.
> - One frozen action codec can serve as a shared interface across SFT, DAgger, and RL, keeping action semantics consistent and letting a zero residual exactly recover the fallback policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses the adaptation of vision–language–action (VLA) foundation models to high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) dexterous robot hands. Recent VLA systems, including RT-2, Octo, π₀, and related models, have been trained on large-scale, multi-task, and multi-embodiment robot datasets. These models provide broad manipulation capabilities and can interpret visual observations, language instructions, and robot state. However, broad pretraining does not guarantee reliable performance on a specific robot, task, or control interface. Deployment typically requires task- and embodiment-specific post-training.

Dexterous manipulation presents a more difficult adaptation problem than manipulation with parallel-jaw grippers. A dexterous hand may expose 20 or more controllable joints per hand, and successful behavior depends on coordinated finger movements, contact maintenance, object regrasping, and hand–arm synchronization. Direct learning in the raw joint space can therefore be inefficient and can generate physically implausible or uncoordinated motions.

The paper connects several established research directions. It uses supervised imitation learning to initialize task-specific behavior, DAgger-style interactive imitation learning to address errors caused by distribution shift, and residual reinforcement learning to improve an existing controller without discarding its prior competence. It also draws on research into hand postural synergies and latent action spaces, which suggests that useful dexterous motions occupy a lower-dimensional structured manifold rather than the full Cartesian product of independent joint coordinates.

The paper’s specific contribution is to use one learned temporal hand-action representation as a common interface across supervised fine-tuning (SFT), corrective data aggregation, and real-world residual reinforcement learning. This provides a unified approach to the action-interface, human-intervention, and exploration problems that arise when adapting a pretrained VLA to a dexterous platform.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to convert a pretrained VLA foundation model into a reliable policy for a specified dexterous manipulation task on a particular bimanual robot. The authors focus on atomic tasks that can be completed in approximately 30 seconds. They do not attempt to solve long-horizon task composition or broad scene-level generalization.

The work is motivated by three technical obstacles.

First, the pretrained VLA does not natively support the robot’s complete action interface. The experimental robot has two 7-DoF arms and two 20-DoF hands, resulting in 54 joint-position commands per frame. The selected pretrained π₀.₅ model accepts a 32-dimensional action representation. Directly expanding the action head to 54 dimensions would remove compatibility with the pretrained interface and would not exploit the coordination structure of dexterous hand motion. The authors therefore seek a compact representation that can encode feasible, temporally coherent hand trajectories.

Second, human takeover during DAgger can introduce discontinuities. The policy and the teleoperator both produce absolute hand commands, but their current commanded poses may differ when intervention occurs. Switching directly to the operator’s command can create an abrupt joint-space change. In addition, the operator may initially spend time repositioning the hand to match the robot’s pose rather than correcting the task failure. This would produce corrective demonstrations containing gesture-alignment motion instead of task-recovery behavior. The paper aims to preserve the absolute-command interface while separating pose synchronization from recovery.

Third, online reinforcement learning in raw joint space is expected to be sample-inefficient. Independent perturbations to many hand joints are unlikely to correspond to useful grasp or manipulation behaviors. Since real-robot trials are costly, the authors aim to constrain residual exploration to a lower-dimensional representation of coordinated hand actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Robot and Data-Collection Setup

The platform consists of two Tianji 7-DoF arms and two Wuji 20-DoF dexterous hands. The robot is controlled in joint position at 30 Hz. Three RGB cameras are used: one head-mounted camera and one wrist-mounted camera on each arm. Teleoperation uses a Vive Tracker for the arms and Wuji Glove and Manus gloves for the hands.

The VLA predicts action chunks of horizon \(H=32\). Only the first \(C=25\) frames, approximately 0.83 seconds, are executed before a new observation is acquired. This receding-horizon procedure provides some temporal consistency while retaining closed-loop responsiveness.

#### 3.2. Step 1: Temporal Hand-Action Codec

The authors train separate variational autoencoder-style temporal codecs for the two hands. Each codec maps a 32-frame, 20-dimensional hand-action chunk into a 32-frame sequence of nine-dimensional latent vectors. The action is represented relative to the measured hand configuration at the start of the chunk. The codec uses temporal Transformer encoders and decoders and is conditioned on the initial hand state.

The training objective combines weighted Huber reconstruction losses for positions, first temporal differences, and second temporal differences. Consequently, the codec is trained not only to reproduce joint positions but also to preserve velocity-like changes and changes in motion. A Kullback–Leibler regularization term constrains the latent distribution.

The two nine-dimensional hand latents are combined with the two seven-dimensional arm blocks, producing a 32-dimensional full action representation: 14 arm dimensions and 18 hand-latent dimensions. The codec is frozen after training. At inference, the VLA predicts the latent chunk, which is decoded into joint commands using the current hand state.

The codec corpus contains approximately 38 million frames per hand from internal teleoperation data, retargeted human-hand trajectories from the VITRA-1M dataset, and 200 task-specific demonstrations for each task. Subsequent DAgger corrections and RL data are excluded from codec training.

#### 3.3. Step 2: Supervised Fine-Tuning

The pretrained π₀.₅ flow-matching VLA is fine-tuned to predict the 32-dimensional latent action rather than a raw 54-dimensional joint action. Inputs consist of three RGB views, the language instruction, and proprioceptive state.

The training objective includes the standard flow-matching loss for arm coordinates and hand-latent coordinates. It also includes a decoded hand-action loss. During training, the predicted clean latent estimate is passed through the frozen decoder, and the resulting hand joint trajectory is compared with the demonstration trajectory. This additional loss accounts for the nonlinear relationship between latent errors and physical joint errors.

All VLA parameters are fine-tuned for 30,000 updates for each SFT or DAgger checkpoint. The main experiments begin with 200 demonstrations per task, while additional dataset sizes are evaluated for comparison.

#### 3.4. Step 3: DAgger with Buffered Takeover

The DAgger procedure uses human-gated intervention. The robot maintains a five-second rolling buffer of recent states. When an operator identifies an impending failure or safety issue, the robot is rolled back to a selected pre-failure state within this buffer.

An interface displays the operator’s hand pose alongside the robot’s current pose. After takeover, the executed hand command is linearly blended over two seconds from the last policy command to the live teleoperation command. This avoids a step change in the command signal. However, the operator’s live command, rather than the blended command sent to the robot, is retained as the supervision trajectory. The correction is then encoded into the shared latent representation and added to the training corpus.

Two rounds of 50 corrections are collected for the main DAgger experiments. Each retraining run uses a minibatch composition of 50% newly collected corrections and 50% previously accumulated data.

#### 3.5. Step 4: Latent Residual Reinforcement Learning

After SFT and DAgger, the reference VLA is frozen. A separate residual actor learns a bounded correction in the 32-dimensional latent action space. A zero residual exactly reproduces the reference policy, providing a fallback behavior.

The residual actor receives a compact RL token derived from frozen VLA image-token features, the current proprioceptive state, and the reference latent action. The RL token is pretrained using feature reconstruction on the reference-policy demonstration data, without action or reward labels. This readout is then frozen for online learning.

The residual actor and twin critics are trained with TD3. Residuals are applied to the first 25 frames of each predicted chunk and decoded jointly through the frozen hand codec. The method uses task-specific residual masks. For the transfer task, both arms and both hand latents can be modified. For in-hand rotation, only the active right-hand latent is modified.

Exploration uses accumulated white noise for arm increments and temporally correlated Brownian noise for hand latents. Safety mechanisms include residual bounds, clipping hand latents to a training-data envelope, and joint-position rate limits. Critics are trained during an initial warm-up phase before actor updates are enabled. Each RL task receives a budget of 3,000 online transitions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments cover five marker-manipulation tasks: picking up a marker, transferring it between hands, rotating it in hand, removing its cap, and writing “WUJI” on paper.

The temporal Transformer codec produced the lowest reconstruction error among the evaluated nine-dimensional representations, outperforming PCA, frame-wise neural models, and a chunk-based MLP. The latent-width sweep showed diminishing reconstruction improvements as width increased. Nine dimensions per hand produced approximately 0.275 degrees of average per-joint reconstruction error and was selected because it fit the pretrained model’s 32-dimensional action interface.

Latent-space imitation preserved performance relative to a raw joint-space action baseline. Across the tested SFT and DAgger budgets, the success curves of the latent and raw-action policies were closely aligned, indicating that reducing each hand from 20 to nine dimensions did not introduce a consistent imitation-learning penalty.

DAgger was more effective than simply adding independently collected demonstrations at matched cumulative episode budgets. At 250 and 300 episodes, DAgger achieved higher success across the tasks that permitted takeover. The effect was attributed to corrections being collected from states reached by the current policy and therefore targeting its remaining failure modes.

The tasks differed in how far imitation learning could progress. Pick Up Marker, Remove Marker Cap, and Write on Paper reached 100% success after DAgger. Pick Up Marker was already close to saturation after SFT. Transfer Marker between Hands improved from 10% after initial SFT to 65% after the first correction round and 70% after the second, but did not reach full success. Rotate Marker in Hand achieved 50% after SFT and could not use DAgger because the object state could not be reliably restored during rollback.

Latent residual RL closed the remaining performance gaps. Starting from the 70% DAgger checkpoint, the transfer policy reached 100% success after 3,000 online transitions. Starting from the 50% SFT checkpoint, the rotation policy also reached 100%. The latent residual method surpassed the frozen policy at the first evaluation point and reached full success within approximately 100 episodes for rotation and 150 episodes for transfer.

Under the same 3,000-transition budget, raw joint-space residual RL reached 80% on transfer and 50% on rotation. Its performance fluctuated rather than improving consistently. The authors observed that raw residuals often produced uncoordinated individual-joint movements, whereas latent residuals generated behaviors such as regrasping, contact maintenance, and recovery from incipient errors.

Ablations showed lower performance when the temporal codec was replaced by frame-wise PCA, when exploration noise was temporally independent, when residual-magnitude or smoothness regularization was removed, or when critic warm-up was omitted. The reported operator-time estimates were approximately two worker-hours per 100 SFT demonstrations, 3.9 hours per 100 DAgger corrections, and 1.2 hours per 100 autonomous RL episodes.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper provides a practical framework for adapting general-purpose VLAs to dexterous hands without requiring the foundation model to operate directly in a large raw joint space. Its shared action representation links imitation learning, interactive correction, and reinforcement learning, reducing the need to design separate interfaces for each post-training stage.

The DAgger procedure is relevant to real-robot deployment because it addresses both physical command discontinuities and the quality of intervention data. The latent residual RL approach also offers a way to perform online improvement while preserving the frozen policy as an exact fallback.

However, the evidence is limited to five tasks on one bimanual platform, with manually reset initial conditions and approximately stable lighting. Evaluations use 20 trials per checkpoint and local variations around the data-collection distribution. The study does not establish generalization to new objects, substantially different scenes, long-horizon task compositions, or other robot embodiments. The nine-dimensional hand latent is partly determined by the π₀.₅ action-interface constraint, so it is not shown to be optimal independently of that model. Finally, residual RL depends on the reference policy already achieving a minimally competent success rate; the authors report little improvement when the base policy succeeds in fewer than roughly 20% of trials and more consistent benefits above approximately 50%. These constraints position the pipeline as a method for late-stage, task-specific reliability improvement rather than a replacement for broad pretraining or initial imitation learning.
