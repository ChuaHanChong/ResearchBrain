# All Roads Lead to Likelihood: The Value of Reinforcement Learning in Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers explain the empirical superiority of two-stage online Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) over offline Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) for fine-tuning foundation models. This advantage stems from a “generation-verification gap,” where learning a simpler reward model (verifier) is statistically more efficient than directly learning a complex policy (generator), effectively constraining the policy search space.

## Problem

- Complex two-stage online Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) consistently outperforms simpler offline Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) in fine-tuning foundation models, despite theoretical arguments suggesting otherwise.
- From an information-theoretic perspective, passing data through an intermediary reward model should only lead to a loss of information, and on-policy sampling does not create new ground-truth information, posing a paradox for RLHF's empirical success.
- Traditional justifications for online RL (e.g., learning from exploration, simpler reward functions) do not readily apply to auto-regressive language models, where rewards can be as complex as policies and 'mistakes' are permanent.

## Method

- The authors establish theoretical equivalences between online and offline ==Preference Fine-Tuning== (PFT) under idealized assumptions (isomorphic function classes, exact optimization) to formally define the discrepancy with empirical observations.
- A series of controlled empirical experiments using Pythia models on a summarization task systematically test and largely falsify several existing and novel hypotheses for the online-offline performance gap.
- A novel hypothesis, the 'generation-verification gap,' is proposed and validated through critical experiments that manipulate the inherent complexity of the generation and verification tasks.

## Results

- Theoretical proofs confirm that online and offline PFT are equivalent under ideal conditions, providing a formal baseline for the observed empirical discrepancy.
- Controlled experiments demonstrate that online DPO consistently and robustly outperforms standard offline DPO on the `tl;dr` summarization task, empirically validating the persistent performance gap.
- The performance gap between online and offline methods significantly diminishes or vanishes when the generation-verification gap is reduced (e.g., in a two-word summarization task) or when the reward function is made complex (e.g., using ROUGE-L as reward), providing strong empirical support for the generation-verification gap hypothesis.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Under ideal theoretical conditions (isomorphic function classes, exact optimization), online and offline PFT methods are mathematically equivalent and should yield identical optimal policies.
> - The observed empirical superiority of online RLHF for LLM fine-tuning is primarily explained by a 'generation-verification gap,' where the optimal reward function (verifier) is often statistically simpler to learn than the optimal policy (generator).
> - Online PFT implicitly constrains the search space to policies optimal for these simpler verifiers, effectively reducing the statistical complexity of the learning problem compared to offline methods that search the entire policy space.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical, yet often under-theorized, aspect of large language model (LLM) development: the final "alignment" step, frequently achieved through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This process, also known as preference fine-tuning (PFT), is widely adopted in training state-of-the-art foundation models like GPT-4, Llama 3, Gemini, and DeepSeek, enabling them to generate helpful, harmless, and aligned outputs.

The broader research landscape for LLM fine-tuning can be broadly categorized into two main approaches:
1. **Offline Fine-tuning:** Methods that directly optimize policy parameters using a fixed dataset of preferences, typically via (regularized) maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Examples include Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Identity Preference Optimization (IPO), and Sequence Likelihood Calibration with Human Feedback (SLiC-HF). These methods are conceptually simpler as they avoid the complex two-stage process of online RL.
2. **Online Fine-tuning (RLHF):** Methods that involve a two-stage procedure. First, a reward model (RM) is trained on human preference data. Second, this RM is used to provide online feedback for a reinforcement learning procedure (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization - PPO) that optimizes the language model's policy.

**The core tension in the current landscape** is the robust empirical observation that the more complex, two-stage online RLHF often outperforms simpler offline MLE techniques. This empirical superiority poses a significant theoretical puzzle. From an information-theoretic perspective, passing data through a reward model can only lose information, and on-policy sampling does not create new information. This suggests that offline MLE, which directly optimizes on the raw preference data, should be at least as effective, if not more so. Traditional justifications for online RL, such as learning from exploration or simpler reward functions, don't straightforwardly apply to auto-regressive language models where "mistakes" (token generations) are permanent and reward models can be as large and complex as the policies themselves.

This paper critically engages with this discrepancy. It systematically scrutinizes several existing and novel hypotheses attempting to explain the online-offline gap, including the intrinsic value of online samples, regularization effects, optimization ease, and data distribution. By ruling out these alternative explanations through controlled experiments and theoretical arguments, the authors pave the way for their own proposed hypothesis.

The work builds upon and challenges prior understanding from areas such as:
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** Classic IRL literature (e.g., Ng et al., 2000) often posits that learning a reward function (verifier) can be simpler than directly learning an optimal policy (generator). This paper leverages and extends this idea within the context of LLM fine-tuning.
- **Computational-Statistical Trade-offs:** The paper subtly touches upon ideas where additional data, even if theoretically redundant, can reduce computational requirements, and vice-versa.
- **Information Geometry:** The theoretical framework employs information geometry tools to analyze the relationships between policy and reward model spaces, offering a rigorous foundation for understanding their equivalence under ideal conditions.

Ultimately, this research seeks to provide a deeper, more principled understanding of *why* RLHF works so well, moving beyond mere empirical observation to a structural explanation centered on the relative complexity of "generation" versus "verification" in LLM tasks. This understanding can significantly inform future developments in LLM alignment and fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to resolve a critical paradox in the fine-tuning of foundation models: the consistent empirical superiority of two-stage, online Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methods over ostensibly simpler, purely offline maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) techniques. The authors articulate this core question as:

**Q: What is the value of two-stage, online FT if we just want to maximize data likelihood?**

The motivation for addressing this question stems from several key observations and theoretical challenges:

1. **Empirical Dominance:** State-of-the-art foundation models universally employ complex, two-stage online RL procedures (e.g., training a reward model, then using RL to optimize the policy) for their final alignment phase. This robust empirical success, observed across academic, industry, and open-source models, begs for a fundamental explanation.
2. **Theoretical Inconsistency:** From a first-principles perspective, standard information theory suggests that passing raw preference data through an intermediary reward model (RM) can only lead to a loss of information. Furthermore, on-policy sampling, where the model generates its own data to be evaluated by the RM, does not *create* new ground-truth information. This leads to the counter-intuitive conclusion that simpler, direct offline MLE approaches should be theoretically superior or at least equivalent.
3. **Limitations of Traditional RL Justifications:** Many standard arguments for the value of online RL (e.g., the ability to observe and correct mistakes through interaction, or the relative simplicity of reward functions compared to policies) do not readily apply to the LLM fine-tuning context. Language models generate sequences of tokens without "undo" capabilities, and the RMs themselves are often as large and complex as the policies they evaluate.
4. **Seeking a Deeper Understanding:** The paper is motivated by a desire to move beyond "black-box" observations of RLHF's success and provide a clear, theoretically and empirically supported explanation for its effectiveness. This deeper understanding is crucial for designing more efficient, robust, and generalizable fine-tuning strategies in the future.

To achieve this, the authors set out to:
- **Formulate theoretical equivalences:** Prove that under idealized assumptions (perfect optimization, isomorphic function classes), online and offline PFT methods should yield identical results, thereby formally establishing the theoretical discrepancy that needs explanation.
- **Systematically falsify alternative hypotheses:** Conduct a series of controlled experiments to test and largely rule out various prevailing or intuitive explanations for the online-offline performance gap.
- **Propose and validate a novel hypothesis:** Develop an alternative explanation centered on the "generation-verification gap" and the concept of "proper learning," and provide strong theoretical and empirical evidence in its favor.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a "yellow-brick road" explanation for why the seemingly circuitous path of online RLHF leads to superior outcomes in practice, despite its theoretical complexities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper is a rigorous blend of theoretical analysis and controlled empirical experimentation, systematically designed to address the central question of the value of online RL in fine-tuning.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Analysis: Establishing the Idealized Baseline

The authors begin by establishing a theoretical baseline under idealized conditions, aiming to show that, in theory, online and offline PFT should be equivalent.

- **Unified Objective Function:** They formulate a general loss function for fine-tuning, encompassing both online and offline PFT as a reverse KL-regularized policy optimization problem. This objective considers both data likelihood and regularization to a reference policy or entropy.
- **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) in PFT:** The first stage of online PFT is framed as MLE over *global* reward models (R) using the Bradley-Terry (BT) model for preference probabilities. In contrast, offline PFT (like DPO) is framed as direct MLE over *policy* parameters (Π), which are interpreted as *local* reward models.
- **Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) in PFT:** The second stage of online PFT, where a soft-optimal policy is computed for the learned RM, is elegantly shown to be equivalent to a reverse KL projection from the ideal policy (induced by the RM) onto the feasible policy space.
- **Equivalences with Isomorphic Classes:** Crucially, the authors prove theorems (Theorem 2.2 and 2.3) demonstrating that if the policy class (Π) and the reward model class (R) are *isomorphic* (i.e., capable of representing the same set of functions), and optimization is exact, then online and offline PFT methods should yield identical optimal policies, even with regularization. This theoretical result sets up the core discrepancy with empirical observations.

#### 3.2. Empirical Scrutiny: Controlled Experiments to Falsify Hypotheses

To bridge the gap between theory and practice, the authors conduct a series of controlled experiments using the task of summarization from preference feedback (tl;dr) on Pythia models.

- **Standardized Setup:** A concerted effort is made to control confounding variables. All experiments use the DPO loss for both online and offline training (bypassing explanations based on different loss functions like PPO). Global RMs are trained using the same preference data and initial SFT checkpoint as offline DPO.
- **Initial Observation:** The experiments consistently show that "online DPO" (where the DPO loss is applied to samples re-ranked by a learned RM) robustly outperforms standard offline DPO, thus empirically contradicting the theoretical equivalences.
- **Systematic Falsification of Alternatives (Appendix A):** The authors systematically test and provide evidence against several prior and novel hypotheses for the online-offline gap:
 - **H1: Intrinsic Value of Online Samples:** Argued against by information theory – on-policy samples don't create new information.
 - **H2: Failure of Offline PFT Regularization:** Shown not to be the sole cause, as the gap persists even when regularization is identical or when it's less critical for the task.
 - **H3: Relative Ease of Online PFT Optimization:** Tested by prompt augmentation (increasing data for online DPO), which yields minimal improvement, arguing against a purely computational-statistical gap explanation in this context.
 - **H4: Global RMs Can Be Trained on More Data:** Tested by training RMs on narrower, on-policy datasets, yet online DPO still shows superiority, suggesting this isn't the primary factor.
 - **H5: Global RMs Generalize Better OOD:** While empirically confirmed (Figure 10 & 11 show global RMs have better in-distribution likelihood and OOD generalization than local/DPO RMs), this hypothesis is refined by asking *why* they generalize better, leading to the core proposal.

#### 3.3. Proposing and Validating H6: The Generation-Verification Gap

Having largely falsified or refined existing explanations, the authors propose a novel hypothesis (H6) and provide compelling evidence.

- **Hypothesis H6:** Online PFT is a form of "proper policy learning" that, for problems with a "generation-verification gap," leverages the relative simplicity of learning the reward model (verifier) over the policy (generator). This implicitly reduces the effective search space for policies (Π(R_sim) ⊂ Π), requiring less data than offline methods that search over the full, more complex policy space. A new theorem (Theorem 4.1) supports this, showing RLHF recovers MLE over this constrained space.
- **Empirical Validation of H6:**
 - **Evidence for Simpler Verifiers:** Experiments show that scaling up reward model size beyond a certain point yields diminishing returns, implying the underlying reward function is well-approximated by a smaller network (Figure 4). In contrast, policy performance often benefits from larger models.
 - **Critical Test: Closing the Gap:** The most compelling evidence comes from experiments designed to *eliminate* or *reduce* the generation-verification gap:
 - **Reduced Horizon (H=1) Task (Two-Word Summarization):** By shortening the generation length, the complexity of the policy (generator) is reduced. As predicted by H6, the performance gap between online and offline DPO nearly vanishes (Figure 5).
 - **Complicated Reward Function (ROUGE-L):** By using a reward function (ROUGE-L against an unobserved reference) that is inherently complex to learn and from which optimal generation is trivial (i.e., no generation-verification gap), the performance gap between online and offline DPO again disappears (Figure 6).

#### 3.4. Interpretation: Isomorphisms vs. Representational Complexity

The methodology concludes by explaining that while mathematical isomorphisms between rewards and Q-functions/policies exist, they do not imply equal *representational complexity* (e.g., circuit depth). Learning a simpler reward function and *then* computationally deriving the policy via RL can be statistically more data-efficient than directly learning the more complex Q-function/policy via MLE, effectively trading computation for data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings, moving from theoretical equivalences to robust empirical observations, and finally to a compelling novel explanation.

1. **Theoretical Equivalence under Ideal Conditions:**
 - The paper formally demonstrates that under idealized assumptions—namely, exact optimization, no reference policy (or identical regularization), and critically, isomorphic function classes for policies and reward models (R = R(Π))—online and offline PFT methods are mathematically equivalent. They should converge to the same set of optimal policies (Theorems 2.2 and 2.3). This establishes a strong theoretical baseline against which empirical observations are then contrasted.
 - Soft RL is formally shown to be equivalent to an RKL (Reverse KL) projection from an optimal distribution (derived from the reward) onto the policy space.

2. **Robust Empirical Discrepancy:**
 - Despite the theoretical equivalences, controlled experiments on the `tl;dr` summarization task using Pythia models consistently show a significant performance gap. Online DPO (which uses a reward model to re-rank samples for DPO training) robustly outperforms standard offline DPO (Figure 3). This discrepancy holds across different model sizes and even when controlling for gradient steps and the specific DPO loss function, highlighting a fundamental phenomenon beyond mere implementation details.

3. **Falsification of Alternative Hypotheses:**
 - The paper systematically provides evidence against several intuitive or previously proposed explanations for the online-offline gap (detailed in Appendix A):
 - **Intrinsic Value of Online Samples (H1):** Information theory suggests on-policy sampling does not generate new information; the observed benefits are not due to an intrinsic informational advantage of online data.
 - **Failure of Offline PFT Regularization to π_ref (H2):** While a contributing factor, it's not the complete story, as the gap persists even when regularization is consistent across methods.
 - **Relative Ease of Online PFT Optimization (H3):** Augmenting prompts for online DPO, which should computationally ease optimization, yields negligible improvement, challenging this hypothesis.
 - **Global RMs Can Be Trained on More Data (H4):** Even with narrower, on-policy datasets, online DPO maintains its advantage, suggesting data diversity is not the sole differentiator.
 - **Global RMs Generalize Better OOD (H5):** Empirically, global RMs exhibit better in-distribution validation likelihood and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization (Figure 10 and 11) than local (policy-implied) RMs. However, this observation itself requires an explanation for *why* global RMs have this advantage, which H5 alone does not provide.

4. **Support for the Generation-Verification Gap Hypothesis (H6):**
 - The authors' primary finding is strong support for the hypothesis that the value of online RLHF stems from problems having a **generation-verification gap**. This means the underlying reward function (verifier) is inherently simpler to learn and represent than the optimal policy (generator).
 - **Evidence for Simpler Verifiers:** Experiments show that reward models significantly smaller than the policy network can achieve comparable performance in Best-Of-N (BoN) evaluations, suggesting the verifier is a "simpler" object to learn. Conversely, larger RMs do not lead to proportional improvements (Figure 4).
 - **Mechanism:** Online PFT effectively constrains the search space to policies that are optimal for these *relatively simple verifiers* (Π(R_sim) ⊂ Π). This reduces the statistical complexity of the learning problem, requiring less data than offline methods that must search over the entire, more complex policy space (Theorem 4.1).
 - **Validation by Closing the Gap:** The most compelling evidence for H6 comes from experiments where the generation-verification gap is intentionally reduced or eliminated:
 - **Two-Word Summarization (Short Horizon):** On a task with a very short generation length (H=1, essentially a contextual bandit), where generation complexity is reduced, the performance gap between online and offline DPO virtually disappears (Figure 5).
 - **ROUGE-L Reward (Complex Reward):** When the reward function is made as complex as the optimal generation (using ROUGE-L against an unobserved reference), the advantage of online PFT vanishes, as predicted (Figure 6).

5. **Isomorphism vs. Representational Complexity:**
 - The paper clarifies that while rewards and Q-functions/policies might be mathematically isomorphic, this does not imply they are equally easy to *represent* or *learn* from data using finite-capacity models like neural networks. Q-functions, embodying multi-step reasoning, can be fundamentally more complex to represent than global reward functions (e.g., maze navigation example, Figure 7). Online PFT leverages this by statistically efficiently learning the simpler reward, and then computationally deriving the policy, trading computation for data.

In summary, the core finding is that online RLHF's advantage lies not in magical information creation, but in its ability to exploit a structural property of many LLM fine-tuning problems: it's statistically easier to learn a good verifier than a good generator, and the two-stage process implicitly directs policy search towards a more tractable subspace.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a profound contribution to the understanding of foundation model fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, with significant theoretical and practical implications:

1. **Fundamental Understanding of RLHF:** The most significant impact is providing a principled, theoretically-backed explanation for the long-observed empirical success of online RLHF. By rigorously testing and largely refuting alternative hypotheses, the paper pinpoints the "generation-verification gap" as the root cause. This moves our understanding beyond heuristics or anecdotal evidence to a structural property of the learning problem. It reconciles the empirical superiority of complex online methods with information-theoretic principles that might otherwise suggest their inefficiency.

2. **Guidance for Future LLM Alignment Strategies:** The findings provide actionable insights for practitioners and researchers developing future LLMs.
 - **Prioritize Reward Model Quality:** For tasks where verification is likely simpler than generation (which is common in many complex reasoning and creative tasks), this work suggests that investing resources in learning robust and simple reward models is a statistically data-efficient strategy for alignment.
 - **Task Design:** The paper suggests that for "simple" tasks (e.g., short-horizon, bandit-like problems) or those with easily computable rewards, the complex online RLHF pipeline might not offer substantial advantages over simpler offline methods, prompting a re-evaluation of when and where to apply RLHF.
 - **Computational-Statistical Trade-off:** The interpretation that online PFT trades computation for data provides a new lens for resource allocation. It suggests that it might be more efficient to spend computational cycles on the RL stage (deriving a policy from a simpler reward) than to collect vastly more data to statistically learn a complex policy directly.

3. **Implications for Reinforcement Learning Theory:** The concept of "isomorphisms aren't always a two-way street" highlights a crucial distinction between mathematical equivalence and representational complexity in function approximation. This insight has broader implications for understanding learning dynamics in deep RL, particularly when function classes for policies and rewards are chosen. It underscores that even with highly expressive function approximators like deep neural networks, implicit biases of optimization can favor learning simpler representations.

4. **Directions for Future Research:** The paper opens up several promising avenues:
 - **Scaling Laws for Generation-Verification Gap:** The authors predict that for increasingly complex problems (e.g., multi-turn RLHF, agentic tasks, long-horizon robotics) where the generation-verification gap is expected to be larger, the separation between online and offline PFT should become even more pronounced. This sets a clear hypothesis for future scaling studies.
 - **Constrained Offline Learning:** An intriguing challenge is to develop offline fine-tuning algorithms that can *explicitly* constrain their search space to Π(R_sim) without first learning an RM and running RL, thereby potentially achieving the benefits of online PFT with the simplicity of offline methods.
 - **Off-policy RL on Learned RMs:** The work also implicitly calls for more research into off-policy RL algorithms that can effectively leverage learned reward models to explore and exploit efficiently.
 - **Reward Model Architectures:** It encourages a deeper look into reward model architectures to ensure they can accurately capture human preferences, especially in the face of complex, potentially intransitive human feedback.
 - **Generalization to Other Domains:** The observed phenomena (online methods outperforming offline) have been noted in SFT and reasoning tasks, suggesting that the generation-verification gap explanation might generalize beyond PFT, offering a unified framework for understanding post-training of foundation models.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust, well-supported theoretical and empirical framework that not only explains *why* RLHF is effective but also offers valuable guidance for its continued development and application in the rapidly evolving field of AI. It represents a significant step forward in building a principled understanding of how to align powerful foundation models.
