# Efficient Reinforcement Finetuning via Adaptive Curriculum Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at USC and the University of Maryland developed ADARFT, a method that uses adaptive curriculum learning to improve the efficiency and accuracy of Reinforcement Finetuning (RFT) for Large Language Models (LLMs) in mathematical reasoning tasks. The system dynamically adjusts problem difficulty based on the model's performance, leading to up to 2x faster training convergence and consistent final accuracy improvements across various models and data distributions.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Finetuning (RFT) for LLMs is sample-inefficient and computationally expensive, requiring extensive resources and training time.
- Existing RFT methods struggle with non-uniform or imbalanced data distributions, leading to suboptimal learning when problems are too easy or too hard.
- Current curriculum learning approaches for RFT are either manually staged and lack adaptivity or incur significant online rollout overhead for data filtering.

## Method

- ==ADARFT== employs a ==dynamic curriculum learning== strategy that adjusts training problem difficulty based on the model's recent reward signals, sampling problems closest to a 'target difficulty'.
- It theoretically justifies setting the ==target success rate (beta=0.5)==, maximizing the learning signal by focusing on problems where the model experiences a productive struggle.
- Difficulty scores are estimated using rollout-based empirical methods, pre-existing labels (AoPS), or lightweight LLM-judged assessments, making it flexible and generalizable as a 'plug-and-play' extension for various RFT algorithms.

## Results

- ADARFT reduced training steps by up to **2x** (e.g., Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B in `skew-difficult` setting, requiring **71.7%** fewer steps than standard PPO to reach comparable performance).
- It consistently achieved higher final accuracy, with gains up to **3.91 percentage points** (e.g., Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B in `uniform` setting, from **37.20%** to **41.11%**).
- The approach maintained effectiveness even when using lightweight LLM-judged difficulty scores, demonstrating practicality without requiring extensive rollouts for difficulty estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training on problems where the model has approximately a 50% chance of success (target reward β=0.5) maximizes the learning signal, leading to the strongest policy updates and highest accuracy.
> - A dynamically adjusted curriculum, as opposed to static or online filtering methods, significantly improves both training efficiency and final model performance, especially in imbalanced data scenarios.
> - ADARFT's lightweight and algorithm-agnostic design allows it to seamlessly integrate with and enhance existing RFT algorithms (e.g., PPO, REINFORCE++, GRPO) without specific modifications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Finetuning (RFT) has emerged as a technique for aligning large language models (LLMs) with specific task goals, particularly in domains such as mathematical reasoning and code generation where correctness can be objectively defined. Despite its potential, RFT is typically characterized by sample and computational inefficiency, requiring extensive training. This inefficiency stems from the iterative process of rollout generation, reward computation, and policy updates inherent to reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

Existing efforts to mitigate RFT inefficiency can be broadly categorized into algorithmic simplifications and data-centric strategies. Algorithmic approaches, such as RAFT, ReMax, GRPO, and REINFORCE++, aim to reduce computational overhead by modifying core RL components. However, these methods may introduce trade-offs in terms of stability or overall sample efficiency. Data-centric strategies, exemplified by LIMO and LIMR, focus on selecting or filtering training data to improve finetuning efficiency. While effective in some contexts, these often rely on manual curation, prompt engineering, or require computationally intensive re-scoring of datasets for each new model or task. Moreover, static data filtering might discard potentially informative examples and does not inherently reduce the number of optimization steps needed for convergence.

More recent research has explored curriculum learning principles within RFT. Early attempts include staged curricula, which segment training into manually defined difficulty phases (e.g., Wen et al., Luo et al., Song et al.). These methods are coarse-grained and lack the flexibility to adapt to a model's dynamic learning progress. Other approaches involve online data filtering, where training samples are repeatedly rolled out and pruned until a target average reward is met (e.g., Bae et al., Yu et al.). While these can prevent models from stagnating on overly easy or difficult problems, they typically incur significant rollout overhead and are not fully adaptive in their difficulty progression.

The work presented in this paper, AdaRFT (Adaptive Curriculum Reinforcement Finetuning), positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of prior RFT efficiency methods. It introduces an adaptive curriculum learning approach that dynamically adjusts the difficulty of training problems based on real-time reward signals. Unlike static or staged curricula, AdaRFT aims for continuous adaptation to the model's evolving skill level. It differentiates itself from online filtering by avoiding repeated rollouts for difficulty reassessment, instead relying on precomputed difficulty scores and a dynamic target difficulty mechanism. AdaRFT is designed as a lightweight, general, and model-agnostic extension to standard RL algorithms, thereby seeking to improve RFT efficiency and performance without altering the underlying policy optimization algorithm or model architecture.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the efficiency of Reinforcement Finetuning (RFT) for large language models (LLMs), particularly in domains requiring complex reasoning such as mathematics. While RFT has demonstrated utility in improving LLMs' mathematical capabilities, the current process is often resource-intensive, demanding significant computational power and numerous training samples.

The key motivations and detailed objectives driving this work are:

- **Addressing RFT Inefficiency:** The core motivation is to overcome the inherent sample and compute inefficiencies of standard RFT. The process involves repeated generation of model responses (rollouts), computation of rewards based on correctness, and subsequent policy updates. This iterative nature makes RFT costly and challenging to scale for larger models or datasets. The paper aims to mitigate these costs.
- **Optimizing Learning through Adaptive Difficulty:** The central intuition is that learning is most effective when tasks are neither trivially easy nor prohibitively difficult. Training on problems that are too easy offers diminishing returns, while problems that are too hard can lead to sparse reward signals and slow learning. The objective is to devise a mechanism that dynamically adjusts the difficulty of training problems to maintain an "optimal difficulty range" – tasks that are challenging enough to promote learning but solvable enough to provide consistent positive reinforcement.
- **Developing a Lightweight and General Solution:** The authors seek a method that improves RFT efficiency without requiring extensive modifications to existing RL algorithms, reward functions, or model architectures. This promotes broad applicability and ease of integration into current RFT pipelines. The goal is to provide a "lightweight extension" to algorithms like PPO, emphasizing practicality and minimal overhead.
- **Accelerating Convergence and Improving Performance:** A specific objective is to demonstrate that this adaptive curriculum strategy can accelerate the convergence of LLMs during RFT, meaning models achieve target performance levels in fewer training steps. Concurrently, the method should either maintain or improve the final reasoning performance of the LLMs on competition-level mathematical datasets.
- **Demonstrating Scalability and Effectiveness:** The research aims to show that the proposed method, AdaRFT, can reduce RFT training time (e.g., by up to 2x) across varying data distributions (uniform, skew-easy, skew-difficult) and different model scales. This objective underscores the goal of creating a more scalable and effective RFT framework.
- **Leveraging Reward Signals for Curriculum Adaptation:** A detailed motivation for the chosen target reward (β = 0.5) is provided. This value corresponds to a success rate of approximately 50%, which is hypothesized to maximize the variance of the reward signal. In entropy-regularized reinforcement learning, the lower bound on the KL divergence between an initial and an optimal policy is proportional to the reward variance. Thus, training on problems where the model succeeds roughly half the time is posited to provide the most informative policy-gradient updates, thereby maximizing the learning signal.

In summary, the motivation is to make RFT a more practical and efficient tool for improving LLM reasoning by intelligently managing the training curriculum, ensuring that the model is consistently challenged appropriately throughout its learning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AdaRFT (Adaptive Curriculum Reinforcement Finetuning) aims to enhance the efficiency and performance of RFT by dynamically adjusting the difficulty of training problems. The method is designed to be compatible with various standard RL algorithms.

**Core Idea: Adaptive Curriculum Learning**
The fundamental principle of AdaRFT is that learning is most efficient when tasks are appropriately challenging—neither too easy nor too hard. AdaRFT formalizes this by continuously adapting a target difficulty level based on the model's recent performance feedback, ensuring a steady progression through solvable yet challenging tasks.

**Algorithm 1: AdaRFT Overview**
The algorithm operates iteratively, encompassing dynamic curriculum sampling, policy updates, and target difficulty adjustments.

1. **Input Parameters**:
 - **Data Source D**: A dataset of mathematical problems, each with a precomputed difficulty score `d_i`.
 - **Policy Model π_θ**: The LLM being finetuned.
 - **Reward Function R(·, ·)**: Assesses the correctness of the model's response.
 - **Batch Size B**: Number of problems sampled per training step.
 - **Initial Target Difficulty T**: Starting difficulty level for the curriculum.
 - **Step Size η**: Controls the magnitude of target difficulty adjustment.
 - **Sensitivity α**: Controls the responsiveness of the target difficulty adjustment to reward signals.
 - **Target Reward β**: Desired average success rate (e.g., 0.5).
 - **Difficulty Bounds [d_min, d_max]**: Range within which the target difficulty must remain.
 - **RL Algorithm A**: The underlying reinforcement learning algorithm (e.g., PPO).

2. **Dynamic Curriculum Sampling (Section 3.1)**:
 - At each training step, the algorithm calculates the absolute difference `∆_i = |d_i - T|` for every problem `i` in the dataset. This metric quantifies how close each problem's difficulty is to the current target difficulty `T`.
 - A batch `X` of `B` problems is then selected by choosing those with the smallest `∆_i` values. This ensures that the model trains on problems closest to its current target capability, focusing learning on the "productive struggle" zone.
 - The authors note that this deterministic nearest-difficulty selection could be extended to probabilistic sampling (e.g., from a Gaussian distribution centered at `T`) for increased diversity, but this is left for future work.

3. **Policy Update (Section 3.2)**:
 - The policy model `π_θ` generates responses `G = π_θ(X)` for the sampled batch `X`.
 - A binary reward `R_i` is computed for each problem (`1` for correct, `0` for incorrect).
 - The `R_avg` is calculated as the average reward across the batch.
 - The policy `π_θ` is then updated using the chosen RL algorithm `A` (e.g., PPO) based on the current batch, generated responses, and rewards.

4. **Target Difficulty Update (Section 3.3)**:
 - The target difficulty `T` is adjusted dynamically based on the `R_avg` from the current batch.
 - The update rule is `T' = clip(T + η · tanh(α · (R_avg - β)), d_min, d_max)`.
 - If `R_avg` is above `β`, `T` increases, presenting harder problems. If `R_avg` is below `β`, `T` decreases, presenting easier problems.
 - The `tanh` function smooths the updates, preventing abrupt changes and saturating for large deviations.
 - The `clip` function ensures `T` remains within the predefined bounds `[d_min, d_max]`.
 - The hyperparameters `η` (step size), `α` (sensitivity), and `β` (target reward) are crucial for controlling this adaptive process. `β` is typically set to 0.5, aiming for a 50% success rate to maximize the learning signal (reward variance).

**Difficulty Estimation (Section 4.1)**:
Accurate problem difficulty scores are prerequisite for AdaRFT. Two methods were explored:
- **Rollout-based Estimation**: A Qwen 2.5 MATH 7B model is used as a scorer. For each problem, `d_i = 100 * (1 - s_i/n)`, where `s_i` is the number of successful attempts out of `n=128` rollouts. This approach provides an empirical, model-relative difficulty. Stability was validated by showing that 64 rollouts provide over 90% confidence in difficulty estimates within ±0.05 of full rollouts. Correlation with AoPS (Art of Problem Solving) derived difficulty levels (r = -0.34) was also observed.
- **LLM-Judged Difficulty**: GPT-4o (gpt-4o-0806) was prompted to assign difficulty ratings based on the AoPS rubric, offering a lightweight and scalable alternative to rollouts. This method also showed a correlation (r = -0.32) with model success rates.

**Dataset (Section 4.2)**:
- **Training Data**: DeepScaleR dataset, comprising competition-level math problems from various sources (AIME, AMC, Omni-MATH, Still datasets).
- **Training Distributions**: To evaluate robustness, three distinct difficulty distributions (10,000 samples each) were created from DeepScaleR:
 - **Uniform**: Problems evenly distributed across difficulty levels.
 - **Skew-Difficult**: Most problems are challenging.
 - **Skew-Easy**: Most problems are relatively easy.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks**: Six benchmarks were used for comprehensive assessment: MATH 500, GSM8K, OlympiadBench, Minerva Math, AMC 23, and AIME 24, covering a range of mathematical domains and difficulty levels.

**Training Setup (Section 4.3)**:
- **Models**: Qwen 2.5 7B and Qwen 2.5 MATH 1.5B were used to assess AdaRFT across different scales and initial performance levels.
- **Baselines**:
 - **Standard PPO**: The base reinforcement finetuning algorithm.
 - **PPO with Filtered Data**: Trains PPO on a subset of data filtered by `pass@k` accuracy, discarding problems with solved rates ≤10% or ≥90%. This static filtering removes a large fraction of data and requires recomputation per model/data.
 - **PPO with Fixed Curriculum (PPO w/ FC)**: The target difficulty increases linearly over training steps, irrespective of model performance.
- **Hyperparameters**: `B=1024`, `β=0.5`, `α=2`, `η=50`, initial `T=0`. Training was conducted on 8 A100 GPUs for approximately 100 steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates AdaRFT (instantiated with PPO) against standard PPO, PPO with filtered data, and PPO with a fixed curriculum across two model sizes (Qwen 2.5 MATH 1.5B and Qwen 2.5 7B) and three data distributions (uniform, skew-difficult, skew-easy).

#### 4.1. Training Efficiency

AdaRFT consistently demonstrated improved training efficiency:
- **Reduced Training Steps**: AdaRFT required fewer training steps to achieve performance comparable to or surpassing baselines. For Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B, standard PPO needed approximately 71.7% more steps in the skew-difficult setting and 56.7% more in the uniform setting to match AdaRFT's performance. PPO with filtered data required even more steps (e.g., 81.7% and 86.7% more, respectively). Even in the skew-easy scenario, AdaRFT achieved comparable performance with 26.7% fewer steps than PPO. Similar efficiency gains were observed with the larger Qwen 2.5 7B model.
- **Faster Average Step Time**: AdaRFT generally exhibited faster average training time per step. This was attributed to the curriculum's tendency to prioritize easier problems early in training, which often require fewer tokens to solve. Shorter response lengths reduce computational costs across rollout generation and PPO updates.
- **Fixed Curriculum Comparison**: While PPO with a fixed curriculum showed modest initial speedups compared to standard PPO, its lack of adaptivity limited its overall convergence speed relative to AdaRFT, highlighting the benefit of dynamic adjustment.

#### 4.2. Model Performance

AdaRFT not only improved efficiency but also maintained or improved final model performance:
- **Higher Final Accuracy**: AdaRFT achieved higher final accuracy at the end of training (step 100) across all tested configurations. For instance, on skew-difficult data, Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B improved from 37.41% (PPO) to 40.48% (AdaRFT). The Qwen 2.5 7B model also showed consistent, though smaller, gains (e.g., 44.17% to 46.83% on skew-difficult data).
- **Generalization**: The improvements were observed across an average of six diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks, indicating broad effectiveness.

#### 4.3. Ablation on Target Reward β

An ablation study on the target reward parameter `β` (which represents the desired success rate) revealed:
- **Optimal β=0.5**: Setting `β = 0.5` consistently resulted in the highest accuracy throughout training for the Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B model on the uniform data distribution. This supports the theoretical motivation that a 50% success rate maximizes reward variance and provides a strong learning signal.
- **Curriculum Misalignment**: Values of `β = 0.2` (overly difficult curriculum) and `β = 0.8` (overly easy curriculum) led to suboptimal performance, confirming that an appropriate balance of difficulty is crucial for effective learning. The average reward and mean difficulty curves correlated with these accuracy trends, demonstrating how `β` steers the curriculum.

#### 4.4. Data Difficulty on Model Performance

Experiments with "easy-extreme" (difficulty ≤ 15) and "hard-extreme" (difficulty ≥ 97) distributions provided insights:
- **Ineffectiveness of Extremes**: Training solely on overly easy or hard problems with standard PPO yielded poor learning signals and lower final accuracies compared to training on a uniform distribution or with AdaRFT.
- **"Productive Struggle" Validation**: The easy-extreme setup achieved rapid initial reward improvement but stagnated, while hard-extreme showed consistently low rewards and accuracy. AdaRFT (on uniform data) effectively maintained an average reward near 0.5, allowing the model to learn at an optimal pace and achieve the highest overall accuracy.
- **Response Length as Proxy**: Response length correlated with problem complexity; AdaRFT showed a gradual increase in response length, reflecting the adaptive progression to more difficult problems.

#### 4.5. AdaRFT with Diverse RL Algorithms

AdaRFT's generality was confirmed by integrating it with other RL algorithms:
- **Broad Applicability**: AdaRFT consistently improved convergence speed and final accuracy when used with REINFORCE++ and GRPO on the Qwen 2.5 Math 1.5B model in the skew-difficult setting. This suggests AdaRFT acts orthogonally to the underlying optimization method and can be plug-and-play.

#### 4.6. Training on LLM-Estimated Difficulty

An alternative difficulty estimation strategy using GPT-4o judgments (instead of rollout-based pass@k metrics) was also evaluated:
- **Feasible Alternative**: AdaRFT remained effective even when guided by LLM-judged difficulty levels, outperforming standard PPO. While rollout-based difficulty yielded stronger gains, the LLM-judged approach provided a "noticeable improvement" and offers a practical, lightweight alternative for resource-constrained scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of AdaRFT presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of large language model (LLM) research and application, particularly in areas requiring structured reasoning.

**Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability of RFT:**
AdaRFT provides a lightweight and scalable method to significantly improve the efficiency of Reinforcement Finetuning (RFT). By dynamically adjusting problem difficulty, it reduces the required training steps and, in many cases, the wall-clock training time (up to 2x reduction in the RFT training phase). This directly addresses a major bottleneck in RFT, which is its computational and sample inefficiency. The improved efficiency makes advanced RFT techniques more accessible for researchers and practitioners, potentially enabling larger-scale finetuning experiments or deployment with reduced resource costs.

**Improved Learning Dynamics and Performance:**
Beyond efficiency, AdaRFT consistently maintains and often improves the final reasoning performance of LLMs. By ensuring models train on tasks that are challenging but solvable, it optimizes the learning signal, preventing stagnation on overly easy problems and avoiding sparse rewards from excessively difficult ones. This "productive struggle" approach contributes to more stable reward trajectories and, consequently, better generalization across diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

**Generality and Broad Applicability:**
AdaRFT is designed as a model-agnostic extension that can be applied on top of various standard RL algorithms (e.g., PPO, REINFORCE++, GRPO) without modifying the reward function or model architecture. This plug-and-play characteristic broadens its applicability across different RFT pipelines and LLM architectures. It reduces the need for algorithm-specific tuning or extensive data preprocessing, making it a versatile tool for various LLM alignment tasks.

**Robustness to Data Distribution Imbalance:**
The method demonstrates particular effectiveness in non-uniform or imbalanced data distributions (e.g., skew-difficult or skew-easy datasets). In such scenarios, static sampling often leads to suboptimal learning. AdaRFT's adaptive nature allows it to dynamically recalibrate the training curriculum to match the model's evolving capabilities, thereby improving robustness and performance where traditional methods might struggle. This is critical for real-world datasets that often exhibit non-uniform difficulty.

**Theoretical and Empirical Validation of Curriculum Principles:**
The ablation study on the target reward `β` empirically supports the theoretical intuition that learning is most effective when the success rate is around 50% (maximizing reward variance). This finding provides a principled guideline for designing adaptive curricula in RFT, offering insights into how to best structure learning progressions for LLMs.

**Practical Alternatives for Difficulty Estimation:**
While rollout-based difficulty estimation yields strong results, the research also demonstrates that AdaRFT remains effective even with heuristic, LLM-judged difficulty scores. This offers a practical, lightweight fallback for scenarios where extensive model rollouts for difficulty assessment are computationally prohibitive. This flexibility enhances the method's utility in resource-constrained environments or for rapid prototyping.

**Implications for LLM Alignment and Reasoning Capabilities:**
By making RFT more efficient and effective, AdaRFT contributes to advancing the alignment of LLMs with human-desired behaviors and their ability to perform complex, structured reasoning tasks. This could lead to more capable and reliable LLMs for fields like scientific discovery, engineering, and education, where accurate mathematical and logical reasoning is paramount. The reduced training time and improved performance suggest a pathway toward making highly capable reasoning models more widely attainable.

The main limitation acknowledged is the upfront cost of offline difficulty estimation (via rollouts), which needs to be considered for one-off training runs. However, this cost can be amortized across multiple training runs, reduced by using fewer rollouts, or substituted by more lightweight LLM-judged methods. Overall, AdaRFT represents a step towards more intelligent and resource-aware approaches to fine-tuning LLMs for complex tasks.
