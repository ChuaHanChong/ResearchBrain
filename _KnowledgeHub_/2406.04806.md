# Streaming Diffusion Policy: Fast Policy Synthesis with Variable Noise Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Norwegian University of Science and Technology and Harvard University developed the Streaming Diffusion Policy (SDP), a method designed to accelerate the inference of diffusion-based robotic policies without relying on distillation. This approach leverages variable noise levels and an action buffer, achieving a 0.85 success rate in a real-world Push-T task with a sampling time of 0.07 seconds per action chunk, surpassing existing diffusion policies.

## Problem

- Diffusion models, despite their capabilities in visuomotor control, are computationally slow due to their iterative denoising process, restricting real-time robotic applications.
- Current diffusion policies often generate a full action trajectory but utilize only the initial actions, leading to inefficient use of computational resources.
- Existing acceleration methods like distillation frequently introduce high training costs, instability, or compromise the quality and desirable properties of generated actions.

## Method

- The **Streaming Diffusion Policy** (SDP) generates partially denoised action trajectories with variable noise levels and maintains a persistent action buffer for future actions.
- It employs a recursive sampling procedure that performs only a fraction of the total denoising steps per control iteration (N/h steps), achieving an h-fold speedup by selectively denoising the immediate action chunk.
- The policy is trained using specific trajectory noise corruption schemes, such as a combination of '80% Chunk-wise 20% Constant' variance, to align with the streaming inference process.

## Results

- SDP achieves comparable or marginally superior performance to Diffusion Policy and generally surpasses Consistency Policy across various simulated robotic tasks (e.g., Push-T, Lift, Can, Square, Transport).
- In real-world Push-T experiments, SDP demonstrates a **0.85** success rate, exceeding Diffusion Policy (**0.50**) and Consistency Policy (**0.40**).
- The method provides substantial inference acceleration, achieving a real-world sampling time of **0.07** seconds per action chunk, which is faster than Diffusion Policy (**0.13** seconds) and exhibits greater robustness with reduced oscillatory movements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A robot requires a noise-free immediate action for execution, but subsequent actions in a planned trajectory can remain partially noisy and be refined as they become immediate.
> - Reusing partially denoised future actions from a persistent buffer and recursively updating them drastically reduces the computational load per control cycle.
> - Optimizing the training noise distribution to match the streaming inference's chunk-wise variable noise levels is crucial for maintaining high policy performance during acceleration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly expanding field of robotic imitation learning and visuomotor control using diffusion models: the slow inference speed of these powerful generative models. Diffusion models have emerged as state-of-the-art tools for learning complex, multimodal distributions over action trajectories, enabling robots to perform intricate dexterous tasks. Pioneering works like Diffuser and Diffusion Policy have demonstrated their capability in motion planning and imitation learning, respectively. Diffusion Policy, in particular, has shown how these models can effectively learn policies from demonstrations by treating action sequence generation as a denoising process conditioned on observations.

However, a fundamental limitation of traditional diffusion models is their iterative nature, requiring many denoising steps—each involving a neural network evaluation—to produce a clean output. This computational expense makes them slow, often restricting their application to static tasks or environments with slow dynamics, where high control frequencies are not essential. This speed bottleneck becomes particularly problematic for reactive robotic policies, mobile robots with limited onboard computational resources, or tasks demanding real-time responsiveness.

To mitigate this, several approaches have been explored. One prominent direction involves **distillation** techniques, exemplified by Consistency Trajectory Models and Consistency Policy. These methods aim to reduce the number of sampling steps required by distilling a multi-step diffusion process into a fewer-step (or even one-step) model. While effective in accelerating inference, distillation often comes with its own drawbacks: it can be computationally expensive during training, prone to instability, and may compromise the quality, diversity, or other desirable properties (like composability, or approximate likelihood computation) of the synthesized samples.

The proposed Streaming Diffusion Policy (SDP) positions itself as an **alternative and complementary approach to acceleration**, directly tackling the speed problem without resorting to distillation. Instead of discarding the bulk of a fully denoised trajectory (as Diffusion Policy often does, executing only the first few actions), SDP leverages the insight that only the immediate next action needs to be perfectly clean. This allows subsequent actions in a planned trajectory to remain partially noisy, only to be further denoised as they become "immediate." This strategy fundamentally changes how diffusion models are applied in a closed-loop control setting.

Related works in sequence generation, such as Rolling Diffusion (for video prediction), Temporally Entangled Diffusion (for character motion), Diffusion Forcing (for next-token prediction), and AR-Diffusion (for language modeling), have explored similar ideas of rolling windows or variable noise levels. However, SDP specifically adapts these concepts to the unique challenges of *closed-loop robotic policies*, where continuous interaction with the environment and real-time responsiveness are paramount. By doing so, SDP aims to integrate seamlessly with existing diffusion-based planning methods, offering a layer of acceleration that enhances their practical utility without altering their core learning capabilities or sacrificing their beneficial properties.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **drastically accelerate the synthesis of actions by diffusion-based visuomotor policies** while preserving their inherent advantages and maintaining high performance in robotic tasks. This acceleration is crucial for enabling the widespread adoption of diffusion models in real-world robotic applications that demand fast, reactive control.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several key observations and challenges:

1. **Inherent Slowness of Diffusion Models:** While powerful for modeling complex, multimodal action distributions, diffusion models are computationally intensive. They typically require numerous iterative denoising steps, each involving the evaluation of a large neural network, to generate a clean action trajectory. This sequential nature inherently limits their speed.

2. **Mismatch with Real-Time Robotic Requirements:** Many robotic environments feature fast dynamics, necessitating high control frequencies (i.e., quick action generation). The slow inference speed of conventional diffusion policies restricts their application to more static tasks or offline planning, making them unsuitable for real-time reactive control where immediate and continuous action updates are required.

3. **Computational Constraints on Mobile Robots:** Mobile and embedded robotic systems often operate with limited onboard computational resources. The significant computational demands of standard diffusion models pose a practical barrier to their deployment in such resource-constrained scenarios.

4. **Drawbacks of Existing Acceleration Methods:** Recent efforts to accelerate diffusion policies have primarily focused on distillation techniques (e.g., Consistency Policy). However, these methods often come with their own set of limitations:
 - **High Training Cost:** Distillation can be computationally expensive during the training phase.
 - **Instability:** The distillation process can be unstable, making models harder to train effectively.
 - **Performance Trade-offs:** Distillation may lead to a degradation in the accuracy or diversity of the synthesized actions.
 - **Loss of Desirable Properties:** Important properties of diffusion models, such as composability (the ability to combine different learned policies) and approximate likelihood computation (useful for uncertainty estimation), can be lost during distillation.

5. **Inefficiency of Current Diffusion Policies:** Existing methods like Diffusion Policy generate a full action trajectory over a certain horizon but then execute only the very first action (or a small chunk), effectively discarding the vast majority of the denoising work. This inefficiency highlights an opportunity for more intelligent reuse of the generated information.

The authors' core insight is that a robot typically only needs a *noise-free immediate action* to execute. Future actions, while important for planning, do not need to be fully denoised at the current timestep. By selectively denoising only what is immediately necessary and maintaining a "streaming" buffer of partially denoised future actions, SDP aims to significantly reduce the computational burden at each timestep without sacrificing the robustness and expressive power of diffusion models. This approach seeks to provide a practical, high-performance solution for deploying diffusion-based policies in dynamic and reactive robotic scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Streaming Diffusion Policy (SDP) introduces a novel approach to accelerate action synthesis in diffusion models by fundamentally changing how action trajectories are generated and reused in a closed-loop control setting. The core idea revolves around generating *partially denoised action trajectories with variable noise levels* and maintaining a persistent action buffer.

#### 3.1. Core Principle: Partially Denoised Trajectories and Action Buffer

Unlike traditional diffusion policies that fully denoise an entire action sequence for each observation and then discard most of it, SDP generates an action trajectory where the immediate action is noise-free, but subsequent actions exhibit increasing levels of noise and uncertainty. These future actions are stored in a persistent "action buffer." This buffer serves as a rolling window of future actions, continuously updated and refined.

#### 3.2. Fast Recursive Sampling with Action Buffers

The key to SDP's speedup lies in its recursive sampling procedure, which intelligently reuses the information in the action buffer:

1. **Initialization:** At the start of policy execution, the action buffer needs to be initialized. The paper explores three "action primers":
 - **Denoise:** Fully denoise an initial action sequence given the first observation.
 - **Constant:** Repeat a fixed initial action `a0` (if known) across the trajectory.
 - **Zero:** Initialize the buffer with a zero-tensor.
 Through ablations, the "Zero" primer is chosen for its effective performance and generality, as it doesn't require prior knowledge of `a0` or extra denoising steps. The chosen primer is then corrupted with temporally increasing noise to set up the initial buffer.

2. **Recursive Denoising and Buffer Update:** In each control loop iteration, given a new observation `O_t`:
 - **Partial Denoising:** The action buffer, which holds actions with increasing noise levels (e.g., `[N/h, 2N/h..., N]`), is subjected to a *few* denoising steps (specifically, `N/h` steps, where `N` is the total diffusion steps and `h` is the number of chunks). This process makes the first chunk of actions noise-free while shifting the noise levels of subsequent chunks down.
 - **Execute and Refresh:** The now noise-free first chunk of actions is executed in the environment. This chunk is then removed from the beginning of the buffer. A new chunk, initialized with pure Gaussian noise, is appended to the end of the buffer. This re-establishes the original distribution of increasing noise levels across the buffer, ready for the next observation and subsequent denoising steps.

This recursive process means that instead of `N` full denoising steps, SDP only needs to perform `N/h` steps to obtain the next clean chunk of actions, where `h` is the number of chunks. This directly translates to an `h`-fold speedup in inference.

#### 3.3. Action Chunking for High Control Frequencies

To further accommodate very high control frequencies, SDP incorporates "action chunking." The prediction horizon `T_a` is divided into `h` chunks, each of length `T_b`. At each observation, a full chunk of `T_b` actions is generated and executed in an open-loop manner. While this chunk is being executed, SDP recursively generates the *next* chunk of actions. This allows for continuous control flow even when network inference takes a non-negligible amount of time.

#### 3.4. Training SDP Policy with Variable Noise Corruption

The training procedure for SDP adapts the standard diffusion model training (e.g., DDPM). Instead of adding noise with the same variance across the entire action sequence, SDP allows for a unique variance (or diffusion level `k_i`) for each action or chunk in the sequence. This leads to a modified denoising loss that accounts for `k` being a vector `k ∈ Z^(T_p)` (where `T_p` is the prediction horizon).

The paper explores different "trajectory noise corruption schemes" applied during training:
- **Constant Variance:** Noise with uniform variance across the entire action sequence (like standard Diffusion Policy).
- **Linearly Increasing Variance:** Noise levels increase linearly across the action sequence.
- **Independent:** Each step `k_i` is sampled independently from a uniform distribution `U[1..., N]`.
- **Chunk-wise Increasing Variance:** Noise levels increase linearly across the *chunks*, with each action within a chunk sharing the same noise level. This scheme is designed to match the sampling process.
The research finds that a combination of schemes, specifically 80% Chunk-Wise and 20% Constant, yields the best performance, indicating the importance of training the model with noise patterns that align with the streaming inference process.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

SDP's implementation builds upon the U-Net architecture used in Diffusion Policy. The primary modification is to the diffusion step encoder, enabling it to accept a unique diffusion step for each element (or chunk) in the buffer, rather than a single global diffusion step. The method typically uses DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models) for the underlying diffusion process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors provide robust evidence for the efficacy of Streaming Diffusion Policy (SDP) in significantly accelerating action synthesis without compromising performance. The findings cover ablation studies, speed comparisons, and benchmarks on simulated and real-world robotic tasks.

#### 4.1. Ablation Studies (State-based Push-T Task)

- **Action Primer Selection:** Three initialization schemes for the action buffer were compared: "Zero," "Constant," and "Denoising."
 - Both "Zero" and "Constant" initialization achieved an average score of 0.90, with a fast prediction time of 0.30 seconds.
 - "Denoising" also achieved a score of 0.90 but required a longer prediction time (0.58 seconds) due to additional denoising iterations.
 - The "Zero" method was chosen for subsequent experiments due to its comparable performance, speed, and greater generality (it doesn't assume knowledge of an initial action `a_0`).
- **Trajectory Noise Corruption Schemes:** Various schemes for applying noise during training were evaluated:
 - "Linear" (0.36) and "Constant" (0.29) schemes performed poorly in isolation.
 - "Independent" (0.85) and "Chunk-wise" (0.86) schemes showed significantly better performance.
 - Combinations were explored, with "67% Independent 33% Linear" (0.87), "67% Independent 33% Chunk-wise" (0.89), and "80% Chunk-wise 20% Constant" (0.90) yielding the best results. The "80% Chunk-wise 20% Constant" combination was selected for its highest performance, underscoring the importance of training the model in a way that aligns with the chunk-wise sampling strategy.

#### 4.2. Sampling Time Experiments (Push-T Task)

- **Speedup with Longer Prediction Horizons:** A key finding is that SDP's prediction time *decreases monotonically* as the prediction horizon (and thus the number of chunks in the buffer) increases. In contrast, the prediction time for standard Diffusion Policy remains constant regardless of the horizon.
- **Inverse Proportionality:** The observed speedup aligns with the theoretical prediction: the sampling time is inversely proportional to the number of chunks (`h`) in the buffer, as SDP only performs `N/h` denoising steps per control iteration. This demonstrates a clear architectural advantage for SDP in terms of inference speed for longer planning horizons.

#### 4.3. Performance on Simulated Robotic Tasks

- SDP was benchmarked against Diffusion Policy and Consistency Policy on several image-based tasks from Robomimic (Lift, Can, Square, Transport) and the Push-T task.
- **Comparable Performance to Diffusion Policy:** SDP consistently achieved performance comparable to, or marginally better than, Diffusion Policy across all simulated tasks. For example, on Push-T, SDP scored 0.84/0.79 (Max/Avg) compared to Diffusion Policy's 0.88/0.84. On Lift and Can tasks, both achieved perfect scores. On Transport, SDP scored 0.97/0.88 vs. Diffusion Policy's 1.00/0.88. This indicates that the acceleration mechanism does not negatively impact the policy's ability to solve complex tasks.
- **Outperforming Consistency Policy:** Consistency Policy, despite requiring twice the training time (for teacher and student models), generally performed worse or equally to SDP and Diffusion Policy, especially on more intricate tasks like Push-T (0.75/0.68) and Transport (0.89/0.71). This highlights SDP's advantage in offering speedup without the performance degradation sometimes associated with distillation.

#### 4.4. Real-world Experiment (Real-world Push-T Task)

- **Superior Real-world Performance:** On a real-world Push-T task with 134 demonstrations, SDP achieved the highest coverage success rate of **0.85**. This significantly outperforms Diffusion Policy (0.50) and Consistency Policy (0.40).
- **Fast Sampling Speed:** SDP achieved a real-world sampling time of **0.07 seconds** per action chunk, making it faster than Diffusion Policy (0.13 seconds). While Consistency Policy was the fastest (0.01 seconds), its notably lower success rate makes SDP a more practical choice for this real-world task.
- **Reduced Oscillatory Movements:** Qualitatively, the authors observed that a common failure mode for Diffusion Policy and Consistency Policy was getting "stuck performing small oscillatory movements" during precise pushes. SDP, with its persistent action buffer, was less prone to this error, suggesting improved robustness in real-world control.

In summary, SDP successfully demonstrates its ability to dramatically speed up diffusion-based policy synthesis, particularly with longer prediction horizons, while maintaining or even improving performance compared to existing methods, especially in real-world scenarios where it exhibited greater stability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Streaming Diffusion Policy (SDP) represents a significant advancement in the application of diffusion models to robotic control, addressing one of their most critical limitations: inference speed. The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted and extends across several dimensions of robotic research and deployment:

#### 5.1. Enabling Real-Time, Reactive Robotic Control

The most direct and impactful contribution of SDP is its ability to make diffusion models viable for real-time, reactive control in dynamic environments. Previously, the slow, iterative nature of diffusion model inference largely confined them to offline planning or tasks with slow dynamics. SDP shatters this barrier by providing an `h`-fold speedup, enabling high-frequency control loops. This is crucial for tasks requiring immediate responses, fine-grained manipulation, or interaction with fast-moving objects.

#### 5.2. A Robust Alternative to Distillation

SDP offers a powerful alternative to distillation-based methods for accelerating diffusion policies. While distillation can provide speedups, it often comes with trade-offs in training complexity, stability, sample quality, and the loss of desirable diffusion model properties (e.g., composability, approximate likelihood computation). SDP achieves comparable or superior performance and speed without these compromises, maintaining the core benefits of the diffusion process. This provides researchers and practitioners with a more robust and flexible approach to acceleration.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Real-World Robot Reliability and Performance

The real-world experiments are particularly compelling, showcasing SDP's superior success rate (0.85) compared to Diffusion Policy (0.50) and Consistency Policy (0.40) on the Push-T task. The qualitative observation that SDP is less prone to oscillatory movements—a common failure mode for replanning-heavy policies—is a critical practical advantage. In robotic deployment, stability and reliability are paramount, and SDP's persistent action buffer appears to contribute to smoother and more consistent task execution, leading to higher success rates. This could make diffusion policies more trustworthy and deployable in real-world settings.

#### 5.4. Optimized Resource Utilization for Mobile Robotics

By drastically reducing the number of denoising steps required per control cycle, SDP lowers the computational load. This makes diffusion policies more amenable to deployment on resource-constrained platforms, such as mobile robots or edge devices, where onboard computation is limited. This expands the applicability of advanced diffusion-based control to a wider range of autonomous systems.

#### 5.5. Extensible and Generalizable Framework

The streaming approach is designed to be compatible with existing diffusion-based planning methods. This means that future advancements in diffusion model architectures, training techniques, or planning strategies can potentially be integrated with SDP's acceleration mechanism, further enhancing their practical utility. This modularity fosters innovation across the broader diffusion-for-robotics landscape.

#### 5.6. Advancing the State of the Art in Visuomotor Learning

SDP pushes the boundaries of visuomotor policy learning by making powerful generative models more efficient. As diffusion models continue to grow in capability, overcoming their speed bottleneck is essential for their widespread adoption in complex manipulation, navigation, and human-robot interaction tasks. SDP provides a blueprint for achieving this efficiency.

#### 5.7. Future Research Directions

The paper also highlights areas for future research, such as optimizing the "large space of possible configurations" (chunk length, buffer length, action primer) for specific tasks, managing the trade-off between buffer length (speed) and policy reactivity (performance), and further mitigating oscillatory behaviors. These open questions indicate that SDP lays a strong foundation for continued exploration and improvement in efficient diffusion-based robotic control.

In conclusion, Streaming Diffusion Policy significantly mitigates the speed limitations of diffusion models in robotics, offering a practical, high-performance, and robust solution for real-time control. Its demonstrated effectiveness in both simulation and the real world, coupled with its architectural elegance, positions it to have a lasting impact on how advanced generative models are integrated into the next generation of autonomous robotic systems.
