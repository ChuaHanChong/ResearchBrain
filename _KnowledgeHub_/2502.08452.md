# Learning to Push, Group, and Grasp: A Diffusion Policy Approach for Multi-Object Delivery

> [!summary] Summary
> An imitation learning framework based on a Diffusion Policy allows robots to perform multi-object delivery tasks by integrating pushing, grouping, and simultaneous grasping actions. Learned policies achieved 85% task completion for 4 objects and 70% for 6 objects, demonstrating improved efficiency and robustness compared to rule-based approaches, along with adaptability to new object types via fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with simultaneous multi-object grasping when objects are initially scattered, limiting work efficiency.
- Existing rule-based methods for pre-grasp pushing lack flexibility and struggle to adapt to diverse object distributions or complex pushing trajectories.
- Reinforcement learning approaches face challenges with designing effective reward functions for multi-stage tasks like pushing-grouping-grasping, hindering rapid convergence.

## Method

- An imitation learning framework is developed, leveraging human expert demonstrations to train a ==Diffusion Policy== network for multi-object delivery.
- A visual teleoperation system collects observation-action data, mapping human hand poses and gripper states to robot actions for pushing, grouping, and grasping.
- The Diffusion Policy network predicts a sequence of future robot actions (position, rotation, gripper state) conditioned on past visual and robot state observations, operating in a closed-loop manner for online adaptation.

## Results

- The learned policy achieved an **85%** task completion rate for 4 objects and **70%** for 6 objects, with an average of **1.77** objects delivered per grasp when trained on 300 demonstrations.
- The method demonstrated superior time efficiency (average planning and execution time of 1s + 71s) and grasping efficiency (**1.77** objects/grasp) compared to an analytical rule-based method (73s planning + 76s execution, **1.44** objects/grasp).
- Fine-tuning with only 50 demonstrations enabled adaptation to real-world snack bars and unseen object shapes (L-shapes, cubes, hexagons), achieving completion rates up to **60%** for specific geometries.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion Policy is effective for learning complex, sequential manipulation tasks that combine strategic object rearrangement (pushing and grouping) with multi-object grasping.
> - Imitation learning enables robots to develop adaptive strategies for multi-object delivery, overcoming the inflexibility of rule-based systems and the reward engineering complexities of reinforcement learning.
> - Increasing the volume of human demonstration data significantly improves the learned policy's performance metrics, including task completion rate and grasping efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The simultaneous grasping and delivery of multiple objects by robots represents a long-standing challenge in robotics, primarily aimed at enhancing work efficiency. Prior research in multi-object grasping has explored various strategies, often categorizable by their assumptions regarding object proximity and their reliance on rule-based or learning-based approaches.

Early studies on multi-object grasping typically assumed objects were already in close proximity or focused on cooperative manipulation using multiple arms. More recent developments have involved inserting grippers into object piles, optimizing transfer to containers, grasping granular materials, and frameworks for multi-object lifting. Specialized grippers, such as vacuum grippers with multiple suction cups or wiring-claw grippers, have also been developed, alongside approaches that exploit kinematic redundancy for dexterous multi-object grasping. A common limitation of many of these methods is their reduced applicability to scenarios where objects are scattered rather than pre-arranged in close clusters.

To address scattered object scenarios, several studies have incorporated pushing actions to rearrange objects prior to grasping. Rule-based methods, such as those by Sakamoto et al. and Agboh et al., have proposed strategies for pushing objects closer to enable simultaneous grasping. Shrey et al. leveraged linear pushing to consolidate object centroids, and Ye et al. used weighted graphs to model distributions for collision-free clustering. Kishore et al. combined pushing and stacking for efficient delivery, emphasizing inward pushing to reduce positional uncertainty. While these pushing-based methods extend applicability to scattered objects, they are predominantly rule-based. This reliance on hand-crafted rules limits their flexibility and ability to adapt to diverse object distributions or generate complex pushing trajectories required for intricate grouping and grasping tasks.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, offering adaptive capabilities to various object arrangements. Zeng et al. used self-supervised deep RL to learn complementary pushing and grasping actions. Chen et al. combined rule-based methods with RL, and Wang et al. employed self-supervised deep RL for integrated pushing and grasping policies in cluttered environments. However, a significant challenge in RL, particularly for multi-stage tasks like pushing-grouping-grasping, is the design of effective reward functions that facilitate rapid convergence.

This research positions itself within the context of learning-based approaches, specifically addressing the limitations of rule-based methods in terms of flexibility and the complexities of reward design in RL. By adopting imitation learning, the work aims to enable robots to learn nuanced pushing, grouping, and grasping strategies directly from human demonstrations. The application of Diffusion Policy, previously shown effective in diverse manipulation tasks, to the problem of choosing among pushing, grouping, and multi-object grasping, represents a novel contribution within the broader landscape of robotic manipulation research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this study is to develop a robotic system capable of simultaneous multi-object grasping through intelligent pushing and grouping actions. This capability is intended to significantly improve robotic work efficiency in tasks involving the delivery of multiple items.

The motivation for this research stems from several critical challenges observed in existing multi-object grasping approaches:
1. **Complexity of Object Distribution:** Robots need to effectively handle scenarios where objects are initially scattered, requiring strategic rearrangement rather than assuming pre-existing proximity.
2. **Integration of Pushing, Grouping, and Grasping:** A robust solution necessitates a coordinated strategy for how to push objects to group them, and subsequently how to execute simultaneous grasping for the resulting groups, all while considering the physical layout of objects.
3. **Adaptability to Diverse Scenarios:** Traditional rule-based methods, while effective under specific, predefined conditions (e.g., specific object distances or gripper widths), struggle to adapt flexibly to the wide array of object configurations and environmental variations encountered in real-world applications. Hand-crafting rules for complex pushing trajectories and dynamic object combinations becomes exceedingly difficult.
4. **Limitations of Reinforcement Learning:** While reinforcement learning offers adaptive potential, designing appropriate reward functions for a multi-stage, complex task like pushing-grouping-grasping is computationally intensive and can hinder learning efficiency and convergence.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose an imitation learning-based approach. The core motivation is to allow robots to learn intricate decision-making processes—specifically, which objects to group, how to push them into graspable configurations, and when to execute simultaneous grasps—directly from human expert demonstrations. This approach aims to imbue the robot with the flexibility and adaptability that are difficult to achieve with predefined rules or through reward-engineered reinforcement learning. By acquiring strategies through imitation, the proposed method seeks to enable automated selection of suitable object combinations and execution of necessary preparatory pushing actions, thereby enhancing the efficiency and versatility of robotic picking tasks beyond the limitations of current methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on an imitation learning framework that leverages expert human demonstrations to train a Diffusion Policy network for multi-object delivery. The overall approach consists of three main phases: data collection, policy learning, and action inference.

**A. Data Collection Using Visual Teleoperation**
A teleoperation system was developed to collect human expert data, similar to the AnyTeleop system. An RGB-D camera was used to detect human hand skeleton poses via WiLoR. The 3D skeleton data from WiLoR, initially in the camera's coordinate system (Σcam), was transformed into actions for a Universal Robots UR3 manipulator equipped with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper.

Key aspects of the mapping and observation definition include:
- **Gripper Position:** The jaw bottom center of the robot's end-effector was mapped to the MCP joint of the human index finger (p5 in Fig. 2), with its 3D position transformed to the world coordinate system (Σwd).
- **Gripper Rotation:** The end-effector's rotation was constrained such that its approaching direction was vertically downward. Rotation around this approaching direction was derived from the relative positions of the human thumb tip (p4) and index finger tip (p8).
- **Gripping State:** The gripper's open/close state (g) was determined by the normalized distance between the thumb and index finger tips, with thresholds μup = 0.8 for open (g=0) and μbot = 0.1 for closed (g=1).
- **Observation (o_t):** At each timestamp t, the observation o_t comprised video frames from a global camera (i_gcam_t) and a local camera mounted on the robot hand (i_lcam_t), along with the robot's state (s_t). The robot state s_t was an 8-dimensional vector including the end-effector's position (p_x, p_y, p_z) and rotation (quaternion q_w, q_x, q_y, q_z), and the gripper state (g).
- **Action (a_t):** The robot's action at timestamp t was defined identically to the robot's state (an 8-dimensional vector representing the desired position, rotation, and gripper state for the next time step).

Human operators provided demonstrations by controlling the robot to group and grasp multiple objects. The reported heuristics included limiting grouped objects to three, selecting spatially close and aligned objects, and initiating grasps with objects surrounded by potential grouping candidates.

**B. Network Selection and Learning**
The collected demonstrations were used to train a Diffusion Policy network.
- **Policy Input and Output:** The diffusion policy takes observations from the past T_o time steps (O_t = {o_{t-To+1}..., o_t}) as input. It then infers a sequence of actions A_t, which includes predicted past actions (for T_o steps) and predicted future actions (for T_p - T_o steps).
- **Closed-Loop Control:** During inference, the robot executes the first T_a predicted future actions ({a_t..., a_{t+Ta-1}}), then collects new observations and re-predicts, operating in a closed-loop manner. T_a is selected to be smaller than T_p - T_o.
- **Network Architecture (Fig. 4):**
 - **Feature Extraction:** Each of the T_o observations' global and local camera images are processed independently by two ResNet-18 networks to extract N-dimensional feature vectors.
 - **Observation Embedding:** These two N-dimensional image feature vectors are concatenated with the 8-dimensional robot state vector for each time step. Across T_o steps, this forms a (2N + 8) × T_o-dimensional observation embedding.
 - **U-Net Core:** This embedding serves as the condition for a U-Net core. The U-Net takes a noisy action sequence (an 8 × T_p-dimensional vector) as input and predicts the noise present in it.
 - **Denoising Process:** The predicted noise (ε_θ) is iteratively subtracted from the noisy action sequence (A_k_t) over K denoising steps, guided by parameters α, γ, σ, and an encoded iteration step index k (using a P-dimensional sinusoidal positional embedding). The process aims to progressively refine the action sequence to A_0_t.
- **Training:** The network's parameters (θ) are learned by minimizing the L2 loss between the added Gaussian noise (ε_k) and the predicted noise (ε_θ) from a noisy expert action sequence (A_0_t + ε_k) conditioned on O_t.

**C. Implementation Details**
- **Hardware:** Universal Robots UR3 manipulator, Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, two Intel RealSense D435 cameras (global and local). An additional Intel RealSense D435 was used for human hand tracking during data collection.
- **Software/Parameters:** RGB image data (240 × 320 pixels) was used. ResNet-18 output dimension (N) and positional encoding dimension (P) were 128. Time step parameters were T_o = 2, T_a = 8, T_p = 16. The U-Net hidden layers were. AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 10^-4 and weight decay of 10^-6 was used. Batch size was 64, and the number of denoising steps (K) was 100.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were structured into four groups to evaluate the proposed diffusion policy-based imitation learning framework under various conditions.

**A. Experiment 1: Randomly Placed 3D Printed Cuboids**
This experiment investigated the impact of training data volume and object quantity on learning performance.
- **Setup:** 3D-printed rectangular cuboids (120 mm × 20 mm × 20 mm) were randomly placed within a 300 mm × 400 mm frame. Datasets of 100, 200, and 300 demonstrations (with 3 to 6 objects) were used to train separate diffusion policy networks. Evaluation involved 20 trials each for 4 and 6 objects.
- **Metrics:** Task completion rate (all objects delivered), delivery rate (average objects delivered / initial objects), and # objects per grasp (average objects delivered per successful grasp).
- **Results:**
 - As the number of objects increased, task completion rates generally decreased. Failures primarily occurred during pushing and grouping, often due to objects slipping or the robot stagnating.
 - Increased training data positively impacted all metrics. With 300 demonstrations, the completion rate for 4 objects was 85%, and for 6 objects was 70%. The delivery rate for 6 objects reached 77%, with an average of 1.77 objects per grasp.
 - The learned policy demonstrated adaptability by generating diverse grouping and grasping sequences based on object distributions.
 - Time efficiency analysis for 6 objects (300-dataset model) indicated that while multi-object picking involved additional grouping operations, the per-object efficiency improved. For example, picking three objects simultaneously reduced the time per object to 7.9 seconds compared to 19.3 seconds for single-object picking.

**B. Experiment 2: Comparison with Analytical Methods**
This experiment compared the proposed learning-based method against an improved rule-based analytical approach designed to support combined pushing and grasping for three objects.
- **Setup:** Evaluated under identical conditions with 6 3D printed cuboids over 20 trials.
- **Results:**
 - The analytical method achieved similar task completion (70%) and object retrieval (87%) rates as the diffusion policy trained on 300 demonstrations.
 - However, the proposed method demonstrated advantages in time efficiency (average planning and execution time of 1s + 71s for learned vs. 73s + 76s for rule-based).
 - The diffusion policy also achieved a higher grasping efficiency (1.77 objects per grasp vs. 1.44 objects per grasp for the analytical method).
 - The rule-based approach exhibited limitations when minor object displacements occurred, leading to invalid precomputed plans. In contrast, the imitation learning approach, operating in a closed-loop, adapted online to such disturbances, enhancing robustness.

**C. Experiment 3: Fine-tuning to Snack Bars**
This experiment assessed the model's adaptivity to real-world objects through fine-tuning.
- **Setup:** Pre-trained models (using 200 and 300 cuboid datasets) were fine-tuned with 50 demonstrations using six snack bars of varying appearances and slightly different dimensions. Evaluation involved 20 trials.
- **Results:**
 - Task completion rates were low (0% for 200+50 model, 25% for 300+50 model).
 - Primary failure reasons included unexpected object deviation during pushing, leading to detachment from the end effector, as well as stagnation and frequent grasping failures attributed to differences in shape and surface texture compared to the training objects.
 - However, a certain level of multi-object grasping was achieved, with delivery rates of 23% and 46% and # objects per grasp of 1.47 and 1.31 for the 200+50 and 300+50 models, respectively.
 - The results indicated that a more robust foundation training (300-dataset model) coupled with a small amount of fine-tuning data was more effective for transferring policy knowledge.

**D. Experiment 4: Unseen Shapes**
This experiment evaluated the generalization ability of the learned policy to objects with significantly different geometries.
- **Setup:** The model trained on 300 cuboid demonstrations was tested directly on L-shaped objects, cubes, and hexagons (each 20 mm edge length, L-shape arms 60 mm long) without fine-tuning. Subsequently, fine-tuning with 50 demonstrations per object shape was performed.
- **Results (without fine-tuning):**
 - No instances of simultaneous multi-object grasping were observed; only single-object grasps were successful. None of the trials were fully completed.
 - Performance varied by shape, with L-shaped objects performing best, while hexagons had the lowest success rate. Cubes often led to stagnation.
 - Failures were largely attributed to geometric deviations from training objects, causing the robot to hesitate or fail to proceed to grasping.
- **Results (with fine-tuning):**
 - After fine-tuning with 50 task-specific demonstrations, the models demonstrated improved performance. The robot acquired the ability to group objects based on their spatial configuration and execute simultaneous multi-object grasps.
 - For L-shapes, the completion rate was 40% with 1.33 objects per grasp. For cubes, it was 40% with 1.67 objects per grasp. For hexagons, it was 60% with 1.54 objects per grasp.
 - These results suggest that even a small amount of task-specific fine-tuning data can significantly enhance the model's generalization and ability to perform complex manipulation behaviors with novel object geometries.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for multi-object delivery using a diffusion policy-based imitation learning approach, contributing to the field of robotic manipulation in several ways.

Firstly, to the authors' knowledge, this work represents an early application of Diffusion Policy for a combined task that explicitly integrates pushing, grouping, and multi-object grasping. This demonstrates the potential of diffusion models in complex, sequential manipulation tasks that involve strategic object rearrangement.

Secondly, the study validates the feasibility and adaptivity of imitation learning for developing flexible robotic manipulation strategies. By learning from human demonstrations, the robot can generate dynamic action sequences for pushing, grouping, and grasping, which contrasts with the inflexibility of purely rule-based systems and bypasses the challenges of reward function design inherent in reinforcement learning for multi-stage tasks. This approach enables the robot to autonomously select appropriate object combinations and execute necessary preparatory actions for grouping, potentially increasing the versatility of robotic picking tasks.

Thirdly, the experimental results provide empirical evidence for the importance of data quantity in imitation learning performance. The positive correlation between training data volume and task completion, delivery rates, and grasping efficiency suggests that robust policies for multi-object manipulation tasks benefit substantially from larger, high-quality datasets. This finding has implications for future data collection strategies in robotic learning.

Furthermore, the fine-tuning experiments highlight the ability of the proposed framework to adapt to new object types and geometries with a relatively small amount of additional, task-specific data. This suggests that pre-trained foundational models could be effectively specialized for new real-world applications, enhancing generalization capabilities. This capacity for adaptation is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic environments where object appearances and properties may vary.

The comparison with an analytical method further substantiates the benefits of the learning-based approach, demonstrating advantages in time efficiency and grasping efficiency, along with improved robustness against minor object displacements due to its closed-loop, online adaptation mechanism.

Potential impacts of this research include:
- **Enhanced Robotic Work Efficiency:** By enabling simultaneous multi-object grasping, robots could significantly reduce the time required for picking and delivery tasks in logistics, manufacturing, and service industries, leading to higher throughput.
- **Increased Task Versatility:** The adaptive nature of the learned policies allows robots to handle a broader range of object distributions and types, making them more suitable for unstructured or dynamic environments.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The study provides extensive experiments and analysis, identifying both the strengths and limitations of the diffusion policy approach in this context. It suggests directions for future work, such as investigating the impact of expert strategies on data efficiency, developing more efficient data collection methods, and exploring the integration of analytical techniques to further enhance robustness and generalization, particularly for larger workspaces and more diverse object characteristics.

In conclusion, this work contributes to advancing robotic manipulation capabilities by proposing and evaluating a data-driven, imitation learning framework for complex multi-object delivery, offering insights into its performance, adaptability, and areas for future development.
