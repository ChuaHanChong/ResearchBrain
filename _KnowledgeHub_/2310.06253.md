# A Unified View on Solving Objective Mismatch in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A taxonomic survey of solution categories for the "objective mismatch" problem in model-based RL — the observation that WM predictive accuracy often fails to correlate with policy action quality. Organizes the literature into interrelated categories and provides a unified framework for comparing decision-aware MBRL methods. No new algorithm is proposed; the contribution is conceptual clarity for future research.

## Problem

- In MBRL, world models are typically trained for likelihood / predictive accuracy, but downstream reward optimization does not track this metric — models that fit data well can still yield poor policies.
- The community has proposed many disparate fixes (distribution correction, value-equivalence, control-as-inference, differentiable planning) without a unifying framework.
- Without a taxonomy, it is hard to identify which failure modes a given method actually addresses.

## Method

- Survey paper - no new algorithm. Central contribution is a ==taxonomy of decision-aware MBRL== that formally defines objective mismatch and traces it across ==distribution correction==, ==control-as-inference==, ==value-equivalence==, and ==differentiable planning==.
- Identifies shared assumptions and failure modes across categories and contrasts their inductive biases.
- Proposes research directions for hybrid methods that combine the strengths of multiple categories.

## Results

- Delivers a structured taxonomy rather than empirical numbers.
- Identifies common patterns across otherwise disparate lines of work.
- Provides a conceptual basis for future decision-aware MBRL research and for evaluating claimed contributions against a unified framework.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Objective mismatch is not a single problem — it manifests differently across method families.
> - Distribution correction, value-equivalence, control-as-inference, and differentiable planning are four corners of a shared design space.
> - Future progress likely comes from hybrid approaches that target *specific* failure modes within the taxonomy rather than a single monolithic fix.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has advanced significantly, demonstrating capabilities in various complex tasks, from game mastery to power plant management. RL methods aim to teach agents to maximize cumulative rewards in dynamic environments. These methods are broadly categorized into Model-Free RL (MFRL), which directly learns policies from environmental samples, and Model-Based RL (MBRL), which learns an explicit model of the environment and uses it to guide policy learning.

MBRL offers several advantages, including improved sample efficiency, adaptability, and explainability for deep RL agents. Prior research has shown MBRL's utility in enabling agents to plan for diverse goals or environments and allowing designers to understand agent decisions, which can aid in failure diagnosis.

Despite these benefits, a persistent challenge in MBRL is the divergence in algorithms regarding how to model the environment and learn the model effectively. A common approach involves training a model to predict environmental transitions accurately, then using this model for policy optimization. However, a crucial observation, highlighted by Lambert et al. (2020), is that model predictive accuracy frequently does not correlate directly with the quality of actions or achieved returns. This phenomenon is termed "objective mismatch," where the model's learning objective (e.g., maximizing likelihood) differs from the policy's optimization objective (maximizing cumulative reward). This mismatch can lead to policies exploiting inaccuracies in the learned models, particularly during long-horizon rollouts due to compounding errors.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by focusing specifically on the objective mismatch problem. It provides an in-depth survey of existing solutions and proposes a taxonomy, thereby complementing broader surveys on MBRL by offering a focused analysis of this fundamental limitation. The work distinguishes itself from related areas such as state abstraction, representation learning, control-aware dynamics learning, decision-focused learning, and meta-RL by concentrating on the mutual adaptation of model and policy objectives to align them directly.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this research is to enhance the capabilities and downstream performance of MBRL agents by resolving the "objective mismatch" problem. This problem arises because dynamics models in MBRL are typically trained for predictive accuracy (e.g., via maximum likelihood estimation), while the ultimate goal of the RL agent is to maximize cumulative rewards. This misalignment often leads to learned models that are inaccurate in critical regions or susceptible to exploitation by the policy, degrading overall agent performance despite high predictive accuracy on the training data.

Specifically, the paper identifies several key aspects driving this motivation:
- **Improving Agent Performance**: The primary objective is to develop MBRL algorithms that achieve higher returns and improved sample efficiency by ensuring the learned model directly contributes to policy optimization.
- **Addressing Model Exploitation**: Inaccuracies in learned models, particularly compounding errors in long-horizon predictions, can be exploited by policies, leading to suboptimal or unsafe behavior. Resolving objective mismatch aims to mitigate this.
- **Enhancing Data Efficiency and Safety**: For domains where environment interaction is costly or unsafe, MBRL is favored. However, objective mismatch can undermine these benefits. Decision-aware MBRL, which the paper surveys, has the potential to simplify or remove the need for post-hoc methods (like pessimistic penalties in offline RL) designed to prevent model exploitation.
- **Guiding Design of Future Objectives**: The complex interaction between model learning and policy optimization lacks guiding principles for designing effective objectives. A core objective of this survey is to synthesize existing literature to identify these principles.
- **Structuring the Research Area**: By providing a taxonomy of decision-aware MBRL approaches, the authors aim to organize existing solutions, clarify relationships between different methods, and foster future research in a more structured manner. This includes discussing the intuition, implementation details, and empirical evaluations for each approach.
- **Bridging Theoretical and Practical Gaps**: The paper acknowledges the nuanced relationship between model accuracy and policy performance. While Bayesian RL theoretically addresses uncertainty, its intractability necessitates practical solutions for aligning objectives. The goal is to bridge this gap with efficient, decision-aware MBRL methods.

Ultimately, the motivation stems from the recognition that for MBRL to fully realize its potential in real-world automated decision-making systems (e.g., autonomous vehicles, healthcare), its constituent modules (model and policy) must be developed with a shared understanding of the overall task objective, moving beyond isolated development and testing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This work is a comprehensive survey and taxonomy aimed at organizing the existing literature on solutions to the objective mismatch problem in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL). The methodology involved a systematic approach to identify, categorize, and analyze relevant research papers.

**Literature Search and Selection**:
The authors initiated a literature search using terms such as "objective mismatch," "model-based," and "reinforcement learning" and their permutations across Google Scholar and Web of Science. This initial search was augmented by examining articles that cited Lambert et al. (2020), a foundational paper that coined the term "objective mismatch," and reviewing their respective reference lists. This process yielded 85 initial results.

**Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria**:
The identified articles were screened for relevance based on specific criteria. To be included in the survey, an article had to:
- Specifically address a solution to the objective mismatch problem.
- Propose "decision-aware" objectives for model learning and/or policy optimization.
Articles that solely discussed the objective mismatch problem without offering solutions were excluded. Furthermore, the survey explicitly delineated its scope by excluding related but distinct research areas such as state abstraction, representation learning for control, control-aware dynamics learning, general decision-focused learning, meta-reinforcement learning, Bayesian RL, and active inference, unless they directly contributed to solving the objective mismatch as defined. After abstract and full-text screening, a total of 46 papers were retained for in-depth analysis.

**Taxonomy Development and Analysis**:
The core of the methodology involved synthesizing these 46 papers to identify common themes and structural similarities in their approaches to objective mismatch. This synthesis led to the proposal of a taxonomy comprising four broad categories of "decision-aware" MBRL:
1. **Distribution Correction**: Approaches that adjust for mismatched training data distributions, either due to model-shift (discrepancy between learned and true environment dynamics) or policy-shift (discrepancy between data-collecting and current/optimizing policies).
2. **Control-As-Inference**: Methods that leverage the control-as-inference framework to formulate both model learning and policy optimization as a single probabilistic inference problem, thereby deriving unified objectives.
3. **Value-Equivalence**: Approaches that focus on learning dynamics models that are equivalent to the true environment dynamics specifically in terms of value function estimation, rather than striving for perfect predictive accuracy across all states. This category further divides into Value-Prediction and Robust Control.
4. **Differentiable Planning**: Methods that embed the model-based policy optimization process within a differentiable program, allowing both the policy and the model to be optimized end-to-end towards a shared higher-level objective, often the true environment return.

For each category, the authors detailed:
- **Intuition**: The underlying idea and theoretical basis for addressing objective mismatch.
- **Implementation**: How these intuitions are translated into concrete algorithms, often involving specific objective functions for model learning, policy optimization, or both (e.g., maximum likelihood estimation, importance sampling, variational inference, Bellman error minimization, policy gradient methods). Specific techniques like discriminators for density ratio estimation, ensemble models, and multi-step predictions are discussed.
- **Evaluations**: A summary of empirical results, including the types of environments used (e.g., MuJoCo, Atari, custom driving simulators), baseline algorithms for comparison (e.g., MBPO, SAC, DQN), types of model misspecification explored (e.g., limited capacity, distracting features, low-resolution), and metrics for assessing model exploitation (e.g., Q-estimation accuracy, policy gradient accuracy). The distinction between online and offline RL settings is also highlighted.

The analysis also included the identification of key components and design choices common across algorithms within each category, such as model architecture (e.g., mixture density networks, Gaussian, deterministic), the use of model ensembles, and multi-step prediction capabilities. The relationships and influences between these different categories are illustrated schematically to provide a unified view.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core contribution of this paper is the presentation of a unifying taxonomy for solutions to the objective mismatch problem in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL), along with a detailed analysis of the principles and algorithms within each category.

**A. The Proposed Taxonomy and its Categories:**
The survey synthesized 46 papers into four main categories of decision-aware MBRL approaches:

1. **Distribution Correction (DC)**: These methods address training errors arising from model-shift (discrepancy between learned and true dynamics) or policy-shift (discrepancy between data-collecting and current policies).
 - **Model-Shift**: Exemplified by **DAM (Discriminator Augmented MBRL)**, which uses importance sampling with the learned model as a proposal distribution. It optimizes a return-weighted model training objective and an importance-weighted policy objective, demonstrating improved performance in environments with multimodal dynamics.
 - **Policy-Shift**: Approaches like **PDML (Policy-adapted Dynamics Model Learning)**, **TOM (Transition Occupancy Matching)**, **AMPL (Alternating Model and Policy Learning)**, and **GAMPS (Gradient-Aware Model-based Policy Search)** re-weight model training data. This re-weighting down-weights samples less relevant to or distant from the current policy's state-action distribution. TOM, for instance, minimizes f-divergence between the learned model's and true environment's transition occupancy measures. AMPL, designed for offline RL, weights MLE by the state-action density ratio and adds a divergence penalty to policy optimization. GAMPS focuses on making policy gradient estimates more accurate by up-weighting samples with higher policy-gradient norms.

2. **Control-As-Inference (CAI)**: This framework formulates optimal control as a probabilistic inference problem, where both model learning and policy optimization objectives are derived from a single marginal likelihood lower bound of optimal trajectories.
 - **VMBPO (Variational Model-Based Policy Optimization)**, **MNM (Mismatched-No-More)**, and **ALM (Latent Aligned Model)** are prominent examples. These algorithms often lead to "optimistic" dynamics models that prioritize sampling high-value states, and policies that visit states where the learned dynamics are accurate or informative. MNM, notably, uses a log-transformed reward in its objective, which results in a lower bound on the RL objective, ensuring performance improvement. ALM extends this to visual RL by integrating latent dynamics and an observation encoder, implicitly performing "self-predictive" model learning. Empirical findings often show these methods outperforming standard MBRL and MFRL baselines, with MNM demonstrating robustness to model exploitation through stable value estimates.

3. **Value-Equivalence (VE)**: This category seeks to learn models that are equivalent to the true environment dynamics specifically in terms of value function estimation, rather than requiring full predictive fidelity.
 - **Value-Prediction (VP)**: Algorithms such as **VAML (Value-Aware Model Loss)**, **MML (Minimax Model Learning)**, **MuZero**, **VPN (Value Prediction Network)**, **TD-MPC**, **VE (Value-Equivalence Principle)**, **VaGradM (Value-Gradient Weighted Model Loss)**, and **PAML (Policy-Aware Model Loss)** train the dynamics model to yield accurate Bellman backups or value estimates. VAML and MML use robust formulations against worst-case value functions or density ratios. MuZero and VPN use Monte Carlo Tree Search or multi-step look-ahead for bootstrapping value estimates. TD-MPC combines value, reward, and self-prediction, achieving strong multi-task performance. VaGradM aims to stabilize training by using value gradients. These methods simplify the training pipeline by focusing on task-relevant model properties.
 - **Robust Control (RC)**: This subcategory, including **LAMPS (Lazy Model-based Policy Search)** and **RAMBO (Robust Adversarial Model-based Offline Policy Optimization)**, builds on value-equivalence but explicitly addresses the inherent optimism or pessimism in learned dynamics. LAMPS aims for optimistic dynamics in in-distribution regions and pessimistic dynamics out-of-distribution. RAMBO, for offline RL, emphasizes learning a pessimistic model for out-of-distribution state-actions to prevent policy exploitation when exploration is not possible. These approaches demonstrate superior performance and robustness, especially in offline settings.

4. **Differentiable Planning (DP)**: This approach embeds the model-based policy optimization process as a differentiable program, allowing end-to-end optimization of both the model and the policy with respect to a higher-level objective (e.g., true environment return).
 - **TreeQN**, **OMD (Optimal Model Design)**, and **DCEM (Differentiable Cross Entropy Method)** are examples. TreeQN interprets multi-step look-ahead search as a neural network layer. OMD uses implicit differentiation to optimize a Bellman error objective under the constraint of Bellman optimality in the learned model. DCEM re-parameterizes the policy using a differentiable trajectory optimizer. These methods bypass explicit return estimation and offer a direct way to align objectives, often showing improved performance with fewer environmental samples or robustness to constrained model capacities.

**B. Synthesis and General Principles:**
The survey identifies a unifying principle: **value optimization-equivalence**. This principle suggests that both the dynamics model and the policy should be trained to optimize the expected return in the real environment. This leads to a hybridization of model-free and model-based RL.
- **Error-Awareness vs. Error-Agnosticism**: Solutions either explicitly model simulation errors (DC, CAI) or construct equivalent MDP dynamics implicitly (VE, DP).
- **Value-Awareness vs. Policy-Awareness**: Approaches can be classified by whether a value function is a necessary component for model learning, similar to policy-based vs. value-based RL.
- **Model Properties**: Decision-aware models often deviate from the true environment model, exhibiting optimism in visited states (CAI, LAMPS) or pessimism in unvisited states (RAMBO). This unidentifiability, while initially counter-intuitive, can be advantageous for learning "useful" models that are easier to optimize.
- **Optimization Strategies**: Approaches range from manually designed component objectives (many DC, CAI, VE methods) to end-to-end differentiable programming (DP), which can leverage advanced techniques like implicit differentiation.

**C. Implications for Evaluations**:
The review highlights the need for more rigorous evaluation protocols in MBRL, beyond just final performance and learning speed. Recommended evaluations include:
- Robustness to model misspecification (varying model capacity, adding distractors).
- Metrics for model exploitation (e.g., value-estimation bias, policy gradient accuracy).
- Probing specific agent properties (e.g., exploration behavior, optimism/pessimism of learned dynamics).
- Consideration of implementation ease, training stability, and debugging capabilities due to the inherent complexity of some methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper offers a significant contribution to the field of Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) by providing a structured and unifying perspective on a fundamental challenge: the objective mismatch problem. Its potential impact spans theoretical understanding, algorithmic design, and real-world application.

**Theoretical Significance**:
- **Unified Framework**: The paper's primary theoretical impact is the introduction of a taxonomy that categorizes a diverse array of existing solutions into four coherent classes: Distribution Correction, Control-As-Inference, Value-Equivalence, and Differentiable Planning. This framework provides clarity and structure to a complex and evolving research area, enabling researchers to better understand the relationships between different approaches.
- **Guiding Principle**: The articulation of "value optimization-equivalence" as a unifying principle for decision-aware objective design offers a conceptual anchor for future research. This principle shifts the focus from merely accurate environment prediction to learning models that are directly conducive to optimizing expected return, representing a hybridization of model-free and model-based RL.
- **Understanding Model Properties**: The analysis reveals how decision-aware models can inherently exhibit properties like optimism or pessimism in different regions of the state-action space, and that the "true" environment model may not always be identifiable. This insight challenges traditional views of model fidelity and opens new avenues for theoretical exploration into the properties of "useful" but not necessarily "accurate" models.

**Algorithmic and Practical Impact**:
- **Improved Algorithm Design**: By dissecting the objectives and mechanisms of successful decision-aware MBRL algorithms, the paper provides a blueprint for designing more effective and robust future algorithms. It highlights the trade-offs between error-aware versus error-agnostic approaches, and value-based versus policy-based model learning.
- **Enhanced MBRL Capabilities**: Addressing objective mismatch directly contributes to the core advantages of MBRL: improving sample efficiency, adaptability, and reliability. This is particularly crucial for applications in data-scarce or safety-critical domains where interaction with the real environment is expensive or dangerous.
- **Robustness to Imperfections**: The survey emphasizes how decision-aware methods contribute to robustness against model misspecification and policy exploitation of model errors, which are common pitfalls in MBRL. This can lead to more stable and trustworthy agents.
- **Guidance for Practitioners**: The detailed overview of methodologies and empirical evaluations (summarized in Tables 1 and 2) can assist practitioners in selecting and implementing appropriate MBRL algorithms for their specific tasks, considering factors like model type, online/offline settings, and computational complexity.

**Broader Societal Implications**:
- **Advancing Automated Decision-Making**: By enabling more capable and robust MBRL agents, the work indirectly supports the development of advanced automated decision-making systems in critical domains like autonomous vehicles, healthcare, and robotics. Resolving objective mismatch is key to improving the reliability and performance of these systems.
- **Challenges for Transparency and Explainability**: The paper also highlights a critical broader impact: the potential challenges for transparency and explainability in decision-aware MBRL. If learned models are intentionally biased or unidentifiable to optimize performance, debugging and understanding agent failures become more complex. This underscores the need for continued research into interpretability, debugging tools, and comprehensive evaluation suites to ensure responsible AI development.
- **Future Research Directions**: The paper concludes by recommending future work to deepen the understanding of decision-aware agents, identify their practical utilities (e.g., in transfer learning, multi-task learning), and develop standardized evaluation benchmarks. This foresight helps steer the research community towards impactful and well-evaluated progress in MBRL.
