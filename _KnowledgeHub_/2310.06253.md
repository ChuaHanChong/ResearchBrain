# A Unified View on Solving Objective Mismatch in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A taxonomic survey of solution categories for the "objective mismatch" problem in model-based RL — the observation that WM predictive accuracy often fails to correlate with policy action quality. Organizes the literature into interrelated categories and provides a unified framework for comparing decision-aware MBRL methods. No new algorithm is proposed; the contribution is conceptual clarity for future research.

## Problem

- In MBRL, world models are typically trained for likelihood / predictive accuracy, but downstream reward optimization does not track this metric — models that fit data well can still yield poor policies.
- The community has proposed many disparate fixes (distribution correction, value-equivalence, control-as-inference, differentiable planning) without a unifying framework.
- Without a taxonomy, it is hard to identify which failure modes a given method actually addresses.

## Method

- Survey paper — no new algorithm. Central contribution is a ==taxonomy of decision-aware MBRL== that groups prior work into interrelated solution categories.
- Defines objective mismatch formally and traces its appearance across ==distribution correction==, ==control-as-inference==, ==value-equivalence==, and ==differentiable planning== approaches.
- Identifies shared assumptions and failure modes across categories and contrasts their inductive biases.
- Proposes research directions for hybrid methods that combine the strengths of multiple categories.

## Results

- Delivers a structured taxonomy rather than empirical numbers.
- Identifies common patterns across otherwise disparate lines of work.
- Provides a conceptual basis for future decision-aware MBRL research and for evaluating claimed contributions against a unified framework.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Objective mismatch is not a single problem — it manifests differently across method families.
> - Distribution correction, value-equivalence, control-as-inference, and differentiable planning are four corners of a shared design space.
> - Future progress likely comes from hybrid approaches that target *specific* failure modes within the taxonomy rather than a single monolithic fix.
