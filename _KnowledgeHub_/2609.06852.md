# ContextFlow: In-Context Flow Matching for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ContextFlow casts in-context imitation learning as conditional flow matching: a context expert encodes demonstrations plus the current observation, while an action expert denoises continuous action chunks conditioned on them. Perceiver-style compressors distill demonstration images, proprioception, and actions into fixed-size latents. On LIBERO unseen configurations it reaches 73.5% success, beating autoregressive ICRT by 35 points and matching fine-tuned pi-0 without any fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Autoregressive in-context imitation policies predict actions token by token, so early errors compound over long rollouts, especially under distribution shift to unseen task configurations.
- Those policies depend on action tokenizers that discretize continuous control, injecting reconstruction error that further degrades execution accuracy on fine-grained manipulation.
- Flow-matching policies handle continuous control but lack an established recipe for conditioning on long multimodal demonstrations; text-prompted VLA models generalize poorly to new configurations.

## Method

- **ContextFlow-Plain**: a ==mixture-of-experts== base where a context expert encodes demonstrations and current observation, and an action expert refines noisy action chunks through ten ==Euler flow-matching steps==.
- **Multimodal context compressors**: three perceiver-style modules for image, state, and action streams, each distilling subsampled demonstrations into 32 latent query tokens via alternating cross- and self-attention.
- ==Block-wise causal attention== lets action tokens attend to context but not the reverse, enabling context caching across flow steps; training uses a ==conditional flow-matching loss== with Beta-sampled timesteps.

## Results

- On LIBERO unseen configurations ContextFlow averages **73.5%** success versus **38.5%** for ICRT, **53.5%** for the stronger ContextAR baseline, and **72.5%** for one-shot fine-tuned pi-0.
- Ablations: compressors add **9.5 points** over ContextFlow-Plain; dropping proprioceptive state and action context costs **44.5 points**; flow matching beats MLP-L1 regression (**58%**) and diffusion (**33.5%**) heads.
- Cumulative rollout MSE falls from **4.7** to **3.0** via flow matching and to **2.2** with compression; real ALOHA bimanual configurations reach **4-6/10** trials versus ICRT's **0/10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parallel continuous action-chunk prediction, not merely better tokenization, is what curbs compounding error once evaluation drifts off the training task distribution.
> - Demonstrations as prompts beat text instructions for configuration-level generalization, and proprioceptive plus action streams carry far more signal than demonstration images alone.
> - Learned query compression of demonstrations preserves task-relevant detail while cutting attention cost, but generalization stays within shared primitives, not fundamentally new task types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges in applying in-context learning (ICL) to robotics, a paradigm that has shown effectiveness in vision and language domains. In-context learning allows a model to adapt to new tasks by conditioning on a small set of input-output examples at test time, without requiring gradient updates or fine-tuning. This approach offers a potential alternative to traditional imitation learning methods that often necessitate extensive fine-tuning for new tasks or configurations.

Existing in-context imitation learning methods in robotics frequently employ autoregressive models (ARMs) adapted from large language models (LLMs). These ARMs typically discretize continuous actions into tokens and predict actions sequentially. This strategy introduces two main limitations:
1. **Compounding Errors**: Sequential prediction can lead to an accumulation of errors, where an early mistake propagates and magnifies over time, particularly under distribution shifts (e.g., unseen task configurations).
2. **Action Discretization**: Discretizing continuous actions introduces quantization errors, potentially reducing precision and exacerbating execution failures.

In parallel, the broader imitation learning literature has explored continuous action modeling techniques such as action chunking and flow matching. Flow matching, in particular, operates directly in continuous action space and can predict chunks of actions in parallel, which helps mitigate compounding errors. However, the integration of in-context learning within a flow-matching framework for robot manipulation has remained largely unexplored. This paper aims to bridge this gap by developing a conditional flow-matching model for in-context imitation learning in robotics, thereby attempting to leverage the benefits of continuous action prediction within an ICL paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of ContextFlow are:

- **Mitigate Compounding Errors**: Existing autoregressive in-context imitation learning models suffer from compounding errors, especially when exposed to unseen task configurations where distribution shifts are more pronounced. The sequential, token-by-token prediction of actions allows early errors to propagate, leading to performance degradation. ContextFlow aims to alleviate this by predicting continuous action chunks in parallel, thereby reducing the opportunity for error accumulation.
- **Address Action Discretization Limitations**: Many autoregressive policies rely on action tokenizers that discretize continuous actions. This discretization introduces reconstruction errors and can limit the precision required for robust robot control. A key motivation for ContextFlow is to operate directly in continuous action space using a flow-matching framework, thus avoiding discretization and its associated drawbacks.
- **Enable Robust Generalization to Unseen Task Configurations**: The goal is to develop an in-context learning policy that can generalize effectively to novel object arrangements, appearances, or spatial configurations within a task family, without requiring fine-tuning on these new tasks. This is a critical capability for deploying robots in dynamic, real-world environments.
- **Efficiently Encode Multimodal In-Context Demonstrations**: Robot manipulation tasks involve rich multimodal data, including visual observations (camera images), proprioceptive states (joint angles, gripper states), and action sequences. Processing long sequences of these high-dimensional modalities with traditional attention mechanisms can be computationally expensive and inefficient due to spatial and temporal redundancy. ContextFlow is motivated to address this by introducing efficient context compression techniques.
- **Build upon Existing VLA Models**: The work builds on the architectural principles of vision-language-action (VLA) flow models, specifically $\pi^0$, to extend their capabilities to multimodal in-context imitation learning. This leverages established continuous control techniques while adapting them for ICL.
- **Demonstrate Real-World Applicability**: The research aims to evaluate the proposed method not only in simulation but also on real-world robotic platforms, including complex bimanual tasks requiring high-frequency control and closed-loop feedback, a setting often underexplored by prior in-context learning work.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ContextFlow is designed as a conditional flow-matching model that learns continuous action distributions for in-context imitation learning. The methodology can be broken down into problem formulation and model architecture.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

- **Learning Objective**: The model aims to learn the conditional distribution $\pi(a_t | o_t, d)$, where $a_t = a_{t:t+H-1}$ represents a chunk of $H$ future actions, $o_t = (I_t, q_t)$ is the current observation (image $I_t$ and proprioceptive state $q_t$), and $d = [T, X, Q, A]$ is a provided in-context demonstration (task instruction $T$, image sequence $X$, proprioceptive states $Q$, and actions $A$).
- **Conditional Flow Matching (CFM)**: Instead of autoregressive token prediction, ContextFlow utilizes the Flow Matching framework. CFM learns a deterministic, time-dependent vector field that smoothly transports samples from a standard Gaussian prior (at time $s=0$) to the target conditional action distribution (at $s=1$). This allows for direct modeling in continuous action spaces.
 - **Intermediate Distributions**: A family of intermediate distributions $p_s(a_s_t | a_t) = \mathcal{N}(a_s_t | s a_t, (1-s)^2 I)$ is constructed, interpolating between noise and the clean action chunk.
 - **Conditional Velocity Field**: For linear interpolation, the conditional velocity field is $u(a_s_t | a_t) = a_t - \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is the noise. This field represents the direct displacement from a noisy action chunk to a clean one.
 - **Neural Network Parameterization**: A neural network $u_\theta(a_s_t, o_t, d, s)$ is parameterized to approximate this velocity field, conditioned on the current noisy action chunk, observation, demonstration, and interpolation time $s$.
 - **Loss Function**: The model is trained by minimizing the Conditional Flow Matching loss: $L_{CFM}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s, a_t, \epsilon} \|u_\theta(a_s_t, o_t, d, s) - u(a_s_t | a_t)\|^2$, where $s$ is sampled from a Beta distribution and $a_t$ from the target policy.
- **Inference**: During inference, action sequences are generated by integrating the learned velocity field from $s=0$ to $s=1$ using a simple forward Euler solver, starting from a noisy action chunk $a_0_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture

ContextFlow employs a mixture-of-experts design with two main components: a context expert and an action expert, augmented by multimodal context compressors.

- **Multimodal Context Compressors**: To handle the high-dimensionality and temporal redundancy of multimodal demonstrations, Perceiver-style architectures are adapted.
 - **Temporal Subsampling**: Raw demonstration sequences (images, proprioception, actions) are first temporally subsampled.
 - **Perceiver-Style Compression**: For each modality (images, proprioceptive states, actions), a dedicated compressor ($C_{img}$, $C_{state}$, $C_{action}$) distills the input token sequences into a compact, fixed-size set of latent context tokens. These compressors use alternating cross-attention and self-attention layers with learnable query tokens to extract task-relevant information. Weights for the image compressor are shared across camera views.
 - **Unified Context Representation**: The output latent token sets from all compressors are concatenated to form a unified context representation, $T_{context}$.
- **Context Expert**: This expert receives the current observation (images encoded by a Vision Transformer, task instruction tokenized by a text tokenizer), and the compressed demonstration context ($T_{context}$). It processes and encodes this combined information into a set of context tokens. During training, it receives demonstrations from the same task; at inference, it processes demonstrations from a novel task.
- **Action Expert**: This expert takes the current proprioceptive state of the robot arm and initial noisy action tokens as input. It then attends to the context tokens produced by the context expert.
 - **Flow Matching Steps**: The action expert refines the noisy action tokens through $K$ flow-matching steps. Each step involves attention interactions between the noisy action tokens and the context expert tokens.
 - **Causal Attention**: A block-wise causal attention mechanism is implemented, allowing action tokens to attend to context tokens, but not vice-versa. This design enables caching of context tokens during inference, preventing recomputation at each flow matching step and enhancing efficiency.
 - **Action Prediction**: The final output after $K$ flow-matching steps is a clean, continuous action chunk of horizon $H$.

#### 3.3. Training and Evaluation Details

- **Vision Encoder**: SigLIP is used as the vision encoder, processing images at 224x224 resolution.
- **Transformer Architecture**: Both experts use Gemma-style transformers with multi-query attention, depth 18, and MLP dimension 4096.
- **Initialization**: SigLIP and the action expert are initialized from $\pi^0$ weights; the context expert is randomly initialized. LoRA is used for fine-tuning the action expert.
- **Data Sampling**: For in-context demonstrations, 8 image frames, 128 proprioceptive states, and 128 actions are sampled. Compressors use 32 learnable query tokens per modality.
- **Environments**: Evaluations are performed on the LIBERO simulation benchmark (spatial reasoning, object manipulation, goal-conditioned, and long-horizon tasks) and a newly collected ALOHA real-world robot dataset (single-arm and bimanual manipulation).
- **Baselines**: Comparisons are made against autoregressive in-context imitation learning methods (ICRT, ContextAR), and large VLA models ($\pi^0$, OpenVLA-OFT), including a version of $\pi^0$ fine-tuned with one demonstration ($\pi^0$ FT).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Benchmark (LIBERO)

- **Autoregressive vs. Flow-Matching Models**: ContextFlow-Plain (flow matching without context compressors) outperformed ContextAR (autoregressive baseline using the same $\pi^0$ pre-training and vision backbone) by 10.5 percentage points in average success rate on unseen task configurations (64.0% vs. 53.5%). This supports the hypothesis that flow matching is beneficial for in-context imitation learning, particularly in mitigating compounding errors under distribution shift. ContextFlow (with compressors) further improved this gap to 20 percentage points (73.5% vs. 53.5%).
- **Text vs. In-Context Demonstrations**: In-context imitation learning models generally performed better than VLA models relying solely on text prompts. ContextAR surpassed OpenVLA-OFT by 13.5 percentage points, indicating that detailed multimodal demonstrations are more effective than text alone for generalization to unseen tasks.
- **Parametric Fine-tuning vs. In-Context Learning**: ContextFlow achieved a comparable average success rate (73.5%) to $\pi^0$ (FT) (72.5%), which was fine-tuned with one demonstration per unseen task. This highlights that ContextFlow can achieve similar performance without the computational cost or potential catastrophic forgetting associated with task-specific fine-tuning.
- **Effectiveness of Context Compressors**: ContextFlow, incorporating multimodal context compressors, outperformed ContextFlow-Plain by 9.5 percentage points in average success rate (73.5% vs. 64.0%). Ablation studies showed that enabling only the image compressor improved success rate by 4 points, and activating both image and state/action compressors led to further gains, suggesting the importance of jointly compressing multimodal context.
- **Influence of Context Modalities**: All three modalities (text instruction, images, proprioceptive states/actions) were found to be important, with a greater impact on unseen task configurations. Removing proprioceptive states and actions resulted in the largest performance drop (44.5 points on average for unseen configurations), indicating their criticality.
- **Hyperparameter Analysis**: Increasing the number of sampled image frames from 4 to 8 improved average success rate by 3 points. For states/actions, 128 was identified as the optimal number.
- **Comparison with Other Action Heads**: Flow matching achieved the highest average success rate (73.5%) among continuous parallel action heads, outperforming MLP L1 regression (58.0%) and diffusion (33.5% after 100k iterations).
- **Expanded Training Data**: Training ContextFlow with additional diverse data (LIBERO-90) improved generalization on previously challenging LIBERO-Goal and LIBERO-10 tasks, raising the overall average success rate from 36.8% to 43.0%.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments (ALOHA)

- ContextFlow demonstrated reasonable performance on unseen configurations of both single-arm (e.g., 5/10 on orange pick-and-place) and fine-grained bimanual tasks (e.g., 4/10 on pen uncap, 6/10 on put red egg in box).
- ICRT, a representative autoregressive baseline, struggled significantly in real-world settings, achieving only 2/10 successes on one single-arm task and 0/10 on all bimanual tasks.
- $\pi^0$ achieved some success on single-arm tasks and the "put red egg in box" task, but failed entirely on the more complex "uncap red pen" bimanual task, where ContextFlow achieved 4/10 success. These results suggest ContextFlow's ability to leverage in-context demonstrations for unseen real-world manipulation.

#### 4.3. Analysis

- **Compounding Error Quantification**: Mean Squared Error (MSE) analysis showed that ContextAR's error grew noticeably over time beyond 30 steps on unseen configurations, while ContextFlow exhibited lower MSE accumulation, particularly after the initial phase of the task. ContextFlow reduced MSE accumulation by 1.7 compared to ContextAR, and context compression further reduced it by 0.8.
- **Failure Mode Analysis**: ICRT frequently failed during the pick-up stage due to misidentification or grasping failures. ContextFlow's failures were more evenly distributed across stages (pick-up misidentification, grasp failure, place failure), with more examples observed in the placing stage.
- **Attention in Image Compressor**: Visualization of the image compressor's attention indicated that learnable queries attended to task-relevant regions, with attention patterns changing dynamically with the task phase (e.g., focusing on the gripper before grasp, then on the object, then on the target placement location).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ContextFlow represents an advancement in in-context imitation learning for robot manipulation by integrating a conditional flow-matching framework with multimodal context compression.

The significance of this work includes:
- **Addressing Fundamental Limitations**: By moving from discrete, sequential action prediction to continuous, parallel action chunk prediction via flow matching, ContextFlow directly addresses the issues of compounding errors and action discretization, which are critical for robust robotic control. This is particularly impactful for generalizing to unseen task configurations where distribution shifts are common.
- **Improved Generalization without Fine-tuning**: The ability of ContextFlow to achieve performance comparable to task-specific fine-tuned VLA models, but without requiring any gradient updates on unseen tasks, significantly improves adaptability and reduces deployment overhead. This facilitates faster adaptation in dynamic environments.
- **Efficient Multimodal Processing**: The introduction of Perceiver-style multimodal context compressors enables efficient encoding of long, high-dimensional demonstrations. This mechanism distills task-relevant information into compact representations, making the in-context learning process more scalable and computationally feasible.
- **Real-World Validation**: The successful demonstration on diverse real-world ALOHA tasks, including complex bimanual manipulation, indicates the practical applicability and robustness of ContextFlow beyond simulation. This suggests a step towards more capable and adaptable real-world robotic systems.

Potential impacts include:
- **Accelerated Robot Deployment**: Enabling robots to quickly adapt to novel objects or spatial arrangements from a few demonstrations, without costly fine-tuning, could accelerate the deployment of robotic systems in various industrial and service applications.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction**: More intuitive and efficient robot learning from human demonstrations could lead to improved human-robot collaboration and programming by demonstration.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The findings regarding the benefits of flow-matching for in-context learning and the efficacy of context compression provide a foundation for further architectural and algorithmic exploration in robot learning. Future work might explore hybrid autoregressive-flow training schemes or even broader generalization to fundamentally different task types.

However, the authors note that the generalization demonstrated in this work is primarily limited to unseen objects and spatial arrangements within tasks that share similar underlying primitives, rather than to fundamentally different task types. Scaling up the diversity and quantity of training data is suggested as a path to improve generalization to broader task distributions.
