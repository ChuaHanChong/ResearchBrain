# Closed-Loop Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning for Deformable Microfiber Shape Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A closed-loop sim-to-real reinforcement learning approach enables autonomous shape control of deformable microfibers on a surface. It shows that a policy trained in a simplified, frictionless simulation transfers successfully to a physical system, achieving sub-millimeter shape control accuracy across various fiber geometries and initial configurations by using real-time visual feedback for continuous correction.

## Problem

- Micromanipulation of deformable objects is hindered by the dominance of unmodeled, history-dependent microscale surface and interfacial interactions (e.g., adhesion, friction).
- Accurate simulation of complex microscale physics is challenging, limiting the effectiveness of sim-to-real transfer for training autonomous, contact-rich robotic control policies.
- Current micromanipulation systems for deformable objects often rely on teleoperation or task-specific heuristic controllers due to difficulties in achieving high levels of autonomy.

## Method

- A ==reinforcement learning== policy is trained in a simplified MuJoCo simulation environment where surface friction is deliberately omitted to focus on learning geometric shaping skills.
- The trained policy is directly deployed on a physical dual robotic microgripper system without retraining or fine-tuning, relying on real-time visual feedback from camera images for iterative shape correction.
- The task is formulated as a partially observed Markov Decision Process, with the state incorporating gripper positions, fiber centerline, target shape, and previous actions, and a reward function penalizing shape error, fiber tension, and action roughness.

## Results

- The transferred policy achieved consistent real-world shape regulation, with a mean point-wise error of 270 ± 80 µm and a maximum error of 390 ± 100 µm for 50 µm diameter silk microfibers.
- The policy generalized across diverse initial configurations and to different fiber specimens (50, 80, 120 µm diameters and 10, 15, 20 mm manipulated lengths), maintaining median maximum shape error below 500 µm.
- Successful control was demonstrated even for target geometries more complex (higher bending energy) than those present in the training data, indicating robust learned skills adaptable to new configurations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Closed-loop control with real-time visual feedback can effectively compensate for significant sim-to-real mismatch arising from deliberately simplified physical models (e.g., frictionless simulation) in microscale manipulation.
> - Decoupling the learning of geometric shaping skills from the requirement for an accurate microscale contact model simplifies the development of autonomous controllers for deformable micro-objects.
> - Unmodeled microscale contact dynamics can be treated as short-impact disturbances that are correctable within a reactive, closed-loop feedback system without needing explicit modeling or long-term history tracking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic micromanipulation is a field focused on the precise handling of micrometer-scale objects, with applications in areas such as biological cell studies, minimally invasive surgery, micro-assembly, and fiber-optic alignment. Despite advancements in micromanipulation systems and control strategies, achieving high levels of autonomy remains a challenge, with many contact-based systems still relying on teleoperation or task-specific heuristic controllers.

A primary difficulty in micromanipulation stems from the governing physics at the microscale, where surface and interfacial interactions—including adhesion (capillary, van der Waals, electrostatic forces) and distributed frictional contact—become dominant over gravity and inertia. These interactions are often history-dependent and difficult to model accurately, which complicates the design of robust and generalizable control policies.

In contrast, at the macroscale, reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved notable successes in autonomous robotic control for complex, contact-rich tasks like in-hand manipulation and drone racing. A key enabler for these successes has been sim-to-real transfer, where RL agents are trained in simulation and then deployed on physical systems, circumventing the logistical difficulties and costs associated with real-world data collection.

Extending the sim-to-real paradigm to the microscale is an active area of research but presents significant challenges due to the aforementioned difficulties in accurately simulating microscale interactions. Previous works have explored sim-to-real RL for contact micromanipulation, such as automatic cell rotation and posture adjustment of zebrafish embryonic cells, often evaluating methods primarily in simulation. Other related studies have focused on slightly larger scales, including millimeter-scale rigid insertion and deformable rope cutting. These studies indicate that sim-to-real RL for contact-rich robotic micromanipulation, particularly involving deformable objects, remains largely underexplored.

This work contributes to addressing this gap by investigating a closed-loop sim-to-real reinforcement learning approach specifically for deformable microfiber shape control. It positions itself by proposing a method to circumvent the challenge of accurate microscale contact modeling through the strategic use of simplified simulation training combined with real-time visual feedback for correction during physical deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop an approach for autonomous contact-based micromanipulation of deformable objects, specifically focusing on microfiber shape control on a surface.

The primary motivations for this work stem from several challenges inherent in micromanipulation:
- **Difficulty in Modeling Microscale Physics:** At the microscale, surface and interfacial interactions (e.g., adhesion, friction) are dominant, history-dependent, and exceptionally difficult to model accurately. This complexity limits the effectiveness of conventional model-based control strategies and hinders the development of reliable simulation environments for training learning-based controllers.
- **Limitations of Current Autonomy:** The inability to accurately model these interactions results in micromanipulation systems frequently relying on teleoperation or ad-hoc, task-specific controllers, thereby restricting the level of achievable autonomy and generalizability.
- **Challenges of Sim-to-Real Transfer at Microscale:** While sim-to-real transfer has proven effective in macroscale robotics for training RL agents, applying this paradigm to the microscale is significantly harder because the critical dominant interactions are difficult to capture faithfully in simulation. A direct transfer of policies trained in physically inaccurate simulators often leads to poor real-world performance.
- **Underexplored Deformable Manipulation:** Existing sim-to-real RL research in micromanipulation has primarily focused on rigid objects or has not fully demonstrated real-world transfer for deformable materials, leaving a gap in addressing the manipulation of compliant micro-objects.

To address these motivations, the key objective is to propose and validate a novel closed-loop sim-to-real reinforcement learning approach. The central idea is to decouple the learning of geometric shaping skills from the requirement for an accurate microscale contact model. The authors hypothesize that a control policy trained in a deliberately simplified, frictionless simulator can be effectively transferred to a physical system if real-time visual feedback is utilized to iteratively correct for the effects of unmodeled surface interactions. This approach seeks to exploit the robustness of closed-loop feedback control to compensate for the inherent sim-to-real mismatch caused by simplified physics modeling.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method involves training a control policy in a simplified, frictionless simulation environment and then directly transferring it to a physical micromanipulation system, relying on real-time visual feedback for iterative shape correction.

**A. Problem Formulation**
Microfiber shaping serves as the testbed. A deformable fiber of length $l$ and diameter $d$ rests on a planar surface and is held at both endpoints by two robotic grippers (L and R). Gripper positions $(x_{L,t}, x_{R,t})$ in the $xy$-plane are the sole controllable inputs. The $z$-axis is fixed to maintain surface contact. The fiber's configuration at time $t$ is described by its planar centerline $\mathcal{C}_t$, represented as $N=20$ uniformly sampled discrete points. The objective is to learn a policy $\pi: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ that maps an observed system state $s_t$ to gripper velocity commands $a_t$ to guide $\mathcal{C}_t$ to a target shape $\mathcal{C}^*$. Success is defined as the point-wise shape error falling below a tolerance $\epsilon_{tol}$ within a fixed time horizon.

**B. Simulation Environment**
The simulation environment is implemented in MuJoCo. It is designed to be simplified to facilitate the learning of geometric shape regulation under stable conditions, deliberately omitting complex microscale contact physics.
- **Fiber Model:** The microfiber is modeled as an articulated chain of $N=20$ rigid cylindrical segments connected by ball joints. Bending compliance is introduced by tuning joint stiffness and damping to approximate real microfiber mechanics.
- **Scaling and Gravity:** A dimensionless geometric scaling factor of 100 is applied, segment masses are kept small for stability, and gravity is disabled.
- **Grippers:** Each gripper is modeled as a rigid body with independent prismatic joints. Fiber endpoints are attached to grippers via equality weld constraints.
- **Friction Omission:** Crucially, surface friction is explicitly set to zero during training. This decision is based on the difficulty of accurately modeling microscale fiber-surface friction, which is coupled with adhesion and depends on various environmental and contact conditions. A simplified or deterministic friction model could introduce non-transferable simulator-specific behaviors. The method proposes to compensate for this omission through closed-loop visual feedback during physical deployment.

**C. Reinforcement Learning Formulation**
The task is formulated as a partially observed Markov Decision Process (MDP) for reactive closed-loop control.
- **State Space ($\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^{52}$):** The agent's input $s_t$ at each timestep includes:
 - Current planar positions of grippers ($\boldsymbol{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^4$).
 - Current planar velocities of grippers ($\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}_t \in \mathbb{R}^4$).
 - Current fiber centerline ($\mathcal{C}_t$, represented by 10 planar points $\in \mathbb{R}^{20}$).
 - Target fiber centerline ($\mathcal{C}^*$, represented by 10 planar points $\in \mathbb{R}^{20}$).
 - Previous action ($\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^4$).
 This results in a 52-dimensional state vector.
- **Action Space ($\mathcal{A} = [-1,1]^4$):** The agent outputs continuous planar velocity commands for both grippers: $\boldsymbol{a}_t = [\dot{x}_L, \dot{y}_L, \dot{x}_R, \dot{y}_R] \in \mathbb{R}^4$. These are normalized and scaled at execution.
- **Reward Function ($R: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$):** The reward function combines four components:
 - **Shape Error Penalty:** $-w_{er} \epsilon_t$, where $\epsilon_t = \sqrt{e^2_{mean,t} + e^2_{max,t}}/2$ combines mean and maximum pointwise deviations from the target.
 - **Tension Penalty:** $-w_{te} P_{te,t}$, activated if gripper separation $d_{grip,t}$ exceeds a safe elongation limit $l_{max} = \rho l$.
 - **Smoothness Penalty:** $-w_{sm} P_{sm,t}$, based on the L2 norm of the difference between current and previous actions ($\| \boldsymbol{a}_t - \boldsymbol{a}_{t-1} \|_2$).
 - **Success Bonus:** $B_t$, a positive reward awarded when $\epsilon_t < \epsilon_{thr}$ and held for 2 seconds.
- **Policy and Training:**
 - The policy $\pi_\theta$ is a feedforward neural network with three hidden layers (128, 128, 64 neurons) and ReLU activations.
 - Training uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in Stable-Baselines3 v2.7 with 30 parallel environments.
 - Training data consists of 6037 geometrically valid fiber configurations. Initial and target shapes are sampled independently from this dataset.
 - A curriculum learning strategy is employed for the success threshold $\epsilon_{thr}$, progressively tightening it from 1.2 mm to 0.01 mm over 13 levels, adjusted based on a 50-episode success rate. Each episode is capped at 8 seconds.

**D. Sim-to-Real Transfer via Closed-Loop Control**
After training, the policy is directly deployed on the physical dual robotic microgripper system without any retraining or fine-tuning.
- **Control Loop:** At each step of the 40 Hz control loop, the real-time image-derived fiber centerline $\mathcal{C}_t$ is combined with the gripper state, target shape $\mathcal{C}^*$, and previous action $\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1}$ to form the policy input $s_t$. This input is normalized using statistics collected during training.
- **Core Assumption:** The methodological assumption is that the unmodeled contact dynamics (e.g., friction) have a sufficiently short impact horizon, allowing their effects to be corrected through repeated feedback updates. The policy reacts to observed geometric deviations without needing to predict or reconstruct a full hidden contact state or long contact histories.

**E. Fiber Centerline Estimation**
- **In Simulation:** $\mathcal{C}_t$ is directly obtained from the MuJoCo state.
- **In Physical System:** $\mathcal{C}_t$ is estimated from camera images. A top-view image is processed (cropped, grayscaled, smoothed, Canny edge detection, resampled along arc length) and transformed to the global coordinate frame using a pre-calibrated homography. This image-derived $\mathcal{C}_t$ is used for state vector construction and error computation. The authors acknowledge that this introduces an observation mismatch due to detection noise and calibration error compared to noise-free simulated states.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides quantitative and qualitative results demonstrating the effectiveness and generalization capabilities of the closed-loop sim-to-real RL approach.

**A. Fiber Shape Control Demonstration**
A representative real-world shaping trial with a 15 mm-long, 120 μm-diameter silk microfiber illustrated the policy's closed-loop behavior. Starting from a configuration with a large shape discrepancy, the transferred policy progressively reduced the error between the observed centerline and the target. The mean shape error ($e_{mean}$) and maximum shape error ($e_{max}$) decreased during an initial correction phase, stabilizing after approximately 18 seconds to final values of $e_{mean} \approx 160$ μm and $e_{max} \approx 320$ μm. This demonstrated the controller's ability to achieve real-world shape regulation under surface contact, despite the simulation-to-real mismatch visible when comparing the real fiber shape to the simulated shape for the same gripper positions.

**B. Repeatability Across Initial Configurations**
The policy's repeatability was evaluated using a 15 mm-long, 50 μm-diameter silk microfiber. Twenty-four trials were conducted, covering three distinct target shapes and eight different initial configurations for each target.
- The final fiber shapes consistently converged towards the prescribed targets.
- The policy achieved a mean point-wise error of $e_{mean} = 270 \pm 80$ μm.
- The maximum error was $e_{max} = 390 \pm 100$ μm.
- This performance indicates that the reactive-regulation strategy, relying on currently observed shape deviation, is sufficient to achieve consistent shaping outcomes across diverse starting conditions.
- The observed real-world error (270 ± 80 μm) was higher than the simulation error (below 100 μm), which is consistent with the expected sim-to-real mismatch, including observation noise and unmodeled physical interactions.

**C. Generalization Across Fiber Specimens**
To assess generalization, the same trained policy was applied without retraining or tuning to nine different silk fiber specimens, spanning three diameters (50, 80, and 120 μm) and three manipulated lengths (10, 15, and 20 mm). The 15 mm length corresponded to the training condition. For each specimen, 20 trials were performed (five target shapes, each serving as a target from four different initial configurations), totaling 180 trials.
- Across all nine conditions, the median maximum shape error ($e_{max}$) remained below 500 μm, indicating successful policy transfer across the tested range of specimen geometries.
- **Effect of diameter:** 120 μm fibers achieved the lowest median $e_{max}$ (251 μm), while 80 μm fibers produced the largest (414 μm), and 50 μm fibers showed intermediate performance (322 μm). This suggests a systematic influence of fiber diameter on shaping accuracy, possibly due to a changing balance between fiber elasticity and surface interaction.
- **Effect of length:** The 15 mm training condition yielded the lowest median $e_{max}$ (202 μm). Fibers with lengths of 10 mm and 20 mm, farther from the training distribution, showed higher median errors of 373 μm and 334 μm, respectively.

**D. Effect of Target Geometry**
The effect of target geometry on shaping accuracy was characterized using bending energy ($E_{bend}$), defined as the arc-length-averaged integral of squared curvature.
- A positive correlation was observed between the mean shaping error ($e_{mean}$) and $E_{bend}$ across all diameter groups. This suggests that geometrically more complex targets (higher $E_{bend}$) are generally more challenging to control accurately.
- Analysis of $E_{bend}$ distributions showed that the experimental targets occupied a higher bending-energy range compared to the shapes used in the simulation training set. This indicates that the policy demonstrated generalization to geometrically more complex configurations than those it was explicitly trained on.
- The feedback loop maintained stability even for the most complex targets, with the mean $e_{max}$ error remaining below 500 μm across all tested fibers. The larger residual errors for high-$E_{bend}$ targets imply increased difficulty in precise shape correction for more curved or multi-inflectional geometries.

**E. RL Model Training Performance (Appendix)**
Training performance in the frictionless MuJoCo environment over 20 million timesteps demonstrated stable convergence. The mean episode reward increased rapidly and stabilized after approximately 2 million timesteps. Concurrently, the simulation shape error decreased sharply to values below 0.1 mm, confirming that the agent successfully learned the geometric shaping task in the simplified simulator.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic micromanipulation, particularly for deformable objects and sim-to-real transfer.

- **Validation of Closed-Loop Sim-to-Real RL:** The study effectively demonstrates that a policy trained in a deliberately simplified, frictionless simulator can be transferred directly to a complex physical micromanipulation system. This is significant because it provides a method to overcome the inherent challenge of accurately modeling microscale surface and interfacial interactions, which often prevents successful sim-to-real transfer.
- **Addressing Microscale Modeling Challenges:** By relying on real-time visual feedback to iteratively correct for unmodeled dynamics, the approach offers a practical solution for tasks where high-fidelity physical modeling is prohibitive or impossible. This bypasses the need for extensive system identification or complex probabilistic contact models, reducing development effort and increasing feasibility.
- **Advancing Autonomous Micromanipulation of Deformable Objects:** The work advances the capability for autonomous manipulation of deformable microfibers, a challenging class of objects. It offers a pathway towards reducing the reliance on teleoperation or heuristic controllers, potentially enabling more sophisticated automated processes in micro-assembly, micro-surgery, and biological studies.
- **Demonstrated Generalizability:** The policy's ability to achieve sub-millimeter shape control accuracy across diverse initial configurations and generalize to a range of fiber diameters and manipulated lengths without retraining or tuning is a key finding. Furthermore, its performance on target geometries more complex than those encountered during training suggests a robust learned skill.
- **Implications for Sim-to-Real Research:** This study reinforces the principle that closed-loop policies can tolerate significant model mismatch between simulation and reality, provided that the task-relevant effects of this mismatch are observable and correctable within the feedback loop. This insight is valuable for the broader sim-to-real community, especially for contact-rich tasks where perfect simulation fidelity is unattainable.

The findings establish a foundation for future research. Potential impacts include the development of faster micromanipulation systems, more adaptive controllers for materials with time-dependent properties (e.g., viscoelasticity), and systems capable of operating reliably across varying contact surface conditions, all by extending the feedback-driven robustness demonstrated here.
