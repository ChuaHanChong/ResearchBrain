# Safe Continual Domain Adaptation after Sim2Real Transfer of Reinforcement Learning Policies in Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA), enables reinforcement learning policies to safely and continuously adapt to evolving real-world conditions after sim2real transfer. It achieves this by integrating safe reinforcement learning and continual learning, demonstrating improved performance and safety on robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation often struggle with the "reality gap," leading to suboptimal or unsafe performance when deployed on physical robots.
- Existing sim2real approaches typically result in fixed policies that cannot adapt to the inevitable changes in real-world system parameters or environmental conditions over time.
- Direct online adaptation of RL policies in real-world robotics is challenging due to sample inefficiency, instability, and high safety risks associated with exploratory actions.

## Method

- The Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA) framework combines domain randomization for initial policy robustness, Policy Constrained Reward and Cost Policy Optimization (PCRPO) for safety, and Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) for continual adaptation.
- A pretraining phase in randomized simulation uses PCRPO to learn a general, safe policy, from which the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) is computed to identify important parameters.
- During real-world adaptation, the pre-trained policy is fine-tuned using PCRPO with an EWC-based regularization term, penalizing changes to important parameters, and operates under stricter safety limits.

## Results

- SCDA consistently minimized safety violations during real-world adaptation, often achieving zero costs, unlike reward-only adaptation strategies that led to unsafe behaviors.
- The framework was the only strategy that improved overall performance across an entire domain of multiple targets, effectively preventing catastrophic forgetting of the general policy.
- On a real robotic grasping task, SCDA increased the grasp success rate from 20% (zero-shot transfer) to 60% while maintaining zero safety violations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating safe reinforcement learning (PCRPO) with continual learning (EWC) enables policies to adapt robustly to dynamic real-world conditions while preventing catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired generalized knowledge.
> - Applying stricter safety limits during real-world adaptation is crucial for ensuring the safe deployment of reinforcement learning policies on physical robotic systems.
> - Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) effectively leverages Fisher information to selectively preserve critical policy parameters from pretraining, balancing specialized adaptation with maintaining generalized capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) has shown immense promise for training complex robotic behaviors. However, a persistent challenge is the "reality gap"—the discrepancy between simulated environments, where RL policies are typically trained due to sample efficiency and safety, and the real world, where these policies must ultimately operate. This paper directly addresses this critical issue and extends beyond it to tackle the even more complex problem of *long-term, adaptive, and safe deployment* of RL policies in dynamic real-world scenarios.

Traditionally, two primary approaches have been used to bridge the sim2real gap:
- **Exact Simulation:** Developing highly precise simulators that meticulously replicate real-world physics and parameters. While offering high fidelity, this approach is often infeasible due to the complexity of real-world phenomena, computational cost, and the fact that real-world parameters can change over time (e.g., wear and tear, temperature variations), rendering a static "exact" simulation obsolete.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Training RL policies in simulators where various physical properties, textures, or lighting conditions are randomized within a wide range. This encourages the policy to learn robust behaviors that generalize across variations, making it more transferable to the real world, which can be seen as one instance within the randomized domain. However, a major drawback is that widely randomized policies can be suboptimal or inefficient when deployed on a specific real system, as they are trained to handle a broad range of possibilities rather than optimized for a single, current reality.

A significant limitation of most existing sim2real approaches, regardless of whether they use exact simulation or domain randomization, is that the trained RL policies are typically deployed in a *fixed* manner. They operate in inference mode, meaning they execute learned actions without further adaptation. This "fixed policy" paradigm inherently conflicts with the dynamic nature of the real world, where system parameters, environmental conditions, and task requirements inevitably evolve over time. Continuously adapting policies online using standard RL methods in the real world is fraught with challenges, including:
- **Instability of online optimization:** RL algorithms are known for their sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and data distribution shifts.
- **Sample inefficiency:** Real-world data collection is expensive and slow, making the high sample requirements of RL impractical.
- **Biased data collection:** The deployed policy's actions can lead to narrow data distributions, hindering further generalization.
- **Safety concerns:** Online exploration, a cornerstone of RL, often involves taking exploitative or novel actions that could be unsafe, leading to damage to the robot or its surroundings.

To address the dynamic nature of the real world, **continual learning (CL)** has emerged as a promising paradigm, allowing models to adapt to evolving data distributions over time without catastrophically forgetting previously acquired knowledge. While CL has seen extensive application in computer vision, its integration with RL, especially for *real-world robotic control* and with explicit **safety guarantees**, remains an active and crucial research area.

This paper positions itself precisely at the intersection of these critical research avenues:
- It builds upon the success of **domain randomization** for initial sim2real transfer.
- It introduces **safe RL techniques** (specifically PCRPO) to ensure that policies adhere to safety constraints during both pretraining and real-world adaptation.
- It incorporates **continual learning** (specifically EWC) to enable ongoing adaptation to changing real-world conditions without forgetting the general robust policy acquired during pretraining.

By integrating these three pillars, the proposed Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA) framework offers a novel and practical solution to move beyond static, simulation-trained policies towards truly autonomous, robust, safe, and adaptive robotic systems capable of operating reliably in dynamic real-world environments over extended periods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of current sim2real transfer methodologies for reinforcement learning policies in robotics, particularly their inability to adapt to evolving real-world conditions safely and continuously. Fixed policies, even if initially successful in sim2real transfer, inevitably degrade in performance as system parameters drift or environmental dynamics change. Furthermore, directly applying online RL adaptation in real-world scenarios is often impractical due to sample inefficiency, instability, and, most critically, the inherent safety risks associated with exploratory actions.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Enable Safe Deployment-Time Policy Adaptation:** The primary objective is to allow RL policies, once transferred from simulation to a real robotic system, to continually adapt to real-world changes without compromising safety. This means designing a framework where the policy can refine its behavior based on ongoing real-world experience.
2. **Mitigate Safety Risks During Adaptation:** A crucial goal is to ensure that the adaptation process on the real system is inherently safe. This involves preventing the policy from taking unsafe or potentially damaging exploratory actions, which is a common concern in online RL. The framework aims to maintain safety adherence throughout the policy refinement process.
3. **Prevent Catastrophic Forgetting:** When continually adapting a policy, there is a risk of "catastrophic forgetting," where the policy loses its general robustness learned during the extensive pretraining phase in the randomized simulation. An objective is to retain this general knowledge while specializing for the current real-world subdomain.
4. **Improve Real-World Performance Over Time:** By adapting to the current real system's dynamics and distribution, the framework aims to improve the policy's efficiency and performance beyond what is achievable with a fixed, zero-shot transferred policy.
5. **Empirically Validate the Approach:** To demonstrate the practical viability of SCDA, the authors aim to conduct rigorous experiments on both realistic simulations and physical robotic systems, evaluating the framework's ability to transfer, adapt, and maintain safety and performance on robotic manipulation tasks. This includes systematically analyzing the contribution of each component (safety optimization, continual learning regularization) to the overall framework's success.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between robust but potentially inefficient pre-trained policies and the need for agile, safe, and continuously adaptive robotic behaviors in the face of inevitable real-world variability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed **Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA)** framework addresses the aforementioned challenges by integrating three key AI paradigms: Domain Randomization, Safe Reinforcement Learning, and Continual Learning. The methodology is structured in two distinct phases: a pretraining stage in simulation and an adaptation stage on the real or realistic system.

**A. Problem Formulation (Constrained Markov Decision Process - CMDP)**
The problem is framed as optimizing a policy $\pi_\rho(a_t|s_t)$ within a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). In a CMDP, an agent aims to maximize expected cumulative reward ($V^\pi_r(\rho)$) while simultaneously satisfying constraints on expected cumulative cost ($V^\pi_{c_i}(\rho) \le b_i$). This formulation explicitly incorporates safety by defining a cost function ($c$) and safety thresholds ($b$).

**B. Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA) Implementation**
SCDA leverages the PCRPO algorithm for safe policy learning and Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) for continual learning.

1. **Pretraining Stage (in Randomized Simulation):**
 - **Goal:** To learn a robust and generally safe policy that can tolerate a wide range of system parameters and environmental variations, which are typical of domain randomization.
 - **Environment:** A highly parallelized simulator with wide randomization ranges applied to robot joint torques and observation noise. This exposes the agent to high uncertainty and potentially unsafe states, forcing it to learn a robust safety-aware policy.
 - **Safe RL Algorithm:** **PCRPO (Policy Constrained Reward and Cost Policy Optimization)** is employed. PCRPO is designed to balance reward maximization with safety constraint satisfaction. It categorizes policy optimization into three stages based on cost value:
 - **Safety Violation Stage:** If the cost exceeds a high threshold ($h^+ + b$), the policy update prioritizes reducing cost (using only the cost gradient $g_c$).
 - **No-Violation Stage:** If the cost is below a low threshold ($h^- + b$), the policy update focuses solely on maximizing reward (using only the reward gradient $g_r$).
 - **Soft Constraint Violation Stage:** If the cost is between the thresholds, PCRPO considers both reward and cost gradients. It differentiates between "gradient conflict" (where reward and cost gradients oppose each other) and "no gradient conflict," adjusting the weighted combination of gradients accordingly to find a direction that improves reward without significantly worsening safety, or vice versa.
 - **Output:** The policy parameters ($\tilde{\rho}$) are learned. Crucially, at the end of this phase, the **Fisher Information Matrix (FIM)** for these parameters is calculated using trajectories collected in the randomized simulation. FIM estimates the importance of each policy parameter, which is vital for the continual learning phase.

2. **Adaptation Stage (on Real/Realistic System):**
 - **Goal:** To fine-tune the pre-trained policy to the specific, current dynamics and distribution of the real system, while mitigating safety risks and preventing catastrophic forgetting of the general policy.
 - **Environment:** The policy is deployed and further adapted on either a realistically simulated system (with parameters closely matched to the real robot) or the physical robotic system itself.
 - **Continual Learning Mechanism:** **Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)** is integrated into the policy optimization process. EWC adds a regularization term to the loss function that penalizes changes to policy parameters that were deemed "important" during the pretraining phase (as indicated by their high Fisher information).
 - **Loss Function for Adaptation:** The adapted loss function is given by:
 $L(\rho) = L_{PCRPO}(\rho) + \frac{\lambda}{2} ||\rho - \tilde{\rho}||^2_{F_{\tilde{\rho}}}$
 where $L_{PCRPO}$ is the original PCRPO loss, $\lambda$ is a regularization constant, $\tilde{\rho}$ represents the policy parameters from the pretraining phase, and $F_{\tilde{\rho}}$ is the diagonal Fisher information matrix. This term ensures that the policy parameters do not drift too far from the robust configuration learned in simulation.
 - **Safety During Adaptation:** During real-world adaptation, a *stricter safety limit* (e.g., 10% of the pretraining phase limit) is imposed. This minimizes unsafe exploratory actions on the physical system, where consequences are tangible.
 - **Value and Cost Networks:** While the actor (policy) network's parameters are regularized by EWC, the value and cost networks (Critics) are *not* directly regularized. This allows them to adapt more freely to the real system's specific reward and cost distributions, providing accurate feedback for the actor. They are indirectly regularized through the actor's actions, which are, in turn, regularized.
 - **Sequential Adaptation:** The adaptation phase involves sequential learning on different target subdomains (e.g., different reach targets). This allows evaluation of the method's ability to adapt to new subdomains without forgetting performance on previous ones.

**C. Experimental Setup:**
- **Robotic Platform:** KUKA LBR iiwa 14 robotic manipulator.
- **Simulation Environment:** VTPRL, which facilitates accurate robotic manipulation simulations.
- **Tasks:**
 - **Reach-and-Balance Task:** A simplified task where the robot must reach targets while avoiding joint limits. This allows for detailed analysis and safe experimentation. The state includes end-effector distance to target and joint positions; actions are joint velocities. Rewards are negative distance to target, and costs are positive distance to target if safety limits are violated (episode termination).
 - **Grasping Task:** A more complex task involving guiding an end-effector to an object's optimal grasping position while avoiding collisions and joint limits. The state includes positional/rotational errors and all seven joint positions; actions are end-effector velocities. Rewards are based on reduction in positional/rotational error, and costs are a penalty for collisions.
- **Comparison Strategies:** The authors compare SCDA against:
 - **Zero-shot Transfer (ZS):** Pre-trained policy deployed directly without adaptation. (Baseline)
 - **Domain Adaptation (DA):** Pre-trained policy adapted on the real system, optimizing *only for reward*.
 - **Safe Domain Adaptation (SDA):** Pre-trained policy adapted on the real system, optimizing for *both safety and reward*.
 - **Continual Domain Adaptation (CDA):** Pre-trained policy adapted on the real system, optimizing *only for reward, with EWC regularization*.
 - **Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA - proposed):** Pre-trained policy adapted on the real system, optimizing for *both safety and reward, with EWC regularization*.
- **Metrics:** Average timestep reward, average timestep cost (safety violations), total performance (reward + cost), and grasp success rate for the grasping task.

This comprehensive methodology, spanning pretraining and adaptation with explicit safety and continual learning mechanisms, forms a robust framework for developing adaptive and safe RL policies for real-world robotics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides compelling evidence for the efficacy of the Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA) framework across two robotic tasks: a reach-and-balance task and a more complex grasping task. The findings address the core research questions regarding performance improvement, handling sequential adaptation, and the impact of different method components.

**A. Pretraining Phase Performance (Figure 3, top row):**
- The pretraining phase successfully trains the agent in a highly randomized simulation environment.
- The agent learns to perform the reach-and-balance task while adhering to predefined average cost limits per timestep, demonstrating that PCRPO effectively enables learning a robust and safe general policy under wide randomization.

**B. Adaptation Phase Performance (Figure 3, middle and bottom rows - Training Mode):**
- **Impact of Safety Optimization:**
 - Strategies that *only* optimize for reward during adaptation (DA and CDA) show an initial slight improvement in reward but quickly lead to highly unsafe behaviors (overshooting targets, violating joint safety limits). They struggle to recover from these safety violations, demonstrating the critical need for explicit safety mechanisms during real-world adaptation.
 - Strategies that incorporate safety optimization (SDA and SCDA) successfully stay within the predefined (and stricter) safety limits during adaptation. They manage to improve the reward *after* satisfying the safety bounds, indicating a principled balance between performance and safety.
- **Impact of Continual Learning (CL) Regularization:**
 - **SDA (Safe Domain Adaptation):** Adapts faster when a target switches, as it is not constrained by a regularization term. However, it exhibits an overfitting trend to the current subdomain. This is evident as its performance significantly degrades when switching to a new target, requiring it to "re-learn" or re-adapt from a much worse state.
 - **SCDA (Safe Continual Domain Adaptation):** Adapts more gradually than SDA but avoids the severe overfitting seen in SDA. It maintains a better balance between adapting to the current subdomain and preserving the general policy learned during pretraining.

**C. Inference Mode Performance (Figure 4 - Realistic and Real Systems):**
- **Zero-Shot Transfer (ZS):** The pre-trained policy (ZS transfer) does not transfer perfectly to the real/realistic system. While it performs reasonably well, it generates costs (safety violations) for some targets, highlighting the reality gap and the need for adaptation.
- **Impact of Safety (Cost Analysis):**
 - DA and CDA (reward-only adaptation) lead to significant safety violations (high cost), confirming that optimizing only for reward on the real system is highly unsafe.
 - SDA and SCDA (with safety optimization) successfully minimize cost under the current distribution, often bringing the cost to zero. This means the robot avoids reaching joint safety limits. This safety comes at a slight trade-off in potential reward gain compared to unsafe strategies, as the agent might command slower speeds or avoid approaching targets too closely to ensure safety.
- **Overall Performance (Total - Reward + Cost):**
 - Both SDA and SCDA improve total performance over the pre-trained (ZS transfer) policy when adapting to the *current* domain distribution.
 - **Addressing Q1 & Q2 (Adaptation & Forgetting):** SCDA is the *only* strategy that can improve the *overall performance across the entire domain* (i.e., when tested on all targets, not just the one it was currently adapting to) compared to the zero-shot general policy. SDA, while adapting well to the "current" target, overfits, leading to significantly worse performance on "other" (previously adapted or unadapted) targets. This demonstrates that SCDA successfully prevents catastrophic forgetting of the general policy while enabling effective adaptation to specific subdomains. This is a critical finding for continuous, long-term deployment.

**D. Fisher Importance Analysis (Figure 5):**
- This analysis visually confirms the role of EWC in SCDA.
- **SDA:** Policy parameter changes are significant and largely uncorrelated with their Fisher importance (i.e., even important parameters change drastically). This explains why SDA overfits and forgets.
- **SCDA:** There is a clear correlation: parameters with higher Fisher importance (deemed more critical to the general policy from pretraining) exhibit much lower relative changes. This mechanism allows SCDA to "remember" the robust general policy while adapting less important parameters to fit the current subdomain dynamics. This implicit regularization of exploratory actions is also a safety benefit.

**E. Verification on a Complex Grasping Task (Table I):**
- On the more complex and safety-critical grasping task, SCDA significantly outperforms zero-shot transfer.
- SCDA achieved higher average reward, completely minimized safety violations (cost of 0.0), and dramatically increased the grasp success rate (60% on the real robot compared to 20% for zero-shot). This validates the framework's effectiveness and scalability to more realistic robotic scenarios involving potential collisions.

In summary, the results conclusively demonstrate that SCDA is a superior strategy for safely adapting RL policies in real-world robotic systems. It effectively bridges the sim2real gap, enables continuous adaptation, prevents catastrophic forgetting, and, critically, maintains safety throughout the deployment lifecycle.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Safe Continual Domain Adaptation (SCDA) framework presented in this paper represents a significant advancement in the field of reinforcement learning for robotics, addressing a crucial bottleneck in the journey toward truly autonomous and robust robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact can be articulated as follows:

1. **Enabling True Long-Term Autonomy:** Current RL policies for robotics are often designed for static environments and, once transferred from simulation, operate in a fixed manner. SCDA shatters this paradigm by allowing policies to *continually adapt* to the inevitable changes in real-world system parameters (e.g., component wear, motor degradation, changing payloads) and environmental conditions (e.g., temperature, lighting, new objects). This capability is fundamental for robots to operate reliably and effectively over extended periods without constant human intervention or costly retraining.

2. **Bridging the "Post-Deployment" Reality Gap:** While much research focuses on initial sim2real transfer, SCDA addresses the equally important "post-deployment reality gap." It acknowledges that even a perfectly transferred policy will become suboptimal over time in a dynamic world. By enabling safe, online adaptation, it ensures sustained high performance and robustness, making RL-driven robots more practical for real-world applications.

3. **Enhanced Safety for Real-World RL:** A major hurdle for deploying RL in safety-critical robotic applications is the risk associated with exploratory actions during learning. SCDA directly tackles this by integrating principled safe RL methods (PCRPO) and applying stricter safety limits during adaptation. This significantly reduces the likelihood of damaging the robot or its environment, which is paramount for industrial, medical, and collaborative robotics. The ability to guarantee safety while still learning on the real system is a cornerstone of this work's impact.

4. **Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting:** The integration of continual learning techniques (EWC) is vital. It allows the policy to specialize and optimize for the current real-world subdomain without "forgetting" the general, robust behaviors learned during the extensive pretraining phase in the widely randomized simulation. This prevents the need for a full retraining cycle every time the environment changes, making the adaptation process much more efficient and scalable.

5. **Practical Applicability and Efficiency:** By combining the strengths of domain randomization (for initial robustness), safe RL (for constraint satisfaction), and continual learning (for adaptive memory), SCDA offers a highly practical framework. It reduces the reliance on perfectly modeled simulations or massive amounts of real-world data for fine-tuning. This could accelerate the development and deployment cycles for complex robotic manipulation tasks.

6. **Paving the Way for More Complex Tasks:** The successful application of SCDA to a complex grasping task, showing significant improvement in success rates and complete elimination of safety violations compared to zero-shot transfer, underscores its potential for a wide range of industrial and service robotics applications where precision, robustness, and safety are critical.

**Future Work Directions:** The paper also wisely outlines its limitations and future directions, which further highlight its foundational nature:
- **Automatic Domain Shift Detection:** A key next step is to enable robots to automatically detect when a significant domain shift or out-of-distribution scenario occurs, allowing the system to decide when and how to initiate adaptation or switch learning modes. This would make the system truly autonomous in managing its adaptation process.
- **Early-Stage Safety Guarantees:** While SCDA ensures safety during adaptation, future work aims to incorporate formal methods and control theory (e.g., barrier functions) to guarantee safety even in the *initial, early stages* of policy learning in simulation, prior to deployment. This would expand the applicability of RL to even higher-stakes safety-critical scenarios.

In conclusion, SCDA represents a significant step towards closing the full loop of robot learning: from simulation-based training to robust, safe, and perpetually adaptive real-world deployment. Its impact extends to making RL a more viable and trustworthy paradigm for developing intelligent robotic systems that can operate autonomously and reliably in the inherently dynamic and unpredictable real world.
