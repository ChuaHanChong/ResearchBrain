# Systematically Exploring the Capabilities of GPT-6 Astra as Embodied Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> A Galbot evaluation of GPT-6 Astra as a general embodied policy across six domains, comparing Direct control (Astra-generated numerical commands via analytic controllers) with Hybrid control (Astra reviewing learned-policy proposals or commanding frozen whole-body controllers). Astra excels at navigation and corrective task decisions, but direct in-hand manipulation and dense-reference locomotion fail, and token use and latency severely constrain practical control.

## Problem

- Prior embodied uses of large language models mostly confine them to high-level planning, skill selection, or program composition, leaving their ability to emit numerical robot actions largely unassessed.
- Earlier frontier-model robot evaluations report uneven manipulation performance on isolated tasks, without systematic cross-domain comparison against learned policies under matched initial states and protocols.
- Task success alone hides which responsibilities the model assumes versus what interfaces and whole-body controllers supply, and existing evaluations rarely account for token cost and inference latency.

## Method

- ==Direct control mode==: Astra generates end-effector, joint, gripper, base, navigation-primitive, or dense motion-reference commands executed by inverse kinematics, PD, or ==operational-space controllers== in a closed observe-act loop.
- ==Hybrid control mode==: Astra reviews π0.5 action-chunk proposals, accepting prefixes, issuing short end-effector corrections, or rewriting subgoal instructions; in humanoids, it commands ==frozen whole-body controllers== via velocities or sparse targets.
- ==Fixed-weight experience adaptation== and resource accounting: written guidance and notes refined between trials without weight updates, plus action-source attribution, cached and uncached token tallies, and per-call latency logging.

## Results

- RoboDojo: Hybrid with π0.5 reaches **48%** success and **62.60** Score versus **26%** for Direct, with Astra authoring only **14.4%** of executed steps; RoboLab zero-shot: Direct **98%** versus π0.5 **36%**.
- Navigation leads local baselines with **92%** RxR and **82%** HM3D success, though MP3D ObjectNav SPL is only **22.43%**; HumanoidBench mean return **581.9** versus **338.2** for TD-MPC2.
- Direct in-hand rotation stays at-goal only **0.51%** of steps versus **76.90%** for RL; five dense-locomotion attempts never reach the goal, the last needing **250 calls** averaging **39.86 s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frontier reasoning models add most value as sparse correctors of learned policies, fixing targets and contact preconditions, rather than generating continuous low-level motion themselves.
> - Policy assistance helps only when the learned motion prior fits the task; a poorly adapted zero-shot policy can make Hybrid control underperform direct reasoning.
> - Detecting an error, proposing a fix, and verifying physical success are separate capabilities; deliberation latency far exceeding motion timescales makes paused-physics results optimistic for real deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In a bimanual disk-stacking task, reaching the stack is not enough. The small disk must actually touch its support before the robot releases it. In Galbot Team's system, the language model GPT-6 Astra can interrupt a learned policy and make a small downward correction. It then returns control so the policy can release and withdraw. Astra completes five of ten experience-guided trials. These are examples from a limited run, not proof of generally reliable placement.

Bimanual disk stacking, shown as saved snapshots rather than continuous footage. Top row (Seed 4, right wrist camera, native success): "Support gap" at step 725, "Contact before release" at 764, and "Released on the stack" at 797. Bottom row (Seed 3, left wrist camera, native failure): the disk remains high at 1,253, a further correction occurs at 1,368, and it is still unseated at 1,500.

The authors' central point is that a language model can make useful robot decisions without thereby becoming a reliable physical controller. Across six domains, they find that outcomes depend on the complete control path. That path includes what Astra can observe and command, how a learned policy or controller turns its decisions into motion, and whether feedback confirms progress.

### 2. Reviewing a learned policy's motion

In **Direct** control, Astra generates commands that run through analytic control. In **Hybrid** control, Astra reviews a learned policy's proposed motion and either accepts part of it or replaces it with a short correction. The learned policy here is [[2504.16054|π₀.₅]], a vision-language-action policy that serves as a motion prior. On RoboDojo, π₀.₅ proposes a 50-step joint-space sequence. Astra accepts its first 1–15 steps or substitutes a 1–5-step end-effector correction, and these branches are mutually exclusive.

The test used a ten-task, bimanual RoboDojo subset. Each method ran five times per task on paired instances, configurations, and seeds. With task-finetuned π₀.₅, Hybrid completed 24/50 trials (48%), compared with 13/50 (26%) for Astra Direct. Mean native partial-completion Scores were 62.60 and 37.81, respectively. Score measures partial progress rather than binary task success. Direct had only 48 valid Score entries, although its success rate uses all 50.

The subset was selected partly for low published π₀.₅ success. Published policy scores were reweighted to this task and scene mix rather than rerun on the paired starts.

Hybrid adds both a learned action policy and Astra's review-and-correction loop. The comparison therefore does **not** isolate the causal effect of Astra's corrections alone. Astra supplies 14.4% of Hybrid's executed control steps and π₀.₅ supplies 85.6%, while Astra reviews every proposal. The gain is also uneven: Direct scores higher on language-based and object classification.

### 3. A secure grasp is not dexterous control

Continuous in-hand rotation is a harder test of physical control. Astra Direct was compared with frozen, task-specific RL from five paired saved hand and object starts. The physical starts are paired, but the two controllers receive different observations and act at different rates.

On the cuboid, Astra retained the object in all five runs. However, it was at the rotation goal for only 4.40% of executed steps, compared with 63.50% for RL. The authors interpret this gap as a contact problem: sustained rotation requires changing finger contacts while keeping the object supported. The comparison supports a performance gap, not a uniquely identified cause.

Green is Astra Direct and blue is RL. On the cuboid, Astra has a low at-goal rate and rotates more slowly than RL, even though the object is retained. ### 4. Cost and boundary

Across 50 RoboDojo instances per condition, Hybrid used 624.8 million tokens and Direct used 1.132 billion. These totals include cached input and exclude earlier attempts. The totals measure computation, not elapsed time.

In one locomotion run, 250 synchronous calls averaged 39.86 seconds each for 30 seconds of simulated motion, with physics paused during inference. This is inference latency, not a real robot's completion time.

Useful control can come from knowing when to intervene and when to hand motion back to a learned policy. Local corrections, however, are not the same as verified, repeatable physical progress. Useful reasoning, motor execution, and verification remain separate requirements.
