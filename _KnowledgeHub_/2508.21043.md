# HITTER: A HumanoId Table TEnnis Robot via Hierarchical Planning and Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> UC Berkeley researchers developed "HITTER," a humanoid table tennis robot employing a hierarchical planning and learning framework that enables agile, whole-body striking motions. The system achieved a 92.3% return rate for incoming balls and sustained 106 consecutive shots in rallies against human opponents on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots traditionally struggle with rapid and agile interaction with dynamic environments through manipulation, especially in tasks requiring sub-second reaction times.
- Prior humanoid table tennis efforts were limited to static robot positions, severely restricting effective hitting range and agility.
- Integrating precise perception, prediction, planning, and whole-body action in dynamic scenarios like table tennis presents a significant challenge for humanoids.

## Method

- A hierarchical framework combines a model-based planner for precise ball trajectory prediction and racket target planning with a reinforcement learning-based whole-body controller for motion execution.
- The learning-based controller, trained in simulation using PPO and human motion references, generates agile, human-like striking motions, including rapid base repositioning.
- The system leverages high-precision motion capture for ball tracking and deploys the learned policy zero-shot on a general-purpose ==Unitree G1== humanoid robot.

## Results

- The model-based planner achieved position prediction errors below **7.5** cm and time prediction errors below 20 ms, **0.5** and **0.3** seconds before a strike, respectively.
- The integrated system demonstrated a **96.2%** hit rate and a **92.3%** return rate for incoming balls, consistently using strategic forehand/backhand strokes.
- The robot engaged in extended rallies, achieving a record of 106 consecutive shots against a human opponent and sustaining continuous rallies in humanoid-humanoid matches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The hierarchical approach effectively combines the strengths of model-based planning (prediction accuracy) and learning-based control (adaptability, human-like motion generation) for complex dynamic tasks.
> - Incorporating human motion references into the reward function significantly enhances the naturalness and effectiveness of the robot's striking movements and whole-body coordination, including waist rotation.
> - Commanding target base positions to the controller, rather than velocities, enables rapid, single-step lateral movements for repositioning, crucial for covering the table effectively.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical challenge in humanoid robotics: enabling rapid and agile interaction with dynamic environments through manipulation. While humanoid robots have made impressive strides in locomotion, motion imitation, and maintaining balance, much of this progress has historically focused on control in free space or with static ground interaction. Interacting with fast-moving objects, especially in tasks requiring sub-second reaction times and whole-body coordination, remains a significant hurdle.

Table tennis serves as an "epitome" of such a challenge, demanding precise perception, prediction, planning, and action within hundreds of milliseconds. This contrasts with simpler robotic manipulation tasks and even other racket sports like badminton or tennis, where longer distances and slower ball speeds offer larger reaction windows. The need for agile whole-body movements—blending arm swings, waist rotations, quick stepping, and balance recovery—differentiates this work from traditional robotic manipulators or fixed-base robot arms.

The paper extensively reviews related work in robotic table tennis (Section II.A), highlighting several key historical and contemporary approaches. Early efforts included biologically-inspired motion generation, learning from human demonstrations, and developing opponent modeling and anticipatory action strategies. More recent advancements have leveraged specialized hardware, model-based control, and significant learning-based techniques, notably from DeepMind's Gemini Robotics team, which achieved amateur human-level performance and sample-efficient learning.

Crucially, prior work on *humanoid* table tennis was limited to robots standing still, which severely restricted their effective hitting range and agility. "HITTER" directly addresses this limitation by focusing on "human-like and agile reaching motions for table tennis hitting, a capability not previously demonstrated on humanoid robots." This work elevates humanoid table tennis from a static demonstration to a dynamic, whole-body interactive task.

In the context of humanoid whole-body control (Section II.B), this paper builds upon recent advances in learning general whole-body motion trackers and expressive control. By developing a whole-body controller "specifically designed for rapid and dynamic interactions with the environment, such as ball hitting," the authors contribute to expanding the repertoire of skills that general-purpose humanoids can perform, moving beyond mere imitation or locomotion towards complex, reactive manipulation. The hierarchical integration of model-based planning with learning-based control also reflects a growing trend in robotics to leverage the strengths of both paradigms for complex tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged gap in humanoid robot capabilities: despite progress in locomotion and whole-body control, humanoids are still limited in tasks requiring rapid, agile interaction with dynamic environments through manipulation. Table tennis is identified as an ideal testbed for these challenges due to its extreme demands:
- **High Dynamics:** Ball speeds exceeding 5 m/s necessitate sub-second perception, prediction, planning, and action.
- **Whole-Body Agility:** Successful play requires coordinated arm swings, waist rotations, quick stepping, and immediate balance recovery for consecutive rallies.
- **Human-like Motion:** Natural and effective striking motions, akin to human players, are crucial for performance and naturalness.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of the "HITTER" project are:

1. **Develop a Hierarchical Framework:** To effectively tackle the complexity of humanoid table tennis, the research aims to establish a modular architecture that separates high-level strategic planning from low-level motion execution. This framework integrates a model-based planner with a reinforcement learning-based whole-body controller.
2. **Achieve Accurate Ball Prediction and Racket Target Planning:** The model-based planner's objective is to precisely estimate ball trajectories and determine optimal striking positions, velocities, and timing, providing stable and accurate commands to the controller.
3. **Generate Agile, Human-like Whole-Body Striking Motions:** The learning-based controller's goal is to produce coordinated arm and leg movements that mimic human strikes, ensuring stability, agility, and quick recovery across multiple shots. This includes learning to rapidly reposition the robot's base to reach the ball effectively.
4. **Demonstrate Real-World Performance on a General-Purpose Humanoid:** A primary objective is to validate the entire system on a physical, off-the-shelf humanoid robot (Unitree G1), without specialized hardware, to showcase its practical feasibility and robustness in real-time, autonomous operation.
5. **Achieve Sustained Rallies against Human and Humanoid Opponents:** The ultimate test of the system's capability is its ability to engage in prolonged table tennis rallies, demonstrating reliable ball returns and continuous play in interactive scenarios.

In essence, the paper seeks to push the boundaries of humanoid robot agility and interactive intelligence by mastering a highly dynamic, complex, and human-centric task.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "HITTER" system employs a hierarchical framework that strategically combines a model-based planner with a learning-based whole-body controller, designed to leverage the strengths of both paradigms for dynamic table tennis play.

**Perception System:**
The system relies on a high-precision motion capture setup. Nine OptiTrack cameras track the ball's position, the robot base position, and its orientation at 360 Hz with millimeter-level accuracy. The ball is covered with reflective tape to aid tracking.

**Model-Based Planner (High-Level):**
The planner is responsible for predicting ball trajectories and determining the optimal racket contact parameters.
- **State Estimation:** To obtain ball velocity, which is not directly measured, a least-squares fit of a second-order polynomial is applied to the 31 most recent ball position measurements. This provides smoothed estimates of position and velocity. Bounce detection clears the buffer to prevent pre-bounce data from corrupting post-bounce estimates.
- **Ball Trajectory Prediction:** A hybrid dynamics model is used:
 - **Continuous Flight:** Governed by `a = -k||v||v + g`, accounting for aerodynamic drag (`k`) and gravity (`g`), while neglecting spin-induced effects like Magnus force.
 - **Discrete Impact:** Models bounces on the table with `v+ = C v-`, where `C` is a restitution matrix `diag(Ch, Ch, -Cv)` for horizontal (`Ch`) and vertical (`Cv`) restitution coefficients.
 - Parameters (`k`, `Ch`, `Cv`) are estimated from 15 recorded ball trajectories. The model is used for explicit step-by-step time integration to predict future ball positions and velocities.
 - A virtual hit plane is pre-defined at `x = -1.37 m` to determine the hitting time and position.
- **Racket-Ball Interaction:** The goal is a valid return, not precise landing.
 - The racket plane is assumed perpendicular to its velocity at impact.
 - A coefficient of restitution `Cr` is used along the racket surface normal, neglecting tangential friction.
 - The desired outgoing ball velocity `vo` is calculated based on a desired landing position (`p_l`, set to the opponent's table center) and a predefined flight time `Δt`.
 - The desired racket velocity `v_racket` is then computed from `vo`, the predicted incoming velocity `vi`, and `Cr`.
 - The planner outputs the desired racket striking position (`p_racket`), velocity (`v_racket`), and strike time (`t_strike`). It also computes a target base position (`p_base`) for the robot based on its current position and the desired racket position.

**Learning-Based Whole-Body Controller (Low-Level):**
The controller, implemented on a 29-joint Unitree G1 humanoid, generates whole-body motions to execute the planner's commands.
- **Training:** The policy (`π_WBC`) is trained in Isaac Lab using the model-free reinforcement learning algorithm PPO, enabling zero-shot deployment to the real robot. Joint position setpoints from the policy are converted to torques by a PD controller.
- **Human Motion References:** Two human motion references (forehand and backhand swings) are incorporated to promote natural, human-like movements. Videos of human swings are converted to SMPL motion clips using GVHMR, then retargeted to the humanoid using GMR. These clips provide desired base poses and joint positions at 50 Hz, with upper body (pelvis, torso, arms) motion tracked.
- **Markov Decision Process (MDP) Setting:**
 - **Separate Commands:** To improve training efficiency, commands are separated for the robot's base (`p_base,xy`) and racket (`[p_racket, v_racket]`). The desired base orientation always faces forward.
 - **Episode Design:** Episodes last 10 seconds to facilitate consecutive strikes and swing type switching. After each swing, the next swing type and targets are randomly sampled, with a fixed hitting plane 0.4 m in front of the robot.
 - **Swing Type Selection (Deployment):** During real-world deployment, the swing type (forehand/backhand) is heuristically determined based on the ball's incoming y-coordinate relative to the robot's base.
- **Reward Functions:** The total reward `r` combines three components:
 - `r_i`: Imitation reward for tracking upper body reference motions (dense).
 - `r_g`: Goal-tracking reward for commanded targets. This includes sparse, high-weight terms for racket position, velocity, and orientation (activated around hitting time) and base position (activated before the strike).
 - `r_r`: Regularization reward (dense).
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** An asymmetric actor-critic framework is used during training, where the critic receives additional "privileged information" unavailable to the actor during deployment (e.g., base linear velocity, full body poses, time left in episode, reference joint positions/velocities). This aids in more accurate value estimation and training stability. Both actor and critic are MLPs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of "HITTER" involved a rigorous evaluation of the planner, the controller, and the integrated system in real-world table tennis scenarios.

**A. Model-based Planner Accuracy:**
The planner's prediction accuracy was assessed over 20 ball trajectories. As expected, prediction errors decreased significantly as the time to strike approached.
- **0.5 seconds before strike:** The position prediction error fell below 7.5 cm, which is the critical threshold corresponding to the racket's radius. This demonstrates that the planner provides sufficiently accurate target locations well in advance for the controller to react.
- **0.3 seconds before strike:** The time prediction error dropped below 20 ms, which is equivalent to one control step of the policy, ensuring precise timing for impact.
- **0.1 seconds before strike:** Both position and time errors reached minimal levels, providing highly reliable command inputs.
This consistent low error throughout the prediction process establishes a robust foundation for the overall system's accuracy and stability.

**B. Whole-Body Control Agility:**
The agility of the learning-based whole-body control policy was evaluated in both simulation and the real world.
- **Simulation Results:** Out of 1000 simulated roll-outs, 943 (94.3% success rate) successfully reached the target base position within 1 cm. When the initial distance between the robot's actual and desired base position was less than 0.75 meters, the robot consistently reached the target in under 0.8 seconds. This is critical because the typical duration from command issuance to strike is 0.86 seconds, indicating that the robot can reposition itself effectively *before* completing the striking motion. The convergence time increased monotonically with initial distance, and convergence was symmetric for left and right displacements.
- **Real-World Observations:** The policy exhibited highly agile motions. For instance, when the ball was directed to the left side of the table while the robot was positioned on the right, it executed a swift, single-step lateral movement to cover the distance and return the ball (Fig. 5). This rapid, one-step motion contrasts with slower, multi-step movements observed in prior approaches using velocity commands, highlighting the benefit of the base position command. Furthermore, the incorporation of human motion references led to striking behaviors that closely resembled human play, including coordinated waist rotation during hits (Fig. 6), enhancing both effectiveness and naturalness.

**C. Real-World Experiments:**
The integrated system was tested extensively in real-world scenarios against both incoming balls, human opponents, and another humanoid robot.
- **Return Performance:** Out of 26 incoming balls projected across a wide area of the virtual hit plane, the robot achieved 24 successful returns, missed 1 after a hit (into the net), and completely missed 1. This translates to an impressive **96.2% hit rate and a 92.3% return rate** (Fig. 7). The robot also demonstrated natural strategic play, consistently using forehand strokes for balls incoming at y < 0 and backhand strokes for y > 0, mirroring human table tennis strategies.
- **Human-Humanoid Rallies:** The humanoid robot was able to engage in extended rallies with human opponents, achieving a remarkable **106 consecutive shots**. This rally length surpasses that of casual human play, indicating the robot's ability not only to reliably track and return the ball but also to maintain balance and readiness for successive strokes over prolonged periods. The system demonstrated a reaction time of just 0.42 seconds when returning human smashes.
- **Humanoid-Humanoid Rallies:** Two humanoid robots, each equipped with the identical "HITTER" policy, were able to sustain continuous rallies autonomously, showcasing the system's robustness and generality in a fully autonomous match setting (Fig. 1).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "HITTER" system represents a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, pushing the boundaries of what these complex machines can achieve in dynamic and interactive environments.

**Significance:**
- **Pioneering Agile Humanoid Interaction:** This work marks the first demonstration of a general-purpose humanoid robot successfully playing table tennis with agile, human-like whole-body motions and reactive control in sub-second timeframes. It directly addresses the limitations of previous humanoid table tennis research that lacked dynamic footwork and range.
- **Validation of Hierarchical Design:** The successful integration of a high-precision model-based planner with a flexible reinforcement learning-based whole-body controller proves the effectiveness of this hybrid approach. It leverages the speed and accuracy of model-based methods for prediction while capitalizing on the adaptability and ability to learn complex, human-like behaviors offered by RL, successfully mitigating the weaknesses of each paradigm when used in isolation (e.g., sparse rewards in end-to-end RL or reliance on perfect models for complex humanoids).
- **High-Performance Benchmark:** The achieved rally length of 106 consecutive shots against a human opponent and a high return rate of 92.3% set a new benchmark for reactive control and sustained performance in dynamic tasks for humanoids. The ability to return human smashes further highlights its real-time capabilities.
- **Demonstration on General-Purpose Hardware:** Achieving these results on a Unitree G1, a general-purpose humanoid robot without specialized hardware, underscores the robustness and transferability of the proposed framework.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerating Humanoid Robot Development:** "HITTER" provides a compelling case study and a foundational framework for developing more agile, intelligent, and interactive humanoid robots. The principles demonstrated here, particularly the integration of dynamic whole-body control with rapid perception and planning, can be generalized to a wide array of complex real-world tasks, from industrial manipulation to assistive robotics in human environments.
- **New Testbed for Robotics Research:** Table tennis is solidified as an excellent, challenging testbed for future research in perception, high-speed control, adaptive planning, and human-robot interaction. It provides a rich environment for exploring multi-agent learning and strategic decision-making.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** The generation of natural, human-like motions is crucial for improving human acceptance and safety in collaborative robotics. As humanoids become more capable of human-like movements, their integration into human-centric environments becomes more seamless and intuitive.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Fixed Virtual Hitting Plane:** This constrains striking strategies and limits the robot's ability to handle very short or deep balls. Future work will relax this assumption.
- **External Motion Capture:** Dependence on OptiTrack restricts deployment to controlled environments. Integrating vision-based sensing is a critical next step for real-world autonomy.
- **Limited Stroke Repertoire and Spin Handling:** The current system assumes negligible spin and performs a "flat push." Professional-level play requires perceiving and generating diverse strokes (top-spin, chop, etc.), which is a significant area for future expansion.

Future research directions articulated by the authors include multi-agent training for competitive play, enabling autonomous serving, and a long-term vision for learning to play against skilled opponents. This will involve strategic learning (observing opponent characteristics) and tactical learning (adapting strokes in real-time), building on previous work in opponent modeling to achieve "championship-quality humanoid robot players."

In conclusion, "HITTER" represents a substantial leap forward in humanoid robot capabilities, demonstrating practical, real-time, and agile interaction in a highly dynamic task, and laying robust groundwork for future advancements towards truly intelligent and versatile humanoid agents.
