# TrAct: Bridging Robot Control and Visual Prediction with Visual Tracks

> [!summary] Summary
> TrAct uses 2D visual point tracks as a shared interface between robot control and video prediction. A flow-matching policy jointly proposes candidate actions and tracks, a track-conditioned diffusion world model rolls out each candidate's visual future, and a vision-language critic picks the best rollout. Success rises from 27% to 55% in simulation and 49% to 76% on a real Franka.

## Problem

- Robot actions are embodiment-specific and low-dimensional, so action-conditioned world models must infer dense visual futures from sparse, weakly aligned control signals.
- This underdetermined action-to-pixel mapping causes poor action following: prior video world models often ignore erroneous actions and hallucinate success from visual priors.
- Existing track and flow methods use motion only to produce or condition predictions; the predicted future is evaluated in latent or geometric space, if at all.

## Method

- **VLAT** extends a ==flow-matching== vision-language-action backbone with a track head, jointly predicting action chunks and 2D ==point tracks== under a weighted joint flow-matching loss.
- **TWM** conditions ==Stable Video Diffusion== via a ==ControlNet== branch that renders tracks as spatial control maps, with per-view color channels for agent and wrist cameras.
- **TrAct** inference proposes K diverse action-track pairs via ==temperature-scaled resampling==, rolls out each with TWM, and scores rollouts with the InternVL2-based **VLAC** reward model.

## Results

- On LIBERO-INTEGRAL, TrAct reaches **0.55** average success versus **0.49** for action-conditioned selection, **0.44** for VLAT alone, and **0.27** for the baseline policy.
- Track conditioning improves prediction on all five video metrics: simulation agent-view PSNR **24.51** versus **15.12**, LPIPS **0.106** versus **0.438**; real wrist-view FVD **140** versus **246**.
- Real Franka tasks average **0.76** versus **0.66** for action conditioning; under unseen backgrounds action-conditioned selection collapses to **0.4** while track conditioning holds **0.7**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Choosing an interface that is dense and aligned with the observation space, not merely executable, is what makes generative world models useful for action selection.
> - Embodiment-agnostic motion representations let human egocentric video supervise a robot policy's prediction head even when no executable action labels exist.
> - Rollout-based selection has diminishing returns in candidate count, five candidates recover most of the gain, so the compute-heavy setting is optional rather than essential.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "TrAct" addresses a challenge in the field of robot learning, specifically concerning the interaction between robot control policies and world models that predict future scene states. Recent advancements in robot learning often combine world models with policies to evaluate candidate actions and select those that best satisfy a task objective. However, a limitation in this paradigm is the interface used to connect the policy and the world model: robot actions.

Robot actions are typically low-dimensional, embodiment-specific, and their visual effects are highly dependent on complex factors such as scene geometry, object configurations, and contact dynamics. This weak alignment between sparse, robot-specific commands and dense visual futures requires action-conditioned world models to infer significant visual detail from limited input. This can lead to issues such as hallucinating successful outcomes based on visual priors, even when actions are erroneous, as noted by prior research.

In contrast, visual tracks offer an alternative representation. They describe how specific, task-relevant points move through image space. These tracks are largely embodiment-agnostic, meaning similar visual motions can be induced by different robots performing different underlying actions. They provide dense, spatially precise guidance for future video prediction, capturing object motion, contact regions, and interaction geometry. Previous research has explored structured motion representations like 2D point tracks and optical flow as intermediate policy representations or as conditioning signals for world models. However, these often evaluate the predicted future in latent or geometric spaces, rather than directly against task instructions in the image domain.

"TrAct" positions itself within this landscape by integrating visual tracks as a unifying interface. It builds on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which combine semantic reasoning with visual-motor control for generalist robot policies, and closed-loop control extensions that improve robustness. The paper aims to bridge the gap between embodiment-specific control and embodiment-agnostic visual prediction by using tracks to render predicted futures directly assessable against language-based task instructions, thereby improving both prediction accuracy and task completion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance robot decision-making by establishing a more effective and robust connection between robot control and visual prediction. The authors identify a core limitation in current robot world modeling: the reliance on robot actions as the conditioning signal for predicting future visual outcomes.

The motivations for this work stem from several observations:
1. **Weak Alignment of Robot Actions with Visual Changes:** Robot actions are inherently specific to a robot's embodiment (e.g., joint commands, end-effector poses) and are often low-dimensional. Their visual consequences are not straightforward, as they depend heavily on the environment's geometry, the state of objects, and the physics of contact. This sparse and indirect connection makes it challenging for action-conditioned world models to accurately infer detailed visual futures. Such models may struggle with "action following," sometimes ignoring an executed action if it deviates from expected patterns and instead hallucinating outcomes based on prior visual knowledge.
2. **Need for Embodiment-Agnostic Representations:** For robot policies to generalize across different robots, tasks, and environments, an embodiment-agnostic representation is beneficial. Robot actions are not inherently transferable between different robot platforms (e.g., a Franka Panda versus a UR5).
3. **Potential of Visual Tracks as an Interface:** Visual tracks, which represent the movement of task-relevant points in image space, offer a natural alternative. They capture direct visual motion, including object movement and interaction points, and are largely independent of the specific robot that generated the motion. This suggests they could serve as a more robust and dense spatial guidance signal for visual prediction.
4. **Integrating Prediction with Reward-Guided Action Selection:** While visual tracks have been explored for policy learning or as world model conditioning, their full potential as an interface for *evaluating* predicted futures against task objectives had not been fully realized. The goal is to leverage tracks to generate visually detailed future predictions that can then be scored by a vision-language reward model, enabling more informed action selection.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the TrAct framework are:
- To develop a policy that can jointly predict both executable robot actions and their corresponding visual tracks from a given observation and language instruction.
- To create a world model capable of accurately predicting future visual outcomes (video frames) conditioned specifically on these predicted visual tracks, rather than directly on raw robot actions.
- To integrate a vision-language reward model that can effectively evaluate these track-conditioned visual predictions against a high-level language goal.
- To demonstrate that this track-mediated decision-making process improves future prediction quality and leads to higher success rates in diverse robot manipulation tasks, including those requiring generalization across distribution shifts and different robot embodiments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TrAct is designed as a world-model-based robot decision-making framework that leverages visual tracks as an intermediate interface between control and prediction. The framework operates in three main stages during inference: generating candidate action–track pairs, rolling out their visual consequences, and scoring these outcomes for action selection.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework Structure (TrAct)

Given an observation $o_t$ and a language instruction $l$, TrAct selects an action $a_t$ that is predicted to maximize task success. The three stages are:
1. **Candidate Generation:** A Vision-Language-Action-and-Track (VLAT) model proposes multiple candidate actions and their corresponding visual tracks.
2. **Visual Rollout:** A track-conditioned world model (TWM) predicts future video frames for each proposed set of tracks.
3. **Outcome Scoring:** A Vision-Language Reward Model (VLAC) evaluates the predicted visual outcomes against the language instruction to select the best action.

#### 3.2. Policy Architecture

- **Vision-Language-Action-and-Track (VLAT) Policy:**
 - VLAT is built upon a pre-trained flow-matching VLA policy, specifically $\pi_{0.5}$.
 - The action head of this policy is modified to output both an executable robot action chunk ($a_i \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d_a}$, where $H$ is the horizon and $d_a$ is the action dimension) and a set of $N$ 2D point tracks ($\tau_i \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times H \times 2}$) over the same horizon.
 - It is optimized using a joint flow-matching objective, $L_{\text{VLAT}} = L_{\text{flow}}(a, a^*) + \lambda L_{\text{flow}}(\tau, \tau^*)$, where $a^*$ and $\tau^*$ are ground-truth actions and tracks.
 - Tracks are predicted for two types of points: (1) key mesh vertices of the robotic gripper and (2) uniformly sampled background and object points on a 5x5 grid.
 - An asymmetric strategy is employed for camera views: the agent-view head predicts only gripper mesh vertices, while the wrist-view head predicts both gripper vertices and grid-sampled scene points.

- **Track-Conditioned World Model (TWM):**
 - The video generation backbone is Stable Video Diffusion (SVD).
 - To condition video generation on predicted tracks, SVD is augmented with a ControlNet branch. This branch encodes the track coordinates as spatial control maps.
 - Given an observation $o_t$ and predicted tracks $\tau_i$, the world model generates a future video $\hat{v}_i$.
 - For multi-view observations, view-specific tracks are rendered into separate ControlNet channels (e.g., agent-view tracks in red, wrist-view tracks in blue) to aid cross-view temporal consistency.

- **Action-Conditioned World Model (AWM) (for baseline comparison):**
 - This variant replaces track conditioning with action conditioning.
 - A predicted action chunk $a_i$ is encoded using an MLP into the CLIP-L/14 feature space.
 - A learnable camera-view embedding is concatenated to the encoded action latent to support multi-view observations.
 - This view-aware action representation is injected into SVD's UNet layers via cross-attention.

- **Vision-Language Reward Model (VLAC):**
 - InternVL2 is utilized as the backbone for the reward model, following the VLAC framework.
 - This model scores the predicted video outcomes ($\hat{v}_i$) based on how well they align with the language instruction ($l$).

#### 3.3. Pretraining

- **VLAT:** Pretrained on large-scale real-world robot datasets, including 76K DROID trajectories and 150K EgoDex trajectories.
 - **Track Supervision:** Ground-truth gripper tracks are obtained by projecting predefined gripper points using robot poses and camera calibration. Scene dynamics are captured by uniformly sampling a 5x5 grid of background points and tracking them over time using CoTracker.
 - **Unified Track Slot Layout:** A fixed index in a view-specific track-token layout (e.g., gripper keypoint, grid-sampled point) is used, with masking for unavailable track types or camera streams. This enables pretraining on diverse datasets (robot, human, different camera setups) by providing a common interface for track supervision.
 - **Action Supervision:** Robot datasets supervise both the track head and the action head. EgoDex (human demonstrations) supervises only the track prediction head, as robot action labels are not available.
- **World Models (TWM and AWM):** Both models are pretrained on 76K DROID trajectories. During finetuning, they are trained on the same LIBERO and real-world datasets.

#### 3.4. Inference Procedure

1. VLAT proposes $K$ candidate action–track pairs $\{(a_i, \tau_i)\}_{i=1}^K$. Diversity is introduced by Temperature-Scaled Resampling (TSR) during flow-matching sampling.
2. The TWM generates a predicted video rollout $\hat{v}_i$ for each of the $K$ candidates, conditioned on its corresponding track $\tau_i$.
3. VLAC scores each rollout $\hat{v}_i$ against the language instruction $l$.
4. The action $a_{i^*}$ from the highest-scoring pair $i^*$ is selected and executed by the robot.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Benchmarks

- **LIBERO-INTEGRAL:** A newly proposed simulation benchmark combining robustness tasks from LIBERO-PRO and LIBERO-Plus (object, position, task generalization, camera viewpoints, robot initialization changes) with cross-embodiment transfer tasks (replacing Franka Panda with UR5).
- **Physical Robot Experiments:** A Franka Emika Panda robot with two RGB cameras (agent-view, wrist-view). Evaluated on five unseen manipulation tasks under both training and visually distinct backgrounds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TrAct involved both simulation and physical robot experiments, comparing its performance against baseline policies and action-conditioned world models.

#### 4.1. Video Generation Quality

- **Quantitative Metrics (Table 1):** On a range of standard video quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID, FVD), the Track-Conditioned World Model (TWM) consistently outperformed the Action-Conditioned World Model (AWM) across both simulation and real-world environments, and for both agent-view and wrist-view cameras. The improvements were substantial, particularly in simulation (e.g., PSNR improved from 15.12 to 24.51 on agent view, LPIPS reduced from 0.438 to 0.106).
- **Qualitative Observations (Figure 3, Figure 5):** TWM produced more spatially accurate and temporally coherent video predictions. AWM frequently exhibited issues such as incorrect gripper poses, disappearing objects, and inconsistencies in object positions across different camera views.

#### 4.2. Robot Experiment Results

- **Standard LIBERO Suites (Table 2):** On standard LIBERO benchmarks where existing methods already show high performance, TrAct achieved an average success rate of 98.3%, slightly outperforming VLAT (98.0%), VLAT+AWM (98.0%), and the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline (96.8%). This indicates that incorporating track prediction and selection did not degrade performance and offered marginal improvements on tasks that were nearly saturated.

- **LIBERO-INTEGRAL Benchmark (Table 3, Table 5):**
 - TrAct demonstrated substantial improvements in generalization on the proposed LIBERO-INTEGRAL benchmark, which includes diverse robustness and cross-embodiment transfer tasks.
 - TrAct achieved an average success rate of 0.55, which is a significant increase over the $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline (0.27), VLAT (0.44), and VLAT+AWM (0.49).
 - TrAct showed superior performance across individual generalization categories, including Object (0.60), Camera (0.60), RobotInit (0.65), and Cross-Embodiment (0.50) shifts, relative to other methods.
 - The consistent gains observed when replacing action conditioning with track conditioning (TrAct vs. VLAT+AWM) support the hypothesis that visual tracks provide a robust interface for generalization.

- **Physical Robot Experiments (Table 4, Figure 4):**
 - On five unseen physical manipulation tasks, TrAct achieved an average success rate of 0.76, outperforming VLAT+AWM (0.66), $\pi_{0.5}$+VLAC (0.65), VLAT (0.55), and $\pi_{0.5}$ (0.49).
 - The performance gap between TrAct and VLAT+AWM was more pronounced under visually distinct, unseen backgrounds. For instance, in the "Close the cabinet door" task with an unseen background, VLAT+AWM dropped to 0.4 (matching VLAT), while TrAct maintained 0.7. Similar gains of approximately 20 percentage points were observed on other tasks under domain shift.
 - These results suggest that track-conditioned video generation maintains robustness under visual domain shifts, as tracks anchor the world model to image-space motion that is less sensitive to background changes, contrasting with action-conditioned generation which appears to degrade.

#### 4.3. Appendix Results

- **Statistical Robustness (Table 6):** Re-evaluation of VLAT+AWM and TrAct on LIBERO-INTEGRAL across three independent seeds confirmed that TrAct's improvement over VLAT+AWM was statistically significant, with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals.
- **Candidate Scaling (Table 7):** Varying the number of sampled candidates (K) for action selection demonstrated that success rates improved monotonically with K, but with diminishing returns. K=5 already recovered most of the benefit, rising from 0.52 to 0.54 with K=10, and to 0.55 with K=20.
- **Larger Pretraining Mixture (Table 8):** A stronger TrAct variant (TrAct+) pretrained on a larger mixture of DROID, BridgeData V2, and the full EgoDex dataset showed further improved performance on challenging UR5 cross-embodiment tasks (e.g., average success rate of 0.38 for TrAct+ vs. 0.32 for TrAct on the five most difficult UR5 tasks), indicating benefits from scaling pretraining data diversity and coverage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "TrAct" demonstrates that visual tracks offer a significant contribution to robot learning, with several areas of potential impact:

1. **Enhanced World Model Accuracy:** By conditioning world models on visual tracks rather than raw robot actions, TrAct enables the generation of more spatially accurate and temporally coherent future video predictions. This addresses a fundamental limitation of previous action-conditioned world models, which often struggled with under-determination and visual inconsistencies. More precise future prediction can lead to better anticipation of consequences and more informed decision-making for robots.

2. **Improved Robot Generalization:** The use of visual tracks, being an embodiment-agnostic representation, facilitates generalization across diverse task variations, unseen visual environments (domain shifts), and different robot embodiments (e.g., Franka to UR5). This is a critical step towards developing more robust and versatile robot policies capable of operating effectively in real-world, dynamic, and varied settings without extensive retraining.

3. **Robust Closed-Loop Control:** TrAct integrates visual tracks into a closed-loop decision-making framework, where predicted visual outcomes are evaluated against language instructions. This reward-guided action selection, grounded in predicted image-space dynamics, provides a more reliable mechanism for robots to adapt to task objectives and recover from unexpected situations, compared to open-loop policies.

4. **Leveraging Heterogeneous Data:** The unified track representation allows TrAct to be pretrained on a diverse range of large-scale datasets, including both robot demonstrations with executable actions and human egocentric manipulation videos without direct robot control. This ability to integrate and learn from heterogeneous data sources can accelerate the development of generalist robot policies by leveraging broader visual motion knowledge.

5. **New Interface for Robot-AI Interaction:** Visual tracks establish a practical and effective "bridge" between the embodiment-specific nature of robot control and the embodiment-agnostic nature of visual prediction. This intermediate interface could inspire future research in robot-AI interaction, where high-level goals are translated into visual trajectories that guide low-level robot actions.

In summary, TrAct provides a foundational framework for improving robot decision-making by demonstrating the effectiveness of visual tracks as a robust, intermediate representation. This approach has implications for advancing the state-of-the-art in robot world modeling, generalization, and reliable autonomous operation in complex environments.
