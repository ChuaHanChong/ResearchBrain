# AtomBridge: Agentic VLA Inference Plugin for Long-Horizon Tasks in Scientific Experiments

> [!summary] Summary
> AtomBridge is an inference-time plugin that composes frozen VLA policies fine-tuned only on atomic laboratory tasks into long-horizon scientific workflows. A CLIP-based completion switch detects task boundaries, a retrieval tool pulls a pre-contact ready state from one stored demonstration, and LLM planning plus coding agents emit collision-checked transition code. Full-sequence success rises from zero to 10-25% on 8-step composed tasks.

## Problem

- VLA policies fine-tuned on atomic laboratory tasks execute each skill well yet fail when those same skills are reordered and chained into composed sequences.
- A skill-chaining gap arises because one skill's terminal robot-state distribution falls outside the valid initial-state distribution the next skill was trained on.
- Fixed manually programmed laboratory automation cannot reconfigure task order or adapt parameters, while prior chaining fixes need retraining, connector policies, or long-horizon demonstrations.

## Method

- **AtomBridge**'s ==intervention switch==: a frozen ==CLIP== image-text encoder scores completion per frame, firing after w consecutive scores exceed threshold, with a per-task timeout fallback.
- ==Ready-state retrieval==: a task-conditioned visual judge plus deterministic sliding-window search over one stored demonstration returns a pre-contact state near the last frame before manipulation.
- ==Transition generation==: planning and coding agents turn current state, retrieved ready state, and calibrated camera views into template Python motion commands gated by schema, limit, and ==collision checks==.

## Results

- Across 60 post-initial atomic-task comparisons in simulation, **53** improved, **2** tied, **5** dropped; full-sequence success was nonzero in **11 of 12** task-model configurations versus **0/20** for every baseline.
- Real thermal-cycler five-step task: the plugin lifted full-sequence success from **0/10** to **4/10**, with gains confined to the boundaries needing a state transition.
- Ablation on the D.1 to D.2 transition: full pipeline **19/20** with **18.00** contact pair-samples, versus **10/20** without collision check and **0/20** without ready-state retrieval.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Composition failure in skill libraries is often a state-distribution mismatch at boundaries, not a deficit in the individual skills themselves.
> - Inserting explicit, verified transitions at inference time can unlock long-horizon behavior from frozen policies without new data or retraining.
> - Transition repair alone does not fix per-skill errors, non-robot object state, or remote LLM latency, which dominated task time here.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the evolving paradigm of AI4Science, which is shifting from single-model approaches to embedding artificial intelligence directly into comprehensive scientific workflows. Robotic laboratories are identified as a critical component in this advancement, offering capabilities for high-precision control, strong reproducibility, and large-scale, continuous experimental execution, thereby accelerating scientific discovery and mitigating human exposure to hazardous conditions.

Traditional robotic laboratories typically rely on manually programmed, fixed experimental procedures, which, while offering stability for high-precision tasks like high-throughput chemical screening, limit flexibility. These rigid workflows hinder the robot's ability to reconfigure task sequences or adapt to unforeseen experimental conditions in open-ended settings.

Recent developments in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models offer a more flexible approach, enabling robots to interpret natural language instructions and dynamically switch between learned manipulation skills. Given that the set of atomic experimental procedures in scientific domains is often fixed and finite, VLA models can be fine-tuned on data collected for these atomic tasks. The challenge then becomes composing and reordering these atomic tasks into complex, long-horizon experimental protocols.

The paper identifies a specific limitation in current VLA models: a "skill-chaining gap" caused by robot-state mismatch. Even when VLA models are proficient at executing individual atomic tasks, the terminal robot state of one task may fall outside the valid initial-state distribution of the subsequent task. This mismatch prevents the smooth transition and successful execution of composed long-horizon sequences.

The work addresses this skill-chaining gap, differentiating itself from existing approaches that focus on error accumulation, task decomposition, or learning hierarchical policies. While some prior research explores backward-reachable options, relay policies, or state-distribution alignment, this paper proposes a method that explicitly generates task-conditioned transitions between fixed VLA policies at inference time, without requiring additional VLA fine-tuning or demonstrations of composed sequences. This places AtomBridge within the research area of robust and flexible robot skill composition for complex, multi-step tasks, particularly in scientific automation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to reliably execute long-horizon tasks in scientific experiments. These tasks are typically composed of multiple atomic manipulation skills. The paper identifies a significant challenge that prevents current VLA models from achieving this objective: the "skill-chaining gap."

The motivation stems from the observation that while VLA models, when fine-tuned on individual atomic tasks, can execute these tasks competently, they often fail when these atomic tasks are sequentially composed into a longer workflow. This failure is attributed to a robot-state mismatch: the terminal state of a robot after completing one atomic task may not align with the valid initial-state distribution required for the subsequent atomic task. For instance, after "opening a thermal cycler," the robot's end-effector might be in a position that is not suitable for immediately "placing a PCR plate" without an intermediate adjustment. This mismatch leads to execution failures and prevents the continuous, autonomous operation necessary for advanced robotic laboratories.

To address this, the specific objectives are:
1. **Identify and Define the Skill-Chaining Gap:** Characterize the phenomenon where the terminal robot state of a pre-task falls outside the valid initial-state distribution of the post-task, impeding inter-skill transitions. The paper formalizes this by defining the "ready state" as a pre-manipulation state within the valid initial-state distribution of a subsequent atomic task.
2. **Develop an Inference-Time Plugin:** Create a modular, agentic plugin, named AtomBridge, that can be attached to existing VLA policies (which are fine-tuned only on atomic tasks) without requiring modifications to their weights or additional training data for long-horizon sequences.
3. **Generate Transitional Actions:** Implement a mechanism within AtomBridge to detect task completion, identify the required "ready state" for the next task, and generate constrained transitional robotic actions (code) to bridge the robot-state mismatch between consecutive atomic tasks.
4. **Enhance Execution Continuity and Success:** Demonstrate that this plug-and-play design improves the continuity of task execution and the success rate of individual atomic steps within long-horizon sequences, ultimately leading to a higher full-sequence success rate in both simulated and real-world scientific experimental environments.

The overarching motivation is to advance autonomous scientific discovery by making robotic laboratories more flexible and capable of executing complex, reconfigurable experimental protocols without extensive manual programming or retraining for every new task composition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AtomBridge is proposed as an agentic, plug-and-play inference plugin designed to mitigate the skill-chaining gap in VLA models for long-horizon scientific manipulation tasks. It operates by inserting transitional actions between consecutive atomic tasks without requiring fine-tuning or retraining of the underlying VLA policy.

The methodology can be broken down into the following key components:

#### 3.1. Core Concept: Skill-Chaining Gap and Ready State

The paper formally defines the skill-chaining gap as the mismatch between the terminal-state distribution of a VLA policy after completing atomic task $\tau_i$ ($p_{\text{end}}^\pi(s | \tau_i)$) and the valid initial-state distribution required for the subsequent atomic task $\tau_{i+1}$ ($p_{\text{ready}}^D(s | \tau_{i+1})$).
A "ready state" is defined as any state prior to the robot beginning the task-specific manipulation or contact with the target, ensuring it is within the valid initial-state distribution of the next atomic task. The goal of AtomBridge is to generate a transitional action sequence, $a_{\text{tr},i}$, that moves the robot from the current terminal state $s_{\text{cur}}$ of $\tau_i$ to a valid ready state $s_{\text{ready}}$ for $\tau_{i+1}$.

#### 3.2. System Architecture (Fig. 2)

AtomBridge intervenes at task boundaries during the inference flow of a long-horizon task. The process follows a completion-transition-resume cycle:
1. A VLA policy executes an atomic task.
2. An **Intervention Switch** detects task completion or a timeout.
3. AtomBridge then generates and executes transitional actions.
4. The next VLA policy resumes execution.

#### 3.3. Intervention Switch (Section 3.1)

The switch determines when an atomic task is complete and when AtomBridge should intervene.
- **Input:** Initial image frame ($I_0$), current image frame ($I_t$), and task description ($\tau_i$).
- **Completion Prediction:** A frozen CLIP image-text encoder with trainable parameters ($f_\theta$) outputs a completion probability $p_t$ by comparing $I_0$, $I_t$, and $\tau_i$.
- **Activation Criteria:** The switch activates ($z_t = 1$) if either:
 - `w` consecutive predictions ($p_j$) exceed a predefined completion threshold ($\eta$). This temporal condition suppresses transient false positives.
 - The maximum execution time ($H_i$) for task $\tau_i$ has been reached, serving as a fallback.
- Upon activation, the current VLA policy is paused, AtomBridge's transition generation module is invoked, and the history is reset before the next VLA policy starts.

#### 3.4. Transition Generation (Section 3.2)

This module is responsible for creating the necessary actions to bridge the robot-state mismatch.
- **Memory (M):** AtomBridge maintains an offline memory for each atomic task. This memory stores one demonstration containing the task description, observations, and corresponding robot states.
- **Ready-State Retrieval Tool:** For the next atomic task $\tau_{i+1}$, this tool searches the matched memory (M) to retrieve a suitable ready state ($s_{\text{ready}}$).
 - A task-conditioned visual judge, along with a fast sliding-window search algorithm, identifies the last pre-operation frame ($r$) in the demonstration, defining the onset of contact or manipulation.
 - The search ensures the retrieved state is a usable pre-contact state, avoiding states where manipulation has already begun or the gripper configuration is unsuitable.
- **Planning Agent:** Given the robot's current state ($s_{\text{cur}}$), the retrieved ready state ($s_{\text{ready}}$), calibrated camera views (V), and calibration parameters ($\kappa$), an LLM-based planning agent determines the transition trajectory.
- **Coding Agent:** The planning agent's output is then converted by an LLM-based coding agent into template Python commands. These commands represent the sequence of actions ($a_{\text{tr},i}$) the robot needs to execute to reach $s_{\text{ready}}$.
- **Action Execution and Constraints:** The generated code consists of fixed motion commands (e.g., `open_gripper`, `translate`, `rotate`). These commands are subject to schema, limit, transition-only, and collision checks by the host system to ensure safe and valid execution.

By integrating these components, AtomBridge aims to provide a robust and flexible way to compose complex scientific experiments from learned atomic skills without requiring extensive re-training or explicit demonstrations of every possible long-horizon sequence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated AtomBridge's performance in both simulated and real-world scientific manipulation tasks, comparing it against baseline VLA policies.

#### 4.1. Scientific Operation Results (Simulation)

- **Base Policies:** The study used $\pi^0$ and $\pi^{0.5}$ VLA models as base policies.
- **Baselines:** Two baselines were established:
 - **Vanilla:** Consecutive execution of atomic-task policies without any inserted transitions.
 - **MP (Motion Planning):** Inserts transitions generated by conventional motion planning (bidirectional joint-space RRT-Connect) between atomic tasks. Its ready state is randomly sampled from initial states of the matched next atomic task.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Seven long-horizon task suites (A-G) were defined, ranging from 5 to 8 atomic steps, representing various scientific workflows (e.g., experiment execution, collection).
- **Per-Atomic-Task Success (Table 1):** AtomBridge consistently improved the success rates of later atomic tasks compared to the matched baselines. Across 60 comparisons for Atomic Task 2 onwards, AtomBridge showed an increase in 53 cases, no change in 2, and a decrease in 5.
- **Full-Sequence Success (Table 1):**
 - All baseline configurations (Vanilla and MP, with both $\pi^0$ and $\pi^{0.5}$) achieved 0/20 full-sequence success. This indicates a complete failure to execute any long-horizon task from start to finish without intervention.
 - AtomBridge demonstrated non-zero full-sequence success in 11 out of 12 task-model configurations, with rates ranging from 2/20 to 14/20.
 - AtomBridge-$\pi^{0.5}$ generally achieved higher full-sequence success rates than AtomBridge-$\pi^0$ in five of the six long tasks, suggesting better compatibility with the more advanced VLA model. Task B was an exception, where AtomBridge-$\pi^0$ outperformed AtomBridge-$\pi^{0.5}$.
 - These results collectively indicate that AtomBridge improves execution continuity across atomic tasks, even if it does not eliminate all failures attributable to the individual atomic policies themselves.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiment (Thermal Cycler, Task G)

- A real-world evaluation was conducted on a five-step thermal-cycler task (Task G).
- **Results (Table 2):**
 - AtomBridge-$\pi^{0.5}$ achieved a 4/10 full-sequence success rate, and also 4/10 success for Atomic Tasks 3, 4, and 5.
 - The vanilla $\pi^{0.5}$ policy achieved 0/10 for Atomic Tasks 3, 4, and 5, and thus 0/10 full-sequence success.
 - Both methods recorded 5/10 success for Atomic Task 2. This is attributed to a close match between the terminal state of Task 1 and the initial state of Task 2 in the collected demonstrations, requiring less intervention.
- These real-environment findings support the efficacy of AtomBridge in providing state transitions at task boundaries where an explicit adjustment is required.

#### 4.3. Completion Model Evaluation (Thermal Cycler Atomic Tasks)

- The completion model, which triggers AtomBridge's intervention, was evaluated on 70 episodes across seven thermal-cycler atomic tasks (34,872 frames).
- **Overall Performance:** The model achieved 92.2% accuracy, 59.1% precision, and 84.7% recall across all frames.
- **Task-Specific Performance (Fig. 4a):**
 - High accuracy, precision, and recall were observed for tasks with persistent end states (e.g., "closing the lid": 99.8% accuracy, 96.2% precision, 100% recall; "removing the PCR plate": 99.9% accuracy, 99.6% precision, 99.9% recall).
 - Tasks involving "placement," "button pressing," and "knob tightening" showed perfect recall but lower precision, indicating a tendency for early completion predictions.
 - The "open-lid" task exhibited 96.7% accuracy but zero precision and recall due to immediate termination of data collection after the lid opened, resulting in a very short positive segment.
- **Temporal Error Distribution (Fig. 4b-d):** Heatmaps showed that ground-truth positives concentrated at the end of trajectories. False positives often occurred before the terminal region for certain tasks (e.g., PCR-plate placement), while false negatives occurred at the end of others (e.g., lid opening). A temporal window of 10 consecutive positive predictions was used to mitigate premature predictions.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Task D.1 $\rightarrow$ D.2 Transition)

- Ablation on the D.1 $\rightarrow$ D.2 transition (instrument opening and high-precision placement) compared the complete pipeline with variants (Table 3).
- **"No-check" (no collision check or ready retrieval):** Achieved 10/20 conditional D.2 success with 165.35 Contact Pair-Samples.
- **"No-retrieval" (no ready retrieval):** Achieved 0/20 conditional D.2 success with 82.50 Contact Pair-Samples.
- **Complete Pipeline ("check+retrieval"):** Achieved the highest conditional D.2 success rate (19/20) and the fewest Contact Pair-Samples (18.00). This indicates the importance of both ready-state retrieval and collision checking for successful and safer transitions.

#### 4.5. Latency and Generation Quality (Task D.1 $\rightarrow$ D.2 Transition)

- A comparison of proprietary GPT-5.2 and three open-source Qwen3.5 models was conducted over 10 trials (Table 4).
- **GPT-5.2:** Demonstrated the highest D.2 success rate (10/10) and the shortest transition generation time (38s).
- **Qwen3.5 Variants:**
 - Qwen3.5-397B-A17B: 8/10 success, 185s generation time.
 - Qwen3.5-35B-A3B: 7/10 success, 69s generation time.
 - Qwen3.5-9B: 5/10 success, 55s generation time.
- These results indicate a trade-off between latency and generation quality within the Qwen3.5 family, with larger models generally performing better but requiring more time.

In summary, AtomBridge consistently improved the success rates of long-horizon tasks in scientific experiments by effectively bridging skill-chaining gaps, demonstrating its utility over baselines in both simulation and real-world scenarios. The performance of its individual components, such as the completion model and LLM agents, also contributed to its overall effectiveness, with specific considerations for latency and precision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AtomBridge presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly within the context of autonomous scientific discovery and robotic laboratories. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Long-Horizon Tasks for VLA Models:** AtomBridge directly addresses a critical limitation of existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which often struggle with the sequential composition of atomic tasks into long-horizon workflows due to robot-state mismatch. By successfully bridging the "skill-chaining gap," the work allows VLA models to realize their potential for executing complex, multi-step scientific protocols.
- **Enhanced Autonomy in Robotic Laboratories:** The ability to execute long-horizon tasks reliably and autonomously reduces the need for human intervention. This can accelerate the pace of scientific experimentation, facilitate high-throughput research, and enable continuous, unattended operation of robotic labs. Such automation is crucial for domains like materials discovery, drug screening, and biological research, where large-scale, reproducible experiments are essential.
- **Plug-and-Play Modularity:** A key aspect of AtomBridge is its inference-time, plug-and-play design. This means it can be integrated with existing VLA policies that have been fine-tuned for atomic tasks without requiring additional retraining or the collection of extensive demonstrations for every possible long-horizon sequence. This modularity makes the approach highly adaptable and resource-efficient, lowering the barrier to deploying VLA models for complex tasks.
- **Improved Safety and Reliability:** The incorporation of ready-state retrieval and collision checking within the transition generation mechanism contributes to safer robot operation. By guiding the robot to a valid pre-contact state and actively checking for collisions during transitions, AtomBridge helps prevent errors and potential damage to equipment or samples, which is particularly important in high-value scientific experiments.
- **Advancement in Agentic AI for Robotics:** The use of LLM-based planning and coding agents for transition generation showcases the growing capability of agentic AI in robotics. It demonstrates that large language models can perform abstract reasoning and generate executable robot code to solve unforeseen state mismatches, adding a layer of intelligence and adaptability to robotic systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies and formalizes the concept of the "ready state" and the skill-chaining gap in VLA contexts. This conceptual clarity can serve as a foundation for further research into robust skill composition, state-space alignment, and hierarchical control in robotics.
- **Limitations and Future Directions:** The paper also acknowledges current limitations, such as AtomBridge's focus primarily on robot states rather than non-robot states (e.g., manipulated objects, instrument configurations) and the latency of remote LLM inference. Addressing these areas in future work could further broaden the impact, enhance the robustness, and accelerate the real-world deployment of such agentic systems in scientific discovery. For example, explicitly reasoning about and restoring object states could enable recovery from a wider range of experimental errors or unexpected environmental changes. Improving the robustness of visual completion detection under challenging real-world conditions like occlusion would also increase the system's reliability.

In conclusion, AtomBridge offers a practical and effective solution for a fundamental challenge in robotic task composition, significantly advancing the capabilities of VLA models in autonomous scientific laboratories and contributing to the broader development of intelligent robotic systems.
