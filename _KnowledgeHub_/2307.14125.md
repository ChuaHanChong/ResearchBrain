# Multi-IMU Proprioceptive State Estimator for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> A multi-IMU proprioceptive state estimator, developed by researchers at Wandercraft and Mines Paris, tracks humanoid robot floating base pose and velocity, and individual link poses, accommodating dynamic gaits with heel-toe motion. The ESEKF-based approach demonstrated an order of magnitude lower vertical drift (e.g., 0.3 mm Average Vertical Drift per Step) and improved overall trajectory accuracy compared to single-IMU methods on a self-balanced leg exoskeleton.

## Problem

- Existing proprioceptive state estimators for humanoid robots assume constant, flat foot pose during ground contact, which is often violated in dynamic walking (e.g., heel-toe motion) and reduces estimation accuracy.
- Most prior methods rely on a single IMU on the floating base, limiting their ability to track individual link movements or account for structural deformations.
- Inaccuracies in the robot's kinematic model due to structural deformations (e.g., joint compliance) degrade the performance of state estimators that rely on these models.

## Method

- An ==Error State Extended Kalman Filter (ESEKF)== framework is implemented to fuse data from multiple IMUs (on pelvis, tibias, and feet), joint encoders, and force sensors.
- The filter employs distinct prediction models for "floating" and "contact" links, with contact links modeled as rotating around their ==Center of Pressure==, allowing for dynamic foot poses.
- Correction steps utilize tilt measurements from contact links and relative pose measurements between links, derived from an ==extended kinematic model== that incorporates estimated structural deformations.

## Results

- In straight-line walking, the 5-IMU-EKM achieved an Average Vertical Drift per Step (AVDS) of **0.3 mm**, an order of magnitude lower than single-IMU baselines (**3.0 mm** and **2.2 mm**).
- During dynamic circular walking with heel-toe motion, the proposed method maintained an AVDS of **0.3 mm** and showed reduced yaw drift (**2 degrees** vs. over **6 degrees**) compared to single-IMU filters.
- Elevation maps constructed using the 5-IMU-EKM trajectory from LiDAR data were significantly more accurate and undistorted, closely matching ground truth, unlike the distorted maps produced by single-IMU estimates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling dynamic foot movement during ground contact using multi-IMU data allows for continuous and accurate use of contact information, even during heel-toe gaits.
> - Leveraging multiple IMUs across the robot's structure, combined with an extended kinematic model that estimates deformations, significantly improves state estimation accuracy by accounting for non-rigid body behavior.
> - The multi-IMU ESEKF architecture offers benefits beyond just an extended kinematic model, providing superior performance metrics compared to single-IMU filters, especially in vertical drift and overall trajectory precision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate state estimation, encompassing floating base pose and velocity, is fundamental for the control, motion planning, and mapping capabilities of humanoid robots. While vision-based methods exist, proprioceptive sensor-based solutions are often favored for their high-frequency operation and insensitivity to environmental conditions such as lighting changes. A common approach in this domain involves fusing data from an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), leg kinematics, and contact information using an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF).

Early work by Bloesch et al. introduced a state estimator for legged robots, which included foothold positions in the filter's state, integrating IMU data and updating estimates using foot positions derived from joint encoders. This was extended by Rotella et al. for humanoid robots, incorporating foot orientation into the state to accommodate large, flat feet, utilizing the Error State Extended Kalman Filter (ESEKF) framework. Variations based on the Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InvEKF), which offers improved convergence and consistency properties, have also been proposed for humanoid robots with flat feet by Hartley et al., Qin et al., and Ramadoss et al..

A pervasive limitation across these prior state estimators for humanoid robots is the assumption that the only link instrumented with an IMU is the floating base (e.g., pelvis). Consequently, these methods typically assume that the pose of the feet remains constant (flat and fixed) while in contact with the ground. This assumption is frequently violated during dynamic walking, especially with human-like gaits that involve heel-toe motion. Such violations either restrict the duration for which contact information can be reliably used or necessitate higher variance estimates to compensate for potential errors, thereby reducing overall estimation accuracy.

The presented work directly addresses this limitation by introducing a multi-IMU state estimator that explicitly models the movement of the feet during ground contact. By incorporating IMUs on the feet and other links, the method aims to exploit contact information more continuously and accurately, including during heel-toe motions. Furthermore, the multi-IMU setup is utilized to estimate structural deformations in the robot, building upon previous work by Vigne et al. (MOVIE), which aims to enhance the kinematic model used within the filter. This approach represents an extension of single-IMU ESEKF methods like that of Rotella et al. to a multi-IMU configuration, offering a more robust and adaptable state estimation solution for dynamic humanoid locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel state estimator for humanoid robots that can accurately determine their floating base pose and velocity, along with the pose and velocity of other instrumented links, particularly during dynamic and anthropomorphic gaits.

The motivation for this development stems from several limitations observed in existing state estimation approaches for humanoid robots:

1. **Addressing the Constant Foot Pose Assumption:** Traditional state estimators for humanoid robots often assume that the feet remain flat on the ground and in a constant position while in contact. This hypothesis simplifies the contact model but is frequently violated during natural walking, especially for gaits that involve heel-toe motion, where the foot's orientation and point of contact continuously change. This violation reduces the time intervals during which reliable contact information can be utilized or necessitates the use of higher variances in the filter, leading to reduced accuracy. The research is motivated to overcome this by enabling the estimator to properly handle any contact configuration, including those with non-flat or dynamically changing foot poses.
2. **Exploiting Multi-IMU Instrumentation:** Existing methods typically rely on a single IMU located on the floating base. This work is motivated to leverage a multi-IMU setup, with IMUs distributed throughout the robot's structure, including on each foot. This instrumentation provides the necessary sensory data to track the individual movements of multiple robot bodies, allowing for a more comprehensive and accurate state representation.
3. **Improving Kinematic Model Accuracy through Deformation Estimation:** Mechanical structures of robots, particularly complex humanoids and exoskeletons, are subject to deformations due to their own weight, payload, and operational forces. These deformations introduce inaccuracies into the robot's kinematic model, which in turn degrades the performance of state estimators that rely on these models. A key motivation is to use the multi-IMU configuration to estimate these structural deformations (similar to methods like MOVIE), thereby improving the accuracy of the kinematic model utilized in the filter.
4. **Enhancing Robustness to Dynamic Gaits:** The ability to accurately estimate state during dynamic, human-like gaits, such as those involving significant heel-toe motion, is crucial for the development of highly capable humanoid robots and exoskeletons. The motivation is to develop an estimator that exhibits low drift and maintains accuracy even under these challenging conditions, where traditional filters may experience significant performance degradation.
5. **Enabling Downstream Applications:** Accurate state estimates are prerequisite for many high-level robotic functionalities, including stable control, precise motion planning, and robust environmental mapping. By providing low-drift and accurate trajectories, the research aims to enable more reliable and precise performance in these downstream applications, such as constructing undistorted elevation maps for navigation.

In summary, the objective is to create a more versatile and accurate proprioceptive state estimator for humanoid robots that is robust to dynamic contact conditions and structural deformations, ultimately enabling enhanced robot autonomy and functionality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed multi-IMU state estimator is based on the Error State Extended Kalman Filter (ESEKF) framework, designed to estimate the pose, velocity, and sensor biases of multiple robot links in a fixed inertial reference frame. The robot is assumed to be equipped with multiple IMUs (at least three, including one on each foot), joint encoders, and force sensors to determine the center of pressure (COP) on the feet.

**A. Estimation Framework (ESEKF)**

The ESEKF is employed to handle variables defined on manifolds (e.g., 3D orientations on SO(3) and IMU tilts on S^2). This involves defining perturbation ($\oplus_M$) and inverse ($\ominus_M$) operators for each manifold, which allow linearization and error propagation in a Euclidean space while operating on the manifold.
- For SO(3) (rotation matrices), the operators are defined using the matrix exponential and logarithm, mapping 3D vectors to skew-symmetric matrices.
- For S^2 (unit vectors/tilts), specific operators are defined using a 3x2 matrix B(u) spanning the subspace orthogonal to the unit vector u, and a rotation matrix Rot(u,v) representing the smallest rotation between two unit vectors.
These operators enable the definition of normal distributions on manifolds and allow for linear approximation of functions, with Jacobians capturing the local linear relationship.

The ESEKF operates in two main steps:
1. **Prediction:** Updates the state estimate ($\hat{x}$) and its covariance matrix (P) based on a system model and IMU measurements. The state is represented as $\hat{x} \oplus \delta X$, where $\hat{x}$ evolves according to a noiseless model, and $\delta X$ (the error state) is updated using a linearized model.
2. **Correction:** Refines the predicted state using external measurements. The measurement error is linearized with respect to the error state, and standard Kalman filter equations are applied to update the error state, which is then mapped back to the manifold.

**B. State Definition**

The full state for the filter, denoted as $x$, is a concatenation of individual link states $x_i$ for $N$ instrumented links. Each $x_i$ comprises:
- Orientation ($R_i \in SO(3)$)
- Position ($p_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$)
- Linear velocity ($v_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$)
- Gyroscope bias ($b_{g_i} \in \mathbb{R}^3$)
- Accelerometer bias ($b_{a_i} \in \mathbb{R}^3$)
The corresponding error state $\delta x_i = [\delta\theta_i \ \delta p_i \ \delta v_i \ \delta b_{g_i} \ \delta b_{a_i}]^T$ is a 15-dimensional vector. The full error state $\delta x$ is the concatenation of all $\delta x_i$.

**C. Prediction Step**

The prediction model for each link is chosen based on its contact status (floating or contact link) and operates independently for each link initially, with the full-state covariance matrix being updated later.
- **Floating Link Model:** For links not in contact with the ground, a standard inertial navigation model, similar to, is used. It integrates gyroscope ($u_{g_i}$) and accelerometer ($u_{a_i}$) measurements to update orientation, position, and velocity. Bias terms are modeled as Brownian motions. The continuous-time differential equations for the state and error state are derived, and discrete-time approximations are used for the prediction step.
- **Contact Link Model:** For feet in contact with the ground, the model assumes instantaneous rotation around their center of pressure (COP). The velocity of the link's origin is derived from its angular velocity (from the gyroscope) relative to the COP, assuming the COP's velocity is approximately zero with a small slippage noise ($\eta_{s_i}$). Notably, the accelerometer measurements are *not* directly used to compute the link's velocity in this model; instead, they are implicitly handled in the correction step via tilt measurements. The prediction for orientation and biases is similar to the floating link model, while position is updated based on the derived velocity. The state for contact links is effectively reduced for Jacobian computation during prediction, but full covariance terms are maintained for future transitions to floating status.

After independent updates, the full covariance matrix $P_k^-$ is computed by diagonally stacking the individual link's $A_k$ and $Q_k$ matrices and applying the EKF prediction formula.

**D. Correction Step**

The correction step uses two types of measurements, processed sequentially due to assumptions of independent noise. These measurements are results of fusion from encoders and IMUs by an external observer (MOVIE).
- **Tilt Measurements:** For each contact link $i$, the tilt ($R_i^T e_z \in S^2$, where $e_z$ is the gravity vector in the inertial frame) is used as a measurement. This measurement, defined on S^2, allows incorporating information from the accelerometers (implicitly via MOVIE's tilt estimation) that were not directly used for velocity updates in the contact link prediction model. The Jacobian matrices $C_{t_i}(\hat{x})$ and $D_{t_i}(\hat{x})$ are derived for the linear error state update.
- **Relative Pose Measurements:** These measurements provide the relative orientation ($R_i^T R_j \in SO(3)$) and position ($R_i^T (p_j - p_i) \in \mathbb{R}^3$) between any floating link $j$ and contact link $i$. These are derived from an *extended kinematic model* that incorporates estimations of structural deformations (e.g., at hip and ankle joints) provided by MOVIE. The uncertainties for these measurements originate from joint angle and deformation noise. These measurements ensure spatial consistency between the independently updated links. All relative pose measurements are processed simultaneously by stacking their respective Jacobian matrices $C_r(\hat{x})$ and $D_r(\hat{x})$ to compute a single Kalman gain.

**E. Experimental Setup**

The method was validated on the Atalante self-balanced leg exoskeleton. This robot features:
- 12 joint encoders.
- Five low-cost IMUs (LSM6DSO): one on the pelvis, one on each tibia, and one on each foot.
- Four 1D force sensors under each foot for contact detection and COP computation.
The MOVIE algorithm is utilized to estimate four structural deformations at the hip and ankle joints based on IMU and encoder data. The filter operates at 1 kHz in real-time. Ground truth for evaluation was provided by an OptiTrack motion capture system.

**F. Comparison Filters**

The proposed 5-IMU-EKM (estimating 5 IMU poses with an Extended Kinematic Model) was compared against two single-IMU filters (only estimating pelvis IMU pose):
- **1-IMU:** Uses a standard rigid kinematic model. Contact is detected when two diagonally opposed force sensors exceed a threshold, indicating a flat foot contact.
- **1-IMU-EKM:** Uses the same extended kinematic model with four deformations estimated by MOVIE, similar to 5-IMU-EKM. Contact detection is also based on two diagonally opposed force sensors.
- **5-IMU-EKM (Proposed):** A foot is considered in contact as soon as any force sensor exceeds a threshold, allowing for non-flat contact.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation on the Atalante exoskeleton involved two distinct gait patterns, comparing the proposed 5-IMU-EKM filter against two single-IMU baselines (1-IMU and 1-IMU-EKM). Performance was assessed using Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE), Relative Pose Error (RPE), and Average Vertical Drift per Step (AVDS).

**Experiment 1: Straight Trajectory**
- **Conditions:** The exoskeleton walked forward in a straight line for 3 meters at 0.15 m/s. The gait involved the foot touching flat but the heel lifting slightly before the toes.
- **Quantitative Results (Table I):**
 - **5-IMU-EKM:** RPE = 0.6 cm, ATE = 5.9 cm, AVDS = 0.3 mm.
 - **1-IMU:** RPE = 1.4 cm, ATE = 10.2 cm, AVDS = 3.0 mm.
 - **1-IMU-EKM:** RPE = 1.0 cm, ATE = 11.5 cm, AVDS = 2.2 mm.
- **Observations:**
 - The 5-IMU-EKM filter demonstrated superior performance across all metrics compared to both single-IMU filters.
 - A notable improvement was observed in the Z-direction, where the AVDS of 5-IMU-EKM was approximately one order of magnitude lower than the others.
 - Orientation errors were minimal for all filters, remaining below 0.5 degrees.
 - While 1-IMU-EKM showed improvements over 1-IMU in RPE and AVDS, its ATE was slightly higher. This suggests that the benefits of the proposed multi-IMU filter formulation extend beyond merely using an extended kinematic model.

**Experiment 2: Circular Trajectory with Heel-Toe Motion**
- **Conditions:** The exoskeleton walked forward at 0.4 m/s, completing one and a half turns in an approximately circular trajectory, covering a total distance of 11.5 meters. This gait involved significant heel-toe motion.
- **Quantitative Results (Table I):**
 - **5-IMU-EKM:** RPE = 2.5 cm, ATE = 14.9 cm, AVDS = 0.3 mm.
 - **1-IMU:** RPE = 3.6 cm, ATE = 19.3 cm, AVDS = 3.4 mm.
 - **1-IMU-EKM:** RPE = 3.0 cm, ATE = 21.4 cm, AVDS = 3.8 mm.
- **Observations:**
 - Similar to the first experiment, 5-IMU-EKM consistently outperformed the single-IMU filters on all metrics, particularly in the Z-direction.
 - The multi-IMU filter also exhibited reduced drift in the yaw angle, with a final error of approximately 2 degrees, compared to over 6 degrees for the single-IMU filters.
 - Despite the more dynamic motion and increased walking distance, the AVDS for 5-IMU-EKM remained consistent with that of the first experiment (0.3 mm). This suggests that the proposed filter formulation effectively captures and models the complex motion of the feet, even in the presence of pronounced heel-toe motion.
 - The consistent performance advantage of 5-IMU-EKM over 1-IMU-EKM further reinforces that the multi-IMU architecture and its associated contact modeling are key contributors to accuracy, rather than solely the extended kinematic model.

**LiDAR Elevation Map Construction**
- To demonstrate the practical implications of the trajectory accuracy, a Velarray M1600 solid-state LiDAR was attached to the exoskeleton's right knee during Experiment 2. Elevation maps were constructed based on the trajectory estimates from each filter and compared to ground truth obtained from a Faro Focus3D-X130 laser scanner.
- **Findings (Fig. 3):**
 - Elevation maps generated using trajectories from the single-IMU filters (1-IMU and 1-IMU-EKM) displayed noticeable distortions. Drift in the Z-direction resulted in height discrepancies across the map (indicated by varying floor colors), and errors in yaw and the XY plane led to misaligned map edges.
 - In contrast, the elevation map constructed using the 5-IMU-EKM trajectory closely resembled the ground truth. It maintained accurate height values and exhibited proper alignment of map edges, indicating a more precise and consistent localization.

These results indicate that the proposed multi-IMU state estimator provides more accurate and robust trajectory estimates, particularly in the vertical direction and during dynamic gaits with heel-toe motion, compared to single-IMU approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in proprioceptive state estimation for humanoid robots by addressing critical limitations inherent in many existing methods. The primary significance lies in its ability to accurately estimate robot state, including the floating base pose and velocity, under dynamic and anthropomorphic gait conditions that previously posed substantial challenges.

The potential impact of this work includes:

1. **Enhanced Robustness to Dynamic Locomotion:** By explicitly modeling the movement of robot feet through multi-IMU instrumentation, the estimator can handle "any contact configuration," including heel-toe motions. This capability liberates humanoid robots from the restrictive assumption of flat, static foot contact, enabling more natural, dynamic, and energy-efficient gaits. This is crucial for real-world applications where ground contact is rarely perfectly flat or constant.
2. **Reduced Drift in Trajectory Estimates:** The experimental results consistently demonstrate a substantial reduction in drift, particularly in the vertical (Z) direction and yaw angle, compared to single-IMU filters. This low-drift characteristic is fundamental for maintaining long-term localization accuracy and preventing error accumulation, which is a common challenge in inertial navigation systems.
3. **Improved Kinematic Model Accuracy:** The integration of multi-IMU data to estimate structural deformations (via MOVIE) enhances the fidelity of the robot's kinematic model. This improved model directly contributes to the filter's overall accuracy, as precise knowledge of relative link poses is critical for consistent state estimation.
4. **Enabling Advanced Downstream Applications:** The demonstrated accuracy of the trajectory estimates has a direct impact on higher-level robotic functionalities:
 - **High-Quality Terrain Mapping:** The ability to construct elevation maps with minimal distortion, as shown with LiDAR data, is vital for autonomous navigation, obstacle avoidance, and interaction with unstructured environments. Robots equipped with this estimator could generate more reliable environmental representations.
 - **Improved Control and Motion Planning:** Accurate and low-drift state estimates are a prerequisite for stable control algorithms and effective motion planning. This work could lead to more robust balance control, smoother trajectory generation, and safer interaction with the environment for humanoid robots and exoskeletons.
 - **Expanded Operational Domains:** By providing reliable state estimation during complex gaits, the technology could facilitate the deployment of humanoid robots and exoskeletons in more challenging and dynamic environments, such as uneven terrain, stairs, or industrial settings, where precise and adaptive locomotion is required.
5. **Broad Applicability to Multi-Body Systems:** While validated on an exoskeleton, the principles of multi-IMU state estimation and handling complex contact mechanics are applicable to a wide range of multi-body robotic systems, including various humanoid robots and legged platforms.

In essence, this research provides a foundational improvement in how humanoid robots perceive their own motion relative to the environment, paving the way for more capable, autonomous, and adaptable robotic systems.
