# TRACE: Ergodic Trajectory Optimization for Active Scene Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> TRACE, from Johns Hopkins University and the University of Pennsylvania, optimizes continuous, dynamically feasible trajectories for active scene reconstruction by employing an ergodic search driven by an online 2D Gaussian-Surfel map. This method achieved a 1.5 dB PSNR gain over baseline approaches on the Replica dataset and enabled direct execution on physical robotic platforms like the Unitree Go2 and Franka FR3 arm.

## Problem

- Next-Best-View (NBV) planners for active 3D reconstruction often select locally optimal, discrete viewpoints, leading to inefficient information gathering and redundant trajectories.
- The decoupled nature of NBV separates information gathering from motion, treating robot movement between views as mere transit, thus wasting sensing capacity.
- Conventional NBV planning typically ignores real-world robot dynamics, frequently generating jerky and dynamically infeasible trajectories for physical robots.

## Method

- **TRACE** formulates active reconstruction as a ==horizon-based ergodic trajectory optimization== problem, where a K-step trajectory of control inputs is continuously optimized.
- Dynamically derives the target information distribution online from a live ==2D Gaussian-Surfel (2DGS)== map and a voxel map, adapting to evolving scene uncertainty and visibility.
- Adapts kernel-based ergodic search to surface-based reconstruction by diffusing surface information into traversable free space, incorporating ==footprint-aware depletion== for coverage, and a ==gaze reward== to attract the camera to high-information surfaces.

## Results

- Achieved an average gain of **1.5 dB** PSNR, **0.013** SSIM, and **0.02** LPIPS over ActiveGS, the strongest NBV baseline, across eight indoor scenes from the Replica dataset.
- An ablation study demonstrated that removing footprint depletion and gaze mechanisms resulted in a **2.1 dB** PSNR loss, highlighting their critical role in effective information gathering.
- Successfully deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadruped and a Franka FR3 arm, generating dynamically feasible, collision-free trajectories that were directly executable by the robot controllers, with **100%** success rate in physical missions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous trajectory optimization allows for accessing highly informative poses that are not available through discrete viewpoint candidate selection, leading to improved reconstruction.
> - Footprint-aware depletion and gaze mechanisms are essential for preventing redundant observations within a planning horizon and effectively guiding the sensor towards under-confident or unexplored areas.
> - Integrating robot dynamics directly into the differentiable trajectory optimization objective generates smooth, control-feasible trajectories that are directly executable by physical robot controllers, enhancing real-world deployment reliability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Active 3D reconstruction is a core problem in robotic perception, involving the autonomous movement of a sensor to build a geometric model of an unknown scene. Recent advancements in differentiable rendering techniques, such as 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) and its surface-aligned variant 2DGS, have significantly improved the fidelity of reconstructable maps. This progress has shifted the primary bottleneck from the map representation itself to the planning strategies employed by the robotic agent to efficiently gather information within finite time, energy, and motion budgets.

A common paradigm in active reconstruction, particularly with Gaussian-splatting maps, is the "Next-Best-View" (NBV) approach. In this paradigm, planners typically operate view-by-view, where at each step, a single next-best viewpoint is selected based on an information-gain criterion. This chosen viewpoint is then connected to the robot's current location via short-horizon path planning. While some approaches introduce hierarchical structures or incorporate topological subgraphs, the fundamental mechanism often remains a commitment to discrete, individual viewpoints followed by a separate motion planning step.

This work addresses several limitations inherent in the decoupled NBV formulation. First, NBV's greedy nature often leads to short-term viewpoint scoring, selecting locally optimal poses without anticipating subsequent sensor travel. Second, the motion between selected discrete views primarily serves as transit, without contributing to active information gathering, leading to inefficient use of sensing capacity. This can result in jagged, redundant trajectories biased towards isolated high-information areas rather than uniform spatial coverage. Third, NBV planning, which typically optimizes over abstract viewpoints, often overlooks real-world robot dynamics such as controllability, actuator limits, and energy efficiency, frequently generating dynamically infeasible trajectories.

TRACE introduces an alternative by formulating active reconstruction as an ergodic coverage problem. Ergodic search aims for the time-averaged spatial statistics of a sensor trajectory to match a target information distribution across a given domain. Unlike traditional ergodic search methods that often assume a static, a priori target distribution, this work dynamically derives the target information distribution online from a live 2DGS map, accounting for evolving uncertainty and visibility. Furthermore, it adapts kernel-based ergodic search to surface-based reconstruction tasks, where information resides on surfaces that the robot cannot physically occupy, by diffusing surface information into traversable free space and incorporating footprint-aware depletion and gaze objectives. This represents a trajectory-level optimization approach, distinct from the viewpoint-level strategies predominant in NBV methods, and is presented as the first active 3D reconstruction method to plan continuous, control-feasible trajectories using ergodic search in conjunction with Gaussian-splatting maps.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an active 3D reconstruction system capable of generating continuous, dynamically feasible, and information-rich trajectories for a sensor, thereby improving reconstruction quality and operational efficiency compared to conventional Next-Best-View (NBV) planning.

The motivation for this work stems from the identified shortcomings of existing active reconstruction systems, particularly those utilizing Gaussian-splatting maps:

1. **Addressing Information Gathering Inefficiency**: Current NBV planners often optimize for discrete viewpoints, treating the motion between these points as mere transit. This approach can lead to a waste of the robot's sensing capacity during movement. The objective is to ensure that all motion contributes to information gathering by continuously optimizing the trajectory to align with the scene's information distribution.
2. **Overcoming Short-Term, Local Optimization**: NBV systems make decisions based on locally most informative poses without considering the global structure of scene information or anticipating future sensor movements. This can result in suboptimal overall coverage and biased trajectories. The motivation is to introduce a global planning strategy that optimizes trajectories for uniform and efficient spatial coverage of the scene's information.
3. **Ensuring Dynamic Feasibility**: Traditional NBV methods plan at the viewpoint level, often disregarding real-world robotic dynamics such as acceleration limits, motor constraints, or energy consumption. This can lead to jerky, dynamically infeasible trajectories that are difficult or impossible for physical robots to execute directly. A key objective is to generate trajectories that are inherently smooth, continuous, and control-feasible, directly accounting for robot dynamics within the planning process.
4. **Adapting to Evolving Information**: Standard ergodic search typically relies on a pre-defined, static target information distribution. However, in active reconstruction, the scene's information state (uncertainty, visibility of surfaces) evolves dynamically as new observations are integrated into the map. The work is motivated to develop a mechanism for online derivation of the target information distribution, enabling the planner to adapt to the live state of the map.
5. **Effective Surface-Based Coverage**: In 3D reconstruction, the information to be gathered is often located on object surfaces, which the sensor's physical body cannot occupy. The objective is to design a planning framework where the robot's *viewing footprint*, rather than its physical position, effectively covers high-information surfaces, while its body navigates collision-free space.
6. **Mitigating Redundant Sensing**: Repeated observations of already well-reconstructed regions yield diminishing returns. The research aims to incorporate a mechanism, such as footprint-overlap depletion, to discourage redundant coverage within a planning horizon and guide the sensor toward unexplored or uncertain areas.

By addressing these motivations, TRACE aims to provide a more integrated, efficient, and robust solution for active 3D reconstruction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TRACE formulates active scene reconstruction as a horizon-based ergodic trajectory optimization problem driven by an online 2D Gaussian-Surfel (2DGS) map.

**Problem Formulation**:
The system considers an autonomous agent equipped with an RGB-D sensor building a 2DGS map (`M_t`) of an unknown scene while also maintaining a voxel map (`V_t`) for occupancy probability. At each replanning step (horizon `t`), the planner optimizes a K-step trajectory `τ_t` consisting of positions and orientations. This trajectory is parameterized by control inputs (`u_t`), such as position velocity, yaw rate, and pitch rate, which are cumulatively integrated under single-integrator dynamics. The objective is to minimize an ergodic optimal-control cost function `J_t`, defined over a target information distribution `ϕ_t` derived from the current map. Hard constraints (e.g., collision-free paths, bounded step sizes) are relaxed into soft penalties within the objective function.

**Information Map Construction**:
TRACE employs a hybrid map representation:
- **2DGS Map (`M_t`)**: Used for rendering and detailed surface representation, updated at the end of each planning horizon.
- **Voxel Map (`V_t`)**: Represents occupancy probability, updated continuously using an OctoMap-like approach.
The raw information value `ϕ_raw_t(v)` for each voxel `v` is computed as a weighted sum of four components:
1. **Unexplored Volume**: `α_u * 1_unexp(v)` for voxels not yet traversed by any depth ray.
2. **Frontier Expansion**: `α_f * 1_front(v)` for free voxels bordering unexplored space.
3. **Unbuilt Surfaces**: `α_b * 1_unbuilt(v)` for voxels marked as occupied in `V_t` but lacking corresponding Gaussians in `M_t`, encouraging the sensor to fill in geometric holes.
4. **Low-Confidence Gaussians**: `β * (1 - c(v)) * 1_low(v)`, where `c(v)` is the mean rendering confidence of Gaussians within voxel `v`. This term attracts the sensor to areas where surface fit is weakly constrained.
The final target information distribution `ϕ_t` is obtained by box-filtering `ϕ_raw_t`, masking it to the observed collision-free regions of `V_t`, and normalizing it to a probability distribution. This masking projects information mass from physical surfaces onto the traversable free space. Both `ϕ_t` and `ϕ_raw_t` (unmasked, for gaze reward) are sampled at continuous locations via trilinear interpolation, ensuring differentiability.

**Kernel-Ergodic Trajectory with Sensor Footprint Depletion**:
The trajectory is planned using a kernel-ergodic metric, which aims to match the trajectory's time-averaged positions to the target distribution `ϕ`. This approach is adapted for active reconstruction through two primary mechanisms:
1. **Time-Varying `ϕ` via Footprint-Overlap Depletion**: To account for the diminishing marginal value of observing already covered regions, the static `ϕ_t(x_k_t)` is replaced by a coverage-discounted value `ϕ_k_t`. This `ϕ_k_t` incorporates a product of `(1 - η * v_jk)` for prior waypoints `j<k` within the current horizon. `η` is a per-visit discount rate, and `v_jk` is a soft footprint-overlap kernel that quantifies the overlap between the sensor footprints at waypoints `j` and `k`. The sensor footprint is approximated by multiple points sampled along the optical axis, making `v_jk` a differentiable proxy for surface co-visibility. This mechanism penalizes re-observing the same region within a horizon. Inter-horizon re-coverage is handled by updating `ϕ_t` from the revised map at each horizon boundary.
2. **Footprint-Based Information Attraction (Gaze Reward)**: As high-information mass often lies on scene surfaces (unreachable by the robot's body), a gaze reward `L_gaze` is introduced. This term averages the unmasked `ϕ_raw_t` over the sensor's footprint samples, attracting the camera's gaze towards high-information surfaces. A weighting term `w_k,d` attenuates footprint samples in unobservable or unsafe space, preventing the camera from being directed through obstacles.

**Full Cost and Finite-Horizon Trajectory Optimization**:
The total cost `J_t(u_t)` for trajectory optimization combines several components:
- **Depletion-aware ergodic metric (`E_dep_kernel`)**: Comprises an information term (sum of `ϕ_k_t`) and a self-correlation term that repels nearby waypoints to promote uniform coverage.
- **Gaze reward (`λ_g * L_gaze`)**: Directs the camera's view.
- **Soft-barrier penalties (`λ_s * L_safe`)**: Enforces collision avoidance, limits step sizes, and prevents out-of-bounds movement.
- **Quadratic control regularizer (`λ_r * ||u_t||^2`)**: Smooths control inputs.
All terms in `J_t` are differentiable with respect to the control inputs `u_t`. The optimization is performed using Adam, with the solution from the previous horizon serving as a warm start. The agent executes the entire K-step trajectory before replanning.

**Real Robot Experiments**:
The identical planner is deployed on two physical platforms:
- **Unitree Go2 quadruped**: For room-scale scene reconstruction, with trajectory constraints to the traversable plane and yaw aligned to motion direction. Waypoints are tracked by a velocity-level controller.
- **Franka FR3 arm**: For object-centric reconstruction, planning directly in joint space. A differentiable forward-kinematics chain maps joint configurations to camera poses, making reachability intrinsic. Collision penalties are applied to forward-kinematic body points. This enables dynamically consistent trajectory execution without an intermediate inverse kinematics solver or feasibility repair.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**Quantitative Performance on Replica Dataset:**
TRACE demonstrates a consistent improvement in reconstruction quality across eight indoor scenes from the Replica dataset:
- **PSNR**: Achieved an average gain of 1.5 dB PSNR over ActiveGS, the strongest NBV baseline, with positive gains observed on every scene. For instance, on the 'Of0' scene, TRACE reached 38.36 PSNR compared to ActiveGS's 36.78.
- **SSIM and LPIPS**: Showed improved structural similarity (higher SSIM) and reduced perceptual distance (lower LPIPS), indicating higher visual fidelity. TRACE's average SSIM was 0.938 compared to ActiveGS's 0.925, and average LPIPS was 0.123 compared to 0.143.
- **Analysis of Gains**: The performance advantage is attributed to two primary mechanisms: continuous-pose optimization, which allows the camera to reach highly informative poses not available to discrete candidates, and the footprint depletion mechanism, which discourages redundant observations and directs the sensor to under-confident regions. The LPIPS improvement on office scenes (6–19%) suggests gains in fine-scale appearance, while broader SSIM gaps on room and hotel scenes (0.012–0.017) indicate structural improvements from better coverage of occluded areas.

**Qualitative Results on Replica Dataset:**
Visual comparisons highlight the benefits of TRACE:
- **Surface Fidelity**: TRACE reconstructions exhibited sharper textures, more consistent lighting, and finer geometric details on furniture and wall surfaces.
- **Coverage**: ActiveGS frequently produced flatter, lower-fidelity reconstructions and sometimes left holes on surfaces. In contrast, TRACE recovered continuous surfaces in these problematic regions, which supports the effectiveness of the depletion mechanism in guiding the planner to effectively cover under-observed areas.

**Ablation Study:**
- **Impact of Denser Sampling**: Two ActiveGS variants were tested: ActiveGS-Random (10 random observations per horizon) performed comparably to ActiveGS (+0.08 dB PSNR), while ActiveGS-Uniform (10 uniformly interpolated poses along the original path) resulted in a 1.7 dB PSNR drop. This indicates that TRACE's gains are not merely due to denser sampling; simply adding more redundant observations can degrade performance by diluting the training weight of informative views.
- **Importance of Footprint Mechanism**: Removing the footprint depletion and gaze mechanisms (Ours-Kernel ES) led to a significant 2.1 dB PSNR loss compared to the full TRACE model, and performed worse than ActiveGS on six of eight scenes. This demonstrates that the footprint-aware mechanisms are critical for preventing redundant observations and effectively guiding the ergodic search, functions that are implicitly handled by confidence updates in NBV.

**Real Robot Execution:**
TRACE was successfully deployed on two physical robotic platforms:
- **Unitree Go2 Quadruped (Room-scale exploration)**: The quadruped executed planned ergodic trajectories directly, achieving a 100% success rate in 5-minute missions within a 42 m² scene, without encountering dynamically infeasible commands or collisions. The trajectories were observed to be continuous and smoothly varied heading, enabling safe navigation through cluttered environments, unlike the NBV baseline which often struggled with collision avoidance and produced jerky motions.
- **Franka FR3 Arm (Object reconstruction)**: Planning directly in joint space, TRACE generated trajectories that were directly executable by the manipulator's controller, requiring no intermediate path planner or inverse kinematics solver. This resulted in dynamically consistent and collision-free movements. In simulation, TRACE maintained a 100% collision-free rate over 10 trials, whereas the NBV baseline caused collisions in every trial due to selecting unachievable or unsafe poses.
- **Practical Implications**: The ability of TRACE to generate dynamically feasible trajectories that are directly executable by robot controllers is a practical advantage. It simplifies the robot's control architecture and enhances reliability in physical deployments, as it avoids the common issue of NBV planners producing commands that are infeasible for real-world robots.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in active 3D reconstruction by proposing a paradigm shift from discrete viewpoint selection to continuous, trajectory-level ergodic optimization. The significance and potential impact of TRACE are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Reconstruction Quality and Efficiency**: By integrating information gathering directly into continuous trajectory planning, TRACE achieves demonstrably higher reconstruction quality (e.g., a mean gain of 1.5 dB PSNR) compared to existing state-of-the-art NBV methods. This leads to more detailed, accurate, and visually consistent 3D models, crucial for applications requiring high-fidelity scene representations. The efficient use of sensing capacity, avoiding redundant observations, contributes to faster and more complete scene understanding within given operational budgets.

2. **Improved Robotic Autonomy and Feasibility**: A major contribution is the generation of dynamically feasible trajectories that are directly executable by physical robots. This eliminates the need for separate path planners and inverse kinematics solvers, which often introduce complexities and potential for infeasible commands in NBV systems. The intrinsic adherence to robot dynamics enhances system robustness, reduces collision risks, and simplifies the overall control architecture for autonomous agents. This is particularly relevant for complex and constrained environments, as demonstrated by the deployment on both a quadruped and a manipulator.

3. **Advancement of Ergodic Search in Robotics**: TRACE extends the applicability of ergodic search methods by demonstrating how to adapt them to online, dynamically evolving information distributions, a common scenario in robotic perception. Unlike prior ergodic control approaches that often rely on pre-defined static distributions, this work shows how to derive target information distributions from live mapping states. This innovation could inspire broader adoption and further development of ergodic principles in other robotic exploration and inspection tasks.

4. **Integrated Planning Framework**: The method unifies information gathering and motion planning within a single, differentiable objective function. This integrated approach allows for joint optimization of where the robot goes and how it observes, leading to globally more optimal and coherent behaviors than decoupled viewpoint selection and path following.

5. **Potential for Broader Applications**: The principles and techniques introduced in TRACE could have an impact beyond active 3D reconstruction. They could be relevant to other tasks requiring continuous information gathering in unknown or partially known environments, such as environmental monitoring, intelligent surveillance, or autonomous inspection. The capability to generate physically executable, information-rich trajectories can enhance the performance and reliability of robotic systems in diverse real-world scenarios.

In summary, TRACE offers a robust and conceptually integrated solution for active scene reconstruction, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in terms of reconstruction quality, operational efficiency, and robotic autonomy in complex environments.
