# PreferenceFlow: Test-Time Guidance of Flow-Matching Robot Policies from Human Interventions

> [!summary] Summary
> PreferenceFlow improves a frozen pretrained flow-matching robot policy at test time without environment rewards or base-policy updates. It trains a state-conditioned preference model on human-intervention chunks paired with robot chunks from the same start state, then adds the preference gradient, evaluated at an estimated clean action, to the flow velocity in QGF style. On four real Franka insertion tasks, mean success rises from 69% to 90.5%.

## Problem

- Flow-matching robot policies model multimodal actions well but still make local execution errors under deployment shift, such as new visuals, object configurations, or contact-rich interactions.
- Fine-tuning every parameter of a robot foundation model on deployment experience is computationally expensive and risks eroding capabilities acquired during large-scale pretraining.
- Critic-guided test-time methods such as Q-Guided Flow need an informative Q-function, but real-robot rewards are sparse, delayed, or unavailable, and critic gradients are poorly constrained outside the training distribution.

## Method

- ==Intervention-derived preference pairs==: each 10-step human takeover chunk is paired with a frozen-policy robot chunk sampled from the same window-start state, then trained with a ==Bradley-Terry ranking loss==.
- ==Per-step cross-attention preference model==: action tokens attend independently to frozen **pi0.5** visual, language, and robot-state tokens, with no ==temporal self-attention==, and per-step scores are averaged.
- ==Gradient-cap loss== penalizes preference gradients above a threshold on intervention pairs, and ==zero-gradient loss== suppresses gradients near noised expert-demonstration actions. **PreferenceFlow** guidance adds the score gradient at a ==one-step clean-action estimate==.

## Results

- Mean success across **four** Franka insertion tasks (**50** trials each) is **90.5%**, versus **69%** for frozen pi0.5, **84.5%** for Intervention BC, **78.5%** for FlowDAgger, and **76%** for QGF.
- At the full model's guidance weight, removing the gradient-cap or zero-gradient loss drops success to **15%** or **5%**. Preference-only training is unstable and reaches just **69%** after lowering guidance.
- Adding temporal self-attention cuts success to **77%**, and randomized preference labels drop it to **63.5%**, below the frozen base. **Ten** intervention episodes already add **9.5** points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human interventions are cheap local preference supervision: pairing a correction with a same-state robot sample targets errors that binary trajectory-success labels describe too coarsely.
> - A learned score's gradient is not automatically safe guidance. Explicit limits on gradient size, plus zero-gradient anchors on routine expert behavior, are what make test-time steering deployable.
> - Scoring action steps independently, without temporal mixing, may stop preference models from exploiting operator-style cues. Reranking five base samples with the same score works about as well.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

PreferenceFlow addresses a practical weakness of generative robot policies. Even a capable policy can make a small visual or contact-related error at deployment, and a tight insertion may fail unless someone corrects it. In a precision-insertion task, the robot must align a connector and port closely enough to insert, and small localization or pose errors can stop the task.

There are two usual ways to get a correction, and both are awkward. Updating every parameter of a large policy can be costly and risks changing its pretrained behavior. Reward-based guidance needs a useful critic and reward signal.

The authors instead learn from human takeovers. A human takeover supplies a correction. The system compares that correction with what the robot would have done from the same starting observation. The learned preference then nudges later action generation toward the correction, while the already task-adapted base policy stays fixed. Across four tested insertion tasks, this gave higher average success than the frozen policy.

### 2. Turning a takeover into a preference

For each complete intervention window, the method pairs the human action chunk with the first 10 actions of a robot chunk generated at that window's starting state. In the evaluated setup, windows are 10 steps long with stride one, and the human chunk is labeled preferred.

The two chunks share a start, but they do not follow identical state trajectories. The human's chunk is collected under closed-loop control, while the robot candidate is generated from the starting observation. This comparison is local supervision, not a learned environment reward. A preference model learns relative rankings with a Bradley–Terry objective, and its score is not calibrated to expected return.

Two safeguards shape the score's gradients:

- A gradient-cap penalty discourages excessively large score gradients on both actions in an intervention pair, without eliminating smaller corrective gradients.
- A zero-gradient penalty uses neighborhoods around expert demonstration actions to discourage guidance near routine behavior.

During flow sampling, the method first estimates a clean action with one Euler extrapolation. It then evaluates the preference gradient there and adds it to the frozen policy's velocity update at each sampling step. This inference update is inherited from [[2606.11087|Q-Guided Flow]]. It is not an exact sampler for the paper's KL-regularized ideal distribution.

Paper Fig. 1, a schematic. The flow policy appears above preference-model training, which takes state/image/prompt and action tokens and uses preference, gradient-cap, and zero-gradient losses. Human-intervention pairs feed the preference model, and preference-guided sampling feeds the frozen base policy. Only the preference-model parameters are updated during adaptation.

### 3. What the tests show

The evaluation used a single Franka robot on four precision-insertion tasks: Ethernet, USB, SATA, and charger. Each method ran 50 trials per task, and success rate is the fraction of successful trials.

The base policy for each task had prior task adaptation. Each task's π0.5 policy was first fine-tuned by supervised learning on 50 expert demonstrations. PreferenceFlow then froze that task-specific checkpoint for adaptation and guided inference.

PreferenceFlow's mean success rate was 90.5%, versus 69.0% for the frozen policy. These means correspond to 181 versus 138 successes out of 200 trials. PreferenceFlow had the highest observed rate on each task.

The gradient-objective ablation is the stronger test of the mechanism. At the full model's guidance weight, removing the gradient-cap loss gives 15.0% mean success, and removing the zero-gradient loss gives 5.0%. The guidance weight was then progressively reduced until deployment was stable. Under those settings, the two variants reach 81.0% and 74.5%, and preference-loss-only guidance reaches 69.0%. None of these variants recovers the full model's result in the tested settings. Removing the zero-gradient loss also removes the demonstration-based anchors, so that ablation tests both together.

### 4. A local, bounded fix

PreferenceFlow shows that human takeovers can be turned into local action preferences. These preferences steer a frozen, task-adapted generative policy without an environment reward. In these insertion tests, the method raised success, and its regularizers mattered under the tested guidance settings.

The method has no formal guarantee of long-horizon improvement. It also depends on the base policy reaching states from which local correction can finish the task. The evaluation covered four insertion tasks on one robot.
