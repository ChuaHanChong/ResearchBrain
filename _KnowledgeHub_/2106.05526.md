# Simplifying Deep Reinforcement Learning via Self-Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SSRL) introduces an algorithm that trains policies by solely using supervised loss functions on self-curated, highly-rewarded trajectories generated by the agent itself. This approach demonstrates competitive performance and improved training stability across various Deep Reinforcement Learning tasks, including those with high-dimensional inputs and hard exploration challenges, while simplifying the overall learning process.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms frequently suffer from training instability, attributed to highly correlated data violating the i.i.d. assumption, high variance in policy gradients, and potential value overestimation.
- Conventional gradient-based DRL methods often complicate training and hinder reproducibility due to inherent instability.
- Obtaining suitable 'demonstrations' for supervised learning is challenging in an RL context, especially for untrained agents or environments with sparse rewards.

## Method

- **SSRL** iteratively refines a policy network using only ==supervised loss functions==, bypassing policy gradient optimization or explicit value estimation.
- A ==ranking replay buffer== continuously curates 'good' demonstrations by storing state-action pairs from past trajectories with high episodic rewards and discarding lower-reward experiences.
- The policy network is updated via ==supervised regression==, employing ==behavior cloning== for discrete action spaces and ==mean-squared error== for continuous action spaces.

## Results

- SSRL demonstrated competitive sample efficiency and faster convergence in terms of running time across numerous OpenAI Gym tasks, significantly outperforming baselines like SIL, PPO, and DDPG in environments like Swimmer-v2.
- The algorithm exhibited consistently smoother and more stable learning curves with smaller standard deviations, successfully training policies in at least **80%** of **100** tested random seeds.
- SSRL scaled effectively to high-dimensional pixel inputs, learning to play Atari Pong, and successfully combined with count-based exploration strategies to solve hard exploration tasks like MiniGrid-MultiRoom.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy improvement in DRL can be achieved through an iterative self-supervised learning process without relying on complex components like policy gradients or value functions.
> - A simple ranking mechanism for a replay buffer can effectively identify and leverage an agent's own improving behaviors as 'demonstrations' for policy updates.
> - The inherent stochasticity of the policy facilitates the discovery of better trajectories, which are then reinforced through the self-supervised imitation process, driving stable learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms frequently utilize deep neural networks as function approximators for policies or value functions. A recurring challenge in model-free DRL is the training instability often observed with gradient-based methods, which assume independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples. However, DRL typically encounters highly correlated data, violating this assumption. Factors contributing to this instability include the high variance of policy gradients and potential overestimation of values. These issues can impede algorithm usability and lead to reproducibility concerns in DRL research.

The present work approaches these challenges from the perspective of supervised learning, a paradigm that has demonstrated greater stability in training deep neural networks. Imitation learning, which involves training a policy through supervised regression on expert demonstrations, serves as a foundational inspiration. Prior research has indicated that training a policy based on demonstrations generated by a reinforcement learning agent itself can result in more stable models and accelerated convergence. Examples include policy distillation and certain self-imitation learning approaches. This paper builds upon these observations, aiming to explore the extent to which supervised learning principles can be leveraged to achieve stable policy improvement without relying on the traditional DRL components such as policy gradients or explicit value function estimation. It seeks to simplify the deep reinforcement learning process by focusing entirely on data collection and supervised imitation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a simplified deep reinforcement learning algorithm, termed Self-Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SSRL), that trains policy networks using exclusively supervised loss functions. This approach aims to achieve stable policy improvement without requiring policy gradient optimization or value estimation, which are common but often problematic components in conventional DRL methods.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and observations within the deep reinforcement learning domain:
- **Instability of Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithms:** Current gradient-based DRL methods are often prone to instability. This instability is attributed to various factors, including the violation of the i.i.d. data assumption by correlated samples, the high variance associated with policy gradient estimation, and the potential for overestimation in value functions. These issues complicate the training process and can hinder the reproducibility of results.
- **Success and Stability of Supervised Learning:** In contrast to DRL, supervised learning techniques are generally recognized for their stability in training deep neural networks. This inherent stability presents an opportunity to address the instability observed in DRL if supervised methods can be effectively adapted.
- **Insights from Imitation Learning and Self-Imitation:** Imitation learning, which involves direct supervised regression from states to actions based on expert demonstrations, has proven effective. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that training a policy by imitating an agent's own past "good" behaviors can lead to increased stability and faster convergence in DRL. These findings suggest that incorporating supervised learning principles more broadly could offer benefits.

A central challenge addressed by this research is the difficulty of obtaining suitable "demonstrations" for supervised learning in a reinforcement learning context. Unlike traditional imitation learning, where expert demonstrations are provided, an untrained or partially trained RL agent typically does not produce optimal trajectories. In environments with sparse rewards, identifying which collected trajectories should be considered "good" demonstrations for policy improvement is non-trivial. The paper is motivated to overcome this by proposing a mechanism for the agent to autonomously identify and leverage its own improving experiences as training data for supervised learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is Self-Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SSRL), an iterative algorithm designed to optimize policies using purely supervised loss functions. The core idea of SSRL is to continuously refine a set of "demonstrations" by selecting highly-rewarded trajectories generated by the agent itself and then improving the policy by performing supervised regression on these selected data. This process is summarized as a two-step self-supervised procedure: "labeling" data and supervised regression.

The iterative procedure involves the following steps:
1. **Data Collection:** The current policy ($\pi_\theta$) is executed in the environment to generate a set of trajectories.
2. **Buffer Update and "Labeling":** A replay buffer, denoted as D, stores state-action pairs from past experiences. For each generated episode, its episodic reward is assigned to all state-action pairs within that episode. These pairs are then added to the buffer. If the buffer's capacity is exceeded, state-action pairs associated with lower episodic rewards are discarded. This ranking mechanism prioritizes "good" trajectories as demonstrations.
3. **Policy Update:** The policy network ($\pi_\theta$) is updated using supervised learning methods. The state-action pairs residing in the buffer are treated as the target demonstrations.
 - For **discrete action spaces**, a behavior cloning approach is used, minimizing the negative log probability: $L_{discrete} = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{a \in A} I(a_i, a) \log \pi_\theta(a|s_i)$.
 - For **continuous action spaces**, a mean-square-error loss is applied: $L_{continuous} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (\pi_\theta(a_i|s_i) - a_i)^2$.
4. **Iteration:** Steps 1-3 are repeated until the policy converges.

The policy $\pi_\theta$ is implemented as a stochastic policy (e.g., sampling from multinomial or Gaussian distributions for discrete or continuous actions, respectively). This stochasticity ensures that $\pi(a|s) > 0$ for all states and actions, allowing the agent to occasionally discover better trajectories through randomness, which is crucial for populating the buffer with "good" experiences. While the basic SSRL does not integrate explicit exploration strategies, the framework is designed to accommodate them.

**Theoretical Justification:**
The paper provides a theoretical justification for policy improvement in deterministic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). Theorem 1 (re-stated as Theorem 2 in the Appendix) establishes that if trajectories in the buffer satisfy a "uniformly-distributed" condition (meaning initial state and transition frequencies are consistent with the environment), then a hypothetical policy ($\tilde{\pi}$) can be constructed whose expected cumulative rewards are at least as good as the current policy ($\pi$). The supervised learning step is shown to be equivalent to imitating this hypothetical policy, thereby leading to policy improvement. The authors argue that the "uniformly-distributed" condition holds in deterministic MDPs.

**Adaptations for High-Dimensional Inputs and Exploration:**
- **High-Dimensional Inputs (e.g., Atari Pong):** To handle high-dimensional image inputs, a variant of SSRL employs a distributed learning setup. Multiple "actors" collect trajectories from the environment, while multiple "workers" sample data from a larger buffer (size 10$^6$) and compute gradients for policy updates. Instead of a full ranking buffer, a ring buffer is used where only "winning" trajectories (positive reward) are stored, simplifying buffer management for large datasets. The policy network and optimizer configurations follow established DRL practices (e.g., A2C's convolutional layers and RMSprop optimizer), with entropy regularization also incorporated.
- **Hard Exploration Domains:** The framework's compatibility with existing exploration strategies is demonstrated by integrating a count-based exploration method. This method adds a bonus to rewards proportional to the inverse square root of state visitation frequency ($\beta/\sqrt{N(s)}$) to encourage visiting novel states.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents empirical evaluations across various environments to address specific questions regarding SSRL's performance and characteristics.

**Q1: Evolution of data in the buffer in deterministic environments (Taxi Environment):**
A case study on the deterministic Taxi environment demonstrated the self-refining nature of the ranking buffer. In the early stages of training, both the highest and lowest rewarded episodes in the buffer showed suboptimal behavior. Over time, the ranking mechanism prioritized and retained better-performing episodes. In the middle stage, the agent progressively discovered and stored more optimal trajectories. By the final stage, both the best and worst episodes stored in the buffer reflected optimal behavior, indicating that the policy had converged to an optimal solution. This evolution illustrates how the ranking buffer effectively guides stable policy improvement by continuously curating desired behaviors.

**Q2: Performance in non-deterministic discrete and continuous control tasks (OpenAI Gym):**
SSRL was evaluated against state-of-the-art DRL algorithms (SIL, PPO, DDPG, DQN) on several OpenAI Gym tasks.
- **Competitiveness:** SSRL successfully solved various control tasks and exhibited competitive sample efficiency compared to the baselines.
- **Outperformance:** In the Swimmer-v2 environment, SSRL significantly outperformed SIL, PPO, and DDPG, achieving average rewards approximately 2-7 times higher than these baselines.
- **Computational Efficiency:** SSRL demonstrated faster convergence in terms of running time across nearly all tasks. This is attributed to its simpler implementation, relying solely on supervised learning updates and requiring a smaller buffer without the complexities of value estimation, advantage computation, or target networks.
- **Stability:** The learning curves for SSRL were consistently smoother and more stable, exhibiting smaller standard deviations compared to PPO and SIL in environments like CartPole-v1, Acrobot-v1, and InvertedPendulum-v2. SSRL also showed almost monotonous improvement during training and maintained stability after convergence, in contrast to some baseline methods that experienced performance drops.

**Q3: Stability across various seeds (100 seeds evaluation):**
An evaluation using 100 different random seeds across the control tasks indicated that SSRL exhibits consistent performance. While individual performance varied with seeds, SSRL successfully trained policies with at least 80% of the tested seeds, suggesting robust stability and reduced sensitivity to initialization compared to the typical reputation of DRL algorithms.

**Q4: Handling raw image pixels in inputs (Atari Pong):**
A variant of SSRL, adapted for high-dimensional pixel inputs with a distributed architecture and simplified buffer management (storing only winning trajectories), successfully learned to play Atari Pong. It achieved competitive sample efficiency when compared against advanced DRL methods like Rainbow, IQN, C51, DQN, A2C, and A2C+SIL. This indicates that the supervised learning-based approach can scale to complex visual input domains.

**Q5: Combining with exploration strategies on hard exploration domains (MiniGrid-MultiRoom-N4-S5-v0):**
Experiments on the MiniGrid-MultiRoom environment, a sparse-reward, hard exploration task, demonstrated that SSRL can be effectively combined with existing exploration strategies. Specifically, when augmented with a count-based exploration bonus, SSRL successfully navigated to the goal, a feat that SSRL alone, A2C alone, and A2C with count-based exploration failed to achieve consistently. This suggests that SSRL's ranking buffer mechanism can effectively leverage positive reward signals, even when sparse, to accelerate learning with exploration bonuses.

**Theoretical Findings:**
The theoretical analysis confirmed that in deterministic MDPs, the proposed iterative process of collecting uniformly-distributed trajectories and performing supervised learning is equivalent to imitating a hypothetical policy that is guaranteed to be at least as good as the current policy, thus driving policy improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Simplifying Deep Reinforcement Learning via Self-Supervision" carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of reinforcement learning:

- **Simplification of Deep Reinforcement Learning:** The primary significance lies in demonstrating that complex DRL tasks can be addressed effectively using a fundamentally simpler approach. By relying purely on supervised learning techniques and iterative data curation, SSRL bypasses the need for intricate components like policy gradients, value functions, and target networks, which are often sources of computational burden and algorithmic instability in conventional DRL. This simplification could make DRL more accessible for implementation and research.
- **Enhanced Stability and Reproducibility:** A critical contribution is the demonstration of improved training stability. The empirical results show smoother learning curves, smaller standard deviations across multiple runs, and consistent performance across a large number of random seeds. This addresses a major challenge in DRL concerning its notorious instability and reproducibility issues, offering a more reliable framework for developing and deploying RL agents.
- **Improved Computational Efficiency:** SSRL exhibits substantially faster running times compared to various baseline algorithms. This efficiency stems from its simpler computational model, which avoids the overhead associated with complex value function estimations, advantage computations, and intricate network architectures common in many DRL algorithms.
- **Broad Applicability and Scalability:** The successful application of SSRL across diverse environments—including deterministic and non-deterministic MDPs, discrete and continuous action spaces, high-dimensional pixel inputs (Atari Pong), and challenging sparse-reward exploration domains (when combined with exploration strategies)—underscores its versatility. This suggests that the core principles of SSRL may be widely applicable to a range of real-world problems.
- **Foundation for Future Research Directions:** This work positions itself as a "pilot study," opening new avenues for research into the potential of self-supervised learning for reinforcement learning. It encourages further investigation into how data collection and demonstration curation can entirely substitute or significantly augment traditional RL optimization methods. Future work could explore more sophisticated data selection strategies, theoretical guarantees for stochastic environments, and integrations with advanced exploration techniques.
- **Rethinking RL Fundamentals:** By challenging the traditional reliance on explicit reward maximization via value functions or policy gradients, SSRL prompts a re-evaluation of the fundamental mechanisms driving policy improvement in reinforcement learning. It suggests that effective learning can emerge from an iterative process of identifying and imitating improving behaviors.
