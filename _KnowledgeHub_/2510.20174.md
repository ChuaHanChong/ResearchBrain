# Reinforcement Learning-based Robust Wall Climbing Locomotion Controller in Ferromagnetic Environment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from KAIST and DIDEN Robotics developed a reinforcement learning-based controller enabling a quadrupedal magnetic climbing robot to robustly traverse vertical ferromagnetic surfaces. The controller, trained with a multi-phase curriculum and a physics-based adhesion model that incorporates stochastic failures, achieved a 90% success rate in simulation and demonstrated recovery from induced adhesion failures during hardware validation.

## Problem

- Traditional Model Predictive Control (MPC) for magnetic climbing robots assumes ideal adhesion, leading to failures when real-world factors like paint or dust degrade attachment strength.
- Applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to climbing is challenging due to frequent catastrophic falls during initial training and difficulties in accurately simulating adhesion uncertainty.
- Real-time computational demands of MPC limit responsiveness to sudden disturbances like unexpected slip events, and model mismatches compromise stability in unstructured environments.

## Method

- A multi-phase curriculum learning strategy progressively trains the robot from ground locomotion to robust wall climbing by gradually introducing gravitational rotation and adhesion uncertainty.
- A physics-based magnetic adhesion model for electropermanent magnets is integrated into the simulation, accounting for partial contact, air gaps, and probabilistic attachment failures.
- Stochastic adhesion failures are deliberately injected during training to force the learning policy to develop robust recovery strategies against unexpected slippage or detachment.

## Results

- The multi-phase curriculum led to a learned policy achieving an average episode success rate of approximately **90%** in simulation, even when the adhesion probability was reduced to **85%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the curriculum, probabilistic adhesion, and adhesion modeling were critical components, with their absence leading to significantly higher termination rates and degraded locomotion.
- Hardware validation on an untethered quadrupedal robot demonstrated successful traversal of vertical steel surfaces, effective recovery from induced adhesion failures, and superior performance compared to an MPC baseline that failed upon adhesion loss.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A structured multi-phase curriculum is essential for stabilizing RL training for complex, safety-critical tasks like wall climbing, preventing early catastrophic failures.
> - Explicitly incorporating a realistic magnetic adhesion model and simulating stochastic adhesion failures during training significantly reduces the sim-to-real gap and fosters robust recovery behaviors.
> - RL-based control, when trained with adhesion uncertainty, yields superior resilience to unmodeled disturbances and adhesion failures compared to traditional model-based (MPC) approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Climbing robots represent a promising technological solution for tasks such as inspection and maintenance of large-scale steel infrastructure, which are often costly and hazardous for human operators. Research in this area has explored various adhesion mechanisms, including suction, gecko-inspired dry adhesion, and magnetic attachment. Magnetic adhesion is particularly noted for its suitability on steel structures due to its strong, repeatable attachment, minimal energy consumption, and robustness to surface conditions like coatings, dust, or moderate irregularities. When combined with legged locomotion, magnetic climbing robots offer advantages such as traversing complex geometries, stepping over obstacles, and maintaining redundant contact points for enhanced stability, which makes them suitable for industrial applications.

Control strategies for such platforms have primarily utilized Model Predictive Control (MPC), which optimizes robot motion over a finite time horizon while considering dynamics and kinematic constraints. MPC has demonstrated its capability in enabling stable crawling and trotting on vertical surfaces by incorporating nominal adhesion forces. However, MPC approaches often operate under the assumption of ideal adhesion conditions. In practical scenarios, factors such as paint, dust, or imperfect foot placement can reduce holding forces or lead to detachment. The inability of typical MPC formulations to explicitly model and account for adhesion uncertainty can lead to slippage or falls. Furthermore, the real-time computational demands of solving constrained optimizations in MPC can limit the robot's responsiveness to sudden disturbances, such as unexpected slip events. Mismatches between the modeled and actual adhesion forces can also compromise the controller's stability and robustness in unstructured environments.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents an alternative control paradigm where policies are acquired through direct interaction with the environment. RL-trained controllers have demonstrated capabilities in robust locomotion over challenging terrains and recovery from significant perturbations, often surpassing the adaptability of model-based methods. Applying RL to vertical climbing is an area of investigation, as RL policies could potentially learn recovery mechanisms for adhesion failures without requiring explicit models of such failures. However, adapting RL to climbing introduces specific challenges: accurate representation of adhesion in simulation is crucial to minimize the sim-to-real gap, and adhesion loss in climbing can result in catastrophic falls, which impedes effective learning. Existing RL research has infrequently addressed climbing, and explicit consideration of adhesion uncertainty during training remains largely unexplored. This work positions itself by addressing these limitations, proposing an RL framework specifically designed for quadrupedal magnetic climbing robots that accounts for adhesion uncertainty through a multi-phase curriculum and realistic adhesion modeling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust locomotion controller for a quadrupedal magnetic climbing robot using a reinforcement learning framework. This controller is designed to explicitly address and manage uncertainties associated with magnetic foot adhesion in ferromagnetic environments.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in the control of climbing robots, particularly those utilizing magnetic adhesion:

1. **Limitations of Model Predictive Control (MPC):** Prior work primarily relies on MPC, which typically assumes perfect adhesion. In real-world applications, this assumption often proves invalid due to environmental factors such as paint, dust, surface irregularities, or partial foot contact, which can degrade adhesion strength or cause detachment. MPC's inability to model such uncertainties makes it susceptible to failures when adhesion is compromised.
2. **Computational Costs and Responsiveness of MPC:** Solving complex optimization problems at each control cycle in MPC demands significant computational resources, potentially limiting the robot's ability to react promptly and adaptively to sudden events like slips or detachments.
3. **Model Mismatches:** Discrepancies between the idealized adhesion models used in MPC and the actual adhesion forces experienced in real environments can destabilize the controller and reduce its robustness.
4. **Leveraging Reinforcement Learning for Robustness:** Reinforcement learning offers a complementary approach that allows control policies to be learned through interaction, potentially enabling robots to acquire adaptive and recovery behaviors without explicit programming of complex failure modes. This is particularly appealing for climbing, where learning to recover from adhesion failures is critical for safety and mission success.
5. **Addressing RL Challenges in Climbing:** Directly applying RL to climbing tasks presents its own difficulties. Untrained policies often lead to frequent catastrophic falls early in training, hindering data collection and stable learning. Furthermore, accurately simulating the nuances of magnetic adhesion and ensuring that learned behaviors transfer reliably from simulation to real hardware (the sim-to-real gap) are significant challenges.

To address these motivations and challenges, the research outlines three specific contributions:

1. **Multi-Phase Curriculum Learning:** To stabilize the learning process and facilitate a smooth transition from ground locomotion to wall climbing, a multi-phase curriculum is designed. This curriculum gradually introduces the complexity of vertical climbing, starting from basic gait acquisition on flat ground and progressively adapting to vertical surfaces.
2. **Physics-Based Adhesion Model:** A realistic adhesion model for electropermanent-magnetic (EPM) feet is incorporated into the simulation. This model accounts for critical factors such as partial contact, sensitivity to air gaps, and probabilistic attachment failures, aiming to accurately represent real-world magnetic adhesion behavior and reduce the sim-to-real gap.
3. **Stochastic Adhesion Failures:** During training, stochastic adhesion failures are deliberately injected. This exposure to intermittent adhesion loss encourages the learning policy to develop robust recovery strategies, enabling the robot to maintain stability and continue locomotion even when individual feet temporarily lose attachment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a reinforcement learning-based control framework designed for robust vertical crawling on steel surfaces, specifically addressing uncertainties in magnetic adhesion.

#### 3.1. A. Hardware

The robotic platform used is an untethered quadrupedal climbing robot. It weighs 8 kg and is equipped with four magnetic feet, each weighing approximately 0.2 kg. These feet integrate electropermanent magnets (EPMs) which provide switchable magnetic adhesion, offering a maximum normal holding force of approximately 697 N. The EPMs are capable of rapid magnetic switching within 5 milliseconds, enabling timely attachment and detachment. The foot design employed in this study is simplified, omitting magnetorheological elastomer (MRE) footpads, to facilitate isolated modeling of adhesion uncertainty during the learning process.

#### 3.2. B. Magnetic Foot Adhesion Model

A physics-based magnetic adhesion model is implemented in simulation to accurately represent the behavior of EPM feet. EPMs generate holding force by magnetizing an AlNiCo core through a coil pulse, requiring a closed magnetic circuit with the steel surface. The model emphasizes that stable adhesion only occurs with proper contact, as air gaps significantly reduce force (e.g., a 1 mm air gap reduces force to ~7% of maximum).

Adhesion in simulation is contingent upon four sequential conditions:
1. **Contact Recognition:** The state estimator provides a contact confidence $\tilde{c}_{\text{foot}} \in$. Adhesion is considered if $\tilde{c}_{\text{foot}} \ge 0.5$, indicating contact ($c_{\text{foot}} = 1$).
2. **Magnet Activation:** The policy outputs an activation signal $a_{\text{magnet}}$. The magnet is considered ON if $a_{\text{magnet}} \ge 0.5$.
3. **Stochastic Adhesion:** Even when the first two conditions are met, adhesion occurs probabilistically to model real-world uncertainties. A random variable $X \sim U(0, 1)$ is sampled when the foot is in swing, and adhesion succeeds only if $X \le Prob_{\text{attach}}$. This component simulates environmental failures like non-ferrous patches or uneven surfaces. The $Prob_{\text{attach}}$ schedule is dynamically adjusted during training.
4. **Geometric Alignment:** Proper adhesion requires full geometric contact, $S_{\text{EPM}} = S_{\text{wall}}$, where $S_{\text{EPM}}$ is the EPM contact surface and $S_{\text{wall}}$ is the wall surface. Any air gap or misalignment substantially reduces effective adhesion.

This sequential modeling exposes the policy to realistic adhesion failures, promoting the learning of recovery strategies and reducing the sim-to-real gap.

#### 3.3. C. Multi-Phase Learning Strategy for Vertical Locomotion under Adhesion Uncertainty

A three-phase curriculum learning strategy is adopted to progressively train the robot from basic locomotion to robust wall climbing.

1. **Phase 1: Gait Acquisition on Flat Ground:** The initial phase focuses on establishing a stable crawl gait on flat ground without active magnetic adhesion. This prevents frequent falls that would occur if training started directly on a wall with an untrained policy. Although the policy outputs magnet actions, the adhesion model is disabled. An auxiliary reward penalizes incorrect magnet timing (ON during swing, OFF during stance), implicitly teaching the policy when to activate magnets. Training uses a concurrent learning approach with an 8-dimensional clock input for gait phase encoding.
2. **Phase 2: Adaptation to Gravitational Rotation:** After acquiring a ground gait, the robot is gradually exposed to inclined and vertical surfaces. This is achieved by rotating the gravity vector in simulation from 0$^\circ$ (flat ground) to 90$^\circ$ (vertical wall). The magnetic adhesion model is activated in this phase, coupling the learned magnet timing with actual adhesion forces. The gravity tilt angle $\theta(t)$ increases linearly from $t=1201$ to $t=21200$ iterations, as defined by $\theta(t) = \min(\frac{\pi}{2}, \max(0, \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{t-1200}{20000}))$.
3. **Phase 3: Robustness to Adhesion Uncertainty:** In the final phase, stochastic disturbances are introduced to simulate real-world adhesion uncertainty. The adhesion success probability $Prob_{\text{attach}}(t)$ is linearly decreased from 1.0 to 0.85 between $t=21200$ and $t=35000$ iterations. This forces the policy to learn to maintain balance and recover from unexpected slippage or detachment caused by probabilistic failures. Adhesion is only triggered if contact recognition, magnet activation, and geometric alignment conditions are met, followed by a successful probabilistic check against $Prob_{\text{attach}}(t)$.

#### 3.4. D. Learning Framework

The learning framework utilizes Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) within the RaiSim simulator. It comprises three multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural networks: an actor (policy), a critic (value function), and a state estimator. The actor and critic share an architecture of three hidden layers, while the state estimator has two hidden layers.

- **Observation and Action Space:** The observation vector includes proprioceptive states (joint positions, velocities, base orientation, angular velocity), historical joint targets, Cartesian foot positions, estimated base linear velocity, foot height, foot contact probabilities, and an 8-dimensional clock input encoding gait phase for each leg. All observations are filtered using a first-order low-pass filter ($\alpha = 0.35$). The action space consists of 12 joint torque commands and 4 binary magnet activation commands.
- **Reward Design:** The reward function is multi-component, promoting stable, efficient, and robust climbing. Key terms include linear and angular velocity tracking, standing command, gait, foot height, foot slip penalty, foot clearance, orientation, joint torque penalty, joint position, joint acceleration, and action smoothness. Rewards are dynamically scaled using a factor $\kappa = 0.99975^{\max(\text{iter}-1200, 0)}$. Velocity tracking rewards increase in weight as the robot tilts, while penalties for foot slip and joint torque decrease, allowing for more aggressive maneuvers during climbing. An exponential term applies a multiplicative penalty for stability, contact, and actuation violations.
- **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer, various parameters are randomized at the start of each episode: joint PD gains, ground friction coefficient, observation noise (orientation, joint angles/velocities, foot positions), and action delay.
- **Compliant 3-DOF Joint Ankle Modeling:** A ball joint connects the calf link and magnetic foot, modeling mechanical compliance. Nominal ankle orientation is fixed (roll-pitch-yaw [0, 0.523599, 0] rad). Position and velocity gains for this joint are randomized during training to enhance robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from learning progress, ablation studies, and hardware validation.

#### 4.1. A. Learning Progress Across Phases

The learning curves illustrate the effectiveness of the multi-phase curriculum:
- **Phase 1 (Flat Ground Locomotion):** The policy rapidly improves, acquiring a stable crawl gait. A temporary reduction in total reward occurs when action smoothness regularization is activated after 1000 iterations, but the policy quickly adapts to produce smoother, more stable motions, and the reward continues to increase.
- **Phase 2 (Gravitational Rotation):** As the gravity vector gradually rotates towards a vertical orientation, the average episode success rate temporarily decreases. This period represents the policy adapting its balance and posture for climbing.
- **Phase 3 (Adhesion Uncertainty):** The introduction of stochastic adhesion failures further increases the task difficulty, leading to an initial decline in overall performance. However, the policy's performance subsequently stabilizes, achieving an average episode success rate of approximately 90% even with the adhesion probability reduced to 85%. This indicates that the curriculum successfully guides the policy to generalize to increasingly challenging climbing conditions and learn robust recovery behaviors.

#### 4.2. B. Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted to quantify the contribution of each key component: curriculum learning, probabilistic adhesion, and adhesion modeling. Evaluation metrics included velocity tracking RMSE, early termination rate, average walking time, retention (fraction of stance with active adhesion), and recovery rate from adhesion failures.

- **w/o Curriculum:** Training without the gravity rotation curriculum resulted in a high early termination rate (88%) and reduced average walking time (5.87 s). The robot frequently remained statically attached to the wall, leading to minimal forward movement. While retention was artificially high (98.5%) due to feet staying attached, the velocity tracking RMSE was also high (0.2055 m/s), indicating a failure to achieve effective locomotion. This suggests the curriculum is essential for learning dynamic climbing.
- **w/o Probabilistic Adhesion:** When the probability of adhesion was fixed at 1.0 (no stochastic failures), performance was comparable to the full model under ideal conditions. However, when evaluated with a reduced adhesion probability ($p_{\text{attach}} = 0.85$), this policy exhibited significantly degraded retention (42.51% vs. 72.93% for full model) and recovery rates (e.g., 58.74% vs. 98.79% at $\Delta T=2.4s$). This indicates that training with stochastic adhesion is crucial for developing robustness against real-world attachment failures.
- **w/o Modeling:** Removing the realistic adhesion modeling (i.e., treating any magnet command over 0.5 as full adhesion regardless of contact geometry) yielded the poorest performance across all metrics. The velocity tracking RMSE was substantially higher (5.1106 m/s), the early termination rate was high (48%), and retention was low (44.36%). This highlights that an accurate representation of magnetic adhesion behavior in simulation is critical for effective learning and reliable transfer to hardware, as simplified models lead to frequent and uncontrolled detachment.

#### 4.3. C. Hardware Validation

The learned RL policy was validated on the untethered quadrupedal magnetic climbing robot.
- **Robustness to Adhesion Failures:** The RL policy successfully traversed vertical steel surfaces, including sections with non-ferromagnetic patches, and demonstrated recovery from induced adhesion failures. When a foot failed to attach, the policy effectively shifted the robot's weight, reattempted adhesion, and continued crawling.
- **Comparison with MPC Baseline:** A comparison with an MPC controller (from previous work) that assumed perfect adhesion showed that the MPC baseline failed immediately upon experiencing adhesion loss. In contrast, the RL policy was able to recover and maintain stable locomotion.
- **Internal Signal Synchronization:** Logged hardware signals demonstrated the synchronization between the policy's magnet activation commands, the estimated foot contact confidence, and the actual ON/OFF states of the EPMs. Despite minor fluctuations in contact estimation due to sim-to-real mismatches, a simple rule (magnet remains ON unless contact probability drops below 0.5 for >0.02s) ensured reliable EPM control.
- **Reproducibility:** Multiple walking tests confirmed the controller's consistent ability to achieve stable climbing locomotion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for robust wall-climbing locomotion, contributing to the field of legged robotics and autonomous inspection.

### 6. Significance

- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The work demonstrates a viable sim-to-real framework for magnetic climbing robots by explicitly addressing the challenges of adhesion uncertainty. The multi-phase curriculum and physics-based adhesion modeling are shown to be effective strategies for bridging the reality gap and stabilizing reinforcement learning in complex physical environments.
- **Addressing Adhesion Uncertainty:** A central contribution is the effective integration of a realistic magnetic adhesion model and stochastic adhesion failures into the training process. This approach enables the learned policy to develop adaptive behaviors and recovery strategies for imperfect or lost adhesion, a critical aspect often overlooked in traditional model-based control or simplified simulation environments.
- **Enhanced Resilience over Model-Based Methods:** The comparison with an MPC baseline highlights that the RL-based controller, trained with explicit adhesion uncertainty, exhibits superior resilience to unmodeled disturbances and adhesion failures. This suggests that learning-based approaches can yield more robust and adaptive controllers for tasks involving complex, uncertain interactions with the environment.
- **Structured Learning for Complex Tasks:** The multi-phase curriculum learning strategy demonstrates a method for training complex motor skills like wall climbing. By incrementally increasing the task difficulty, the framework avoids early catastrophic failures and efficiently guides the policy towards acquiring sophisticated behaviors, including stable gait acquisition, adaptation to gravity, and slip recovery.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Industrial Applications:** This research has the potential to enhance the reliability and autonomy of climbing robots for critical industrial applications, such as inspection and maintenance of large-scale steel structures (e.g., bridges, storage tanks, ships, power plants). By enabling robots to operate effectively despite surface imperfections and transient adhesion losses, it can reduce operational costs and human exposure to hazardous environments.
- **Advancements in Legged Robotics:** The methodology contributes to the broader field of legged robotics by offering a framework for learning robust locomotion in environments with uncertain interaction forces. The principles of curriculum learning, realistic physical modeling, and stochastic disturbance injection could be generalized to other challenging terrains and adhesion mechanisms.
- **Development of Autonomous Systems:** The ability of robots to autonomously recover from unexpected physical interactions is fundamental for advanced autonomous systems. This work contributes to this by demonstrating learned recovery capabilities for a challenging and safety-critical aspect of robot locomotion.
- **Future Research Directions:** The findings lay a foundation for future work, including extending the framework to more diverse ferromagnetic environments (e.g., curved surfaces, gaps, irregular structures) and enabling richer climbing skills, such as seamless transitions across different orientations (floor-wall-ceiling).
