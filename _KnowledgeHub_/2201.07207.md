# Language Models as Zero-Shot Planners: Extracting Actionable Knowledge for Embodied Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley, CMU, and Google developed an inference-time procedure to enable large language models (LLMs) to serve as zero-shot planners for embodied agents in simulated household environments. Their method, which combines semantic translation and autoregressive correction, dramatically increased the executability of LLM-generated action plans from under 10% to over 70% while maintaining competitive semantic correctness.

## Problem

- Leveraging the vast world knowledge embedded within LLMs for zero-shot planning by embodied agents in interactive environments without specific training.
- Translating free-form, natural language action plans generated by LLMs into concrete, syntactically valid, and admissible actions executable by a robotic agent.
- Improving the executability of LLM-generated plans without relying on extensive domain-specific data collection or computationally expensive fine-tuning.

## Method

- An ==inference-time procedure== extracts actionable knowledge from frozen, pre-trained LLMs, using a single in-context example for zero-shot planning in the VirtualHome environment.
- ==Semantic translation== utilizes a separate language model (e.g., Sentence-RoBERTa) to map LLM-generated natural language action phrases to admissible environment actions based on semantic similarity.
- ==Autoregressive trajectory correction== guides plan generation step-by-step, translating each LLM-generated action phrase into an admissible environment action and appending it to the prompt for subsequent step generation, along with dynamic example selection.

## Results

- Vanilla LLMs (e.g., GPT-3 175B) generated plans with high human-judged semantic correctness (**77.86%**) but very low executability (**7.79%**) in VirtualHome.
- The proposed inference-time procedure significantly boosted executability, increasing it from **18.07%** to **78.57%** for Codex 12B and from **7.79%** to **73.05%** for GPT-3 175B.
- While executability improved, the human-evaluated correctness of translated plans saw a slight decrease (e.g., Translated GPT-3 175B at **66.13%**) compared to vanilla LLM outputs, although remaining competitive with human-written plans (**70.05%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large language models (LLMs) inherently possess extensive common-sense knowledge, enabling them to generate semantically plausible action plans for complex, high-level tasks.
> - A significant gap exists between the semantic correctness of raw LLM-generated plans and their practical executability in constrained, interactive embodied environments.
> - Inference-time procedures, without modifying LLM parameters, can effectively ground free-form natural language plans into admissible actions, substantially improving executability but introducing a trade-off with peak human-judged correctness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: Large Language Models (LLMs), Embodied Artificial Intelligence (Embodied AI), and Natural Language Grounding.

Recent years have seen an explosion in the capabilities of LLMs like GPT-3 and Codex, which have demonstrated unprecedented abilities in language generation, understanding, and even reasoning across a wide array of linguistic tasks. These models, trained on vast corpora of human text, are believed to internalize a significant amount of "world knowledge" and common sense. A critical question, however, is whether this linguistic knowledge can be effectively *grounded* in the physical world and translated into actionable decisions for agents operating in interactive environments.

Prior work in embodied AI often focused on training agents directly from demonstrations or through reinforcement learning within specific environments. While effective, these methods typically require extensive, domain-specific data collection and fine-tuning. The challenge has been to generalize beyond seen examples and leverage broader human common sense, which is difficult to encode directly into traditional learning pipelines.

This paper represents a significant departure from, and advancement upon, these prior approaches by exploring the **zero-shot** capability of LLMs for planning in embodied environments. Instead of training models on explicit step-by-step demonstrations for a specific environment, the authors investigate whether pre-trained LLMs *already contain* the necessary information to decompose high-level tasks (e.g., "make breakfast") into a sequence of actionable, mid-level steps (e.g., "open fridge," "grab milk"). This shifts the paradigm from learning *how to act* in a new environment through explicit examples to *extracting pre-existing knowledge* about actions from a general-purpose language model.

Furthermore, the work addresses a crucial challenge in grounding LLM outputs: their often free-form, ambiguous, or non-executable nature in a constrained interactive environment. By proposing a procedure for semantic translation and autoregressive correction, the authors aim to bridge the gap between human-like linguistic plans and environment-specific admissible actions. This positions the paper as a bridge-builder, demonstrating practical inference-time techniques to harness the power of LLMs for embodied agents without requiring expensive fine-tuning or model modification. It directly contributes to the goal of building more general-purpose and adaptable AI agents by leveraging the rich, implicit knowledge stored within large-scale pre-trained models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether the vast world knowledge embedded within Large Language Models (LLMs) can be directly leveraged to enable embodied agents to plan and act in interactive environments, without any additional training or fine-tuning specific to that environment.

More specifically, the paper aims to answer the following questions and address related challenges:

1. **Zero-Shot Planning:** Can LLMs, solely through appropriate prompting and their pre-trained knowledge, effectively decompose high-level, natural language tasks (like "make breakfast") into a sequence of mid-level, plausible action plans? The authors hypothesize that sufficiently large LLMs might inherently possess common-sense knowledge about human activities.
2. **Actionability and Grounding:** Given that LLMs produce free-form natural language, can these plans be translated into concrete, admissible actions that can be executed by a robotic agent in a simulated environment (VirtualHome)? The motivation here is to overcome the typical disconnect between human-like natural language and the precise, constrained syntax required by embodied platforms.
3. **Improving Executability:** How can the executability of LLM-generated plans be improved without invasively modifying the LLM's parameters or requiring extensive domain-specific training? This objective seeks efficient, inference-time solutions that are compatible with existing LLM serving pipelines.
4. **Understanding Trade-offs:** What are the trade-offs between the "semantic correctness" (human-judged plausibility) of LLM-generated plans and their "executability" in a concrete environment? The motivation is to empirically quantify these tensions and identify areas for future research.

In essence, the core motivation stems from the desire to unlock the latent common-sense reasoning and planning capabilities of LLMs for real-world (or simulated real-world) robotic applications, thereby reducing the dependency on task-specific, hand-engineered knowledge or massive amounts of labeled interaction data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose an inference-time procedure to extract actionable knowledge from frozen, pre-trained LLMs for embodied planning, primarily evaluated in the VirtualHome environment.

#### 3.1. Evaluation Environment: VirtualHome

The chosen environment, **VirtualHome**, is a simulator designed for modeling complex human activities in household settings. It is critical for this research because it supports a diverse set of interactions and high-level tasks, which many existing embodied environments do not. Activities in VirtualHome are expressed as programs, a sequence of textual action steps in `[ACTION] <arg>(idx)` syntax (e.g., `[WALK] <living_room>(1)`). The environment enforces common-sense constraints (e.g., a fridge must be open before an item can be grabbed from it). The authors utilize the ActivityPrograms knowledge base, which contains high-level task names, detailed instructions, and executable programs, for sampling tasks and demonstrations.

#### 3.2. Metrics

To evaluate the generated action plans, two primary axes are considered:

1. **Executability:** Measures whether an action plan can be correctly parsed by VirtualHome and satisfies its common-sense pre- and post-conditions. It indicates if the plan is syntactically valid and adheres to environmental logic.
2. **Correctness:** Due to the open-ended and ambiguous nature of natural language tasks, correctness is primarily assessed via **human evaluation** on Amazon Mechanical Turk. Humans judge whether an action sequence meaningfully accomplishes the stated task. As a proxy for correctness in extensive analysis, the **Longest Common Subsequence (LCS)** metric is used, comparing generated plans to human-annotated ground truth programs.

#### 3.3. Querying LLMs for Action Plans

The core idea is to use LLMs in a **zero-shot, in-context learning** setting.
- **Planning LM:** An autoregressive LLM (e.g., GPT-3, Codex) is prompted with a *single fixed example* consisting of a high-level task and its associated action plan from the demonstration set, followed by the query task. The LLM then completes the sequence, generating a natural language action plan.
- **Sampling and Selection:** Multiple action plans are sampled from the LLM (using temperature and nucleus sampling). The best plan is chosen based on the highest mean token log probability, avoiding repetitive trial-and-error in the environment.
- **Natural Language Mapping:** Since LLMs work with natural language, a bi-directional mapping is defined to convert between VirtualHome's specific program format (e.g., `[WALK] <living_room>(1)`) and a natural language template (e.g., "walk to living room").

#### 3.4. Admissible Action Parsing by Semantic Translation

A significant challenge is that LLM-generated natural language plans often don't map directly to VirtualHome's admissible actions due to syntactic errors, unrecognized vocabulary, or ambiguity.
- **Translation LM:** To address this, a separate BERT-style language model (e.g., Sentence-RoBERTa, fine-tuned with Sentence-BERT objective) is used as an embedding function `f`.
- **Semantic Similarity:** For each predicted action phrase (`hat{a}`) from the Planning LM, its semantic similarity to all possible admissible environment actions (`a_e`) is calculated using cosine similarity `C(f(hat{a}), f(a_e))`. The environment action with the highest similarity is chosen.
- **Pre-computation:** To improve efficiency, embeddings for all admissible environment actions are pre-computed.

#### 3.5. Autoregressive Trajectory Correction

Instead of translating the entire plan after generation, which can lead to compounding errors, the authors propose an interleaved approach:
- **Step-by-step Generation & Translation:** At each step, the Planning LM generates `k` single-step action phrases. For each phrase, it is then translated into an admissible environment action using the Translation LM, combining semantic similarity with the Planning LM's log probability for ranking.
- **Prompt Augmentation:** The chosen admissible action is then appended to the prompt for generating the *next* step. This ensures subsequent generations are conditioned on valid, executable actions, guiding the trajectory towards greater executability.
- **Early Termination:** The process can terminate early if a high percentage of samples are 0-length or if the highest-scoring translated action falls below a confidence threshold, preventing the generation of ungrounded or nonsensical steps.

#### 3.6. Dynamic Example Selection for Improved Knowledge Extraction

To provide more relevant in-context learning, the authors improve upon using a single fixed example in the prompt:
- **Similarity-based Selection:** For a given query task `Q`, the Translation LM is used to find the most semantically similar task `T*` from the demonstration set. Its corresponding example plan `E*` is then used in the prompt `(T* + E* + Q)`. This provides weak supervision by biasing the LLM towards relevant knowledge within its pre-trained parameters.

**Final Method:** The combination of these techniques—querying LLMs for action plans, admissible action parsing by semantic translation, autoregressive trajectory correction, and dynamic example selection—constitutes the "Translated LLM" approach (e.g., "Translated GPT-3 175B").

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings regarding the capabilities of LLMs as zero-shot planners for embodied agents:

1. **LLMs Possess Actionable Knowledge, Surpassing Human Correctness (Initially):** When prompted appropriately, large LLMs like GPT-3 (175B parameters) and Codex (12B parameters) can generate remarkably plausible and sensible action plans expressed in free-form natural language. Human evaluators, in some cases, judged the *semantic correctness* of these vanilla LLM plans (e.g., GPT-3 175B at 77.86%) to be even higher than human-written plans (70.05%) in VirtualHome, which are constrained by the environment's specific syntax and vocabulary. This suggests LLMs indeed internalize rich common-sense knowledge about how to perform complex tasks.

2. **Vanilla LLM Plans are Largely Inexecutable:** Despite their high semantic correctness, the action plans produced naively by LLMs are largely *not executable* in the VirtualHome environment. Vanilla GPT-3 175B, for instance, achieved only 7.79% executability. Smaller models (e.g., GPT-2 117M at 18.66%) showed slightly higher executability, but this was often due to a failure mode where they generated shorter, incomplete plans or simply repeated the prompt example, rather than correctly planning for the queried task. This highlights the crucial gap between linguistic plausibility and environmental grounding.

3. **Proposed Procedure Substantially Improves Executability:** The authors' proposed "Translated LLM" procedure significantly boosts the executability of the generated plans. For Codex 12B, executability jumped from 18.07% (vanilla) to 78.57% (translated). Similarly, for GPT-3 175B, it increased from 7.79% to 73.05%. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the semantic translation and autoregressive correction mechanisms in mapping free-form LLM outputs to admissible environment actions. The LCS metric also improved, indicating greater similarity to human-written, syntactically correct plans.

4. **Trade-off Between Executability and Correctness:** While executability drastically improves, the human-evaluated correctness of the "Translated LLM" plans shows a noticeable drop compared to their vanilla counterparts. For example, Translated GPT-3 175B achieved 66.13% correctness, lower than Vanilla GPT-3 175B's 77.86%, though still competitive with human-written plans (70.05%). This trade-off is attributed to the Translation LM struggling with compounded instructions and the limitations of VirtualHome's expressivity, sometimes leading to early termination or mapping to less ideal, albeit executable, actions.

5. **Importance of Each Procedural Component:** Ablation studies confirm that each of the three proposed components (action translation, dynamic example selection, and autoregressive trajectory correction) contributes positively to performance (executability and LCS). Action translation showed the most significant impact on executability, emphasizing its role in grounding.

6. **Improved Grounding of Executable and Correct Plans:** The percentage of plans that are *both* executable and human-judged correct saw substantial improvement with the proposed methods. Translated GPT-3 175B achieved 35.23% of plans being both executable and correct, a significant leap from Vanilla GPT-3 175B's 6.82%. This demonstrates progress towards truly grounded actionable knowledge, though still below human-level performance (65.91% for human-written plans).

7. **Impact of Translation LM and Step-by-Step Instructions:**
 - Using more sophisticated Sentence-BERT/RoBERTa models as Translation LMs significantly outperforms simple averaged GloVe embeddings, highlighting the need for robust semantic embedding.
 - When provided with *step-by-step instructions* in addition to high-level task names, Translated Codex/GPT-3 models achieved LCS scores (32.87% and 31.05% respectively) comparable to supervised LSTM baselines (34.00%) without any fine-tuning, suggesting LLMs can leverage detailed guidance effectively.

8. **Program Length Analysis:** Translated models strike a balance in program length, generating plans that are expressive enough (average length around 7.13-7.36 steps) while being highly executable, contrasting with smaller LLMs that generate shorter, less correct plans, or larger vanilla LLMs that generate longer but often unexecutable ones.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds substantial significance and potential impact for the fields of embodied AI, robotics, and natural language processing:

1. **Pioneering Zero-Shot Planning for Embodied Agents:** The most significant contribution is demonstrating that large pre-trained language models can serve as **zero-shot planners** for complex, open-ended human activities in interactive environments. This is a paradigm shift from traditional methods that rely on extensive domain-specific training or hand-coded rules. It suggests that the vast, implicit common-sense knowledge within LLMs can be directly harnessed for goal-driven decision-making, offering a path towards more general-purpose AI agents.

2. **Bridging the Language-Action Gap at Inference Time:** The proposed inference-time procedure—involving semantic translation, autoregressive correction, and dynamic example selection—provides practical tools to **ground free-form natural language plans into executable actions**. This is crucial for real-world application, as LLM outputs, while semantically rich, are often incompatible with the precise, constrained interfaces of robotic systems. The fact that these tools operate without modifying the LLM's parameters or requiring additional training data makes them highly scalable and adaptable to existing LLM deployment pipelines.

3. **Reducing Data and Training Requirements:** By leveraging pre-existing knowledge in LLMs, this work substantially reduces the need for large, manually annotated datasets of task-specific demonstrations and extensive fine-tuning. This can accelerate the development and deployment of embodied AI agents, particularly in domains where data collection is expensive, time-consuming, or unsafe.

4. **Highlighting the Challenge of Balancing Correctness and Executability:** The paper's finding of a trade-off between human-judged semantic correctness and environmental executability is a critical insight. It illuminates a fundamental challenge in embodied AI: while LLMs can generate highly plausible narratives of action, ensuring these narratives are perfectly aligned with environmental constraints and the agent's capabilities remains an active research area. This will guide future research into more sophisticated grounding mechanisms, potentially incorporating environment feedback or richer semantic representations.

5. **Informing Future LLM Development:** The findings provide valuable feedback to the LLM community. The observed limitations in mapping complex natural language instructions to succinct admissible actions, or handling environmental constraints, indicate areas where LLMs could be improved (e.g., better reasoning about implicit pre-conditions and post-conditions of actions, or generating more structured outputs).

6. **Foundation for More Capable Robotic Systems:** This work lays a foundation for building more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems. Imagine a robot that can be given a high-level command like "prepare a meal" and, by leveraging LLM knowledge and these grounding techniques, generate a sequence of executable steps without prior explicit training for that specific meal preparation. This could lead to robots that are more intuitive to interact with and capable of handling a wider range of tasks in unstructured human environments.

In conclusion, this paper provides compelling evidence that LLMs are not just powerful language generators but also latent common-sense planners. By offering a robust set of inference-time techniques to bridge the gap between linguistic knowledge and physical action, it makes significant strides towards unlocking the full potential of large language models for building truly intelligent, embodied agents.
