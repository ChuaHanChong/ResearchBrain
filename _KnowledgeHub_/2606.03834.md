# Let the Dynamics Flow: Stable Flow Matching Dynamical Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KTH Royal Institute of Technology and Universidad de Chile developed Stable Flow Matching Dynamical Systems (SFMDS), a framework that combines flow matching's generative capabilities with formal stability guarantees for robot motion generation. The method ensures stable, multimodal, and scalable robot behaviors, achieving 100% stability on benchmark datasets while successfully capturing complex multimodal distributions and being deployed on a real humanoid robot for 6D tasks.

## Problem

- Generative models like Flow Matching (FM) are effective for synthesizing complex robot motion but lack formal safety or stability guarantees crucial for real-world applications.
- Existing stable dynamical system models for robot motion learning often exhibit limited expressivity and are typically unimodal, restricting their ability to represent diverse action distributions.
- Many current stable dynamical system approaches primarily assume Euclidean state spaces, limiting their practical applicability to robot motions in non-Euclidean Lie groups like SE(3).

## Method

- Proposed Stable ==Flow Matching== Dynamical Systems (**SFMDS**), which implicitly models robot dynamics via a flow matching process and constrains the learned solutions to a family of stable behaviors.
- Utilized LaSalle’s invariance principle to constrain the flow matching vector field, ensuring positive invariance (state remains within specified regions) and asymptotic stability (convergence to a goal pose).
- Developed 'Soft **SFMDS**' (penalty-based) and 'Hard **SFMDS**' (projection-based) variants, extending the framework to discrete-time systems and non-Euclidean Lie groups for motions involving rotations.

## Results

- Achieved **100%** asymptotic stability on R² and T² LASA datasets, significantly outperforming unconstrained Behavior Cloning (BC) which failed in over 32-**40%** of trajectories, while maintaining competitive accuracy.
- Successfully captured and reproduced multimodal behaviors with higher accuracy (Chamfer distance) than unimodal baselines, and learned stable 6D (position and orientation) pouring dynamics with less than **0.13%** non-convergent trajectories.
- Deployed Hard SFMDS on a real humanoid robot for an SE(3) insertion task, demonstrating convergence from unseen initial poses and robustness to disturbances at real-time control frequencies (around 13 Hz).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formal stability guarantees can be directly integrated into high-capacity generative models by applying LaSalle’s invariance principle to constrain the vector field learned via flow matching.
> - The framework successfully bridges the gap between expressive multimodal generative models and formally stable dynamical systems, enabling both rich behavior generation and guaranteed convergence.
> - SFMDS can effectively model and guarantee stability for robot motions in non-Euclidean spaces like Lie groups (e.g., SE(3)), enhancing its relevance for complex robotic manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper is situated within the field of imitation learning for robot motion generation, specifically at the intersection of generative models and control theory. Recent advancements in generative models, particularly Flow Matching (FM) techniques, have demonstrated efficacy in synthesizing complex robot motion skills through imitation learning. These methods are noted for their scalability, expressivity, and ability to capture multimodal action distributions, outperforming some classical behavior cloning approaches. However, a limitation of general-purpose generative models like flow matching is their lack of inherent formal safety or stability guarantees, which are considered crucial for dependable and generalizable robot behaviors in real-world applications.

Conversely, a parallel line of research in robot motion learning has emphasized the importance of stability guarantees by modeling motion policies as learned dynamical systems. These dynamical systems-based approaches often incorporate Lyapunov stability principles to ensure safe, robust, and generalizable robot behaviors. A common drawback of many existing dynamical system models, however, is their tendency to be unimodal and exhibit limited expressivity, which can restrict their capacity to represent the rich action distributions encountered in complex robotic tasks.

This work addresses the aforementioned limitations by proposing a framework, Stable Flow Matching Dynamical Systems (SFMDS), which aims to bridge the gap between high-capacity generative modeling provided by flow matching and the formal Lyapunov stability guarantees essential for robot control. It builds upon prior work in learning stable dynamical systems through structural Lyapunov constraints, learned Lyapunov functions, or diffeomorphic transformations, while aiming to integrate the multimodal capabilities of modern generative models. The paper positions itself as an advancement beyond approaches that offer only weak stability guarantees via regularization or those that leverage normalizing flows but remain limited in capturing complex multimodal behavior. It also extends existing concepts by applying LaSalle’s invariance principle to constrain the solution space of flow matching models to enforce stability on robot trajectories directly, rather than solely on the support of the target behavior distribution. Furthermore, it explicitly considers the practical requirement of modeling robot motions in non-Euclidean spaces, such as Lie groups (e.g., SE(3) for position and orientation), an aspect often overlooked in many Euclidean-focused approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel framework, Stable Flow Matching Dynamical Systems (SFMDS), that enables the learning of stable and expressive multimodal dynamical systems for robot motion generation. This framework seeks to integrate the generative capabilities of flow matching with formal stability guarantees.

The motivation for this work arises from several identified challenges in robot imitation learning:

- **Safety and Generalizability:** Robot behaviors, particularly in physical interaction and real-world deployment, require rigorous safety and stability guarantees. Unstable robot motions can lead to unpredictable behavior, damage to the robot or environment, or pose risks to human safety. Existing flow matching policies, despite their generative power, lack these intrinsic stability properties.
- **Expressivity and Multimodality:** Many stable dynamical system learning methods are often unimodal, meaning they learn a single, deterministic movement pattern. This limits their ability to capture and reproduce the diverse, often multimodal (i.e., multiple valid ways to perform a task) action distributions observed in complex human demonstrations or desired in flexible robotic tasks. The paper aims to leverage flow matching's capacity for multimodal generative modeling to overcome this limitation.
- **Scalability:** Robot control problems often involve high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., joint angles, end-effector poses, or complex sensor inputs). The proposed framework intends to provide a scalable solution that can handle these high-dimensional systems effectively.
- **Geometric Fidelity (Non-Euclidean Spaces):** Robot motions frequently involve both translational and rotational components, which naturally reside in non-Euclidean spaces known as Lie groups (e.g., SO(3) for orientation, SE(3) for rigid body poses). Most existing stable dynamical system approaches assume Euclidean state spaces, limiting their practical applicability. A key motivation is to extend stability guarantees to these geometrically rich, non-Euclidean domains.
- **Bridging a Methodological Gap:** The research is motivated by a recognized gap between two successful paradigms: the high-capacity, expressive, and scalable generative models (like flow matching) and the formally stable, but often less expressive, dynamical system models. The objective is to synthesize the advantages of both, creating policies that are both robustly stable and capable of generating diverse, complex behaviors.
- **Addressing Limitations of Prior Work:** The authors note that previous attempts to introduce stability (e.g., via diffeomorphic mappings or Lyapunov functions) often involve limitations such as dependency on the expressivity of invertible networks, explicit maximum-likelihood estimation with Jacobian determinants, or provide only weak stability guarantees. This work seeks to offer a more robust and flexible approach by integrating stability directly into the flow matching objective.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SFMDS framework implicitly models robot dynamics through a flow matching process and then constrains the learned solutions to a family of stable behaviors. The core methodology involves:

**A. Flow Matching Dynamical Systems Formulation:**
The paper models time-invariant autonomous dynamical systems as ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of the form $\dot{x}_t = f_{\theta}(x_t, \omega_t)$, where $x_t$ is the state, $\dot{x}_t$ is its time derivative, $f_{\theta}$ is a learnable vector field, and $\omega_t$ is a stochastic input sampled from a simple distribution (e.g., Gaussian).
The vector field $f_{\theta}$ is implicitly defined as the output of a flow $\phi_{\theta}$, which is obtained by integrating a state-conditioned, time-invariant flow matching vector field $u_{\theta}(z_{\tau}; x_t)$ over an auxiliary time $\tau \in$. The initial state of this flow is $z_0 = [\omega_t, 0]^T$, and the final state $h_1$ (at $\tau=1$) corresponds to the velocity $\dot{x}_t$. The flow matching process regresses $u_{\theta}$ to a target vector field, which for rectified flows, is based on a linear interpolation between a base sample $h_0$ (here, $\omega_t$) and a target sample $h_1$ (here, the demonstrated $\dot{x}_t$).

**B. Imitation Learning Objective:**
The learning process aims to minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the distribution of demonstrated trajectories and the distribution induced by $f_{\theta}$, subject to $f_{\theta}$ belonging to a set of admissible functions $F_A$. This is achieved using a conditional flow matching loss, $\ell_{IL}$, which evaluates the squared norm difference between the learned $u_{\theta}$ and a target constant-velocity vector field derived from demonstrations. To mitigate distribution shift and improve multi-step predictions, an additional loss $\ell_{\kappa}^{IL}$ is introduced, which measures the error over rollouts of the learned dynamics using a single-step approximation of the terminal flow state.

**C. Constraining Flow Matching Dynamical Systems to Admissible Sets:**
To enforce stability, the learned velocity $\dot{x}_t = h_1$ must belong to a predefined admissible velocity set $\dot{X}_A(x_t; \theta)$. This is achieved by constraining the flow matching vector field $u_{\theta}$ such that its generated flow state $h_{\tau}$ converges to $\dot{X}_A$ by $\tau=1$.
LaSalle’s invariance principle is leveraged for this purpose. A function $H(h_{\tau})$ is defined as the squared distance from $h_{\tau}$ to $\dot{X}_A$. The conditions of LaSalle's principle are then used to define an admissible set of values $U_A(h_{\tau})$ for $u_{\theta}$, ensuring that $h_{\tau}$ approaches $\dot{X}_A$. For cases where $\dot{X}_A$ is a half-space (relevant for positive invariance), a closed-form projection is used to define the projection vector $\delta_h$ and the margin $m_u$ for the admissible $u$-space. The margin $m_u$ is determined to ensure convergence to $\dot{X}_A$ by $\tau=1$.

**D. Enforcing Stability Properties:**
SFMDS is designed to enforce two key stability properties:

1. **Positive Invariance:** To ensure the robot's state remains within a specified region $S$ (e.g., workspace), the set of admissible velocities $\dot{X}_A(x_t)$ is defined such that at the boundary $\partial S$, velocities point inwards or along the boundary. This is achieved by setting $\dot{X}_A$ as a half-space with the outward-pointing normal $n_{\partial}$ at $\partial S$.

2. **Asymptotic Stability:** To ensure convergence to a goal pose $x_e$, stability is enforced in a latent space $L \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ defined by a neural network encoder $\psi_{\theta}: X \rightarrow L$.
 - A simple Lyapunov candidate $V_L(y_t) = d(y_e, y_t)^2$ is used in the latent space, where $y_t = \psi_{\theta}(x_t)$ and $y_e = \psi_{\theta}(x_e)$.
 - Condition 1 (Injectivity): $\psi_{\theta}(x_t) = y_e \Leftrightarrow x_t = x_e$ is implicitly enforced by requiring $\dot{y}_t \neq 0$ for $x_t \neq x_e$.
 - Condition 2 (Decreasing Lyapunov Function): The latent dynamics $\dot{y}_t = J_{\psi_{\theta}}(x_t)f_{\theta}(x_t, \omega_t)$ are constrained to an admissible latent set $\dot{L}_A(y_t; \theta)$, where $\nabla_y V_L(y_t)^T \dot{y}_t < 0$, ensuring $V_L$ decreases. The latent flow dynamics $\tilde{u}_{\theta}$ are related to $u_{\theta}$ by $\tilde{u}_{\theta} = J_{\psi_{\theta}}u_{\theta}$.

**E. Two Variants for Constraint Implementation:**

1. **Soft SFMDS:** Weakly enforces stability via a hinge-type penalty term $\ell_{PI}$ (for positive invariance) or $\ell_{AS}$ (for asymptotic stability) added to the total loss. This typically leads to local asymptotic stability.

2. **Hard SFMDS:** Strictly guarantees stability by architecturally constraining $u_{\theta}$. This is achieved by projecting the learned vector field onto the admissible $u$-space $U_A(h_{\tau})$ or $\tilde{U}_A(\tilde{h}_{\tau})$ at each step. For global asymptotic stability, this variant requires $\psi_{\theta}$ to be a diffeomorphism, implying $J_{\psi_{\theta}}$ is invertible and $\text{dim}(L) = \text{dim}(X)$.

**F. Discrete-Time Systems (Forward-Euler Case):**
The framework accounts for the effect of numerical integration (e.g., Forward Euler) on stability. The continuous-time Lyapunov conditions are adapted to discrete-time equivalents, which define admissible velocity sets as balls rather than half-spaces. This is then integrated into the soft and hard constraint formulations.

**G. Lie Group Extension:**
SFMDS is extended to Lie groups (e.g., SE(3), T2) to handle robotic motions involving orientations. Velocities are represented in the Lie algebra. For hard SFMDS on Lie groups, the mapping $\psi_{\theta}$ operates between Lie algebras, and special considerations are made to handle "wrapping effects" (e.g., in SO(3)) by restricting the domain to the "first cover" using specific transformations (e.g., tanh-based mappings).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SFMDS encompassed six distinct experimental setups, ranging from benchmark datasets to real-robot tasks and high-dimensional systems. Key findings include:

- **General Performance and Stability:** SFMDS successfully learns stable, scalable, and multimodal dynamical systems. Across all experiments, SFMDS variants demonstrated consistent asymptotic stability, significantly outperforming unconstrained Behavior Cloning (BC) baselines, which frequently exhibited non-convergent trajectories.

- **LASA Dataset (R² and T²):**
 - On the R² LASA dataset, both soft and hard SFMDS variants achieved accuracy comparable to, or slightly surpassing, state-of-the-art methods (CLF-DM, Euclideanizing Flows, PUMA). Notably, all SFMDS variants exhibited zero or negligible percentages of unsuccessful trajectories (failures to converge), in stark contrast to BC, which failed in over 40% of cases.
 - On the newly introduced LASA T² dataset (2-torus), SFMDS also demonstrated competitive accuracy and 100% stability, while BC failed in over 32% of trajectories. This validates the framework's effectiveness in non-Euclidean spaces.
 - The 1S variant (sampling $\omega_t$ only at $t=0$) showed comparable performance to the non-1S variant (sampling $\omega_t$ at every time step), simplifying practical implementation.

- **Multimodal R² Dataset:**
 - SFMDS variants successfully captured the complex, intersecting multimodal behaviors of the dataset while ensuring asymptotic stability.
 - Quantitatively, both soft and hard SFMDS significantly outperformed unimodal state-of-the-art baselines (Euclideanizing Flows, PUMA) in terms of Chamfer distance, indicating superior accuracy in reproducing multimodal distributions. This highlights SFMDS's capacity to integrate multimodality with stability.

- **Simulated Water Pouring on SE(3):**
 - SFMDS learned smooth and convergent dynamics for complex 6D (position and orientation) pouring tasks.
 - Soft SFMDS generally achieved higher accuracy than hard SFMDS, attributed to its less restrictive constraints, while both qualitatively resembled demonstrations closely.
 - High stability was maintained, with less than 0.13% non-convergent trajectories, further supporting the method's applicability to Lie groups.

- **Real Humanoid Robot Insertion Task on SE(3):**
 - A hard SFMDS model was successfully deployed on a real RB-Y1 humanoid robot for an insertion task.
 - The robot demonstrated convergence from both seen and unseen initial poses and exhibited robustness to external disturbances during execution.
 - Despite the computational demands of hard SFMDS (requiring Jacobian computations), custom Taylor-series approximations enabled real-time control frequencies (around 13 Hz), sufficient for manipulation tasks.
 - Observed failures were primarily due to physical robot constraints (workspace limits, self-collisions) rather than model instability, suggesting the learned policy itself remained stable.

- **High-dimensional Reaction–Diffusion System (1682D):**
 - SFMDS demonstrated scalability on a 1682-dimensional Gray-Scott reaction-diffusion system.
 - Both soft and hard SFMDS accurately reproduced demonstrated trajectories, significantly outperforming BC.
 - Hard SFMDS consistently converged to the fixed-point equilibrium even when initialized from out-of-distribution (OOD) states. Soft SFMDS, while generally stable, sometimes converged to spurious attractors or exhibited temporary deviations from bounds when starting from OOD conditions, underscoring the stronger guarantees of hard constraints.

- **Inference Times:** While hard SFMDS (especially when using Neural ODE or Glow blocks for $\psi_{\theta}$) exhibited slower inference times compared to soft SFMDS or BC, it still achieved practical control frequencies (12-17 Hz) for complex tasks, particularly with optimized Jacobian computations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Stable Flow Matching Dynamical Systems (SFMDS) framework introduces a methodological advancement in robot imitation learning by successfully integrating the high-capacity generative modeling capabilities of flow matching with formal stability guarantees derived from control theory. This combination addresses a critical trade-off that has historically limited the deployment of learned robot behaviors in real-world scenarios.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Enhanced Robot Safety and Reliability:** By providing formal Lyapunov stability guarantees, SFMDS contributes to the development of safer and more reliable autonomous robot systems. This is crucial for applications in human-robot collaboration, industrial settings, and other safety-critical environments where unpredictable or unstable behaviors are unacceptable.
- **Greater Expressivity and Multimodality in Robot Skills:** The ability of SFMDS to capture and reproduce multimodal action distributions, while maintaining stability, allows robots to learn more diverse and flexible skills from demonstrations. This moves beyond deterministic, unimodal behaviors, enabling robots to adapt to variations in tasks or environments. This could lead to more versatile and robust robot manipulation, locomotion, and interaction capabilities.
- **Scalability to Complex Systems:** The demonstration of SFMDS on high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., 1682-dimensional reaction-diffusion system, 6D robot poses) suggests its applicability to more complex robotic systems, such as whole-body control, deformable object manipulation, or multi-robot coordination, where high-dimensional representations are common.
- **Applicability to Geometric Spaces:** The extension of SFMDS to Lie groups (e.g., SE(3) for robot end-effector poses) is significant for addressing real-world robot control problems that inherently involve rotations and translations in non-Euclidean geometries. This broadens the practical scope of stable imitation learning.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** SFMDS provides a robust baseline and a conceptual framework for several promising research directions. These include:
 - **Convergence to Sets of Goal States:** Many tasks require reaching a region of goals rather than a single point, which could expand the framework's utility.
 - **Integration of Physical Constraints:** Incorporating explicit robot physical limits (e.g., joint limits, obstacle avoidance, self-collision avoidance) directly into the dynamics could further enhance safety and robustness in complex environments.
 - **Richer Policy Conditioning:** Extending the conditioning mechanisms to include complex sensor inputs like images or point clouds could enable more sophisticated, perception-driven robot skills and open new avenues for stability analysis in these contexts.

In summary, SFMDS advances the state of the art by providing a method to learn complex, diverse, and formally stable robot motion policies, addressing a key challenge in bringing imitation learning closer to reliable real-world robot deployment.
