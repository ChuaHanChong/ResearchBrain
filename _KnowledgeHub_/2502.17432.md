# FACTR: Force-Attending Curriculum Training for Contact-Rich Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed FACTR, a framework combining a low-cost bilateral teleoperation system for data collection and a force-attending curriculum training strategy for robotic policies. This approach improved generalization capabilities in contact-rich tasks by effectively integrating force information, achieving an 87.5% success rate on unseen test objects and outperforming standard baselines by 43%.

## Problem

- Robot policies trained via imitation learning, primarily relying on visual feedback, struggle to generalize to novel objects and environments in contact-rich tasks.
- Collecting high-quality demonstration data for force-intensive tasks is difficult with traditional low-cost teleoperation systems due to the lack of force feedback to the human operator.
- Even when force data is available, policies often 'overfit' to the visual modality, effectively ignoring or underutilizing critical force information during training.

## Method

- A low-cost, intuitive bilateral teleoperation system with actuated leader arms provides mediated force feedback, active gravity/friction compensation, and customizable redundancy resolution for high-quality data collection.
- **FACTR** (==Force-Attending Curriculum Training==) employs a curriculum learning strategy that progressively introduces visual information by decaying visual corruption (e.g., Gaussian blur, downsampling) over training time.
- The policy model, an encoder-decoder transformer, processes both visual and external joint torque inputs, with the curriculum designed to initially force attention towards the force modality.

## Results

- The FACTR bilateral teleoperation system achieved a **64.7%** higher task completion rate and **37.4%** faster completion times compared to an un-actuated baseline.
- FACTR policies demonstrated superior generalization, achieving an **87.5%** success rate on unseen test objects across four contact-rich tasks, a **43%** improvement over a standard vision-plus-force baseline.
- Policies trained with FACTR exhibited better recovery capabilities for perturbations (e.g., knocked-down box), maintaining **90%** success on second attempts with test objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Force information is crucial for robust and generalizable robotic manipulation in contact-rich tasks, especially when visual cues are ambiguous or sparse.
> - A curriculum training strategy that progressively introduces visual detail while initially emphasizing force signals can effectively prevent policies from overfitting to visual input and neglecting force data.
> - Low-cost, bilateral teleoperation systems providing haptic feedback of robot interaction forces significantly improve data collection efficiency and quality for complex contact tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape and Motivation

#### 1.1. The Challenge of Contact-Rich Tasks

Many human daily activities, from lifting a box to rolling dough or cracking an egg, inherently involve complex physical interactions and precise force adjustments. These are termed "contact-rich tasks," and humans intuitively rely on continuous force feedback to execute them reliably and adapt to variations in objects and environments. Despite the critical role of force, modern robotic systems, particularly those relying on data-driven policy learning, predominantly focus on visual feedback. This reliance on vision alone significantly limits their ability to generalize to novel objects or handle unexpected physical interactions. For instance, a robot attempting to lift a box solely based on vision might struggle if the box's geometry or weight changes, whereas force feedback would provide a direct signal for necessary adjustments.

#### 1.2. Limitations of Current Approaches

The current landscape of robot learning, especially in imitation learning and behavior cloning (BC), has seen impressive advancements with techniques like Diffusion Policy and Action Chunking Transformers (ACT). However, these methods, largely vision-centric, often struggle with generalization when faced with variations in object visual appearance or geometry, which are common in contact-rich scenarios. The paper highlights two main reasons for the underutilization of force information in robot learning:

1. **Lack of Intuitive and Low-Cost Data Collection:** Traditional teleoperation systems, particularly low-cost leader-follower setups, are often passive and unilateral. This means the human operator doesn't receive force feedback from the follower robot, making it exceedingly difficult to collect high-quality demonstration data for tasks requiring intricate force control. Without sensing the robot's interaction forces, operators may inadvertently command actions that lead to loss of contact, excessive force, or instability, hindering effective data collection for learning.
2. **Difficulty in Effectively Incorporating Force into Policy Learning:** Even when force data is available, simply concatenating it with visual inputs for policy training often leads to suboptimal results. Policies tend to "overfit" to the visual modality, effectively disregarding or underutilizing the force information. This phenomenon occurs because contact force signals are often sparse—they remain near zero for extended periods when the robot is not in contact with the environment. Consequently, the visual modality, which is always active and typically richer in information, dominates the learning process, preventing the policy from properly attending to the critical, albeit sometimes subtle, force signals.

#### 1.3. Why Force Feedback is Underutilized

While some prior works have explored imitation learning with gripper force or tactile signals for specific tasks (e.g., vegetable peeling, handling fragile objects), utilizing raw robot arm force data, such as external joint torques, remains largely unexplored. Existing methods often rely on end-effector force sensors or infer 6D wrenches for low-level control, but integrating these into high-level policy learning for generalization has been challenging. Approaches like FoAR attempt to regulate vision-force fusion by predicting contact phases, but this requires additional data labeling. The lack of robust, generalizable methods for seamlessly integrating force and vision, without necessitating complex manual supervision or suffering from visual overfitting, represents a significant gap in the field. This paper directly addresses these critical limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **significantly improve the generalization capabilities of robotic policies for complex, contact-rich tasks by effectively leveraging robot force information**. To achieve this overarching goal, the authors pursue two interconnected key objectives:

1. **Develop a Low-Cost, Intuitive, Bilateral Teleoperation System with Force Feedback:** The motivation here is to overcome the challenge of data collection for force-intensive tasks. Current low-cost teleoperation systems often lack force feedback, making it difficult for human operators to provide high-quality demonstrations that capture the nuances of physical interaction. The objective is to design a system that:
 - Is affordable, using off-the-shelf components.
 - Provides intuitive, bilateral force feedback (relaying follower arm's external torques to the leader arm).
 - Incorporates features like active gravity compensation and customizable redundancy resolution to enhance teleoperation ease and precision.
 - Ultimately, facilitates the collection of high-fidelity, force-rich demonstration data necessary for training robust policies.

2. **Introduce FACTR (Force-Attending Curriculum Training) for Policy Learning:** The motivation behind FACTR is to address the prevalent issue of policies overfitting to visual input and neglecting available force information. Even with high-quality force data, traditional behavior cloning often fails to fully exploit this modality. The objective is to propose a novel curriculum training strategy that:
 - Systematically encourages the policy to attend to the force modality.
 - Mitigates the tendency of models to disregard force signals, especially when visual information is abundant or distracting.
 - Improves policy generalization to novel objects and scenarios by enabling a balanced fusion of visual and force inputs.
 - Achieves this without requiring explicit contact phase labeling or complex architectural modifications, relying instead on a clever input corruption and scheduling strategy.

In essence, the paper aims to close the loop between effective force-aware data collection and robust force-aware policy learning, thereby enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks with human-like adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FACTR system encompasses two main methodological pillars: a novel low-cost bilateral teleoperation setup for data collection, and a force-attending curriculum training strategy for policy learning.

#### 3.1. Low-Cost Bilateral Teleoperation System with Force Feedback

To address the data collection challenge for contact-rich tasks, the authors design an intuitive leader-follower teleoperation system building upon existing low-cost frameworks (like GELLO or ALOHA) but with significant enhancements:

- **Actuated Leader Arms with Force Feedback:** Unlike passive leader arms, FACTR's leader arms incorporate actuated servo motors. These motors are controlled to relay external joint torques sensed by the follower robot (e.g., Franka Panda, KUKA LBR iiwa) back to the human operator. This provides a "mediated force feedback" (scaled down) which has been shown to improve accuracy and reduce cognitive load. Crucially, the system *only* transmits external forces, ensuring the operator does not feel the follower arm's internal friction or inertia, leading to a clearer perception of environmental interactions. For the gripper, where an external force sensor might be absent, current readings from the gripper servo are used to infer and provide haptic feedback.
- **Customizable Redundancy Resolution:** For kinematically redundant manipulators, traditional mechanical regularization (e.g., springs) can lead to unintuitive end-effector wrenches and restrict reachability. FACTR leverages the active motors to implement a null-space projection control law. This allows the system to stabilize joint positions at a user-defined resting posture, improving reachability in confined spaces and preventing undesirable joint drift without interfering with end-effector control.
- **Active Gravity and Friction Compensation:** The servo motors on the leader arm also enable active gravity compensation, allowing the leader arm to remain stationary when not being manipulated, which is crucial for pausing teleoperation. A dynamic friction model is used to compensate for static, Coulomb, and viscous friction, resulting in smoother and more responsive teleoperation by reducing resistive forces.
- **Joint Limit Avoidance and Dynamic Collision Avoidance:** An artificial potential-based control law prevents the leader arm from exceeding the joint limits of the follower arm. For bimanual setups, a Riemannian Motion Policy (RMP) dynamically generates joint-space targets, incorporating real-time collision avoidance between the two follower arms and with external obstacles.

These enhancements collectively transform a basic leader-follower system into a sophisticated, yet low-cost, bilateral teleoperation interface capable of capturing high-quality data for complex, force-dependent robotic tasks.

#### 3.2. FACTR: Force-Attending Curriculum Training for Policy Learning

The second core contribution is FACTR, a curriculum training strategy designed to overcome the tendency of policies to overfit to visual information and instead effectively integrate force data.

- **Base Policy Model:** The policy is an encoder-decoder transformer, similar to Action Chunking Transformers (ACT). It takes as input an RGB image (processed by a pre-trained Vision Transformer, ViT) and an external joint torque reading (processed by an MLP-based force encoder). These are converted into visual and force tokens, concatenated, and fed into a transformer encoder. A transformer decoder then processes action tokens using cross-attention to the encoded visual and force tokens, finally projecting them to a chunk of future joint position targets (behavior cloning objective). A key design choice is to exclude current arm joints from proprioception to encourage the model to extract object information from images.

- **Force-Attending Curriculum Design:** The central idea of FACTR is to progressively introduce visual detail throughout training. This is achieved by corrupting the visual input (image or visual latent tokens) with an operator whose intensity decreases over time.
 - **Curriculum Operators:** Two types of operators are explored:
 - **Gaussian Blur:** Applies a 2D Gaussian kernel to the input image (pixel space) or a 1D Gaussian kernel to visual latent tokens (latent space).
 - **Downsampling:** Uses MaxPool followed by nearest interpolation in either pixel or latent space.
 The scale parameter (σ) of these operators (e.g., standard deviation for blur, kernel size for MaxPool) controls the amount of visual corruption.
 - **Curriculum Schedulers:** Various decay schedules for σ are evaluated: Constant, Linear, Cosine, Exponential, and Step decay. A warm-up phase is also incorporated where σ is kept constant at a high value initially to allow the force encoder to be "warmed up" with minimal visual interference.

- **Theoretical Intuition (NTK Perspective):** The paper provides a theoretical sketch using the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework. It argues that when the visual input is heavily blurred (high σ), distinct visual inputs become very similar in the feature space. In the extreme, the NTK approaches a constant, making the model effectively learn a single global output for all visual inputs. This forces the gradient updates in the early stages of training to focus on the *force modality* to differentiate between inputs and minimize the loss. As the blur decreases (σ decreases), more visual detail is gradually unveiled, allowing the policy to integrate the increasingly discriminative visual information while having already learned to leverage force effectively. This prevents the model from defaulting to vision-only learning.

By combining an advanced, yet low-cost, data collection platform with a theoretically grounded curriculum training strategy, FACTR aims to unlock the full potential of force feedback in generalizable robot manipulation policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FACTR is presented across four challenging contact-rich tasks: Box Lift (bimanual), Non-Prehensile Pivot, Fruit Pick and Place, and Rolling Dough. Both the teleoperation system and the learned policies are rigorously tested.

#### 4.1. Teleoperation Evaluation

A user study comparing FACTR's bilateral teleoperation system against an un-actuated leader-follower baseline (similar to GELLO/ALOHA) yielded significant improvements:
- **Completion Rate:** FACTR achieved a 64.7% higher task completion rate. This was particularly evident in tasks requiring continuous contact (e.g., non-prehensile pivoting, bimanual box lifting), where the un-actuated system often caused contact loss due to lack of force feedback.
- **Completion Time:** Tasks were completed 37.4% faster with FACTR. The intuitive force feedback prevented operators from commanding actions that would lead to large joint-space errors and subsequent abrupt movements, which often occurred in the un-actuated system when contact was lost.
- **Ease of Use:** Subjective user feedback indicated an 83.3% improvement in ease of use. Operators found the force feedback crucial for perceiving environmental constraints, making teleoperation more intuitive and precise.

These results validate the effectiveness and user-friendliness of the proposed low-cost bilateral teleoperation system for collecting high-quality demonstrations in contact-rich environments.

#### 4.2. Policy Evaluation

The core evaluation focuses on the performance and generalization of autonomous policies trained with FACTR, compared to baselines:
- **ACT (Vision-Only):** Action Chunking Transformer using only visual observations.
- **ACT (Vision+Force):** ACT using both visual and force observations, but without the FACTR curriculum.
- **FACTR (Ours):** ACT trained with force-attending curriculum (latent space curriculum with Gaussian blur and linear scheduler).

Key findings:

- **Superior Generalization:** FACTR significantly improved generalization to unseen objects. On average across the four tasks, FACTR achieved an 87.5% success rate on test objects, compared to 61.2% for ACT (Vision+Force) and a mere 21.3% for ACT (Vision-Only). This represents a 43% improvement over the standard Vision+Force baseline.
- **Impact of Force Information:** For tasks like rolling dough, where visual distinction during oscillatory motions is difficult, the vision-only policy completely failed (0% success). Policies incorporating force (ACT (Vision+Force) and FACTR) achieved 70% and 80% success respectively, demonstrating the critical role of force signals (oscillatory torque patterns) in such scenarios.
- **Mode Switching and Attention Behavior:** Visualization of cross-attention weights showed that FACTR policies learned to attend more strongly to force signals during critical "mode-switching" moments, such as when the robot arms established contact with the box in the lifting task. In contrast, policies without the curriculum often failed to adequately utilize force attention, leading to task failures. This empirically supports the curriculum's design to encourage force attention.
- **Improved Recovery Behavior:** In the box-lifting task, FACTR policies demonstrated significantly better recovery capabilities. While all policies performed well on initial attempts with training objects, for *test objects*, FACTR maintained a high success rate (90%) even on second attempts after the box was knocked down. Vision-only policies dropped drastically from 31.7% to 13.3%, often remaining static. FACTR policies could detect loss of contact via force feedback, revert to a pre-lift state, and re-attempt the task, highlighting robustness to perturbations.

#### 4.3. Ablations on Curriculum

Ablation studies on the Non-Prehensile Pivot task investigated different curriculum parameters:
- **Curriculum vs. Fixed-Scale Operator:** Training with a decaying smoothing scale (curriculum) consistently outperformed fixed-scale blurring. This confirms the hypothesis that gradual reduction of visual corruption is crucial; fixed high blur prevents necessary visual detail learning, while fixed low blur risks visual overfitting.
- **Operator and Scheduler Robustness:** The results did not show a uniform advantage for specific pixel/latent space operators (Gaussian blur vs. downsampling) or scheduler types (linear, cosine, exponential, step). This suggests that FACTR's benefits are relatively robust to the precise choice of curriculum parameters, as long as a decaying curriculum is employed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FACTR framework represents a significant step forward in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks that involve complex physical interactions. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Enabling Generalization in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The most direct impact of FACTR is its ability to enable robotic policies to generalize effectively to unseen objects and environments in contact-rich scenarios. This is a critical challenge in robotics, as real-world environments are inherently varied. By showing a 43% improvement in generalization success rate over strong baselines, FACTR pushes the boundaries of what data-driven robot learning can achieve in practical, real-world applications.
- **Highlighting the Crucial Role of Force Feedback:** The research compellingly demonstrates that force information, often underutilized, is indispensable for robust and generalizable manipulation, especially for tasks involving delicate interactions, continuous contact, or ambiguous visual cues (e.g., rolling dough). It advocates for a multimodal approach where force and vision are integrated in a balanced and effective manner.
- **Practical and Accessible Data Collection:** The development of a low-cost, intuitive, bilateral teleoperation system with force feedback is a major practical contribution. It democratizes the process of collecting high-quality, force-rich demonstration data, which has historically been a bottleneck due to the high cost and complexity of haptic devices. This makes advanced robot learning more accessible to a wider range of researchers and practitioners.
- **Novel Curriculum Learning Strategy:** FACTR introduces a simple yet highly effective curriculum training method that addresses the common problem of modality overfitting in multimodal learning. By systematically guiding the policy to first attend to a less-discriminative but crucial modality (force) before gradually introducing more detailed visual information, it provides a generalizable principle for training robust multimodal neural networks, not just for robotics. The theoretical intuition via NTK further strengthens this contribution.
- **Improved Robot Robustness and Recovery:** The demonstrated ability of FACTR policies to exhibit better recovery behavior after unexpected perturbations (e.g., a knocked-down box) is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured environments. This enhances the reliability and safety of autonomous systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The paper candidly acknowledges several limitations, which also suggest avenues for future research:
- **Sensor Precision:** The current reliance on external joint torque sensors in follower arms has precision limitations, which might affect tasks requiring very subtle force adjustments or fine-grained manipulation. Future work could explore integrating higher-resolution tactile sensors or haptic gloves to enhance feedback fidelity.
- **Sensor Availability Assumption:** The approach assumes the availability of external joint torque sensors. Adapting the system for robots with only end-effector force-torque sensors would broaden its applicability.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** The effectiveness of the curriculum can be influenced by hyperparameter choices (blurring operator, scheduling strategies), which might be task-dependent and require extensive tuning. Developing adaptive or self-tuning curriculum strategies that dynamically adjust parameters based on task requirements is a promising direction.

In conclusion, FACTR makes substantial contributions by providing both a practical hardware solution for data collection and a theoretically inspired algorithmic innovation for policy learning. This work not only advances the state-of-the-art in contact-rich robot manipulation but also lays the groundwork for more adaptable, generalizable, and robust robotic systems capable of performing complex tasks in the real world.
