# How to Train Your Robots? The Impact of Demonstration Modality on Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research empirically compared kinesthetic teaching, VR teleoperation, and spacemouse teleoperation for robot imitation learning across diverse manipulation tasks. Findings indicate that kinesthetic data generally produces higher policy success for non-contact tasks, and a hybrid data collection strategy, combining kinesthetic data with teleoperation, improved policy success rates by an average of 20% compared to single-modality training.

## Problem

- The effectiveness of imitation learning policies is highly dependent on the quality of human demonstrations, which can vary significantly based on the chosen collection modality.
- A disparity exists between the intuitive nature of kinesthetic teaching and the widespread use of teleoperation for large-scale robot dataset generation, implying unaddressed trade-offs between user experience and scalability.
- There was a lack of comprehensive empirical studies systematically comparing common low-cost demonstration modalities across user experience, data quality metrics, and downstream robot policy performance.

## Method

- Researchers systematically compared three common low-cost robot demonstration modalities: kinesthetic teaching, VR teleoperation, and spacemouse teleoperation.
- Data quality, user experience, and the performance of trained imitation policies were evaluated for each modality across three distinct tabletop manipulation tasks.
- A hybrid data collection paradigm was proposed and validated, which combined a small quantity of kinesthetic data with a larger volume of teleoperation data.

## Results

- Kinesthetic teaching resulted in the highest policy success rates for the Open Drawer and Flip Glass tasks, but policies trained on kinesthetic data underperformed for the contact-heavy Push Sanitizer task.
- Participants required significantly less practice time for kinesthetic teaching and reported lower mental demand and frustration, yet preferred teleoperation for extensive data collection due to lower physical demands.
- Policies trained on mixed-modality datasets (kinesthetic + VR teleoperation) achieved an average of **20%** higher success rates compared to policies trained solely on data from individual modalities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Kinesthetic teaching is more intuitive and yields data with higher action consistency, leading to superior policy performance for tasks not involving strong contact forces.
> - Teleoperation methods offer higher state diversity and are favored for large-scale data collection due to reduced physical burden, despite users reporting challenges with intuitiveness for certain motions.
> - A mixed-modality data collection approach can effectively balance the trade-offs between data quality, user effort, and scalability, leading to improved learning outcomes compared to single modalities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL) is a prevalent approach for enabling robots to acquire policies by observing human demonstrations. The efficacy of IL-based policies is significantly influenced by the quality of the provided data, a factor that can be mediated by the chosen demonstration modality. Existing research explores various methods for providing robot demonstrations, broadly categorized into teleoperation and shadowing.

Teleoperation modalities involve users controlling a robot remotely, often using devices such as 3D spacemouse controllers or virtual reality (VR) interfaces. These methods are frequently utilized in the creation of large-scale robot datasets, such as the Open X-Embodiment dataset. Shadowing approaches, like kinesthetic teaching or puppeteering, involve physically guiding the robot or a leader robot, respectively. Kinesthetic teaching allows direct physical interaction with the robot arm, potentially providing a more intuitive experience and direct feedback on joint limits and contact forces, though it typically requires replaying recorded poses to derive actions.

Previous studies have investigated control interfaces and their implications for user experience, with some suggesting that kinesthetic teaching is more intuitive than teleoperation for human users. Other work has focused on specific aspects of kinesthetic teaching, VR-based teleoperation, or vision-based teleoperation systems. Additionally, the broader field of imitation learning is increasingly concerned with formalizing, evaluating, and improving data quality, including methods for optimizing data mixtures or guiding data collection for consistency and generalization.

This research distinguishes itself from prior work in several key aspects. Firstly, while previous studies have often assessed demonstration performance based on the user's ability to successfully provide demonstrations, this work closes the evaluation loop by training downstream imitation policies with the collected data and measuring their learning performance. Secondly, much prior research has focused on the experiences of novice users; this study examines the experiences of expert robot users, particularly those engaged in large-scale data collection. Lastly, the paper contributes by proposing and evaluating a data collection scheme that combines multiple demonstration modalities, addressing the trade-offs between data quality, user effort, and scalability that often emerge in practical robot learning scenarios. By systematically comparing common low-cost modalities across multiple dimensions—policy performance, data quality, and user experience—this study offers insights complementary to existing literature and aims to provide practical guidance for future data collection designs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the influence of different demonstration modalities on downstream imitation learning performance and user experience in robotic manipulation tasks. The motivation for this investigation stems from several observations within the field of robot learning:

1. **Impact of Data Quality:** The performance of imitation learning policies is highly dependent on the quality of the demonstrated data. The method by which these demonstrations are provided, or the "demonstration modality," is a significant factor affecting data quality. Understanding how different modalities bias data collection is crucial for optimizing learning outcomes.
2. **Discrepancy in Modality Usage vs. Intuition:** While kinesthetic teaching has been noted for its intuitiveness and ease of use for humans, teleoperation methods, particularly those utilizing VR controllers or spacemouse devices, are predominantly employed for collecting large-scale robot manipulation datasets. This disparity suggests a trade-off between perceived user experience/data quality and practical considerations like scalability and physical effort. The research aims to empirically examine the nature of this trade-off.
3. **Comprehensive Evaluation Gap:** Existing research has explored individual aspects of demonstration modalities, such as user experience or specific interface designs. However, a comprehensive study that systematically compares common low-cost modalities across the entire spectrum—from user interaction and data quality metrics to the ultimate performance of learned policies—has been largely absent. This work seeks to fill this gap by providing a holistic evaluation.
4. **Identification of Optimal Data Collection Strategies:** Given the varying characteristics of different modalities, there is a need to identify effective data collection paradigms. The researchers hypothesize that no single modality is universally superior, and a hybrid approach leveraging the strengths of different modalities might offer an optimal balance between data quality, collection effort, and policy performance. This motivates the proposal and evaluation of a mixed-modality data collection scheme.

To achieve these objectives, the study focuses on three common low-cost demonstration modalities: kinesthetic teaching, teleoperation with a VR controller, and teleoperation with a spacemouse. These modalities are evaluated across three distinct tabletop manipulation tasks designed to incorporate various motion constraints. The evaluation encompasses both quantitative measures of policy success rates and qualitative analyses of user feedback and objective data quality metrics (action consistency and state diversity).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed an experimental methodology involving robotic hardware, specific manipulation tasks, controlled data collection procedures across different modalities, policy training using a fixed learning algorithm, data quality analysis, and a user study.

**Setup:**
A 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robotic arm was utilized. The robot was controlled using a Cartesian impedance controller in Polymetis, with actions represented as desired delta end-effector poses and a binary gripper command.

**Tasks:**
Three tabletop manipulation tasks with varying motion constraints were selected as testbeds:
1. **Open Drawer:** Involves free-space reaching, precise alignment for grasping a knob, and a constrained linear motion to pull the drawer.
2. **Flip Glass:** Requires free-space reaching, picking up a glass, a 180-degree rotation (operating near joint limits), and placement.
3. **Push Sanitizer:** Demands precise positioning on the sanitizer pump and exerting a downward pressing force, involving significant contact force.

**Data Collection Interfaces:**
- **Kinesthetic Teaching:** Demonstrators physically guided the robot arm in gravity-compensated mode, recording end-effector poses and gripper actions. Actions for policy training were derived by replaying these poses and computing the delta pose from the robot's current pose. A heuristic was implemented to address challenges with contact forces: if a replay failed, an error compensation term was added to the action in subsequent iterations, which increased replay time for contact tasks and could introduce action jerkiness.
- **VR Teleoperation:** An Oculus Quest controller was used. Robot end-effector motion was linked to the controller's movement relative to a user-defined reference pose. A button press activated/deactivated teleoperation, allowing users to reset the reference for full rotational control.
- **Spacemouse Teleoperation:** Input from the spacemouse was directly mapped to robot end-effector delta poses, scaled by a factor.

**Training Data:**
To minimize confounding factors from demonstrator variability, data for policy training was collected by a single expert demonstrator equally experienced with all three modalities. A total of 100 trajectories per modality were collected for each task. The demonstrator switched modalities every 10 trajectories, and initial states were marked to ensure consistent collection conditions.

**Policy Learning:**
Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (Diffusion Policy) were used as the imitation learning algorithm. The models received two RGB observations (wrist and external camera views) and proprioception data as input. They were trained to predict a sequence of 16 consecutive actions. During evaluation, only the first 8 predicted actions were executed per inference step. Models were trained for 800,000 gradient steps. The best-performing checkpoint was selected by probing the last 5 checkpoints (10,000 steps apart) using in-distribution tests. Final evaluation involved 20 trials, evenly split between in-distribution and out-of-distribution tests, with success rate as the primary metric.

**Data Quality Metrics:**
Data quality was approximated using metrics from Belkhale et al.:
- **Action Consistency:** Measured as the inverse of action variance among K nearest neighbors (K=200 for expert data, K=50 for user data) in the proprioceptive state space.
- **State Diversity:** Estimated similarly by computing state variance among K nearest neighbors.
- **Jerkiness:** Calculated as the second derivative of recorded actions.

**User Study:**
A user study was conducted to assess user experience:
- **Participants:** 12 university students (4 female, 8 male, aged 19–29) from computer science or engineering backgrounds. Six identified as "Expert" in robot policy training, and six had limited experience.
- **Independent Variables:** Kinesthetic teaching, VR teleoperation, and spacemouse teleoperation. VR and spacemouse were later grouped as "teleoperation methods" due to similar results.
- **Dependent Variables:** Objective measures included practice time until confidence. Subjective measures included NASA-TLX survey responses (mental demand, physical demand, frustration, perceived performance, etc.) and a final comprehensive questionnaire on modality preferences for robot control and large-scale data collection.
- **Hypotheses:** H1 proposed that kinesthetic teaching would require less practice time than teleoperation. H2 hypothesized that kinesthetic teaching would be preferred for robot control, while teleoperation would be preferred for large-scale data collection.
- **Procedure:** Participants first practiced a test task with each modality until confident. They then provided 5 successful demonstrations per modality for two assigned tasks (Open Drawer/Push Sanitizer and Flip Glass). Modality order was randomized within tasks. NASA-TLX surveys were completed after each modality, followed by a final questionnaire.

**Mixed Modality Data Scheme:**
Based on insights from the data analysis, a hybrid data collection paradigm was proposed, combining a small amount of kinesthetic data with a larger amount of teleoperation data. Experiments were conducted mixing kinesthetic and VR data for Open Drawer and Flip Glass tasks (Push Sanitizer was excluded due to the low quality of kinesthetic data in contact tasks).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The investigation yielded several key findings regarding the impact of demonstration modality on policy learning, user experience, and data quality:

**Policy Learning Performance:**
- **Kinesthetic Advantage:** For tasks not involving strong contact forces, kinesthetic teaching produced data that resulted in the highest policy success rates. Specifically, kinesthetic data led to the best-performing models for Open Drawer and Flip Glass, with a notable margin for Open Drawer.
- **Kinesthetic Limitation:** In the Push Sanitizer task, which required significant contact force, kinesthetic teaching underperformed compared to teleoperation methods. This was attributed to the replay heuristic used to derive actions, which introduced jerkiness into the actions and impeded effective policy learning, despite the task being completed in the second replay iteration.

**User Experience and Intuitiveness:**
- **H1 Supported (Intuitiveness):** Participants required significantly less practice time with kinesthetic teaching compared to teleoperation methods (p-value = 0.038), supporting the hypothesis that kinesthetic teaching is perceived as more intuitive for robot control.
- **VR Challenges:** Users reported that controlling rotational motions with VR teleoperation was unintuitive, often requiring frequent resetting of the reference pose.
- **NASA-TLX Feedback:** Kinesthetic teaching consistently received lower ratings for mental demand (p < 0.01) and frustration (p < 0.01), and higher ratings for perceived performance (p < 0.01). However, it was rated highest in physical demand (p = 0.016).
- **H2 Supported (Preference for Large-Scale Collection):** Despite the positive subjective ratings for kinesthetic teaching in terms of intuitiveness and perceived performance, the majority of users, when asked about large-scale data collection, preferred teleoperation-based modalities over kinesthetic teaching. This supports the hypothesis that physical burden and replay time make kinesthetic teaching less desirable for extensive data collection.

**Data Quality Analysis:**
- **State Diversity:** Data collected through teleoperation (VR and spacemouse) generally exhibited higher state diversity, with trajectories spread out more widely in space.
- **Action Consistency and Jerkiness:** Kinesthetic teaching produced data with lower action variance (higher consistency) and lower maximum jerkiness for tasks without strong contact forces (Open Drawer, Flip Glass).
- **Contact Force Impact:** For the Push Sanitizer task, kinesthetic data showed high action variance and jerkiness, confirming the negative impact of the replay heuristic for tasks with substantial contact forces.
- **Task-Specific Observations:** In the Flip Glass task, expert kinesthetic trajectories showed high overlap, indicating operation near joint limits. In contrast, non-expert teleoperation data for Flip Glass displayed widely dispersed trajectories, suggesting user difficulty.

**Learning from Mixed Modality Data:**
- **Improved Data Quality:** Datasets combining a small amount of kinesthetic data with additional VR teleoperation data demonstrated an improved balance of data quality metrics. These mixed datasets displayed higher state diversity than single-modality datasets and slightly higher action consistency than pure VR data for certain tasks (e.g., Flip Glass).
- **Enhanced Policy Performance:** Policies trained on these mixed-modality datasets achieved an average of 20% higher success rates compared to policies trained exclusively on data from individual modalities. For example, a model trained with 100 mixed demonstrations for Flip Glass achieved a 75% success rate, surpassing the 70% success rate of a model trained solely on 100 kinesthetic demonstrations. This indicates that a hybrid approach can effectively mitigate the limitations of single modalities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robot imitation learning:

1. **Empirical Validation of Modality Trade-offs:** The study provides systematic empirical evidence quantifying the trade-offs associated with different demonstration modalities, bridging the gap between user experience, data characteristics, and downstream policy performance. This comprehensive evaluation is crucial for understanding the full implications of data collection choices.
2. **Informed Data Collection Strategies:** The findings offer practical guidance for researchers and practitioners. They indicate that kinesthetic teaching generally yields higher quality data and more performant policies for tasks without significant contact forces, while teleoperation offers scalability despite perceived user challenges. This insight can inform modality selection based on task requirements and resource constraints.
3. **Introduction of a Hybrid Data Collection Paradigm:** The proposed and validated hybrid data collection scheme (combining a small quantity of high-quality kinesthetic data with a larger volume of more easily collected teleoperation data) represents a practical advancement. This approach demonstrates a method to balance the often-conflicting demands of data quality, human effort, and scalability, potentially leading to more efficient and effective data acquisition pipelines for real-world robotic applications.
4. **Challenging the Status Quo:** The research challenges the prevailing trend of exclusively relying on teleoperation for large-scale datasets by highlighting the benefits of kinesthetic teaching. By demonstrating that even a small proportion of kinesthetic data can significantly boost policy performance when combined with teleoperation data, it advocates for a more nuanced approach to dataset creation.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The study opens avenues for future investigations, such as developing methods for automatically determining the optimal ratio of data from different modalities to maximize learning performance while minimizing collection costs. This could lead to adaptive and intelligent data collection systems.
6. **Enhanced Understanding of Data Quality:** By analyzing action consistency, state diversity, and jerkiness across modalities, the work contributes to a deeper understanding of what constitutes "high-quality" data in imitation learning and how different collection methods influence these attributes.

In conclusion, this work provides a detailed analysis of how demonstration modality affects the entire imitation learning pipeline, from user interaction to policy success. Its primary impact lies in offering a validated, hybrid data collection strategy that leverages the strengths of different modalities, thereby enabling more effective and resource-efficient training of robot policies.
