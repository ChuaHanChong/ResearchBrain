# Achieving Precise and Reliable Locomotion with Differentiable Simulation-Based System Identification

> [!summary] Summary
> A control framework that folds system identification into the RL training loop using the differentiable simulator MuJoCo-XLA. It fits MuJoCo motor parameters (armature, frictionloss, damping) by minimizing squared error between simulated and real trajectory segments, needing only positions, velocities, and control inputs. Policies trained on the identified model cut rotational deviation 75% and raised commanded-direction travel 46% on a bipedal robot.

## Problem

- Classical actuator identification depends on torque sensors or dedicated test stands, which are costly, noisy, calibration-sensitive, and rarely available during normal robot operation.
- Domain randomization never improves the simulator itself; it demands range tuning and yields conservative policies that sacrifice agility to tolerate parameter spread.
- Learned actuator models still consume torque data, and prior differentiable-simulator identification work was not integrated into an RL loop or validated on real legged hardware.

## Method

- ==Torque-free identification objective==: minimize squared deviation between real and simulated states over the rollout, with states restricted to joint positions and velocities and actions as PD desired angles.
- ==Segmented trajectory fitting== splits data into short horizons of length N with per-segment state resets, curbing accumulation of ==explicit-Euler integration error== during gradient-based parameter optimization.
- ==Gradient optimization== through **MuJoCo-XLA** tunes built-in armature, frictionloss, and damping; excitation data come from ==Fourier-mode desired-velocity trajectories== tracked by a low-gain PD controller.

## Results

- On held-out motor test data, the identified model reduced trajectory MSE roughly **8x** relative to the default baseline; training loss dropped about **20%** from the initial parameter guess.
- Replaying a fixed policy over **10** seven-second trials, mean position drift versus the real robot fell from **1.13 m** (baseline) to **0.16 m**, an **86%** accuracy gain.
- Policies trained on the identified model walked **1.64 m** versus **1.12 m** in the commanded direction and deviated **27 deg** versus **109 deg** in heading.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fitting a few physically meaningful simulator parameters can beat richer neural actuator models when data is sparse, since networks show high variance in unvisited operating regions.
> - Short-horizon segmented rollouts are the practical trick that makes gradient-based identification stable; long rollouts let integrator error swamp the parameter gradient.
> - Lowering PD gains during data collection exposes intrinsic actuator dynamics that stiff controllers mask, so excitation design matters as much as the optimizer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Accurate simulation models are a foundational requirement for the successful application of reinforcement learning (RL) and optimization-based control strategies in robotics. A primary challenge in achieving high-fidelity simulations is system identification, which involves precisely estimating a robot’s physical parameters to accurately bridge the gap between simulated and real-world behavior, often referred to as the "sim-to-real gap."

Traditional system identification methods frequently rely on direct torque measurements to infer model parameters. These approaches often necessitate specialized hardware, such as torque sensors, which can be expensive, susceptible to noise, and not universally available in robotic systems. Furthermore, they typically require controlled experimental environments, which may not be practical for dynamic robotic applications.

An alternative strategy employed in reinforcement learning is domain randomization. This involves training a policy across a range of simulated environments with varying parameters, such as mass and control gains, to promote robustness. While this can prevent overfitting to a single simulation, it often requires extensive tuning and can lead to the development of more conservative policies, potentially limiting the robot’s agility and optimal performance. Other approaches like transfer learning and offline RL aim to reduce real-world data requirements but still depend on the accuracy of initial simulations or the quality of pre-collected datasets. Many existing RL applications continue to operate with simplified system models, which can result in performance degradation when deployed on physical robots.

The emergence of differentiable simulators, such as Brax, Tiny Differentiable Simulator (TDS), and MuJoCo-XLA (MJX), has introduced new possibilities for system identification. These simulators enable gradient-based optimization of system parameters, aligning simulated dynamics with real-world observations. MuJoCo, specifically, is recognized for its accuracy, numerical stability, advanced physics modeling, and widespread adoption in robotics research. Previous work has demonstrated the feasibility of system identification using differentiable simulators, particularly with TDS.

This work addresses the limitations of traditional system identification methods and domain randomization by proposing a novel, torque-measurement-free approach that leverages differentiable simulation. It builds upon the capabilities of differentiable simulators to offer a scalable method seamlessly integrated into the RL training loop, aiming to improve simulation fidelity and, consequently, real-world robot control and performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the challenge of trajectory drift in bipedal locomotion, a common issue in reinforcement learning and model-based control of robots. This drift is primarily attributed to discrepancies between the simulated robot model and the physical robot, leading to a significant "sim-to-real gap."

The paper identifies specific limitations of existing system identification techniques that hinder the development of high-fidelity simulations. Traditional methods often require direct torque measurements, which are problematic due to the high cost, susceptibility to noise, and limited availability of torque sensors in many real-world robotic setups. Additionally, these methods typically demand highly controlled experimental environments, reducing their practical applicability. While domain randomization attempts to address the sim-to-real gap in RL by training policies across varied environments, it does not fundamentally improve the underlying simulation model and often results in policies that are overly conservative, sacrificing optimal performance for robustness.

Against this background, the key objectives of this research are detailed as follows:

- **Develop a novel control framework:** Introduce a new system identification step designed to be integrated directly into the reinforcement learning training loop.
- **Eliminate reliance on torque sensors:** The framework aims to estimate crucial system parameters solely from observable trajectory data (positions and velocities) and control inputs, thereby removing the need for direct torque measurements.
- **Leverage differentiable simulation:** Utilize the differentiable simulator MuJoCo-XLA (MJX) to enable gradient-based optimization of system parameters. This optimization seeks to minimize the discrepancy between the simulated robot's behavior and real-world motion.
- **Achieve scalable and flexible parameter optimization:** The method is designed to support the optimization of fundamental physical properties, such as mass and inertia, as well as more complex, nonlinear system behaviors like friction and delays, potentially through neural network approximations. Its compatibility with MuJoCo models is intended to ensure applicability across diverse robotic systems.
- **Validate the system identification approach:** Empirically demonstrate that optimizing motor parameters using this method significantly improves the alignment between simulated and real motor trajectories through real-world motor trajectory data collection.
- **Improve RL policy learning and real-world robot performance:** Show that policies trained using the identified and optimized simulation model result in superior real-world robot performance, specifically in terms of reduced trajectory drift and increased travel distance, compared to policies trained on unoptimized or baseline simulation models.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for a more practical, accurate, and adaptable method for system identification that can enhance the fidelity of robotic simulations, ultimately leading to more precise and reliable robot locomotion in real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on formulating and solving an optimization problem to identify system parameters by minimizing the discrepancy between real and simulated robot trajectories. The differentiable simulator MuJoCo-XLA (MJX) is a core component, enabling gradient-based optimization.

**Problem Formulation:**
The core problem is defined as finding a unique parameter set `z*` that accurately models the system's dynamics. This is achieved by minimizing the squared Euclidean distance between the real state trajectory `{s_i}` and the simulated state trajectory `{s'_i}`. The simulated states are generated by a dynamics function `Φ_z(s, a, δ)` which integrates the system's state `s` under action `a` over a prediction step `δ` with parameters `z`.
The primary optimization objective is:
`z* = arg min_z sum(||s'_i - s_i||^2)`
`s.t. s'_{i+1} = Φ_z(s'_i, a_i, δ), s'_0 = s_0.`
To mitigate the accumulation of numerical integration errors over extended trajectories, the problem is further refined by dividing the trajectory into multiple segments of length `N`, and minimizing the error within each segment. This segmentation helps in maintaining accuracy over longer durations.

**Limitations of the Approach:**
The method assumes that the parameter vector `z` primarily captures the differences between simulation and reality. If unmodeled factors, such as external disturbances or significant parameter variations not encoded in `z`, are present, the accuracy of state predictions may be compromised. Furthermore, achieving exact equality between real and simulated dynamics (as implied by Equation 1 in the paper) can be impractical due to the inherent complexity of dynamics modeling; however, the method is considered effective as long as `Φ_z*` provides a sufficiently close approximation.

**Practical Implementation - Motor Identification:**
The research focuses on identifying motor dynamics, specifically for the Mini π robot's motors. After considering neural network approximations for motor output torque, which were found to exhibit high variance in data-sparse regions and require risky data collection, the authors opted to optimize MuJoCo's built-in motor parameters: `armature`, `frictionloss`, and `damping`. This choice offered a balance between flexibility and data requirements.

1. **Dataset Generation:**
 - A real motor was extracted from a Mini π robot and securely mounted with a rod of known mass and inertia to serve as a load.
 - A diverse set of desired velocity trajectories `{v_des_i}` was generated using a sum of Fourier modes with randomly sampled coefficients and frequencies. These velocities were clipped to the motor's maximum velocity.
 - The desired angles `{q_des_i}` were obtained by integrating these velocities.
 - The input command `a_i` for the motor was set to `q_des_i`.
 - A PD controller with reduced gains (K_p = 20, K_d = 1) was applied to the motor to enhance the prominence of its intrinsic dynamics.
 - The real motor's measured angles and velocities `{s_i = [q_i, v_i]^T}` were recorded.
 - The entire dataset was resampled to a 1 ms time step (δ).

2. **Motor Identification Results:**
 - The dataset was split into training and testing sets, with the training data further divided into segments of length `N=4`.
 - MuJoCo's default explicit Euler integrator was chosen for `Φ_z` due to its faster convergence (approximately four times quicker) without significant loss in accuracy compared to higher-order methods like RK4.
 - The optimization process minimized the objective function (Equation 3), leading to smooth convergence of the motor parameters.
 - A baseline model, characterized by zero `frictionloss` and `damping`, and minimal `armature`, was used for comparison. The optimized model was initialized with the identified parameters and applied control inputs from an unseen test dataset.

**Validation with Real Robot:**
To assess the effectiveness of the identified motor parameters, they were incorporated into a full MuJoCo robot model. The baseline robot model, representing common RL practice, used zero joint parameters. A pre-trained RL policy, which used only time, joint positions, and joint velocities as inputs and fixed control inputs at zero, was deployed in three environments: the real world, a simulation with the baseline model, and a simulation with the optimized model. Each experiment was repeated 10 times for 7 seconds. The mean Euclidean distance between the robot’s starting and final positions was measured to quantify simulation accuracy.

**Applying the Optimized Model to RL Policy Learning:**
To demonstrate the impact on RL policy performance, a walking policy was trained using the optimized robot model and compared against a policy trained on the baseline model (unadjusted parameters). Training was conducted using the LeggedGym framework with its built-in reward functions, without domain randomization. Both policies were then deployed on the real robot under identical conditions (angular velocity scale reduced by half, commanded linear velocity set to maximum). Ten runs, each lasting 7 seconds, were performed for each policy. Performance metrics included mean travel distance in the commanded direction and mean rotational deviation from the original heading.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings, demonstrating the efficacy of the proposed system identification framework across motor characterization, simulation-to-reality alignment, and real-world reinforcement learning performance.

**Motor Identification Results:**
The optimization process for identifying motor parameters, specifically `armature`, `frictionloss`, and `damping`, exhibited smooth convergence to optimal values (Figure 3). This stability indicates the robustness of the gradient-based identification method. A quantitative comparison revealed that the optimization reduced the loss function by approximately 20% relative to an initial parameter guess. When evaluated on an unseen test dataset, the simulated motor trajectory generated with the optimized parameters showed a significantly improved alignment with the real motor trajectory compared to a baseline model (which had zero `frictionloss` and `damping`, and minimal `armature`). The mean squared error (MSE) between the optimized simulated trajectory and the real trajectory was approximately eight times smaller than that of the baseline model, as illustrated in Figure 4. This indicates a substantial improvement in the fidelity of the motor model.

**Validation with Real Robot:**
To evaluate the impact of the identified motor parameters on a complete robotic system, the parameters were integrated into a MuJoCo simulation of the robot. A pre-trained RL policy was then tested across three conditions: on the real robot, in a simulation with the baseline (unoptimized) model, and in a simulation with the optimized model.
Observations revealed that the real robot exhibited a gradual drift over time. Crucially, the simulated robot with optimized motor parameters successfully replicated this gradual drift, indicating a close alignment with real-world behavior (Figure 5). In contrast, the baseline simulated model failed to capture this behavior and instead fell over. Quantifying this, the mean Euclidean distance between the robot's starting and final positions (position drift) was 0.16 meters for the optimized model, while the baseline model showed a drift of 1.13 meters. This represents an 86% improvement in the accuracy of the robot's displacement replication in simulation compared to the real robot (Table I).

**Applying the Optimized Model to RL Policy Learning:**
The study further investigated the benefits of the optimized model for reinforcement learning. A walking policy was trained using the optimized robot model and compared against a policy trained on the baseline (unadjusted) model. Both policies were subsequently deployed on the real robot.
The policy trained on the baseline model exhibited considerable rotational deviation during walking, resulting in limited forward travel. Specifically, the robot traveled only 1.12 meters in the commanded direction and incurred a rotational deviation of 109 degrees from its original orientation (Figure 6, Table I).
Conversely, the policy trained using the refined motor model enabled more stable and efficient locomotion. The robot traveled 1.64 meters in the commanded direction and maintained a rotational deviation of only 27 degrees. These results indicate a 46% improvement in travel distance and a 75% reduction in rotational deviation when using the policy trained with the optimized model (Table I). This demonstrates that the adjusted simulation model leads to policies that perform more effectively and stably in real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotics, particularly in enhancing the fidelity of simulation for advanced control strategies like reinforcement learning and model-based control.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to **bridge the sim-to-real gap** without relying on traditional, often problematic, direct torque measurements. By employing a novel system identification framework that uses only observable trajectory data (positions, velocities) and control inputs, the method overcomes the limitations associated with expensive, noisy, and often unavailable torque sensors. This makes the approach more practical and accessible for a wider range of robotic systems and research environments.

The integration of system identification directly into the reinforcement learning training loop, leveraging the differentiability of simulators like MJX, represents a **methodological advancement**. This approach allows for gradient-based optimization of physical parameters, enabling the simulation model to continuously adapt and more closely mimic real-world dynamics. The demonstrated capability to optimize fundamental physical properties (e.g., armature, frictionloss, damping) and the stated potential for approximating complex nonlinear behaviors (e.g., advanced friction models, delays) through neural networks highlights the framework's **flexibility and scalability**. Such flexibility implies applicability across diverse robotic systems, regardless of their intrinsic complexity, as MJX supports all MuJoCo models.

The experimental results underscore the **tangible impact** of this framework. The significant reduction in mean squared error for motor trajectories (8 times smaller than baseline), the 86% improvement in simulated robot displacement accuracy relative to real-world behavior, and the substantial enhancements in real-world RL policy performance (46% increased travel distance, 75% reduced rotational deviation) collectively demonstrate that this method leads to **more precise and reliable robot locomotion**. This increased fidelity in simulation can accelerate RL policy development, reduce the need for extensive real-world experimentation, and ultimately lead to more robust and capable robots.

Looking ahead, the potential impact extends to several areas:

- **Expanded System Identification:** The framework can be extended to identify other system parameters beyond individual motor dynamics, making it a versatile tool for comprehensive system parameter tuning.
- **High Gear Ratio Motors:** Its applicability to motors with high gear ratios, where traditional methods often struggle, further expands its utility in advanced robotic platforms.
- **Enhanced Domain Randomization:** The identified parameters can provide a more constrained and informed range for domain randomization. This could lead to learned policies that are less conservative than those derived from standard, broad domain randomization, striking a better balance between robustness and optimal performance.
- **Modeling Complex Behaviors:** Future work could involve incorporating historical motor data to capture unobservable system states, such as temperature-dependent effects and command delays, and to denoise input signals, further improving overall system accuracy.

In conclusion, this research provides a robust and practical solution for addressing a fundamental challenge in robotics, paving the way for more accurate simulations and, consequently, more capable and reliable autonomous robotic systems.
