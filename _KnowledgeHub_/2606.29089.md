# TAP-VLA: Tactile Annotation Prompting for Vision Language Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Michigan developed TAP-VLA, a framework that visually annotates tactile shear fields onto camera inputs, enabling pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to perform contact-rich manipulation tasks. This approach achieved an overall 78% success rate across four tasks, outperforming vision-only and alternative tactile-fusion baselines, which scored below 50%.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are structurally blind to contact forces, which are critical for many manipulation tasks.
- Integrating tactile sensing into pre-trained VLAs often introduces challenges like distribution shift or requires training new models from scratch, sacrificing pre-training benefits.
- Large-scale, high-quality tactile datasets necessary for direct tactile VLA pre-training are currently scarce and heterogeneous.

## Method

- Developed **TAP-VLA** (Tactile Annotation Prompting for Vision-Language-Action) which renders ==tactile shear fields== as ==visual annotations== directly onto the policy's multi-view RGB camera inputs.
- Utilized ==GelSight visuo-tactile sensors== to compute shear fields via ==optical flow== (Farneback algorithm), with a ==base-image reset== strategy to isolate external contact forces.
- Downsampled 2D shear fields were lifted to 3D and projected onto camera views as colored lines, indicating shear magnitude, thus preserving the VLA's original ==visual input signature== during fine-tuning of the ==π0.5== backbone.

## Results

- TAP-VLA achieved the highest overall success rate of **78%** across four contact-rich manipulation tasks, significantly outperforming all vision-only and tactile-fusion baselines (all below **50%**).
- Demonstrated superior performance in tasks requiring reasoning about object mass (**80%** success rate vs. **33-37%** for baselines) and center of mass (**83%** success rate vs. **20-43%** for baselines).
- Enabled robust error-correcting behaviors in insertion tasks, achieving **77%** for gear insertion and **73%** for plug insertion, exceeding the performance of baselines in both cases.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Presenting tactile information as visual annotations allows pre-trained VLAs to effectively leverage contact feedback without architectural changes or inducing a significant distribution shift.
> - The method of integrating a new modality, specifically by operating within the model's native observation space, is crucial for preserving and enhancing pre-trained VLA capabilities.
> - This visual annotation technique offers a practical, computationally lightweight, and generalizable approach for endowing VLAs with various physical signals that are typically difficult to observe visually.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a notable direction for developing general-purpose manipulation policies. These models leverage large-scale imitation learning over extensive robot demonstration datasets and build upon pre-trained vision-language backbones, enabling generalization across diverse tasks and facilitating open-world behaviors through semantic reasoning. Existing VLA models have primarily demonstrated their generalization capabilities along visual, semantic, and spatial dimensions.

However, many manipulation tasks, particularly those involving contact, depend on physical quantities that are not readily observable through visual feedback alone. Phenomena such as tool-environment forces, object mass, and friction, which are critical for tasks like in-hand reorientation, often do not manifest clearly in camera images. Consequently, vision-only VLAs are inherently limited in their ability to perform contact-rich manipulation effectively.

Tactile sensing offers a direct measurement of these contact forces and has been successfully integrated into visuomotor policies, both in isolation and in conjunction with visual information. Nevertheless, extending tactile integration to VLAs presents challenges. The large-scale corpora used for VLA pre-training typically lack tactile data. Introducing tactile data as a new input modality during fine-tuning can induce a distribution shift, potentially diminishing the benefits derived from the initial pre-training that makes VLAs effective.

Prior research on integrating tactile sensing into VLAs has explored several avenues. Some approaches involve training Tactile-Language-Action (TLA) or Vision-Tactile-Language-Action (VTLA) models from scratch using new backbones. This strategy, however, forfeits the advantages of large-scale policy pre-training and necessitates extensive tactile datasets, which are scarce and heterogeneous. Other methods start with pre-trained VLAs and augment them with dedicated encoders or input branches for various force and tactile signals (e.g., force/torque, joint torque, taxel, or vision-based tactile inputs). These augmented models are then fine-tuned on newly collected demonstrations, sometimes incorporating auxiliary objectives for improved cross-modal reasoning. A common limitation of these architectural modification approaches is the distribution shift they introduce, which may hinder effective policy learning by moving away from the pre-training distribution.

Concurrently, the broader field of VLA and VLM research has seen advancements in visual augmentation. Techniques such as pixel-space annotations (e.g., drawing circles or alphanumeric labels) have been shown to enhance the reasoning and grounding capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) without modifying model weights. Similar ideas have been applied in robotics to elicit spatially-grounded actions, involving overlays of action proposals, 2D keypoint affordances, end-effector trajectories, or target joint positions. While these visual augmentations convey geometric, kinematic, or motion cues, they generally do not transmit contact-level force information.

This paper positions TAP-VLA to address the identified gap by proposing a method to endow pre-trained VLAs with tactile feedback without architectural changes or significant distribution shift. By rendering tactile shear fields as visual annotations directly onto the policy's existing camera inputs, TAP-VLA seeks to provide explicit force information in a format that VLAs are already designed to process, thereby avoiding the limitations of prior tactile integration strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central problem addressed by this research is the structural blindness of existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to contact forces, which are critical for a wide range of manipulation tasks. While VLAs exhibit strong generalization capabilities in visual, semantic, and spatial domains, they generally lack direct perception of the loads and deformations occurring during physical contact, as these forces seldom manifest clearly in visual feedback.

The primary motivation for this work stems from the observation that integrating tactile sensing into VLAs, though crucial for contact-rich manipulation, typically introduces challenges. Conventional methods involve either training entirely new tactile-aware models from scratch, which sacrifices the benefits of large-scale pre-training that make VLAs effective, or modifying the architecture of pre-trained VLAs by adding dedicated tactile input streams. Such architectural modifications, particularly during fine-tuning, can induce a distribution shift away from the original pre-training data, potentially eroding the model's performance and generalization.

Given these challenges, the overarching question driving this research is: **Can a pre-trained VLA be endowed with tactile feedback without requiring architectural changes or incurring a significant distribution shift?**

To address this question, the key objectives of the TAP-VLA framework are:
- **To provide tactile feedback to VLAs through visual augmentation:** This approach aims to leverage the VLA's native observation space by rendering tactile information directly into the multi-view RGB images. This avoids the need for new input modalities or architectural modifications.
- **To create a clear and interpretable tactile cue:** The tactile information, specifically shear fields extracted from visuo-tactile sensors, is designed to be presented as spatially-grounded vectors, making it readily understandable by the VLA.
- **To avoid the need for tactile pre-training:** By integrating tactile data as a visual annotation, the method bypasses the requirement for large-scale, sensor-specific tactile pre-training, which is currently unfeasible due to data scarcity and heterogeneity.
- **To add negligible computational overhead:** The visual annotation process is designed to be lightweight, preserving computational efficiency during policy execution.
- **To maintain proximity to the pre-training distribution:** By operating within the VLA's existing visual input signature, TAP-VLA aims to minimize the distribution shift that typically arises from introducing new input types.
- **To demonstrate improved performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks:** The method seeks to enable VLAs to reliably perform tasks requiring reasoning about object mass, center of mass, and contact alignment, where vision-only or alternative tactile-fusion baselines typically struggle.

By achieving these objectives, TAP-VLA aims to offer a practical and effective solution for enhancing the physical interaction capabilities of pre-trained VLA models without undermining their core strengths.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Tactile Annotation Prompting for Vision-Language-Action models (TAP-VLA), integrates tactile feedback into a pre-trained VLA by rendering it as visual annotations onto the policy's existing camera inputs. This approach maintains the input signature of the base VLA, aiming to minimize distribution shift during fine-tuning.

**Policy Formulation:**
A standard VLA policy is described as mapping multi-view RGB images ($I^K_t$), proprioception ($\theta_t$), and a language instruction ($l$) to an action chunk ($a_{t:t+H}$). TAP-VLA modifies this by introducing an annotation function $f$. This function overlays tactile shear onto each camera image, producing annotated images ($\hat{I}^K_t$) of the same dimensions as the original images. The fine-tuned policy then operates on these annotated images, preserving its original input/output mapping. The method assumes that the cameras are calibrated, providing known projection matrices ($P_i$).

**Visual Annotation Procedure:**
The process of generating annotated images from raw tactile data involves three stages:
1. **Tactile Sensor and Data Acquisition:** The system utilizes GelSight visuo-tactile sensors attached to a parallel-jaw gripper. Each finger sensor outputs an RGB image ($I^L_t, I^R_t$) of a marker-printed gel membrane that deforms under contact.
2. **Tactile Shear Field Computation:**
 - For each finger and at each timestep, a shear field ($S_t$) is computed using the Farneback algorithm. This algorithm measures the optical flow of the gel markers from a reference base image ($I_r$) to the current tactile image ($I_t$). The shear field represents the per-pixel deformation caused by forces on the sensor.
 - **Base-image Reset:** To isolate external contact forces (e.g., object mass, center of mass, contact misalignment) from internal grasp forces, the base image ($I_r$) for each finger is reset whenever its grasp state transitions (from grasped to released or vice versa). Subsequent shear measurements are then relative to this freshly-grasped state.
3. **Visual Augmentation with Shear Fields:**
 - **Downsample and Lift to 3D:** The computed shear field is first mean-pooled to a lower-resolution grid ($\tilde{S}_t$). A regular grid of 3D points ($G^s_t$, representing start points) is conceptually placed on the sensor surface within the fingertip frame. Each pooled 2D shear vector from $\tilde{S}_t$ is then used to define a corresponding 3D end point ($G^e_t$) by displacing its respective start point in the plane of the sensor. The 2D shear vector is lifted to 3D by appending a zero in the sensor-normal direction, and a scaling factor $\alpha$ is applied to the displacement magnitude.
 - **Project and Draw:** For each camera view, the 3D grid points ($G^s_t$ and $G^e_t$) are transformed from the fingertip frame to the world frame using the fingertip pose ($T_t$), which is recovered from the robot's proprioception ($\theta_t$). These transformed points are then projected to 2D pixel coordinates using the camera's projection matrix ($P_i$). Finally, a colored line is drawn in the image space from each projected start point to its corresponding end point. The color of the line conveys the magnitude of the shear, providing an interpretable visual cue. This process is repeated for both the left and right fingers across all camera views, resulting in the fully annotated images ($\hat{I}^K_t$).

**Experimental Setup and Implementation:**
- **Robot System:** A 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robot equipped with GelSight sensors on its gripper.
- **Cameras:** Three RealSense cameras are used: two D435 cameras for over-the-shoulder views and one wrist-mounted D405 camera for a local view. All external cameras are calibrated to the robot, and the wrist camera pose is tracked via robot proprioception.
- **VLA Model:** The $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA model is used as the base architecture.
- **Inputs to VLA:** Shear-annotated multi-view images, 7-dimensional robot joint state, and a language task label.
- **Actions:** 8-dimensional delta joint angles and a binary gripper open/close command, with an action horizon of H=10.
- **Training:** The model is fine-tuned using the AdamW optimizer for 20,000 steps with a batch size of 32.
- **Shear Annotation Scaling:** To ensure interpretability and reliability, the scaling factor $\alpha$ for shear vectors is determined per-task based on the 95th percentile of shear magnitudes observed during training. Shear vectors exceeding twice this 95th percentile are clipped to prevent anomalous visuals.
- **Demonstration Data:** 100 teleoperated demonstrations per task were collected using an Oculus Quest 2 VR device, recorded at approximately 10 Hz, with variations in spatial arrangements and lighting.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
TAP-VLA is evaluated against three baselines, all fine-tuned under the same conditions (optimizer, learning rate, dataset, steps):
1. **$\pi_{0.5}$ (Vision-Only):** The base VLA model fine-tuned solely on visual feedback.
2. **$\pi_{0.5}$ + Tactile:** The raw GelSight tactile images are included as additional auxiliary visual inputs, treated as extra "viewpoints" alongside the standard camera feeds.
3. **$\pi_{0.5}$ + Encoder:** Following existing multi-modal VLA approaches, raw GelSight images are processed by a small, dedicated convolutional encoder (four-layer stride-2 CNN with specific channel widths, kernel sizes, activations, and LayerNorm). The resulting feature tokens are then concatenated into the $\pi_{0.5}$ model's prefix. The encoder weights are shared between the left and right tactile sensors and trained jointly with the VLA.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TAP-VLA and the baseline methods was conducted across four distinct contact-rich manipulation tasks: "medicine" (object mass reasoning), "balance" (center of mass reasoning), "gear" (insertion alignment), and "plug" (insertion alignment). Each method was tested over 30 trials per task.

**Overall Performance:**
TAP-VLA demonstrated the highest overall success rate, achieving 94 out of 120 successful trials (approximately 78%). In contrast, all alternative tactile-fusion baselines and the vision-only baseline consistently performed at success rates below 50% across the tasks.

**Task-Specific Performance (Success Rate out of 30 trials):**
- **medicine:** This task required distinguishing between a full and empty bottle based on mass.
 - TAP-VLA: 24 successes (80%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ (Vision-Only): 11 successes (37%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Tactile: 11 successes (37%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Encoder: 10 successes (33%)
 - On this task, all baselines performed at or below chance (50% for binary choice). TAP-VLA significantly outperformed them, indicating its ability to interpret mass cues from tactile annotations.
- **balance:** This task involved balancing an object with an adjustable center of mass.
 - TAP-VLA: 25 successes (83%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ (Vision-Only): 12 successes (40%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Tactile: 13 successes (43%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Encoder: 6 successes (20%)
 - Similar to the medicine task, baselines performed at or below chance (1/3 for three center-of-mass locations). TAP-VLA showed a marked advantage in reasoning about the object's center of mass for stable placement.
- **gear:** This task required aligning and inserting a gear onto a peg.
 - TAP-VLA: 23 successes (77%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ (Vision-Only): 19 successes (63%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Tactile: 17 successes (57%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Encoder: 14 successes (47%)
 - TAP-VLA achieved the highest success rate, exhibiting robust error-correcting behavior during insertion attempts. The tactile-fusion baselines also showed some corrective actions but were less successful, while the vision-only $\pi_{0.5}$ frequently resulted in wedging or jamming.
- **plug:** This task involved inserting a cordless plug end into a socket.
 - TAP-VLA: 22 successes (73%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ (Vision-Only): 18 successes (60%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Tactile: 19 successes (63%)
 - $\pi_{0.5}$ + Encoder: 18 successes (60%)
 - TAP-VLA maintained a lead in success rate, demonstrating effective alignment correction informed by tactile feedback.

**Key Observations from the Results:**
- TAP-VLA was the only method that consistently performed reliably across all four contact-rich tasks.
- The three baselines, despite employing different tactile integration strategies, yielded comparable overall results to each other but were significantly inferior to TAP-VLA. This indicates that the mere availability of tactile data is not sufficient; the method of integrating it into the policy plays a crucial role.
- For tasks requiring explicit reasoning about physical properties not directly visible (medicine and balance), baselines struggled to perform better than chance. TAP-VLA, however, largely succeeded by providing an interpretable visual cue for these properties.
- In insertion tasks (gear and plug), TAP-VLA's tactile annotations enabled effective real-time corrective actions, preventing failures like wedging. This suggests that while coarse alignment might be achieved visually, fine-grained, contact-aware interaction benefits substantially from tactile input, especially when presented in an accessible format.
- Qualitative analysis (Figure 4) corroborated these findings, illustrating how the shear annotations visually differentiate object states (full/empty bottle), reveal implied centers of mass, and guide continuous corrective alignment during interactions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces TAP-VLA, a framework that addresses a critical limitation of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in contact-rich manipulation tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be understood in several dimensions:

**Enhanced Capabilities for Pre-trained VLAs:**
The primary impact is enabling pre-trained VLAs to effectively utilize tactile feedback without requiring architectural modifications or inducing a significant distribution shift. This is a notable achievement, as it allows existing generalist VLA policies, which benefit from extensive vision and language pre-training, to extend their capabilities to physical interaction tasks that necessitate contact force reasoning. By preserving the original VLA architecture and input signature, TAP-VLA offers a method to harness the power of large-scale pre-training for contact-rich scenarios.

**Practical and Efficient Tactile Integration:**
TAP-VLA provides a lightweight and computationally efficient method for incorporating tactile sensing. The visual annotation process adds negligible compute overhead compared to architectural modifications involving dedicated encoders, which might require more complex network structures and potentially longer training times. This practicality makes the approach attractive for real-world robotic deployments where computational resources and deployment simplicity are considerations.

**Improved Performance in Contact-Rich Tasks:**
The experimental results demonstrate that TAP-VLA significantly outperforms vision-only and alternative tactile-fusion baselines across a range of tasks demanding reasoning about object mass, center of mass, and contact alignment. This performance improvement highlights the framework's ability to provide VLAs with critical physical information that is otherwise unavailable or difficult to infer from visual data alone. The success rates on tasks where baselines performed at chance levels particularly underscore its utility for tasks requiring nuanced physical interaction.

**Insight into Multi-modal Integration for VLAs:**
The findings suggest that for pre-trained VLAs, the *method of presenting a new modality* (i.e., integrating it within the model's native observation space) can be as crucial as, if not more important than, the raw signal itself. The fact that direct tactile image input or encoded tactile features performed poorly compared to visually annotated tactile information offers a valuable insight for future research in multi-modal VLA development, potentially guiding the design of more effective multi-modal integration strategies that respect pre-training distributions.

**Generalizability and Future Directions:**
The authors propose that the core "tactile annotation prompting" recipe might not be limited to GelSight sensors or shear fields. In principle, any physical signal that can be meaningfully represented and rendered as a 2D visual overlay—such as other tactile representations, force/torque time-series data, audio spectrograms, or depth information—could potentially be integrated into pre-trained VLAs using a similar framework. This opens avenues for extending the VLA's perception to a broader spectrum of physical phenomena without extensive architectural redesigns, making this work a foundational step towards more versatile generalist robot policies.

**Complementary Role to Long-term Research:**
TAP-VLA is positioned as complementary to longer-term research efforts aimed at large-scale tactile pre-training and the development of native tactile reasoning architectures. While such efforts mature, TAP-VLA offers an immediate and effective means to leverage existing pre-trained VLAs for contact-rich manipulation tasks, bridging a current gap in capabilities.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The identified limitations, such as the potential loss of fine-grained tactile information due to shear field computation and downsampling, or visual occlusion by annotations in cluttered scenes, also indicate directions for future research. These could involve exploring richer tactile representations suitable for visual overlay, adaptive annotation strategies (e.g., dynamic placement or transparency), or providing unannotated views in parallel. Addressing the challenge of denser overlays for multi-fingered hands is another area for development.

In conclusion, TAP-VLA represents a significant advance in endowing pre-trained VLA models with effective tactile perception. By demonstrating a pragmatic and high-performing method for tactile integration, this work contributes to the development of more capable and general-purpose robotic manipulation systems, particularly for tasks requiring intricate physical interaction.
