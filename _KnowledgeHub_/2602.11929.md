# General Humanoid Whole-Body Control via Pretraining and Fast Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> The FAST framework provides a general humanoid whole-body control solution, integrating large-scale motion pretraining with a stable and efficient residual policy adaptation method. It achieves robust tracking of diverse, low-quality, and out-of-distribution motion references, successfully demonstrating real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Existing learning-based humanoid controllers often struggle with performance degradation when encountering noisy, inconsistent, or physically implausible real-world motion references due to distribution shift.
- Rapidly specializing controllers to novel motion distributions through lightweight adaptation without extensive retraining or catastrophic forgetting of previously learned skills remains a challenge.
- Maintaining robust balance and stability in high-dynamic scenarios, particularly when tracking aggressive or inconsistent reference motions, is a persistent limitation for humanoid robots.

## Method

- A general motion tracking policy is pretrained using ==Reinforcement Learning== with a Mixture-of-Experts architecture and a comprehensive, augmented motion dataset.
- Center-of-Mass-Aware Control is introduced, explicitly incorporating CoM/CoP signals and adaptively down-weighting tracking rewards to prioritize balance during physically challenging motions.
- Parseval-Guided Residual Policy Adaptation is employed, training a lightweight residual policy with Parseval regularization and KL-divergence constraints to enable stable, fast specialization to new motion distributions while mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

- FAST's base model consistently achieved the highest success rates on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution datasets, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines in long-horizon robustness and generalization.
- The residual adaptation mechanism enabled rapid convergence and superior performance on new motion distributions while significantly mitigating catastrophic forgetting on the original training data.
- Successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, the framework demonstrated zero-shot high-dynamic motion tracking, robust real-time teleoperation, and stable adaptation to low-quality motions derived from text or monocular video.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically trading off strict reference tracking for physical stability via CoM-Aware Control significantly enhances a humanoid's ability to handle aggressive or inconsistent motion inputs.
> - A frozen, pretrained base policy combined with a small, regularized residual policy allows for computationally efficient and stable adaptation to novel motion distributions.
> - Parseval regularization applied to the residual policy's layers and KL-divergence constraints against the base policy are critical for ensuring stable adaptation, promoting exploration, and preventing catastrophic forgetting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is rapidly advancing, with a critical goal being the development of general whole-body control (WBC) systems. This work, "General Humanoid Whole-Body Control via Pretraining and Fast Adaptation" (FAST), positions itself at the forefront of addressing key limitations in current learning-based humanoid control.

Historically, humanoid control has often relied on task-specific approaches, with controllers meticulously engineered for isolated skills like locomotion, agile movements, or recovery behaviors. While effective for their specific tasks, these methods lack the generality required for robots to operate flexibly in diverse, unstructured real-world environments. The paradigm of **whole-body motion tracking**, which leverages retargeted human motion as reference trajectories, has emerged as a promising direction to achieve more generalizable and coordinated humanoid control, avoiding arduous manual reward engineering.

Recent progress in learning-based whole-body control, often employing deep reinforcement learning (RL), has led to controllers capable of executing a wide range of motions. Many of these rely on large, curated motion datasets such as AMASS, OMOMO, and LaFan1. However, FAST identifies several critical gaps:
1. **Distribution Shift and Motion Quality:** Real-world motion references (e.g., from teleoperation, video estimation, text-to-motion) are inherently heterogeneous, noisy, temporally inconsistent, and physically implausible compared to pristine training datasets. Existing controllers often struggle with this **distribution shift**, leading to performance degradation or outright failure, especially for highly dynamic or stylistically complex motions.
2. **Fast Adaptation:** While large-scale pretraining (e.g., SONIC) can improve generalization, it doesn't intrinsically solve the problem of rapidly specializing to *novel* or *out-of-distribution* motions without extensive retraining. Existing adaptation methods, like **residual reinforcement learning** (ResMimic, ASAP) or **transfer learning from pre-trained priors** (SPiRL, Octo), primarily focus on sim-to-real transfer or refining skills within similar distributions. They often don't effectively address rapid adaptation to truly novel motion distributions or mitigate **catastrophic forgetting** of previously learned skills.
3. **Robust Balance in High-Dynamic Scenarios:** Humanoids need exceptional balance and stability, particularly when tracking aggressive or inconsistent reference motions. Prior methods often rely on filtering out such motions during training, which is impractical in real-world deployment.
4. **Computational Constraints:** Scaling up model capacity for broader generalization leads to larger models, which are challenging to deploy on robots with limited onboard compute and power budgets, potentially causing latency and stability issues.

FAST distinguishes itself by specifically tackling the intertwined challenges of **robustness to distribution shift**, **fast adaptation**, and **physical stability** in a computationally efficient manner. Unlike approaches that primarily scale up model size or retrain task-specific policies, FAST proposes a framework that combines a robust pretrained controller with a lightweight, stable, and efficient adaptation mechanism. This positions it as a significant step towards practical, deployable, and truly general humanoid whole-body control, addressing the crucial need for robots to safely and effectively interact with the unpredictability of real-world inputs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a **general humanoid whole-body control framework (FAST) that enables fast adaptation and stable motion tracking**, particularly when encountering diverse, low-quality, or out-of-distribution motion references. The core motivations stem from the limitations of existing learning-based humanoid control systems:

1. **Achieve Truly General Motion Tracking:** Current methods, despite progress, often fail to robustly track motions that deviate significantly from their training distributions. The goal is to extend the boundary of controllable motions beyond curated datasets to include noisy, inconsistent, and physically challenging real-world inputs from various sources (e.g., teleoperation, text, video).
2. **Enable Fast and Efficient Adaptation:** Large-scale pretraining alone is insufficient for practical deployment due to computational constraints and the need for immediate specialization to novel tasks or environments. A key objective is to allow the controller to rapidly specialize to new motion distributions through lightweight adaptation, rather than requiring exhaustive retraining or large-scale models that are difficult to deploy in real-time.
3. **Enhance Robustness and Balance in High-Dynamic Scenarios:** Humanoid robots operating in the real world will inevitably encounter aggressive or physically inconsistent motions. The controller must be able to maintain robust balance and stability in such high-dynamic scenarios, even if it means conservatively deviating from a physically implausible reference motion.
4. **Mitigate Catastrophic Forgetting:** During adaptation to new motions, it is crucial to preserve the general motion tracking capabilities learned during pretraining. Unconstrained adaptation can lead to "catastrophic forgetting," where the policy loses its ability to perform previously mastered behaviors.

In essence, FAST aims to provide a robust, adaptable, and computationally efficient solution for humanoid control that can bridge the gap between idealized simulation training and the unpredictable demands of real-world operation, forming a foundational layer for more intelligent and versatile humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FAST is a unified framework designed for general humanoid whole-body control, integrating large-scale motion pretraining with stable and efficient policy adaptation. The methodology is structured into three main stages:

#### 3.1. Motion Dataset Curation

The process begins with creating a comprehensive and robust dataset for training.
- **Human-to-Humanoid Retargeting:** Publicly available human motion capture datasets (AMASS, OMOMO, LaFan1, MotionX) and an in-house MoCap dataset are retargeted to the humanoid robot's kinematics. This broad data ensures diversity.
- **Data Augmentation:** To enhance robustness to distribution shifts, retargeted motions are augmented by adjusting global translational velocity and perturbing lower-limb joint configurations, generating variations in speed and kinematic styles while maintaining physical coherence.
- **Auxiliary Physical Signals:** Each motion is augmented with contact masks (from foot-ground interactions) and computed Center of Mass (CoM) and Center of Pressure (CoP) signals. These provide explicit balance and support cues, crucial for physically plausible tracking.

#### 3.2. General Whole-Body Controller (Pretraining)

A general motion tracking policy is trained using Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Observations:** The policy receives proprioceptive information (velocities, joint positions, keypoint positions/rotations, root state) and command information from the reference motion (target joint angles, keypoint positions/velocities, CoM/CoP). A separate critic network receives additional "privileged" observations (e.g., full contact masks).
- **Actions:** The policy outputs target joint position offsets, which are then fed to low-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controllers to control the robot's 29 Degrees of Freedom (DoF).
- **Policy Architecture:** A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture is adopted, consisting of multiple three-layer MLPs as expert networks and a gating network. This allows different experts to specialize in distinct motion dynamics, reducing interference when learning diverse behaviors.
- **Center-of-Mass-Aware Control:** This is a key innovation for physical robustness.
 - **CoM-related Observations and Objectives:** Reference CoM and CoP signals are explicitly incorporated into observations and rewards.
 - **Adaptive Tracking Weight:** When the reference CoM-CoP distance exceeds a threshold (indicating aggressive or unstable motion), tracking-related reward coefficients are progressively down-weighted. This allows the policy to "relax" strict tracking and prioritize balance, ensuring safer execution.
 - **Explicit Stability Reward:** An additional reward term encourages minimizing the distance between the robot's actual CoM and CoP, directly promoting balance maintenance. This dynamic trade-off is critical for handling challenging or physically inconsistent motion references.

#### 3.3. Parseval-Guided Residual Policy Adaptation

To enable fast and stable specialization to new motion distributions without sacrificing pretrained capabilities, FAST employs a novel residual adaptation scheme.
- **Delta Policy Parameterization:** A lightweight, three-layer MLP residual policy (π_r) is trained to predict *additive action corrections* (a_r) on top of a *fully frozen* pretrained base policy (π_b). The final action is a_t = a_b + a_r. Only π_r and the critic (initialized from the pretrained model) are optimized, significantly reducing the parameter space and computational cost for adaptation.
- **Parseval Regularization for Stable Adaptation:** To address instability and suboptimal convergence during adaptation, Parseval regularization is applied to the linear layers (except the output) of the residual policy: L_Parseval(W) = ||W^T W - sI||_F^2. This encourages approximate orthonormality of weight columns, which implicitly constrains the layer's spectral norm. This promotes smoother gradient propagation, better-conditioned feature representations, and aids exploration beyond the base policy's behavior, facilitating escape from local optima.
- **KL-Constrained Policy Update:** To mitigate catastrophic forgetting and preserve general motion tracking capabilities, a KL-divergence constraint is imposed: L_KL = E[D_KL(π(·|s_t) || π_b(·|s_t))]. This regularizes the *combined* policy (π) to remain close to the *base policy* (π_b) in terms of action distributions. This is crucial as the residual policy adapts solely on new motion data, lacking supervision from the original domain.
- **Total Objective:** The residual policy is trained with a combined objective: L_total = L_RL + λ_p L_Parseval + λ_k L_KL.
- **Stability Analysis:** The paper provides theoretical propositions demonstrating that Parseval regularization bounds the functional sensitivity (Lipschitz continuity) of the residual policy, while the KL constraint limits its deviation from the base policy. These complementary constraints ensure smooth and stable adaptation even with limited new data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of FAST rigorously addresses four key research questions, demonstrating its superior performance in simulation and real-world deployment.

- **Q1: Comparison with State-of-the-Art Humanoid Whole-Body Controllers:**
 - FAST's base model consistently achieved the highest success rate on both the in-distribution training dataset (AMASS) and an out-of-distribution (OOD) test dataset (MotionX, a leg-dominant subset). This highlights its superior long-horizon robustness and generalization capabilities compared to SOTA baselines like GMT and TWIST2.
 - While not always optimal in local joint-angle errors (E_mpjpe), FAST significantly improved the tracking accuracy of global keypoints (E_mpkpe). This indicates a beneficial trade-off, where FAST prioritizes globally consistent and physically stable motions over strict local joint-level accuracy, which is often more critical for overall robot behavior.
 - The paper notes that lower error metrics reported by baselines on OOD datasets were often misleading, as they were computed over much shorter successful segments due to frequent early terminations, underscoring FAST's advantage in sustaining long-duration tasks.

- **Q2: Efficiency and Performance Preservation of Fast Adaptation:**
 - On target adaptation datasets (LaFan1 and MotionX), FAST achieved the best overall performance, demonstrating rapid convergence and higher success rates compared to training from scratch, full fine-tuning, or unregularized residual models. This confirms its effectiveness for fast adaptation to new motion distributions.
 - Crucially, FAST significantly alleviated performance degradation on the source dataset (AMASS), effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting. It achieved the lowest E_mpjpe and E_mpkpe among adapted models on AMASS while maintaining competitive success rates, striking a favorable balance between specializing to new data and preserving original capabilities.

- **Q3: Impact of CoM-Aware Control on Balance and Stability:**
 - Ablation studies showed that incorporating explicit CoM-Aware Control consistently improved stability and task completion for high-dynamic motions, both in-distribution and OOD.
 - The full model achieved lower mean CoM-CoP positional deviation (E_mpd) and reduced slippage (Slip), indicating enhanced balance maintenance and more stable contact during aggressive movements.
 - Qualitative examples visually demonstrated that CoM-Aware Control suppressed excessive root drift and prevented balance loss in challenging scenarios like forward bending, high knee lifts, and side-to-side swaying gaits.

- **Q4: Real-world Transfer and Diverse Motion Sources:**
 - FAST was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, showcasing its practical applicability.
 - **Zero-shot high-dynamic motion tracking:** The pretrained general policy successfully executed complex motions like single-leg squats and side kicks without adaptation, demonstrating strong real-world robustness and balance.
 - **Real-time teleoperation:** The robot remained responsive and stable during live teleoperation, performing diverse behaviors including walking, jumping, martial arts, and object manipulation, validating the pretrained policy's foundation for teleoperation.
 - **Adaptation to low-quality motions:** For motions derived from text-to-motion generation or monocular video estimation (which are noisy and inconsistent), FAST's residual adaptation mechanism enabled rapid and stable specialization. After adaptation, the robot robustly tracked these synthesized motions, including single-leg jumping, 360-degree hopping, and complex dance sequences.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FAST framework represents a significant advancement in general humanoid whole-body control, with substantial potential impact across several domains:

1. **Enabling General Humanoid Intelligence:** By providing a robust, adaptable, and stable low-level motor foundation, FAST moves humanoid robots closer to truly general intelligence. It allows them to interpret and execute a wider array of behaviors from diverse, often imperfect, human commands or learned high-level plans. This is a critical prerequisite for embodied AI systems that need to operate autonomously and adaptively in complex environments.

2. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap and Enhancing Real-World Deployment:** The demonstrated success on a physical Unitree G1 robot, handling inputs from teleoperation, video, and text generation, directly addresses the persistent sim-to-real gap. FAST's ability to maintain robustness and balance with noisy, real-world data is crucial for deploying humanoids in practical applications, from hazardous operations and large-scale data collection to assistive robotics. The framework makes advanced control capabilities more accessible and reliable outside of highly controlled laboratory settings.

3. **Robustness to Unpredictability and Distribution Shifts:** Real-world environments are inherently unpredictable, and motion references will rarely be perfectly curated. FAST's emphasis on handling out-of-distribution and low-quality motion inputs through both robust pretraining and fast adaptation is a game-changer. It means robots can adapt to novel situations and continue functioning even when faced with unexpected or imperfect commands, greatly increasing their utility and reliability.

4. **Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** The lightweight residual adaptation scheme is a key innovation for practical deployment. By freezing the large base policy and only adapting a smaller residual network, FAST bypasses the computational and latency issues associated with fine-tuning massive models on resource-constrained onboard robot hardware. This efficient adaptation strategy allows for rapid specialization without sacrificing the benefits of large-scale pretraining.

5. **Methodological Contributions to Reinforcement Learning:** The introduction of Parseval-Guided Residual Policy Adaptation and Center-of-Mass-Aware Control offers novel methodological contributions. Parseval regularization provides a principled way to stabilize adaptation and encourage diverse learning, while KL-constrained updates effectively mitigate catastrophic forgetting. CoM-Aware Control offers a concrete strategy for integrating physical stability into learning-based control, a valuable lesson for designers of dynamic robotic systems. These techniques could inspire further research in adaptive control, transfer learning, and robust policy design for complex robotic systems.

In conclusion, FAST not only pushes the boundaries of what is achievable in general humanoid whole-body control but also provides a practical and principled framework for integrating robustness, adaptability, and efficiency. Its successful real-world deployment solidifies its potential to accelerate the development and broader adoption of humanoid robots.
