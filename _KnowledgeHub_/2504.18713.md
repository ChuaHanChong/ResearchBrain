# Certifiably-Correct Mapping for Safe Navigation Despite Odometry Drift

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Michigan developed a framework for certifiably-correct mapping that guarantees regions claimed as obstacle-free genuinely are, even with odometry drift. Their approach uses a novel "deflation" strategy, shrinking perceived free space based on quantifiable uncertainty, which demonstrated near-zero violation rates in simulations (e.g., 0.0002-0.0047% for Certified SFC) and successfully prevented collisions in real-world rover experiments.

## Problem

- Robotic planners and controllers critically rely on accurate environmental maps, but real-world odometry drift, inherent in systems like VIO/SLAM, causes significant map inaccuracies.
- These map errors can lead to misclassification of obstacle-free regions, creating a direct threat of collisions for autonomous robots navigating in complex environments.
- Existing safety methods often assume perfect perception or lack formal guarantees for the correctness of the generated maps, especially concerning locally perceived free space.

## Method

- Introduces a novel **"deflation" strategy** that shrinks declared safe regions based on the estimated incremental odometry error and its associated covariance at each timestep.
- Develops a mathematical framework to maintain map correctness in the robot's ==body-fixed frame==, ensuring that any region claimed to be obstacle-free genuinely is, even with unbounded global odometry drift.
- Applies and formally proves the correctness of this deflation framework for two common obstacle map representations: ==Safe Flight Corridors (convex polytopes)== and ==Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (ESDFs)==.

## Results

- Simulations on the Replica dataset demonstrated that Certified Safe Flight Corridors (SFC) achieved near-zero violation rates (**0.0002-0.0047%**) and sub-millimeter maximum violation distances, outperforming baseline methods by orders of magnitude.
- Certified Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (ESDF) also significantly reduced violation rates (**0.01-2.4%**), providing strong correctness guarantees despite the inherent discretization of voxel maps.
- Real-world experiments with a robotic rover confirmed that baseline mapping led to collisions due to accumulated drift, while the certified ESDF successfully prevented collisions by dynamically deflating unsafe regions and triggering safety stops.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Map correctness can be formally guaranteed by conservatively shrinking the perceived free space based on quantifiable odometry uncertainty, rather than relying on global consistency methods or heuristics.
> - Maintaining map correctness in the robot's body-fixed frame is crucial, as pose estimation errors propagate into map errors that directly violate safety guarantees.
> - The proposed "deflation" approach provides computational and mathematical advantages over inflating obstacles, allowing for manageable map size and potential re-inclusion of regions upon re-observation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Safe robotic navigation fundamentally relies on accurate perception, including state estimation (localization) and environmental mapping. Historically, planning and control algorithms designed for safety often operate under the simplifying assumption of perfect or near-perfect perception. However, in real-world scenarios, sensor noise and estimation errors, particularly odometry drift in vision-based localization systems (VIO/SLAM), are inevitable. These errors can lead to discrepancies between the robot's perceived environment and the true environment, resulting in incorrect obstacle maps and potentially catastrophic collisions.

The broader research landscape has seen significant advancements in Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) and Visual Inertial Odometry (VIO) over the past decades, with modern systems achieving impressive accuracy (e.g., sub-1% translation error rates). These improvements have enabled robots to operate in complex environments, as showcased by challenges like DARPA SubT. Despite this, formal error analysis and guarantees for the perception outputs, especially for maps, have often lagged.

Current approaches to handle perception uncertainty for safety typically fall into a few categories:
- **Safe Control Design:** Methods like Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) ensure safety given a known environment or accurate real-time obstacle detection. However, they generally assume the *perception* itself is flawless.
- **Accurate State Estimation:** Research aims to improve the accuracy and robustness of VIO/SLAM systems. Some works focus on certifiable *localization* or *point cloud registration*, proving bounds on pose estimation errors. This paper is distinct as it translates these ideas to the *map* itself.
- **Mapping Consistency:** Approaches exist to manage map consistency, often by fusing overlapping information only when certain consistency criteria are met. These often rely on heuristics or "forgetting" mechanisms (e.g., time- or distance-based) to discard old, potentially inconsistent data.

This paper explicitly addresses a crucial gap: how to build *certifiably-correct maps* despite the fundamental problem of odometry drift. Unlike previous work that might focus on global consistency through loop closures (which are not always available) or bounding pose errors (which doesn't directly translate to map correctness for safety), this work proposes a novel approach to maintain local map correctness around the robot. It directly confronts the issue of how pose estimation errors propagate into map errors that violate safety guarantees. The key innovation lies in its strategy of *deflating* (shrinking) perceived free space based on quantifiable uncertainty, rather than heuristically discarding data or inflating obstacles, which provides formal guarantees of safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the critical reliance of autonomous robot planners and controllers on accurate environmental perception for making safety-critical decisions. Existing mapping solutions, while increasingly accurate, often make an unrealistic assumption: perfect pose estimates. This flawed assumption means that maps generated by state-of-the-art systems can misclassify obstacle-free regions, leading to potential collisions—a direct threat to safe navigation.

The overarching objective of the paper is to introduce a framework for "certifiably-correct mapping" that guarantees the accuracy of the obstacle map, specifically ensuring that any region claimed to be "obstacle-free" genuinely is, even when the robot's localization system experiences odometry drift.

More specifically, the paper's key objectives and contributions are:
- **Theoretical Framework Development:** To establish a mathematical framework for constructing and defensively deflating "free space" within obstacle maps. This framework explicitly accounts for odometry drift, assuming the odometry algorithm provides pose estimates and a covariance for incremental transformations. The core idea is to shrink the certifiably safe region `S_{k+1}` relative to `S_k` to maintain `S_k ⊂ F` (safe region is a subset of true free space).
- **Applicability to Popular Mapping Paradigms:** To formally prove the correctness and applicability of this deflation framework on two widely used and distinct obstacle mapping representations:
 - **Safe Flight Corridors (SFCs):** Representing free space as unions of convex polytopes.
 - **Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (ESDFs):** Representing the environment as a signed distance to the nearest obstacle.
- **Empirical Validation (Simulation):** To validate the proposed approaches through extensive simulations using the high-quality Replica dataset, comparing their efficacy against state-of-the-art baseline and heuristic methods based on metrics like violation rate, maximum violation distance, and free-space volume.
- **Real-World Demonstration:** To demonstrate the practical utility and safety benefits of the framework through real-world experiments on a robotic rover, showing that the proposed methods prevent collisions where baseline approaches fail due to accumulated odometry drift.

A crucial motivation for the deflation strategy, as highlighted by the authors, is its computational and mathematical advantages over inflating known obstacles. Inflating obstacles based on accumulated error would cause them to continuously grow, potentially occupying the entire domain. Deflating the safe region, conversely, shrinks the certifiably safe area, eventually classifying it as "unknown" and allowing it to be "forgotten" from memory until re-observed. This approach maintains a manageable map size and allows for re-inclusion of regions once certainty is re-established (e.g., via loop closures).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this work revolves around a novel "deflation" strategy applied to the perceived obstacle-free regions in a map. The fundamental principle is to shrink the declared safe regions based on the estimated incremental odometry error (and its covariance) at each timestep. This ensures that the map remains "certifiably correct," meaning any point declared free is indeed obstacle-free in the real world, even as the overall odometry error relative to the global inertial frame accumulates unboundedly. The critical element is maintaining correctness in the robot's *body-fixed frame*.

The paper formalizes the problem by defining the world `W` as a union of free space `F` and obstacles `O`. A map `M_k` at timestep `k` consists of a claimed safe space `S_k`, unknown space `U_k`, and claimed obstacle space `R_k`. A map is "correct" if `S_k` is always a subset of the true free space `F`. The authors identify two primary sources of error due to odometry drift:
1. **Errors in map construction:** When mapping an obstacle from the body frame to the map frame using an inexact transform `bT_M_B_k`, obstacles can be wrongly placed, and lines of sight (marked free) can be inaccurately cleared.
2. **Errors in map querying:** When the robot queries the map using an inexact transform `bT_M_B_k` to check if a point is free, it can misinterpret free space.

To address these, the proposed framework relies on **Assumption 1**: that when a point is transformed between frames using an uncertain transform (modeled as a Gaussian perturbation on the Lie algebra of SE(3)), the transformed point is contained within an ellipsoid whose size is determined by the covariance matrix. This assumption allows bounding the error propagation.

The methodology is then detailed for two popular mapping paradigms:

**Approach 1: Certified Safe Flight Corridors (SFCs)**
- **Background:** SFCs represent free space as a union of convex polytopes, defined by a set of half-space constraints (H-representation). When the robot moves, these polytopes are transformed to the new body frame. Standard transformation (Eq. 11) is exact and doesn't account for odometry uncertainty.
- **Proposed Deflation:** Instead of the H-representation, the approach leverages the V-representation (vertices) of the polytope.
 - **Lemma 1** provides the mathematical basis for deflating a single polytope. For each face of a polytope, it calculates a `ρ_i` (deflation amount) by considering the maximum possible shift of the hyperplane due to the odometry uncertainty at each vertex of that face. This `ρ_i` is derived from the ellipsoid defined by Assumption 1 for each vertex.
 - The polytope is then transformed by the mean odometry estimate, and each hyperplane constraint `a_k+1,i p ≤ b_k+1,i` is modified by subtracting `ρ_i` from `b_k+1,i`. This effectively shrinks the polytope.
 - **Theorem 1** states that if the initial map `M_k` (union of polytopes) is correct, applying Lemma 1 to each polytope ensures the updated map `M_k+1` also remains correct.
- **Practicality:** The method acknowledges the computational cost of vertex enumeration for H-representation but notes its feasibility for 3D polytopes encountered in typical SFC applications.

**Approach 2: Certified Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (ESDFs)**
- **Background:** ESDFs represent the environment by storing the signed distance from each point to the nearest obstacle. Positive distances indicate free space, negative indicate obstacles. Typically, a query point in the body frame is transformed by the mean odometry estimate to the map frame to get its ESDF value. This leads to overestimation if the transform is uncertain.
- **Proposed Deflation:**
 - **Definition 2** introduces the "Certified-ESDF" (C-ESDF), which guarantees that the estimated distance `d_k^M` (using the mean transform) is always less than or equal to the true distance `d(p|B_k)`.
 - **Theorem 2** provides a recursive formula (Eq. 26) to maintain the C-ESDF. At each timestep `k+1`, the ESDF value at any point `p|M` in the map frame is updated by subtracting `sqrt(λ_max(Σ_p))`, where `Σ_p` is the covariance ellipsoid of `p` in the previous frame `B_k`. This term corresponds to the radius of the smallest sphere containing the error ellipsoid, ensuring a conservative (underestimated) distance. This is based on the Lipschitz continuity of the ESDF, implying that the true minimum distance within the uncertainty ellipsoid is bounded by the distance at the center minus the ellipsoid's maximum extent.
- **Practicality:** This approach is computationally simpler, often implemented on GPUs, and can be integrated into existing ESDF libraries.

**Integration with Safe Navigation:** The authors suggest a practical implementation where both a baseline (uncertified) map and a certified map are maintained. The baseline map can be used for nominal trajectory planning (e.g., exploration, goal-seeking), while the certifiably-correct map is used for critical safety checks and local obstacle avoidance, often via a safety filter.

**Odometry Covariance Handling:** A practical challenge is that most VIO/SLAM systems report pose covariance relative to the *initial* frame, not between *successive* frames. The paper provides a method (Appendix D, Lemma 3) to derive the covariance of the incremental transform `Σ_B_k_B_k+1` from successive absolute pose covariances `Σ_B_0_B_k` and `Σ_B_0_B_k+1` by assuming a high correlation between successive pose estimates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper evaluates its proposed certified mapping frameworks through both extensive simulations on a high-fidelity dataset and real-world experiments with a robotic rover.

**Simulation Results (Replica Dataset):**
The simulations used the Replica dataset, perturbing ground-truth trajectories to simulate odometry drift at different noise levels (Σ = 10⁻⁵I and Σ = 10⁻⁶I), representative of state-of-the-art VIO performance (1-3% Absolute Translation Error). The proposed Certified SFC and Certified ESDF methods were compared against "Baseline" (no error handling) and "Heuristic" (time/distance-based forgetting) methods using three key metrics:
- **Violation Rate:** Percentage of ground-truth obstacle points incorrectly classified as free space. (Target: 0%)
- **Maximum Violation Distance:** Maximum penetration depth of an obstacle into claimed free space. (Target: 0 mm)
- **Free-Space Volume:** Total volume claimed to be obstacle-free. (Target: as large as possible, while remaining correct)

**Key Simulation Findings:**
- **Violation Rate (Table I):** Certified methods dramatically reduced violation rates. Baseline methods showed high rates (6-60%), indicating significant map errors. Heuristic methods showed some improvement but were inconsistent. Certified SFC achieved near-zero violation rates (0.0002-0.0047%), demonstrating its strong correctness guarantees. Certified ESDF also significantly reduced violations (0.01-2.4%), though slightly higher than SFC, likely due to the discretization inherent in voxel-based ESDFs.
- **Maximum Violation Distance (Table II):** Certified methods severely limited how deeply obstacles could penetrate the claimed free space. Baseline methods showed large penetration distances (up to 990.8 mm). Certified SFC achieved sub-millimeter maximum violations (0.0-1.7 mm), effectively eliminating unsafe regions. Certified ESDF achieved 66.3-141.4 mm, a significant reduction compared to baselines but influenced by the 20mm voxel size, implying errors on the order of a few voxels.
- **Free-Space Volume (Table III):** As expected, certified methods resulted in a smaller claimed free-space volume compared to baselines. This is the inherent trade-off: a smaller, certifiably correct volume versus a larger, but potentially unsafe, volume. Heuristic methods also reduced volume but without guarantees. The authors argue that a smaller, trustworthy volume is preferable for safety-critical tasks.
- **Qualitative Visualization (Figures 3 & 4):** Visualizations clearly showed baseline maps misclassifying obstacles as free space (red regions intersecting ground truth mesh), especially for older mapped areas or thin obstacles. Certified maps (green regions) were smaller but showed no intersections, confirming their correctness.
- **Effect of Odometry Covariance (Appendix G, Table VII, Figure 9):** Decreasing odometry covariance (less drift) leads to certified methods claiming a larger free volume, approaching the true free space, while maintaining a consistently low violation rate. Uncertified methods, conversely, show increased (incorrectly claimed) free space volume as drift increases, along with higher violation rates.

**Real-World Experiment Results (Robotic Rover):**
A robotic rover (AION R1 UGV with Intel Realsense D455 and Nvidia Orin NX) was teleoperated through a narrow tunnel, mapped the environment, and then tasked to reverse blindly through the tunnel, relying on its previously constructed map. This scenario specifically highlights the impact of accumulated odometry drift.
- **Setup:** Human operator uses FPV feed and the onboard-generated ESDF map. A safety filter attempts to stop the robot if its planned trajectory intersects claimed obstacles or unknown space.
- **Baseline ESDF Failure (Figure 6a, c):** The rover, using the baseline map, perceived itself to be safely within the tunnel's free space. However, due to odometry drift, the map was inaccurate, leading to the robot *colliding* with a physical obstacle. The visual map available to the operator incorrectly indicated safety.
- **Certified ESDF Success (Figure 6b, d):** With the certified ESDF, as the rover reversed, the map's safe regions dynamically "deflated" (turned from green to red) in areas where the uncertainty due to odometry drift made the "free" claim no longer certifiable. When the robot approached these deflated (now "unknown") regions, the safety filter activated, bringing the rover to a safe stop *before* collision (approximately 15 cm away from the obstacle). This consistent safe behavior was observed across multiple trials.
- **Larger Scale Experiments (Figure 8):** In a larger room, the certified method maintained a lower violation rate than both baseline and heuristic methods. While its claimed free volume might be smaller than the baseline, it provided a *reliable* estimate of safe distances, even for points further away than the sensor's maximum depth integration distance.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that while uncertified mapping methods may produce larger maps, these maps are often erroneous and unsafe. The proposed certified methods, through their principled deflation, provide maps that are provably correct, leading to significantly reduced violation rates and successful collision avoidance in challenging real-world scenarios where odometry drift is a factor.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes a highly significant contribution to the field of robotics, particularly in the domain of autonomous navigation and safety. Its primary impact lies in bridging the crucial gap between increasingly accurate but inherently uncertain perception systems and the demand for provably safe robot operations.

**Key Significance Points:**
- **Formal Guarantees for Mapping:** The most important contribution is the introduction of a theoretical framework and practical algorithms for generating *certifiably-correct maps*. This moves beyond simply improving localization *accuracy* to guaranteeing map *correctness* despite odometry drift, a fundamental and pervasive challenge in long-term and GPS-denied autonomous operations.
- **Addressing Perception-Induced Safety Failures:** By formally tackling how perception errors propagate into mapping inaccuracies, the work directly addresses a major cause of safety violations (collisions) in autonomous systems. The real-world experiments powerfully demonstrate that state-of-the-art uncertified methods *will* lead to collisions in scenarios involving accumulated odometry drift, whereas the proposed certified methods prevent them.
- **Novel Deflation Strategy:** The concept of "deflating" the claimed safe space rather than "inflating" obstacles or relying on arbitrary "forgetting" heuristics is novel and mathematically sound. It ensures that the certifiable region remains valid, while allowing for re-integration of regions once certainty is regained (e.g., via loop closure), promoting computational efficiency and memory management.
- **Applicability to Common Representations:** The successful application and proof of correctness for both Safe Flight Corridors (polytopic maps) and Euclidean Signed Distance Fields (voxel-based maps) highlight the broad applicability of the framework to diverse mapping paradigms used in robotics.
- **Enabling Trustworthy Autonomy:** For safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous vehicles, inspection robots, search and rescue), the ability to trust the map's declared free space is paramount. This work provides a foundation for building more robust and trustworthy autonomous systems. The proposed dual-map strategy (uncertified for nominal planning, certified for safety checks) offers a practical path to integrate these guarantees into existing planning architectures without excessively limiting agility.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Robotic Safety:** Directly leads to safer autonomous robots, reducing the risk of collisions in complex, unstructured, and dynamic environments where odometry drift cannot be entirely eliminated.
- **Wider Deployment of Autonomous Systems:** By providing stronger safety assurances, this research could facilitate the deployment of robots in more challenging and sensitive environments where human intervention is minimal or impossible.
- **Influence on Perception Algorithm Design:** The paper implicitly highlights the need for future odometry/SLAM algorithms to not just report high-accuracy pose estimates and global covariances, but also to provide robust, bounded estimates of *incremental* pose uncertainties. This could drive new research in uncertainty quantification within perception.
- **Integration with Formal Methods:** This work strengthens the bridge between perception and formal methods in control (e.g., Control Barrier Functions, Reachability Analysis), enabling end-to-end safety guarantees from sensing to actuation.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by authors):**
The authors openly discuss limitations, providing a clear roadmap for future research:
- **Assumption 1 Fidelity:** The reliance on truncating normal distributions to bound pose perturbations could be improved with higher-order approximations or alternative uncertainty models (e.g., non-Gaussian).
- **Odometry Error Characterization:** A more rigorous characterization of real-world odometry error distributions, especially regarding outliers, is needed.
- **Incremental Covariance Reporting:** A strong call is made for modern perception algorithms to directly report incremental pose transform covariances, simplifying the integration of methods like theirs.
- **Volume vs. Safety Trade-off:** Further analysis is needed on how the deflation rate affects the usable free space volume and how this trade-off can be optimized.
- **Re-inflation upon Loop Closure:** Future work could explore mechanisms to "re-inflate" deflated regions when global consistency is re-established (e.g., through certifiable loop closures), potentially requiring new map representations.
- **Other Error Sources:** The framework focuses on odometry drift, but other perception errors (e.g., depth sensor failures, latency) also contribute to map inaccuracies and warrant future investigation.

In conclusion, "Certifiably-Correct Mapping for Safe Navigation Despite Odometry Drift" is a foundational paper that pushes the boundaries of perception-for-safety in robotics. By offering principled and provable methods to ensure map correctness, it significantly enhances the trustworthiness and reliability of autonomous systems, paving the way for safer and more widespread robotic deployment.
