# Blind Bipedal Stair Traversal via Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Oregon State University and Agility Robotics developed a proprioceptive reinforcement learning controller, enabling the Cassie bipedal robot to robustly ascend and descend diverse real-world stairs without external vision. This approach, leveraging extensive terrain randomization in simulation, successfully transferred to hardware, achieving an 80% ascent and 100% descent success rate on outdoor stairs.

## Problem

- Bipedal robots struggle with robustly traversing complex real-world terrains like stairs due to reliance on dynamic stability and the inherent fragility of exteroceptive sensors (vision) in varied conditions.
- Existing bipedal stair traversal methods often depend on accurate external sensors or operate in controlled lab settings, limiting their applicability in diverse human-centric environments.
- A gap exists in developing robust bipedal controllers that operate effectively in unknown environments using minimal information, ideally proprioceptive feedback alone.

## Method

- A ==sim-to-real reinforcement learning== paradigm was employed, training an **LSTM-based control policy** entirely in simulation using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)==.
- Extensive ==dynamics randomization== (joint damping, friction, encoder offsets) and, crucially, ==stair-like terrain randomization== were introduced during training to foster robustness and enable sim-to-real transfer.
- The solution utilized a minimally modified existing RL framework, updating only the terrain randomization without altering the reward function or introducing stair-specific objectives.

## Results

- Simulated Stair LSTM policies achieved high success rates for stair ascent and descent (up to **80-90%** at optimal speeds), while Flat Ground LSTM policies largely failed, demonstrating the necessity of terrain randomization.
- The learned recurrent policies successfully transferred to the physical Cassie robot without fine-tuning, exhibiting robust and error-correcting behavior on various real-world stairs, logs, and curbs.
- On an outdoor staircase, the robot achieved an **80%** success rate in ascending and a **100%** success rate in descending over **ten trials**, exhibiting emergent gait strategies like increased foot clearance and faster leg swing retraction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robust stair traversal can be achieved by a human-scale bipedal robot using solely proprioceptive feedback, demonstrating that accurate exteroceptive sensors are not always indispensable for complex terrain.
> - A memory mechanism (LSTM) is critical for developing robust policies in partially observable environments like unknown staircases, significantly outperforming feedforward networks.
> - Targeted domain randomization, specifically of terrain features (stair rise, run, number of steps, and noise), is sufficient to elicit complex behaviors like stair climbing without complex reward function engineering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of bipedal and humanoid robots capable of operating in diverse real-world environments is a significant challenge in robotics. A critical aspect of this capability is the ability to robustly traverse complex terrains common in human-centric environments, such as stairs, raised platforms, and sudden drops. While quadrupedal robots benefit from inherent stability due to multiple ground contact points and have demonstrated proprioceptive stair negotiation, bipedal robots rely on dynamic stability, making recovery from missteps particularly challenging on uneven terrain.

Previous research on bipedal stair traversal often relies on accurate exteroceptive sensors like vision systems or is confined to carefully controlled laboratory environments with known terrain models. However, such reliance on exteroception introduces fragility to real-world conditions, where factors like occlusion, fog, varying lighting, or computational constraints can compromise sensor reliability. The integration of sophisticated computer vision into high-speed controllers also presents technical difficulties for computationally limited mobile robots. Consequently, a gap exists in developing robust bipedal controllers that can operate effectively in varied, unknown environments with minimal reliance on external perception.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for achieving robust legged locomotion in real-world settings. RL-based approaches can learn proprioceptive reflexes and strategies for handling unexpected terrain disturbances by training over diverse conditions, contrasting with optimization or heuristic methods that often depend on prescribed ground contact schedules. Prior work on bipedal RL for the Cassie robot has primarily focused on flat or slightly inclined terrains, or employed curricula of rough terrains for quadrupeds. This current work positions itself to explore the limitations of this RL approach, specifically investigating whether a bipedal robot can achieve robust stair traversal using only proprioceptive feedback, and with minimal modifications to existing RL training frameworks. The paper claims to present the first controller for a human-scale bipedal robot that reliably traverses a variety of real-world stairs and disturbances using only proprioception, thereby addressing a crucial gap in autonomous bipedal locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the necessity for bipedal and humanoid robots to effectively operate alongside humans in diverse, human-centric environments. Such environments frequently present obstacles like stairs, curbs, and varying ground elevations. The conventional reliance on accurate external perception (e.g., cameras for terrain estimation) for navigating these terrains introduces significant fragility. Exteroceptive sensors are susceptible to real-world challenges such as occlusion, fog, poor lighting, or computational delays, which can compromise their reliability and thus the robot's locomotion robustness. The authors argue that foundational controllers should be as robust as possible, relying on minimal information about the world, ideally primarily proprioception, to handle unexpected ground conditions. This robustness is considered a prerequisite for robots to safely and reliably interact with and assist humans outside of controlled laboratory settings.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work were:

- **To investigate the limits of blind bipedal locomotion:** The research aimed to determine the extent to which a bipedal robot, specifically Cassie, could navigate complex stair-like terrains without any external perception or prior terrain models, relying solely on its proprioceptive feedback.
- **To demonstrate the feasibility of sim-to-real reinforcement learning for blind stair traversal:** A central objective was to show that an RL-based approach, involving training in simulation and transfer to the real world, could enable a bipedal robot to robustly ascend and descend stairs.
- **To achieve this with minimal modification to existing RL frameworks:** A specific objective was to evaluate if robust stair locomotion could be learned by primarily adapting an existing flat-terrain RL training pipeline. This involved modifying only the terrain randomization during training to include stair-like features, without requiring any changes to the reward function or introducing stair-specific objectives.
- **To validate the learned controller's performance in both simulation and real-world environments:** The study sought to rigorously test the policy's ability to traverse a range of stair geometries and other unexpected disturbances in both a simulated environment and on the physical Cassie robot.

Ultimately, the research was motivated by the question: "how robust can a blind bipedal robot be?" and sought to provide an empirical answer through a practical demonstration on a human-scale bipedal platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) paradigm to develop a blind bipedal controller for stair traversal. The core methodology involved training a control policy entirely within a simulated environment, incorporating significant dynamics and terrain randomization, and subsequently deploying the learned policy on the physical Cassie robot.

**RL Formulation:**
The RL framework largely followed previous work on bipedal gaits over flat terrain, requiring minimal changes. The primary adjustment was the introduction of stair-like terrain randomization during training.

- **State Space (s_t):** At each discrete timestep, the policy received a state vector comprising:
 - **Robot's physical state:** Pelvis orientation (quaternion), pelvis angular velocity, joint positions, and joint velocities.
 - **Command inputs:** Human-operator defined commands for forward speed, sideways speed, and turn rate. These commands were randomized during training (Table I) to ensure policies experienced a wide range of desired motions.
 - **Cyclic clock inputs:** Two phase variables, offset by 0.5, to synchronize the left and right legs' gait phases.
- **Action Space (a_t):** The policy output an 11-dimensional action vector at 40Hz:
 - **PD targets:** The first 10 dimensions were proportional-derivative (PD) targets for each joint, fed into a higher-rate (2KHz) PD controller.
 - **Clock delta:** The final dimension was a delta (δ_t) that allowed the policy to modulate the gait's stepping frequency, updating the phase variable φ_t.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function adopted the method from and did not include any novel stair-specific terms. It was designed to encourage desired locomotion by penalizing specific quantities at certain phases of the gait cycle. Key components included:
 - Penalties for foot forces during the "stance" phase (to encourage foot lifting).
 - Penalties for foot velocities during the "swing" phase (to encourage precise foot placement).
 - Costs for matching commanded translational velocity and orientation.
 - Terms encouraging smooth actions, energy efficiency, and reduced pelvis shakiness.
 - A probabilistic framework using Von Mises distributions defined the expectation of these cyclic penalty terms (Appendix, Table IV).
- **Dynamics Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer and prevent overfitting to simulation inaccuracies, several dynamic parameters were randomized at the beginning of each training episode or timestep (Table II). These included joint damping, joint mass, ground friction, joint encoder offsets, and the execution rate.
- **Policy Representation and Learning:**
 - **Architecture:** The control policy was represented as an LSTM recurrent neural network with two hidden layers of 128 dimensions, chosen for its memory-enabled capabilities in partially observable environments. For ablation studies, a feedforward neural network was also used.
 - **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a KL-threshold-termination variant was used for training.
 - **Symmetry:** A mirror loss term was incorporated to ensure the learned gaits were symmetric.
 - **Training Details:** Training involved 300 million timesteps in a MuJoCo simulation. Episodes were 300 timesteps (7.5 seconds). Hyperparameters included a replay buffer size of 50,000 timesteps, batch sizes of 64 trajectories (recurrent) or 1024 timesteps (feedforward), and an Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0005.

**Terrain Randomization (The Key Modification):**
Unlike previous work that trained on flat or slightly inclined ground, this research introduced extensive randomization of stair-like terrain during training.

- **Ground Incline:** The plane's incline (pitch and roll) was randomized between -0.03 and 0.03 radians at the start of each rollout.
- **Stairs Placement:** The starting position of the stairs was randomized, allowing episodes to begin with the robot on top of, or up to 10 meters in front of, the stairs.
- **Stair Dimensions:** Stair dimensions were randomized within typical city code ranges (Figure 2):
 - Per-step rise: 10 cm to 21 cm.
 - Per-step run (depth): 24 cm to 30 cm.
 - Number of steps: 1 to 8.
 - A small amount of noise (±1 cm) was added to the rise and run of individual steps to prevent overfitting to perfectly uniform stairs.

**Experimental Groups for Evaluation:**
Four groups of policies (each with five random seeds) were trained and evaluated:
1. **Stair LSTM:** LSTM policies trained with stair terrain randomization.
2. **Stair FF:** Feedforward policies trained with stair terrain randomization (to evaluate memory's role).
3. **Flat Ground LSTM:** LSTM policies trained without stair terrain randomization (to evaluate terrain randomization's role).
4. **Proximity LSTM:** LSTM policies trained with stair terrain randomization and an additional binary input indicating stair presence within one meter (to evaluate limited exteroceptive information).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research produced several key findings regarding the capabilities and characteristics of blind bipedal stair traversal via sim-to-real reinforcement learning.

**Simulation Results:**

- **Probability of Successful Stair Ascent and Descent:**
 - **Stair LSTM** policies demonstrated the highest success rates in simulation for both ascending and descending a five-step staircase with typical dimensions. Success rates were generally high (up to 80-90% at optimal speeds) but varied with commanded speed. Policies exhibited higher failure rates at very low speeds (due to insufficient momentum) and very high speeds (due to more dynamic and potentially less stable gaits).
 - **Flat Ground LSTM** policies, which had no exposure to stair-like terrain during training, showed a high rate of failure for both ascent and descent, particularly for ascending stairs, highlighting the necessity of terrain randomization.
 - **Stair FF** policies, despite being trained on stairs, performed significantly worse than Stair LSTM policies. This indicated that a memory mechanism (as provided by the recurrent neural network) was an important component for developing a robust strategy for handling the partially observable, challenging stair terrain.
- **Energy Efficiency Comparison (Cost of Transport - CoT):**
 - **Flat Ground LSTM** policies achieved the most energy-efficient gaits for locomotion on flat ground (mean CoT of 0.38).
 - **Stair LSTM** policies, optimized for stair robustness, learned less energy-efficient gaits for flat ground (mean CoT of 0.46), suggesting a trade-off between robustness to varied terrain and efficiency on flat ground.
 - **Proximity LSTM** policies, which received a binary input indicating stair presence, showed improved flat-ground efficiency (mean CoT of 0.39) compared to Stair LSTM. This suggests that even minimal external information can help the controller adapt its gait for better efficiency when stairs are not immediately present.

**Behavior Analysis:**

- **Swing Foot Motion:**
 - Comparison between Stair LSTM and Flat Ground LSTM policies revealed significant differences in swing foot paths (Figure 5a). Stair LSTM policies exhibited a much **higher foot clearance** during the swing phase, providing additional space to step onto raised steps.
 - The Stair LSTM policy also showed a **steeper foot descent path**, limiting the forward movement of the swing foot to 14 cm in the critical height range, which is hypothesized to prevent the foot from stubbing against the stair face.
 - An emergent behavior in the Stair LSTM policy was a **faster leg swing retraction rate** towards the end of the stance phase (Figure 5b), similar to observations in human and bipedal bird locomotion. This behavior can reduce impact forces and improve disturbance rejection for varying ground heights.
- **Ground Reaction Forces:**
 - Analysis of ground reaction forces (Figure 6) showed that the maximum nominal vertical leg force after impact remained relatively constant across different ground heights, indicating a stable leg swing policy.
 - The magnitude of the second hump in the double-humped vertical ground reaction force profile was increased during step-downs and decreased during step-ups.
 - Horizontal forces exhibited oscillations matching the policy's execution rate, hypothesized to be the policy controlling the pelvis's attitude.
 - The total vertical impulse was larger for step-ups and smaller for step-downs, aligning with the intuition of applying more force to lift the robot and less to lower it.
 - The horizontal impulse revealed a significantly larger forward impulse during step-downs and a slight reduction during step-ups, consistent with the observed leg swing retraction, which contributes to forward acceleration or deceleration based on contact.

**Hardware Results:**

- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The recurrent policies successfully transferred to the physical Cassie robot without requiring any additional fine-tuning or notable difficulties.
- **Real-World Robustness:** The robot demonstrated robust and error-correcting behavior while traversing various real-world staircases, logs, and curbs on a university campus (Figure 3). It also showed robustness to inclines and deformable terrain, such as walking through a wet grass field and up a small hill, none of which were explicitly modeled in training.
- **Quantitative Real-World Trials:** On an outdoor staircase, the selected Stair LSTM policy achieved an 80% success rate in ascending stairs and a 100% success rate in descending stairs over ten trials each.
- **Limitations:** The learned strategy appeared to rely on a solid stair face; policies struggled with "slatted stairs" in simulation, even when included in training. However, the policies were robust to stairs with randomly inclined steps and could handle angled approaches. The policy was generally robust to missteps, but could still fall under particularly egregious errors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of bipedal locomotion by demonstrating the surprising capabilities of blind, proprioceptive control for complex terrain traversal.

**Significance:**

- **Proof of Concept for Blind Bipedal Robustness:** The most significant finding is the successful demonstration of robust stair traversal by a human-scale bipedal robot (Cassie) using *only proprioceptive feedback* and *without external perception*. This challenges the prevalent assumption that accurate vision systems are indispensable for bipedal navigation over such complex terrain.
- **Efficiency of RL Training:** The work highlights that complex behaviors like stair climbing can emerge from an existing sim-to-real RL framework with minimal modifications. Specifically, only the terrain randomization needed to be updated to include stair-like features, while the reward function remained unchanged. This suggests that well-designed domain randomization can unlock capabilities without requiring laborious task-specific reward engineering.
- **Importance of Memory in Partially Observable Environments:** The comparative analysis between LSTM and feedforward policies underscores the critical role of a memory mechanism (recurrent neural network) for robust operation in partially observable environments like unknown staircases. This memory allows the robot to integrate past proprioceptive information to infer environmental context and adapt its gait.
- **Emergent Biologically-Inspired Strategies:** The observed emergent behaviors, such as increased foot clearance, steeper foot descent, and faster leg swing retraction, are consistent with strategies seen in biological systems for robust locomotion over varied terrain. This provides insights into the types of solutions RL can discover when faced with complex physical interaction challenges.
- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The seamless transfer of the learned policies to the physical hardware, without additional real-world tuning, further validates the effectiveness of the dynamics and terrain randomization strategies employed.

**Potential Impact:**

- **More Deployable Bipedal Robots:** By reducing reliance on potentially fragile exteroceptive sensors, this work paves the way for bipedal robots that are inherently more robust and reliable for deployment in real-world human-centric environments, where lighting conditions, occlusions, or sensor failures are common. This could accelerate the adoption of bipedal robots for various applications, from logistics to assistive robotics.
- **Hierarchical Control Architectures:** The findings suggest a new paradigm where a highly robust, proprioceptive "blind" controller forms the foundational layer, responsible for reactive adaptation to unexpected terrain. Exteroceptive sensing (vision, lidar) could then be leveraged for higher-level functions, such as long-term planning, path optimization, or improving energy efficiency, rather than being solely relied upon for basic locomotion. The "Proximity LSTM" results support this, showing how even a simple exteroceptive cue can improve efficiency.
- **Understanding Limits of Proprioception:** This research pushes the boundaries of what is considered achievable with purely proprioceptive control in bipedal robots. It opens avenues for future investigations into the absolute limits of blind locomotion and how sophisticated reactive behaviors can compensate for a lack of explicit environmental knowledge.
- **Advancements in RL for Robotics:** The success achieved through targeted domain randomization, without complex reward function design, offers valuable lessons for the broader reinforcement learning community in robotics, emphasizing the power of environmental variability in training.
