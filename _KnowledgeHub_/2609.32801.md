# PlanGuard: A Guardrail for Multi-Step Plan Safety in Embodied Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> PlanGuard is a pre-execution safety detector that judges whether a complete multi-step robot plan, given the current scene image and instruction, would cause physical harm. It is trained on MSP-Safe, a new dataset of about 16K plans labeled by three judges, and compressed by STAC-OPD, a teacher-to-student distillation method. PlanGuard-2B reaches 87.15% accuracy and 87.21% F1, beating GPT-5.5 and existing guardrails.

## Problem

- General-purpose guardrails such as Llama-Guard-4 moderate semantic content and miss physical risks created when plan actions interact with the real environment.
- Embodied safety detectors such as EMBGuard judge each subtask on its own, so they miss hazards that only appear from cross-step dependencies and action order.
- Fine-tuned large models detect plan risks well but are too slow for real-time deployment, while compact models trained by plain SFT plateau well below them.

## Method

- **MSP-Safe** dataset: paired Base/Risk tasks across 28 scenes and 12 hazard types, where one factor changes, either the instruction or the environment, to isolate what causes the hazard.
- Plans come from six planners in three families: general VLMs, embodiment-pretrained VLMs, and VLAs. Three judges label each plan Safe or Unsafe with a reason, decided by ==majority vote==.
- **STAC-OPD** distills a fine-tuned Qwen3.5-27B teacher into 0.8B/2B students. It uses ==token-level reverse-KL== on the student's own outputs, then retrains on routed targets: the student's answer if correct, otherwise the teacher's.

## Results

- PlanGuard-2B averages **87.21%** F1 across all four test subsets, ahead of Qwen3.5-Plus (**81.48**), Claude-Opus-4.6 (**80.70**), GPT-5.5 (**77.10**), and EMBGuard-2B (**68.96**).
- On the 0.8B model, SFT lifts F1 from **64.38** to **78.96**, on-policy distillation adds **2.51** points to **81.47**, and hard compensation brings it to **82.25**.
- On a test set where every step looks safe alone, judging the full plan beats per-step judging by **15.04** F1 points for PlanGuard-2B; EMBGuard shows no gain.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Plan safety is compositional: steps that are each harmless can become dangerous in sequence, so detectors must reason over the whole plan and the environment it changes.
> - A small specialist trained on targeted data can beat much larger general models on a narrow safety task while running fast enough for real-time use.
> - On-policy distillation can miss the answer branch the student rarely picks; switching to full teacher answers when the student still leans wrong fills that gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

An embodied agent turns a high-level instruction into several physical actions, and a safeguard must decide whether to allow the proposed plan before the robot acts. Those actions can be individually innocuous yet dangerous in sequence. Turning on a stove and then putting napkins beside it can create a fire risk. Neither a text-focused content filter nor a detector judging each step against the initial scene necessarily sees that risk. PlanGuard instead assesses the **whole proposed plan in its current visual environment before execution**, rather than classifying only its wording or one action at a time.

A kitchen plan: place a pot on the burner, turn on the burner, place napkins nearby, and fetch bowls. The general-purpose guardrail and the single-subtask guardrail mark the first two steps safe and do not identify the interaction. PlanGuard marks the plan unsafe and explains that steps 2 and 3 make it hazardous. This illustrates the paper's argument; it is not a real-robot trial.

### 2. What the safeguard judges

PlanGuard assesses whether the complete planner-generated plan would introduce physical risk, given its instruction and current visual environment, before execution. The closest prior work, [EMBGuard](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.30924), judges an observed scene and a candidate action. It is not a detector of cross-step plan interactions. Llama Guard 4 is a content-safety classifier. [Its model card](https://github.com/meta-llama/PurpleLlama/blob/main/Llama-Guard4/12B/MODEL_CARD.md) documents text and image prompt/response classification under content-hazard categories.

To train this judgment, the authors built MSP-Safe. Paired Base/Risk tasks vary either the instruction or a risk-relevant environmental factor while holding the other component fixed. Multiple planner families generate complete plans. Three judges annotate each plan with a Safe/Unsafe decision and rationale. The dataset spans 28 scenes and 12 hazard types. MSP-Safe has 13,692 training plans and 1,992 expert-reviewed test samples. The test samples are divided among in-domain, scene-held-out, hazard-held-out, and planner-held-out subsets, and all test subsets are balanced between Safe and Unsafe.

A large model may assess plans more effectively than a compact one, while costlier inference can hinder real-time use. PlanGuard therefore trains compact students with a fine-tuned larger teacher. First, the student generates its own responses, and the frozen teacher supplies token-level feedback on the prefixes the student actually reaches. Then a routing step selects complete student or teacher responses for additional training. The teacher's reconstruction does not receive the original label or decision, so it is not guaranteed to reproduce the reference label.

### 3. Whole plan versus step by step

The sharpest test uses an auxiliary set of 100 plans. Every subtask in each unsafe plan appears safe when independently evaluated against the initial image. Under the stepwise protocol, each subtask is judged independently against the initial image, and the plan is Unsafe if any step is predicted Unsafe. Full-plan evaluation judges the complete plan in one pass. Both protocols use only the initial environment.

For PlanGuard-2B, F1 is 57.69% stepwise and 72.73% full-plan, a 15.04-point difference. EMBGuard-2B's F1 is 55.65% stepwise and 54.55% full-plan. On this constructed diagnostic, the results favor PlanGuard's complete-plan approach. They do not isolate every possible cause of the difference.

Stepwise versus full-plan ACC and F1 (%) for EMBGuard-2B, PlanGuard-0.8B, and PlanGuard-2B. PlanGuard-2B's F1 rises from 57.69 to 72.73, while EMBGuard-2B's F1 does not rise. These are diagnostic-set measurements, not MSP-Safe-Test averages.

On the main benchmark, MSP-Safe-Test, PlanGuard-2B reports an average of 87.21% F1 and 87.15% accuracy across the four subsets. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, with Unsafe as the positive class. The strongest general-purpose VLM baseline by average F1, Qwen3.5-Plus, scores 81.48%, a reported 5.73-point difference.

### 4. Scope

PlanGuard treats the safety decision as a question about the interaction and order of planned actions in a scene, beyond whether each action looks acceptable in isolation. Its benchmark and diagnostic results support evaluating that larger unit before execution. These are results on a synthetic, expert-reviewed benchmark, not evidence of safe operation on physical robots.
