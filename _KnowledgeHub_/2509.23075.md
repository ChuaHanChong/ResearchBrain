# In-Hand Manipulation of Articulated Tools with Dexterous Robot Hands with Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> This research enables dexterous robot hands to manipulate articulated tools in-hand in real-world settings by combining a disturbance-driven sim-to-real training pipeline with a cross-attention tactile-force adaptation module. The method achieved 100% success rates for various articulated tools and demonstrated enhanced robustness to disturbances compared to baseline approaches.

## Problem

- Existing sim-to-real transfer methods for robotic manipulation are brittle for articulated tools due to their complex internal kinematics and contact-rich dynamics.
- The sim-to-real gap is exacerbated by unmodeled joint phenomena (e.g., friction, stiction) and limitations of current tactile sensing, hindering robust policy transfer.
- Many learning-based approaches require either highly accurate physical models or extensive, costly human demonstrations, limiting scalability.

## Method

- A three-stage pipeline trains a privileged oracle policy in simulation with curriculum-based force-torque perturbations to inherently improve contact robustness.
- The oracle policy is distilled into a proprioceptive student policy for sim-to-real transfer, retaining robustness learned from simulated disturbances.
- A Cross-Attention Tactile Force Adaptation (CATFA) module provides online adaptation on hardware by fusing whole-hand tactile and motor torque feedback with the base policy's action intent via cross-attention.

## Results

- CATFA achieved 100% success rates for in-hand manipulation across five diverse articulated tools (surgical clamp, tong, pliers, laparoscopic tool, stapler), outperforming sim-to-real student and proprioceptive behavior cloning policies.
- The approach demonstrated superior articulation precision, achieving 0.0 mm residual tip gap for the surgical clamp and 0.15 
0.09 mm for the stapler in the closed state.
- CATFA exhibited enhanced robustness to motion-planning disturbances, with significantly lower mean pose deviation (e.g., 0.022 
0.018 m for Clamp Open) and attenuated high-frequency oscillations compared to baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured force-torque random-walk perturbations during simulation training are effective in bridging the sim-to-real gap for complex, contact-rich articulated tool manipulation.
> - An intent-conditioned cross-attention mechanism for sensor fusion (CATFA) is more effective than direct concatenation for integrating imperfect real-world tactile and force-torque feedback for online adaptation.
> - Combining a disturbance-robust proprioceptive base policy with a real-time, sensor-driven adaptation module enables stable and precise articulation despite unmodeled real-world dynamics and external perturbations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has made significant advancements, particularly in the control of rigid objects using reinforcement learning (RL) and sim-to-real transfer techniques. Approaches like extensive domain randomization have enabled robots to perform tasks such as reorienting cubes and even dynamic pen spinning. However, a significant challenge remains in extending these capabilities to articulated tools, which possess internal kinematics and exhibit complex, contact-rich dynamics. Tools such as scissors, pliers, and surgical instruments require not only stable grasping but also precise in-hand articulation, coordinating finger and palm movements to operate the tool's joints.

Existing learning-based strategies for dexterous manipulation, including imitation learning and foundation models, often rely on extensive human demonstrations. While effective, the scalability of these methods is limited by the high cost and time associated with collecting demonstration data. Hierarchical policy learning and task planning offer structure but can suffer from cascading errors if low-level skills lack robustness, a problem amplified in contact-rich settings where precise force regulation is critical.

The prevailing sim-to-real transfer paradigm, while successful for rigid objects, often proves brittle for articulated tools held by high-degree-of-freedom robot hands. This brittleness stems from several factors: the difficulty in accurately modeling internal joint coupling, friction, stiction, and backlash in simulators; the challenge of multi-body contact under complex kinematic constraints; and the limitations of current tactile sensing technologies in terms of coverage, sensitivity, and specificity. Although tactile and visuo-tactile feedback can mitigate some state uncertainty, simulators still struggle to accurately represent these nuanced physical interactions, leading to a "reality gap" that degrades policy reliability.

Previous work on articulated object manipulation has largely focused on objects treated as part of the environment, such as opening doors or drawers, rather than objects actively held and manipulated in-hand. While some research has explored specific articulated tools, these efforts often rely on privilege-informed controllers or are limited to single tools, which can reduce generalizability and bypass the challenge of online skill discovery. This paper aims to address this fundamental gap by proposing an approach that enables dexterous robotic hands to reliably manipulate articulated tools under real-world contact dynamics, without requiring perfectly modeled simulations or extensive teleoperated demonstrations. It integrates disturbance-driven sim-to-real transfer with a sensor-driven online adaptation mechanism to improve robustness and transferability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable dexterous robot hands to reliably perform in-hand manipulation of articulated tools in real-world environments. This overarching goal is motivated by several challenges and limitations inherent in current robotic manipulation capabilities:

1. **Addressing Brittleness in Articulated Tool Manipulation:** Existing reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer methods, while successful for rigid objects, are frequently brittle when applied to articulated tools. These tools involve complex internal kinematics and contact-rich dynamics that demand stable grasping alongside simultaneous free articulation. The motivation is to overcome this brittleness by developing policies that can handle these intricate interactions robustly.

2. **Mitigating the Sim-to-Real Gap for Complex Dynamics:** Real-world articulated objects and robot hands exhibit unmodeled joint phenomena such as friction, stiction, and backlash, which are difficult to accurately simulate. These discrepancies amplify the sim-to-real gap, leading to policies that perform well in simulation but fail in hardware. The research aims to reduce this gap by developing a training pipeline that accounts for real-world uncertainties and dynamics not perfectly captured in simulation.

3. **Compensating for Imperfect Tactile Sensing:** Current robot hands often lack the ideal tactile sensing capabilities of human hands in terms of coverage, sensitivity, and specificity. This limitation can hinder a robot's ability to perceive and respond to contact interactions crucial for dexterous manipulation. The motivation is to integrate and effectively utilize available, albeit imperfect, tactile and force-torque feedback from hardware to refine manipulation policies.

4. **Reducing Reliance on Perfect Models and Extensive Demonstrations:** Many approaches require either highly accurate physical models or large-scale teleoperated demonstrations, both of which are resource-intensive and limit scalability. This work is motivated by the desire to achieve robust articulated tool manipulation without these stringent requirements, making the learning process more efficient and applicable.

5. **Enabling Online Adaptation to Instance-Specific Properties:** Articulated tools can have instance-specific properties (e.g., varying joint friction or wear) and real-world interactions involve unexpected perturbations. The objective is to develop a mechanism that allows the robot to adapt online to these specific properties and stabilize contact interactions under disturbances, rather than relying solely on a pre-trained, open-loop policy.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines specific contributions:
- Developing a **disturbance-driven sim-to-real training pipeline** that distills a privileged simulation policy into a student policy. This student policy is trained with structured force-torque random-walk perturbations to inherently improve contact robustness.
- Introducing **Cross-Attention Tactile Force Adaptation (CATFA)**, an intent-conditioned adaptation module. This module fuses whole-hand tactile and force-torque feedback from hardware with the base policy's action intent using cross-attention, producing contact-aware actions for online refinement.
- Conducting **comprehensive real-world evaluation** across diverse articulated tools, including perturbation analysis and quantitative robustness benchmarking, to validate the method's effectiveness.

The core motivation is therefore to move beyond fragile manipulation policies for articulated tools by incorporating learning from both simulated disturbances and real-world sensory feedback, leading to robust and adaptive control for complex in-hand tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for learning dexterous in-hand manipulation of articulated tools consists of a three-stage pipeline: (1) training a privileged oracle policy in simulation with disturbance augmentation, (2) distilling this oracle into a deployable proprioceptive base policy for sim-to-real transfer, and (3) enabling online adaptation on hardware using a Cross-Attention Tactile Force Adaptation (CATFA) module.

**A. Oracle Policy Definition and Training**
The first stage involves training a privileged oracle policy ($\pi_{oracle}$) in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This policy benefits from privileged observations, providing comprehensive state information unavailable in hardware, to learn a base controller for stable in-hand articulation.

- **Disturbance Augmentation:** A critical component is the curriculum of force-torque perturbations applied to the articulated tool during training. These disturbances are simulated as a random walk, with magnitude gradually increasing over time. This emulates arbitrary gravity vectors and external contact dynamics, enhancing the policy's robustness to real-world disturbances.
- **Observations:** The oracle observes joint positions and velocities of the Inspire hand's active degrees of freedom ($q_t, \dot{q}_t$), the previous joint target ($u_{t-1}$), articulation state ($\theta_{art_t}, \dot{\theta}_{art_t}$), tool pose relative to the articulation joint axis ($x_{obj_t}, \dot{x}_{obj_t}$), a binary articulation command ($s_t$), and raw simulated joint force signals ($\tau_{raw_t}$). The previous action $u_{t-1}$ is a privileged signal removed during distillation.
- **Actions:** The policy outputs absolute joint targets ($u_t$) for the hand, which are smoothed using an exponential moving average to improve stability.
- **Reward Function:** The reward encourages articulation progress and grasp stability under perturbations. It includes terms for pose regularization (penalizing deviation from initial pose), task completion (minimizing articulation angle error and rewarding sustained target achievement), incremental shaping (bonuses for crossing articulation thresholds), contact stability (penalizing deviations from desired finger contacts), slippage (penalizing object drops), and an action penalty (regularizing joint motion).

**B. Base Policy Distillation**
Since privileged observations are unavailable on hardware, the simulation-trained oracle policy is distilled into a deployable student policy ($\pi_{student}$) that relies only on observable proprioceptive inputs.

- **Proprioceptive Inputs:** The student policy uses only joint positions ($q_t$) and the articulation command ($s_t$) as observations. This choice is made because proprioceptive inputs are consistent between simulation and hardware, unlike visuotactile inputs which can suffer from significant sim-to-real gaps due to occlusions and sensing discrepancies.
- **Distillation Process:** The student policy is trained via behavior cloning on stable rollouts generated by the oracle policy. Unlike standard DAgger, which can be problematic due to early object drops with partially informed students, this approach ensures the student learns from successful manipulation trajectories. The random-walk force-torque perturbations are retained during this distillation to ensure the student inherits robustness.

**C. Online Adaptation via Cross-Attention Tactile Force Adaptation (CATFA)**
The distilled student policy is open-loop and lacks real-time sensory feedback, which limits its ability to compensate for unmodeled contact dynamics and disturbances in the real world. CATFA is introduced as an online adaptation mechanism to address this.

- **Partial Observability Reduction:** The hardware policy's proprioceptive observations are a partial projection of the full physical state, which includes latent compliance and frictional effects. CATFA augments these observations with real tactile force ($f_{tact_t}$) and motor torque ($\tau_{motor_t}$) signals. The assumption is that these load-bearing contact signals provide information about the latent dynamics, thus reducing uncertainty and moving the representation closer to Markovian.
- **Action-Space Correction:** CATFA learns a residual correction ($\Delta u_t$) that is added to the base policy's action ($u_{base_t} = \pi_{student}(q_t, s_t)$). This correction, $\hat{u}_t = u_{base_t} + \Delta u_t(q_t, s_t, y_t)$, where $y_t = (f_{tact_t}, \tau_{motor_t})$, implicitly captures the conditional dependence of the optimal action on tactile-force measurements.
- **Hardware Sensing:** The Inspire Hand is equipped with resistive tactile skins (providing quantized force readings over the pads, augmented with a foam-plate-pad layer for improved sensitivity) and motor current sensing for torques. These real-world signals differ from simulated estimates due to transmission effects (e.g., backlash, friction), which CATFA directly incorporates.
- **Model Architecture:** CATFA operates by taking the base policy's internal intent embedding ($z_t$) as a query. Tactile data ($f_{tact_t}$) is processed by a convolutional encoder, and motor torques ($\tau_{motor_t}$) by an MLP, to produce compact feature vectors. These sensor encodings are concatenated to form the keys and values for a multi-head cross-attention module. This intent-conditioned design means corrections are applied selectively based on the policy's internal intent and sensory evidence, preserving nominal articulation while correcting for discrepancies.
- **Training Adaptation:** CATFA is trained via behavior cloning on a small dataset of human-labeled successful real-world rollouts (fewer than 50). The base policy $\pi_{student}$ remains frozen during this fine-tuning, allowing CATFA to learn real-time corrective feedback without altering the core motion prior.

**D. Hardware and Simulation Setup**
Experiments use an Inspire Hand (6 active DoFs, 6 mimic joints) mounted on a Franka arm. Control frequency is 30 Hz for joint targets (120 Hz PD controller). Tactile sensing is at 75 Hz. Policies are trained in IsaacLab using 8192 parallel headless environments on an Nvidia RTX 4090. Tools are scaled with mechanical fixtures to fit the Inspire Hand, and object properties are randomized in simulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the proposed framework, particularly the Cross-Attention Tactile Force Adaptation (CATFA) module, through comprehensive real-world experiments on five articulated tools: a surgical clamp, a tong, pliers, a laparoscopic tool, and a stapler.

**A. Quantitative Evaluation on Articulated Tool Manipulation (Table II)**
This evaluation compared the sim-to-real student policy ($\pi_{student}$), a Proprioceptive Behavior Cloning (BC) policy (without tactile or force inputs), and CATFA. Metrics included success rate, maximum tip separation in the open state (Opening Disp.), and residual tip gap in the closed state (Closure Residual).

- **Success Rate:** CATFA consistently achieved the highest success rates, reaching 100% across all five tools. In contrast, the Sim-to-Real Student policy showed lower success rates for some tools (e.g., 20% for Surgical Clamp, 30% for Plier), indicating significant sim-to-real transfer challenges without adaptation. The Proprioceptive BC policy also showed improved success over the raw student but generally lagged behind CATFA (e.g., 90% for Surgical Clamp, 70% for Plier).
- **Opening Displacement:** CATFA generally achieved superior or comparable maximum tip separation in the open state. For example, for the Tong, CATFA achieved 64.23 ± 0.36 mm, surpassing the Sim-to-Real Student (51.23 ± 3.97 mm) and Proprioceptive BC (51.55 ± 0.87 mm). Similar improvements were observed for the Plier and Stapler.
- **Closure Residual:** CATFA demonstrated the lowest or among the lowest residual tip gaps in the closed state across all tools, indicating more complete and stable closure. For the Surgical Clamp and Stapler, CATFA achieved a 0.0 mm and 0.15 ± 0.09 mm residual respectively, signifying full closure, which was not consistently achieved by the baselines.

These results indicate that CATFA significantly improves articulation performance and grasp stability in real-world settings compared to policies without such sensory adaptation.

**B. Robustness to Perturbations (Table III and Fig. 6)**
A second study evaluated robustness to motion-planning disturbances. The dexterous hand, mounted on a Franka arm, executed predefined end-effector trajectories with randomized accelerations. Pose deviation ($e_{pose}$) was used as the metric, combining translational and rotational errors. This study included additional baselines: Proprio–Force BC, Proprio–Tactile BC, and Proprio–Tactile–Force BC, where sensory embeddings were directly concatenated without cross-attention.

- **Overall Pose Deviation:** CATFA consistently yielded the lowest mean pose deviation (e.g., 0.022 ± 0.018 m for Clamp Open, 0.033 ± 0.018 m for Plier Close) or was among the best performers across all tested articulated tools (Clamp, Plier, Laparoscopic tool) in both open and closed states.
- **Comparison to Concatenation Baselines:** CATFA outperformed the baselines that used direct concatenation of tactile and/or force embeddings (Proprio–Tactile-Force BC, Proprio–Tactile BC, Proprio–Force BC). This finding supports the hypothesis that the intent-conditioned cross-attention mechanism in CATFA is more effective than symmetric feature aggregation for integrating sensor feedback.
- **Closed-Loop Disturbance Response (Fig. 6):** Visual analysis of trajectory plots (Figure 6) further confirmed CATFA's superior performance. It showed a uniformly lower mean error and attenuated high-frequency oscillations during both open and closed articulation phases under dynamic perturbations. In contrast, the proprioceptive BC policy accumulated drift, and the distilled student exhibited large transient error spikes, highlighting the improved closed-loop robustness provided by CATFA.

**C. Simulation Validation**
In simulation, policies trained with random-walk force-torque perturbations (as described in the methodology) exhibited improved stability under disturbances. This finding provides simulated evidence supporting the observed robustness gains in hardware, confirming the efficacy of the disturbance-driven training pipeline.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the proposed framework, particularly the CATFA module, enables robust sim-to-real transfer and online adaptation for in-hand manipulation of articulated tools. It achieves higher success rates, more precise articulation, and superior disturbance rejection compared to baseline methods lacking real-time, intent-conditioned sensory feedback.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of dexterous robotic manipulation, particularly concerning articulated tools in human-centric environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Advancing Dexterous Manipulation of Articulated Tools:** The work directly addresses a core challenge in robotics: the brittle performance of existing manipulation policies when confronted with the coupled kinematics and contact dynamics of articulated tools. By demonstrating reliable in-hand manipulation of diverse tools like scissors, pliers, and surgical instruments, it pushes the boundaries of what dexterous robot hands can achieve, moving beyond rigid object manipulation.

2. **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer for Complex Tasks:** The proposed disturbance-driven sim-to-real training pipeline offers a method for effectively transferring complex, contact-rich skills from simulation to real hardware. This is crucial for reducing development time and costs associated with extensive real-world training, making robot learning more practical and scalable. The ability to learn contact-rich skills that reliably transfer through proprioceptive policies, augmented by simulated perturbations, is a key step towards real-world deployment.

3. **Novel Online Adaptation Mechanism:** The Cross-Attention Tactile Force Adaptation (CATFA) module introduces an innovative way to integrate real-world sensory feedback (tactile and motor torque) for online policy refinement. Unlike traditional multimodal fusion, its intent-conditioned cross-attention mechanism allows for targeted corrections, preserving the base policy's learned motion prior while adaptively responding to unmodeled contact dynamics and perturbations. This capability for online adaptation is critical for robust operation in dynamic and uncertain real-world environments.

4. **Reduced Reliance on Perfect Models and Large Datasets:** The approach reduces the dependency on perfectly modeled simulations and large-scale teleoperated demonstrations, which are often costly and time-consuming to acquire. By leveraging structured disturbance training in simulation and fine-tuning CATFA with a limited number of real-world demonstrations (fewer than 50 successful rollouts), the methodology offers a more efficient learning paradigm.

5. **Improved Disturbance Rejection and Stability:** Quantitative results demonstrate that CATFA significantly improves grasp stability and disturbance robustness across a variety of articulated tools. This enhanced resilience to external perturbations and unmodeled internal dynamics is vital for reliable operation in practical applications. The observed reduction in minor joint micro-oscillations in hardware also attests to its stabilizing effect.

6. **Modular and Extensible Design:** The modular nature of the framework, separating the base policy learning from the adaptation module, offers extensibility. Articulation policies can serve as reusable skill primitives, and additional sensing modalities could be integrated through dedicated adaptors, facilitating future research and development.

7. **Implications for Humanoid Robotics and Human-Centric Environments:** The ability of dexterous robot hands to manipulate tools designed for human hands has direct implications for the development of humanoid robots. This work contributes to the advancement of humanoids operating in human-centric environments, potentially enabling them to perform complex tasks in fields such as healthcare (e.g., surgical assistance with laparoscopic tools), manufacturing, or domestic assistance.

While the current formulation has limitations, such as assuming binary articulation commands and sensitivities to motor torque scaling, its advancements lay a foundation for more sophisticated and generalized in-hand manipulation capabilities. Future work addressing continuous articulation targets and adaptive torque calibration could further enhance its impact.
