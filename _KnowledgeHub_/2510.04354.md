# Reliable and Scalable Robot Policy Evaluation with Imperfect Simulators

> [!summary] Summary
> SureSim introduces a framework that integrates imperfect simulation with limited real-world trials to provide statistically rigorous and cost-effective evaluation of robot policies. It provides finite-sample valid confidence intervals on real-world policy performance, achieving an average reduction of 20-25% in hardware evaluation effort compared to real-only methods.

## Problem

- Real-world robot policy evaluation is costly, time-consuming, and lacks statistical rigor due to the reliance on a small, statistically insufficient number of hardware trials.
- Physics-based simulators offer scalability but their inherent 'sim-to-real gap' prevents direct, statistically sound inferences about real-world performance from simulation data alone.
- There is a need for methods that provide finite-sample valid confidence intervals on individual robot policy performance, robustly combining imperfect simulation with scarce real-world data.

## Method

- **SureSim** formalizes policy evaluation as a ==Prediction-Powered Inference== (**PPI**) problem, leveraging a small dataset of paired real-world/simulation outcomes with a large dataset of additional simulation predictions.
- It employs a meticulous `real2sim` pipeline to create corresponding simulation environments for real-world setups, ensuring a strong correlation between paired data.
- The framework utilizes a uniform **PPI** estimator and the ==Waudby-Smith and Ramdas== (**WSR**) algorithm to compute finite-sample valid and tighter confidence intervals for mean real-world policy performance.

## Results

- SureSim reduced the required real-world hardware evaluation effort by an average of 20-**25%** compared to traditional real-only methods for achieving similar statistical confidence bounds.
- The uniform PPI variant of SureSim efficiently tightened confidence interval widths, reducing them by **14.4%** for a diffusion policy when incorporating 700 additional simulations.
- Extensive Sim2Sim experiments confirmed that SureSim consistently provides provably valid confidence interval coverage, unlike control variate methods which often showed miscoverage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) effectively combines imperfect simulation with limited real-world data to yield statistically valid and tighter confidence intervals on robot policy performance.
> - The effectiveness of simulation augmentation is directly tied to the correlation between real and simulated outcomes; low correlation (large sim-to-real gap) diminishes the inferential benefits.
> - Finite-sample valid statistical methods are essential for reliable robot policy evaluation, as methods relying on asymptotic guarantees can exhibit miscoverage in practical, data-limited scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in robot learning, particularly with imitation learning, foundation models, and large-scale datasets, have led to increasingly capable robot manipulation policies that can generalize across a wide range of tasks and environments. However, a significant bottleneck has emerged in the rigorous and scalable evaluation of these policies. This paper addresses this critical challenge, positioning itself at the intersection of robot learning, statistical inference, and simulation-to-real transfer.

Historically, progress in fields like computer vision (e.g., ImageNet, COCO) and natural language processing (e.g., SquaD, GLUE/SuperGLUE) has been driven by standardized, static benchmarks and metrics. In contrast, robot policy evaluation requires physical interaction with dynamic environments, making it inherently resource-intensive. Current real-world evaluations often rely on a small number of hardware trials (e.g., 20-40), which fundamentally lack statistical assurance and hinder reliable assessment of policy generalization and robustness.

Simultaneously, physics-based simulators (e.g., ManiSkill3, MuJoCo, Isaac Gym) and emerging action-conditioned video world models offer scalable and cost-effective alternatives to real-world testing. While simulation performance often correlates with real-world outcomes, the notorious "sim-to-real gap"—stemming from mismatches in visual features, physics modeling, and environmental parameters—precludes direct, statistically rigorous inferences about real-world performance from simulation alone. This gap leads to biased performance bounds if only simulation data is used.

Prior work has explored various avenues to address evaluation challenges. In real-world policy evaluation, efforts focus on establishing standardized benchmarks (e.g., RoboArena, AutoEval, DROID) and cloud-based platforms to enhance reproducibility and reduce manual effort. For simulation-based evaluation, researchers aim to mitigate the sim-to-real gap through techniques like system identification (e.g., SIMPLER) or by leveraging world models. Statistically confident policy evaluation has been explored in domains like self-driving cars using importance sampling, or in manipulation for policy comparison using A/B testing or sequential methods. However, providing *finite-sample valid confidence intervals* on *individual policy performance* for robotic manipulation, particularly when combining imperfect simulation with costly real-world data, remains an underexplored area.

SureSim contributes to this landscape by bridging the gap between scalable but imperfect simulation and costly but ground-truth real-world evaluation. It leverages the mathematical framework of Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI), a paradigm traditionally applied in fields like protein structure analysis or galaxy classification, to robot policy evaluation. By formalizing the problem as PPI, SureSim offers a statistically robust method to combine limited real-world data with large-scale simulation to obtain *provably valid* and *tighter* confidence intervals on real-world performance than either approach could provide alone. This work is distinct from concurrent efforts in off-policy evaluation or control variates in self-driving by specifically focusing on finite-sample validity and the unique challenges of robotic manipulation, where policies are highly sensitive to environmental perturbations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables **reliable and scalable evaluation of robot policies by combining large-scale simulation with a relatively small number of real-world hardware trials**, while providing **trustworthy statistical assurances on the policy's real-world performance**.

This overarching objective is driven by several key motivations:
1. **Addressing the Cost and Lack of Rigor in Real-World Evaluation**: Evaluating robot policies in the real world is inherently expensive, time-consuming, and labor-intensive. Consequently, most research studies rely on a limited number of real-world trials (e.g., 20-40), which are statistically insufficient to draw robust conclusions about a policy's true performance or its generalization capabilities across diverse environments. The motivation is to overcome these practical limitations without sacrificing statistical confidence.
2. **Mitigating the "Sim-to-Real Gap" in Simulation Evaluation**: While physics-based simulators and world models offer a scalable and cheap alternative, the inherent discrepancies between simulation and reality (visual differences, inaccurate physics models) mean that performance metrics derived solely from simulation cannot be rigorously translated to real-world performance. The goal is to leverage the scalability of simulation while correcting for its inherent bias.
3. **Providing Finite-Sample Valid Confidence Intervals**: A core motivation is to move beyond empirical success rates or asymptotic guarantees. The authors aim to provide **finite-sample valid confidence intervals** on the mean real-world policy performance, ensuring that the estimated performance bounds are statistically robust and trustworthy, even with limited real-world data. This is crucial for decision-making, policy comparison, and certification in safety-critical applications.
4. **Optimizing Hardware Evaluation Effort**: The research seeks to demonstrate that by intelligently combining simulation and real-world data, it is possible to achieve tighter statistical bounds on policy performance with significantly less hardware evaluation effort than traditional real-only methods. Specifically, the paper aims to quantify the savings in hardware trials.
5. **Enabling Evaluation of Modern Robot Learning Models**: With the rise of complex robot foundation models and diffusion policies that generalize to diverse tasks, there is an urgent need for robust evaluation methodologies that can assess their performance across broad environment distributions. This work provides a tool for such evaluations.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a statistically sound methodology that allows researchers and practitioners to confidently assess how well a robot policy will perform in the real world, using a resource-efficient blend of real and simulated testing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SureSim framework addresses the policy evaluation challenge by formalizing it as a **Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI)** problem, a statistical paradigm designed to leverage a large dataset of learned predictions alongside a smaller set of ground-truth labels for valid statistical inference.

The core approach is outlined as follows:

1. **Problem Formulation**: The objective is to estimate the mean policy performance, $\mu^* = E_{X \sim D_{env}}[Y(X)]$, where $Y(X)$ is the outcome of policy $\pi$ in a real-world environment $X$ from distribution $D_{env}$, and the metric $M$ is bounded, typically in $$. The goal is to compute a confidence interval $CI = (l, u)$ such that $P(\mu^* \in CI) \geq 1-\alpha$ for any finite number of real-world evaluations $n$ (finite-sample validity).

2. **Real2Sim Pipeline**: A crucial component is the `real2sim` function $g: X \rightarrow \tilde{X}$, which translates a real environment setup $X$ into its corresponding simulation environment $\tilde{X}$. This function ensures that for each real trial, a matched simulated counterpart can be generated. The paper details a sophisticated `real2sim` pipeline for robotic manipulation:
- **Object Modeling**: 3D models for real objects are created (e.g., using Meshy from single images) and scaled to match real-world dimensions.
- **Environment Setup**: Simulation environments (e.g., in ManiSkill3) are carefully replicated from real ones, including robot models (Franka Panda), gripper types, camera parameters (wrist-mounted and third-person), and workspace features (table mesh with real texture, 3D scanned background).
- **Lighting and Dynamics Tuning**: Lighting parameters are tuned for visual quality and simulation speed, and contact physics are optimized to maximize policy performance in simulation on randomly selected initial conditions.

3. **Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) Integration**:
- **Data Collection**: The framework requires two types of data:
- **Paired Evaluations ($D_{paired}$)**: A small number $n$ of real environments $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are sampled from $D_{env}$. For each $X_i$, both a real-world outcome $Y_i$ and its corresponding simulation prediction $f(\tilde{X}_i)$ (where $\tilde{X}_i = g(X_i)$) are collected.
- **Additional Simulation Evaluations ($D_{sim}$)**: A large number $N$ of additional environments $X_{n+1}, \dots, X_{n+N}$ are sampled, for which only simulation predictions $f(\tilde{X}_i)$ are obtained.
- **Uniform PPI Estimator**: SureSim primarily employs the uniform PPI estimator (Equation 3 in the paper). This estimator combines the average of all simulation predictions with a "rectifier" term that corrects for the bias of simulation predictions using the paired data:
 $\mu^{unif}_{PPI} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i \in D_{paired}} (Y_i - f(\tilde{X}_i))}_{\text{Rectifier}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n+N} \sum_{i=1}^{n+N} f(\tilde{X}_i)}_{\text{Simulation evaluations}}$
 The rectifier term effectively estimates and subtracts the systematic bias between real and simulation outcomes observed in the paired dataset.
- **Confidence Interval Calculation**: Finite-sample valid confidence intervals for $\mu^{unif}_{PPI}$ are computed using the **Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (WSR) algorithm**. WSR is a non-asymptotic mean estimation procedure that provides valid confidence intervals for bounded random variables without strong distributional assumptions, making it suitable for policy performance metrics.
- **SureSim (2-Stage) Variant**: An alternative PPI method (Equation 4) is also implemented, where confidence intervals for the rectifier and the additional simulation data are computed separately (each using WSR) and then combined via a Minkowski sum. This approach is generally less efficient for small $n$ due to the separate budgeting of significance levels and bloating of rectifier bounds.

4. **Mitigating Real-Simulation Gap for Paired Data**: To enhance the effectiveness of the rectifier and ensure higher correlation between paired real and simulation outcomes, specific measures are implemented:
- Using the same random seed for stochastic policies in both real and simulation runs.
- Averaging simulation results over multiple perturbed initial conditions around a real initial condition (e.g., 20 sim runs for each real (x,y) initial condition) to obtain a more robust simulation counterpart score.

5. **Policies and Metrics**: The framework is evaluated on:
- A single-task **diffusion policy** trained from scratch for pick-and-place.
- A generalist robot foundation model, **π0**, fine-tuned for multiple pick-and-place tasks.
- A partial success score (0-1) is used as the evaluation metric, capturing nuances beyond binary success/failure.

6. **Baselines**:
- **Classical**: This baseline computes confidence intervals solely from real-world evaluations using the WSR procedure, representing the standard hardware-only approach.
- **Control Variate**: Although implemented in sim2sim, it is not used as a primary baseline for hardware experiments due to its lack of provable Type-I error control in finite samples, which is a critical requirement for SureSim.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using SureSim on real robot hardware and extensive simulations yielded several significant findings:

1. **Reduced Hardware Evaluation Effort (20-25% Savings)**: A primary result is the substantial reduction in the number of real-world hardware trials required to achieve similar statistical confidence bounds on policy performance. SureSim demonstrated average savings of **20-25% of hardware evaluation effort** compared to the "Classical" method (real-only evaluation) (Figure 5). This directly translates to significant cost and time savings in robot learning research.

2. **Efficient Confidence Interval Tightening with SureSim**:
- The **SureSim (Uniform PPI)** method efficiently tightens confidence intervals as the number of additional simulation evaluations ($N$) increases. For the diffusion policy, at $N=700$ additional simulations, SureSim reduced the mean interval width by 14.4% compared to the Classical method (Figure 4).
- SureSim's interval width quickly approaches a lower bound dictated by the rectifier variance, indicating efficient utilization of simulation data up to the limit imposed by the real-simulation gap.
- The **SureSim (2-Stage)** family exhibited a slower decrease in interval width with scaling simulations, indicating less efficiency compared to the SureSim family, especially for smaller numbers of paired real trials ($n$).

3. **Impact of Real-Simulation Correlation on Effectiveness**:
- **Moderate Correlation (e.g., Diffusion Policy with $\rho=0.72$, $\pi_0$ with $\rho=0.59$)**: When there is a moderate correlation between paired real and simulation evaluations, SureSim methods consistently yield tighter confidence intervals than the Classical baseline (Figure 4, Figure 6). This demonstrates that even imperfect simulators can provide substantial benefits when their predictions correlate reasonably well with real outcomes.
- **Low Correlation (e.g., $\pi_0$ on specific initial conditions with $\rho=-0.05$)**: In scenarios with very low or negative correlation between real and simulation paired outcomes, SureSim methods *did not outperform* the Classical (real-only) approach (Figure 8). This crucial finding validates that the effectiveness of simulation augmentation is contingent on the predictive utility of the simulator; if the sim-to-real gap is too large (low correlation), simulation provides little to no inferential benefit. This aligns with the theoretical underpinnings of PPI, where a high rectifier variance (low correlation) limits the gains.

4. **Validation of Finite-Sample Coverage (Sim2Sim Experiments)**:
- Through extensive "Sim2Sim" experiments (using one simulator setting as "real" and another as "sim" with a known true mean), SureSim and its variants demonstrated **provably valid coverage rates**. This means that the confidence intervals generated by SureSim consistently contained the true mean with the specified probability ($1-\alpha$) across various settings (Figure 11).
- **Comparison with Control Variate**: The Control Variate method, while sometimes yielding tighter intervals at higher $\alpha$ in some cases (Figure 9), exhibited **miscoverage** (failed to meet the desired confidence level) when the amount of simulation data grew, especially at higher significance levels (Figure 14). This highlights the critical advantage of SureSim's finite-sample validity: practitioners can trust SureSim's bounds, whereas Control Variates can be overly optimistic and provide misleadingly tight but incorrect intervals in certain regimes.
- SureSim also showed superior scaling with respect to confidence level ($\alpha$), achieving poly-logarithmic dependence (O(log 1/$\alpha$)), making it suitable for safety-critical applications requiring very high confidence, unlike Control Variate's O(1/$\sqrt{\alpha}$) scaling.

5. **Applicability to Robot Foundation Models**: The framework successfully evaluated both a single-task diffusion policy and a multi-task fine-tuned $\pi_0$, demonstrating its relevance for assessing the generalization capabilities of modern, complex robot learning models.

In summary, SureSim offers a robust, statistically grounded, and resource-efficient method for robot policy evaluation, providing verifiable confidence intervals on real-world performance by intelligently combining imperfect simulation with minimal real-world testing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SureSim framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and evaluation, with far-reaching potential impacts:

1. **Accelerated Robot Learning Research and Development**: By significantly reducing the need for costly and time-consuming real-world hardware evaluations (saving 20-25% of trials), SureSim can drastically accelerate the iterative cycle of policy development, testing, and refinement. Researchers can explore more policy variations, hyperparameter settings, and environment distributions with fewer physical robot experiments, fostering faster innovation.

2. **Statistically Rigorous and Trustworthy Policy Deployment**: The provision of finite-sample valid confidence intervals is paramount for deploying robots in real-world, especially safety-critical, applications. SureSim ensures that performance claims are backed by strong statistical guarantees, preventing overconfidence and enabling more informed decision-making about a policy's readiness for deployment. This builds trust in autonomous systems.

3. **Scalable Evaluation for Generalist Robot Policies**: The emergence of robot foundation models and large behavior models capable of generalization necessitates evaluation frameworks that can scale beyond single-task, limited-environment testing. SureSim provides a scalable solution that can evaluate these complex policies across broad distributions of objects, tasks, and environmental conditions, which is crucial for understanding their true generalization capabilities.

4. **Foundation for Standardized Robot Benchmarks**: By demonstrating a reliable method to combine simulation with real-world data, SureSim paves the way for the development of more comprehensive and standardized benchmarks in robotics, akin to those in computer vision and natural language processing. Such benchmarks are vital for objective comparison of different policies and driving community-wide progress.

5. **Informing Simulator Development and Real2Sim Transfer**: The framework's effectiveness is directly linked to the correlation between real and simulation outcomes. By explicitly quantifying the impact of the "sim-to-real gap" (via the rectifier variance), SureSim provides a clear metric for guiding efforts in improving simulator fidelity, real-to-sim transfer techniques, and the `real2sim` pipelines themselves. It highlights when investment in simulation refinement will yield the greatest inferential benefits.

6. **Broadening the Applicability of Prediction-Powered Inference**: This work successfully demonstrates the utility of the Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) paradigm in a complex, high-stakes engineering domain like robotics. This could inspire further applications of PPI in other areas where ground-truth data is scarce and expensive, but large-scale, imperfect predictions are readily available.

7. **Guidance for Experimental Design**: The paper offers practical insights and best practices for configuring real-world and simulation evaluations, such as pairing trials, managing stochasticity, and robustly generating simulation counterparts. This practical guidance can help practitioners design more effective and statistically sound robot experiments.

In conclusion, SureSim significantly lowers the barrier to rigorous robot policy evaluation, making advanced statistical methods accessible for a field that has historically struggled with evaluation scalability and reliability. Its impact will be felt in accelerating research, enhancing trust in robotic systems, and providing a robust methodology for the next generation of robot learning.
