# SPELL: Self-Play Reinforcement Learning for Evolving Long-Context Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPELL framework, developed by researchers at Sun Yat-sen University and Alibaba Group's Tongyi Lab, introduces a multi-role self-play reinforcement learning approach that enables large language models to autonomously improve their long-context reasoning capabilities without human supervision. This method consistently enhances performance across various LLMs, achieving significant gains (e.g., 13.9 points average for Qwen2.5-7B at 16K context) and demonstrating superior efficacy compared to traditional RL methods with static datasets.

## Problem

- Achieving robust reasoning capabilities over long document contexts remains a challenge for large language models (LLMs).
- Human annotations for long-context tasks are prohibitively expensive, inconsistent, and unreliable, with accuracy significantly decreasing for very long contexts.
- A lack of universally applicable, programmatically verifiable reward mechanisms limits the application of traditional reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) to complex long-context reasoning tasks.

## Method

- **SPELL** (==Self-Play== Reinforcement Learning for Evolving Long-Context Language Models) is introduced as a multi-role self-play framework where a single LLM dynamically assumes the roles of a questioner, responder, and verifier.
- An iterative, closed-loop self-play process generates new question-answer pairs, provides solutions based on document context, and evaluates semantic equivalence using an ensemble-based verifier.
- The framework employs role-specific reward functions to incentivize intermediate question difficulty and reliable verification, and utilizes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for efficient, on-policy optimization of the unified policy.

## Results

- SPELL consistently improved long-context reasoning performance across 12 open-source LLMs (**4B** to **32B** parameters), with unaligned base models like Qwen2.5-**7B** gaining 13.9 average points at **16K** context length.
- The framework outperformed an RLVR baseline trained on static data, yielding more reliable and larger gains for capable models, and demonstrated effective generalization to **100K** token contexts without additional tuning.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of continuous questioner updates, history memory for curriculum development, and the self-consistent verifier for addressing semantic equivalence beyond lexical matching.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single LLM can autonomously generate, solve, and verify its own long-context reasoning tasks, effectively bypassing the need for human labels and external supervision.
> - A dynamic curriculum, where question difficulty adapts to the model's evolving capabilities (e.g., via a Gaussian-shaped reward), is more effective than static datasets for sustaining performance improvements in capable LLMs.
> - The self-consistent verifier, which learns to assess semantic equivalence, is crucial for generating stable and accurate reward signals in complex long-context reasoning tasks where programmatic verification is insufficient.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a challenge within the field of large language models (LLMs): the difficulty of achieving robust reasoning capabilities over long document contexts. Existing advancements in LLMs, particularly those leveraging reinforcement learning (RL) with verifiable rewards (RLVR), have predominantly focused on short-context scenarios (e.g., less than 1024 tokens) where tasks like mathematics, logical reasoning, or software engineering allow for programmatic verification of correctness. These RLVR methods often employ algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to optimize policies based on rule-based or programmatic reward signals.

However, the application of RLVR to long-context reasoning, such as long-context question answering, is challenged by two primary issues. First, processing long texts intrinsically increases complexity. Second, the cost and unreliability of human annotations for very long contexts become prohibitive, with human accuracy on some extra-long tasks dropping significantly. This limitation also impacts scalability, especially as LLMs approach or exceed human capabilities. Furthermore, the absence of universally applicable, programmatically verifiable reward mechanisms for complex long-context reasoning tasks, where semantic equivalence might not entail strict string matching, further restricts the widespread applicability of traditional RLVR.

SPELL positions itself within the emerging paradigm of self-play reinforcement learning, which aims to overcome the reliance on human labels and external supervision by enabling models to generate and solve their own tasks. While previous self-play approaches have been explored, many are restricted to short-context tasks (e.g., coding, math) with programmatically verifiable outcomes (e.g., via code executors) or involve multi-model architectures that increase systemic complexity. Some single-model self-play approaches incorporate self-rewarding mechanisms using self-consistency or internal confidence scores. SPELL extends this single-policy, self-play paradigm specifically to the domain of long-context understanding and reasoning. It differentiates itself by introducing a multi-role framework (questioner, responder, verifier) within a single LLM to generate and verify its own solutions in long-context settings, thereby aiming to address the limitations of human annotation and the lack of verifiable rewards for complex semantic tasks. This work seeks to fill a gap in self-play learning research by focusing on autonomous self-evolution for long-context comprehension without direct human supervision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable, label-free optimization framework for enhancing the long-context reasoning capabilities of large language models. This objective is motivated by several detailed challenges:

1. **Lagging Progress in Long-Context Reasoning:** The introduction highlights that despite significant advances in other areas of LLM development, progress in handling and reasoning over extensive textual contexts has been comparatively slow. This indicates an inherent difficulty in processing and synthesizing information from long documents.
2. **Scarcity and Unreliability of Human Annotations:** For long-context tasks, creating high-quality, reliable human-annotated datasets is prohibitively expensive and often inconsistent. The paper cites evidence from benchmarks like LongBench-V2, where human accuracy for extra-long multiple-choice reasoning tasks can drop to near-random chance levels (25.1%). This makes human supervision an unreliable and unscalable bottleneck for training LLMs that aim for superhuman reasoning in long contexts. The cost and instability of producing reliable annotations increase with context length, and supervision diversity tends to diminish.
3. **Absence of Programmatically Verifiable Reward Signals:** A fundamental limitation for extending current Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods to long-context tasks is the lack of easily verifiable ground truth. In complex long-context reasoning, answers might be semantically correct but expressed in varied forms, making simple string matching or rule-based verification insufficient. This prevents the generation of stable and accurate reward signals needed for effective RL training.
4. **Need for Autonomous Self-Improvement:** To overcome the aforementioned bottlenecks, the motivation shifts towards self-play reinforcement learning, where a model can generate its own tasks and learn from them without external human labels. However, applying self-play to long-context reasoning requires more than just generating questions and answers; it necessitates a robust mechanism to *verify* the correctness of generated solutions, especially when semantic equivalence is complex and not amenable to strict rule-based checks. This specifically motivates the need for a verifier role within the self-play loop.

In summary, the research aims to enable LLMs to autonomously improve their ability to reason over long documents by creating a self-sufficient learning ecosystem. This system is designed to bypass the limitations imposed by human annotation costs and the difficulty of programmatic verification in complex, long-context scenarios, thereby facilitating continuous and scalable enhancement of long-context reasoning skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPELL (Self-Play Reinforcement Learning for Evolving Long-Context Language Models) is a multi-role self-play reinforcement learning framework designed to enhance LLM long-context reasoning without external supervision. The core principle involves a single policy model, $\pi_\theta$, dynamically assuming three complementary roles: a questioner ($\pi^\text{que}_\theta$), a responder ($\pi^\text{res}_\theta$), and a verifier ($\pi^\text{ver}_\theta$). This integrated approach enables autonomous task generation, solution, and evaluation, fostering continual self-improvement. The framework utilizes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for efficiency in long-context settings.

#### 3.1. The Self-Play Evolutionary Loop

The SPELL framework operates in an iterative, closed-loop process. For each iteration, given a cluster of documents and a task type, the policy executes the three roles in sequence:

- **Questioning:** The questioner ($\pi^\text{que}_\theta$) generates new question–answer pairs. In the initial iteration, it samples a subset of documents. In subsequent iterations, it is conditioned on both a newly sampled subset of documents ($C_{\text{new}}$) and a "history memory" ($H_C$). This history memory stores the $L$ most recent solvable question–answer pairs and their source documents. By expanding the context with both new and historical documents and question-answer pairs, the questioner is encouraged to generate more complex questions that integrate information across multiple documents and avoid redundancy, thereby progressively increasing task difficulty. The questioner is also penalized for non-grounded or ill-formatted questions.
- **Responding:** The responder ($\pi^\text{res}_\theta$) attempts to answer the generated question based on the complete set of documents. To ensure document-grounded reasoning and prevent reliance on parametric memory, the full set of documents, including those not seen by the questioner, serves as context (with the unseen ones acting as distractors). A "grounding filter" discards questions answerable without document context. The responder generates $G$ independent candidate responses to promote exploration of diverse reasoning trajectories.
- **Verifying:** The verifier ($\pi^\text{ver}_\theta$) evaluates the semantic equivalence between each responder's output ($y_i$) and the questioner's reference answer ($a$). For each $y_i$, it produces $G$ independent binary judgments ($v_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$), which are then aggregated through majority voting to produce a stable, semantically aware consensus judgment ($v^\text{ver}_i$). This ensemble-based verification reduces variance in reward signals, which is crucial for the stability of the self-play system.

#### 3.2. Role-Specific Reward Design

To align the objectives of the three roles within the shared policy, SPELL employs specialized reward functions:

- **Verifier Reward:** The verifier is trained to improve its judgment reliability through self-consistency. Each of its $G$ judgments for a candidate output $y_i$ receives a reward of 1 if it matches the majority vote ($v^\text{ver}_i$) over all $G$ verifier rollouts for that $y_i$, and 0 otherwise.
- **Responder Reward:** The responder's reward ($r^\text{res}_i$) for the $i$-th solution is the maximum of a deterministic, rule-based check (Cover Exact Match, CEM) and the verifier's consensus score ($v^\text{ver}_i$). CEM provides a binary reward if the ground-truth answer appears in the response. Taking the maximum of these two rewards helps stabilize learning by accommodating semantically correct paraphrases that CEM might miss, preventing false-negative penalties.
- **Questioner Reward:** The questioner is incentivized to generate questions of intermediate difficulty, specifically where the responder's average success rate ($\bar{r}_\text{res}$) is around 0.5. This point maximizes reward variance and provides the richest learning signal. The reward ($r^\text{que}$) is defined by a Gaussian function centered at $\mu=0.5$ with $\sigma=0.5/3$. This reward function penalizes questions that are either too easy or too difficult. Additionally, the questioner is penalized for ill-formatted question–answer pairs or questions that are not grounded in the provided documents, enforcing quality and relevance.

#### 3.3. Unified Policy Optimization

Samples generated across the three roles supervise a single policy, $\pi_\theta$.

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):** GRPO is used for optimization, chosen because the quadratic complexity of attention in long-context inputs makes traditional PPO (which relies on a value network) computationally prohibitive. GRPO estimates advantage through group-wise reward z-score normalization, removing the need for a separate value network. The paper modifies GRPO for a strictly on-policy setting without KL regularization or clipping (i.e., $\beta=0$ and $\rho_{i,t}(\theta)=1$).
- **Role-Specific Dynamic Sampling:** To manage the imbalance in generated samples (one questioner sample, $G$ responder samples, $G^2$ verifier judgments), a dynamic sampling strategy is applied. All responder groups with non-zero reward variance are retained. Questions associated with these positive responder outcomes are labeled as positives for the questioner, and an equal number of negative questions (from those with non-positive reward) are sampled. For the verifier, instances where the majority vote aligns with a rule-based check are preserved, and conflicting groups are subsampled. This strategy aims to improve sample efficiency and balance gradients.
- **Advantage Estimation:** For the responder and verifier, group-level advantage estimation (Eq. 8) is used. For the questioner, which generates a single output per instance, its reward is normalized against other questioner rewards within the training batch (Eq. 9), similar to REINFORCE++-baseline.
- **Unified Policy Update:** All collected and sampled batches are used to jointly optimize the GRPO objective across all three roles (Eq. 10). The updated policy is then used for the next iteration, closing the self-evolutionary cycle.

#### 3.4. Training Details and Evaluation Setup

- **Implementation:** The framework is implemented using VeRL.
- **Generation Parameters:** Sampling temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95. Max input length 16K tokens, max output length 4K for non-reasoning models, 20K for reasoning models. Group size $G=8$.
- **Curriculum Parameters:** History memory size $L=3$ (most recent solvable QA pairs). Subset of documents for questioner $m=5$.
- **Optimization:** Purely on-policy RL training, batch size 128, learning rate $2 \times 10^{-6}$.
- **Task Formats:** Randomly samples one of three predefined task formats: document general QA, financial math QA, or multiple-choice.
- **Training Data:** Constructed from DocMath (financial reports) and a general-domain corpus derived from Ultra-Fineweb (filtered, deduplicated, clustered). Total of 6,450 unique document sets, 2,150 per task.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:** Six long-context QA benchmarks: LongBench-V2 (multiple-choice), Frames, HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue (multi-hop QA), and DocMath (financial report reasoning). Evaluated at 16K and 100K token input lengths. Performance metrics include average accuracy (pass@1) and pass@k. Multi-hop QA uses max of CEM and LLM-as-a-judge (gpt-oss-120b).
- **Baselines:** Unaligned base models, instruction-tuned models, reasoning models. Also, an RLVR baseline trained on a static dataset synthesized by DeepSeek-R1-0528 and filtered by gpt-oss-120b.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the efficacy and properties of the SPELL framework for long-context reasoning.

- **Consistent Performance Enhancement Across Diverse Models:** SPELL consistently improves performance across 12 open-source LLMs ranging from 4B to 32B parameters, encompassing dense and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, and various model families (Qwen, Llama). For unaligned base models, average improvements at 16K context length were substantial (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B gained 13.9 points, Qwen2.5-14B gained 14.4 points, Qwen2.5-32B gained 9.1 points). Notably, SPELL-trained base models often surpassed their instruction-tuned counterparts, suggesting data efficiency for label-free self-play. Instruction-tuned models (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct improved by 9.0 points) and specialized reasoning models (e.g., R1-Distill-Qwen-14B improved by 3.4 points) also showed gains. MoE models like Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking improved by 4.4 and 2.0 points, respectively. These results indicate broad applicability of the framework.

- **Superiority over Traditional RL with Static Data:** Compared to an RLVR baseline trained on a fixed dataset synthesized by DeepSeek-R1-0528, SPELL demonstrated more reliable and larger gains, especially for more capable models. While RLVR showed comparable performance for weaker models like Qwen2.5-7B, for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, SPELL yielded an average gain of 2.0 points, whereas RLVR showed no gain and even decreases on certain challenging benchmarks (DocMath and LongBench-V2). This suggests that SPELL's dynamic curriculum, which adapts question difficulty to the model's evolving capabilities, is more effective in sustaining performance improvements as models become more proficient, contrasting with the limitations of static datasets.

- **Generalization to Longer Contexts:** Models trained with a 16K input limit exhibited consistent performance gains when evaluated at 100K tokens without additional tuning. For instance, Qwen2.5-14B's average improvement increased from 14.4 points at 16K to 15.0 points at 100K. This indicates that the long-context reasoning skills acquired through SPELL generalize effectively beyond the training context window, suggesting a fundamental strengthening of document-grounded reasoning.

- **Enhanced Exploration and Higher Performance Ceiling:** Analysis of test-time exploration using the pass@k metric at 100K input length showed that SPELL-trained models, particularly Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, exhibited a steeper improvement curve as $k$ increased. Its pass@8 score reached 74.5, outperforming the RLVR baseline (68.1) and the original base model (66.9). The SPELL-trained model also surpassed gemini-2.5-pro in pass@4 performance. This indicates that SPELL effectively broadens the model’s test-time search space and elevates its maximum achievable performance.

- **Ablation Studies Confirm Design Component Importance:**
 - **Questioner Components:** Removing the format penalty or grounding filter resulted in minor performance drops (1.1 and 1.0 points average, respectively). Disabling the questioner's update mechanism led to a significant 4.6-point average drop, and removing the history memory caused a 2.9-point drop. These studies confirm that continuous updates and access to historical data are critical for a stable and progressively challenging curriculum.
 - **Verifier Components:** Removing the verifier and relying solely on rule-based rewards led to a 3.2-point average decrease (6.4-point drop on DocMath), highlighting the verifier's role in handling semantic equivalence beyond lexical matching. While disabling verifier updates or majority voting had smaller impacts, removing the consistency update mechanism resulted in a 1.5-point drop. This suggests that the verifier benefits from aligning its judgments with rule-based outcomes on verifiable tasks to enhance its reliability for non-verifiable outputs.

- **Questioner Reward Mapping Stability:** Comparison with AZR and R-Zero reward mappings for the questioner indicated that SPELL's Gaussian-shaped reward, centered at a 0.5 success rate, provides a more stable training process. This mapping effectively concentrates rewards on questions of moderate difficulty, mitigating the impact of data noise and leading to more controlled growth in response length and higher overall performance.

- **Hyperparameter Robustness:** Analysis confirmed that the theoretically derived standard deviation $\sigma=0.5/3$ for the Gaussian reward function is empirically effective, with wider curves degrading performance. The framework also demonstrated robustness across different group sizes ($G=4$ to $G=16$), with $G=8$ selected for balancing performance and efficiency.

- **Role of External Judges in Verification:** Replacing the rule-based judge with a stronger external model (gpt-oss-120b) did not yield a significant overall improvement, suggesting that the internal verifier could learn semantic verification through self-play. However, the internal verifier became less critical when an external judge was used, underscoring its crucial role in complementing brittle CEM-based rewards when an external judge is unavailable.

- **Generalization to Short-Context Reasoning (Appendix F.2):** Models trained exclusively on long-context data with SPELL showed performance improvements on short-context reasoning benchmarks, including mathematical tasks (AIME, AMC, MATH, GSM8K) and general knowledge (MMLU-Pro). Average scores increased by 6.93, 8.74, and 7.24 points for Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, and Qwen2.5-32B, respectively. This indicates that the reasoning competencies are transferable.

- **Performance on Additional Long-Context Benchmarks (Appendix F.3):** SPELL consistently outperformed base models and the RLVR baseline on MRCR (multi-needle "Needle in a Haystack") and subsets of HELMET (RAG, ICL, Summarization), demonstrating strong generalization beyond standard QA.

- **Outperformance against Long-Context Alignment Baselines (Appendix F.4):** SPELL consistently outperformed reimplementations of recent long-context alignment methods like SoLoPO, LongPO, and QwenLong-L1 on the same document corpus.

- **Evolutionary Dynamics and Reward Hacking Mitigation (Appendix F.5 & F.6):** The questioner's task distribution became more balanced over training, indicating exploration of diverse task types. The verifier's disagreement rate with rule-based judges consistently decreased, suggesting calibration. Specific architectural components (history memory, prompt templates, consistency checks, CEM-based reward) were identified as crucial for mitigating reward hacking risks such as questioner stagnation, responder mode collapse, and verifier self-delusion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPELL framework represents a contribution to the field of large language models, particularly in the domain of long-context understanding and reasoning. Its significance and potential impact stem from its ability to enable autonomous, label-free self-improvement, addressing critical bottlenecks in LLM development.

The work directly confronts the challenges posed by the scarcity and unreliability of human annotations for training LLMs on long and complex documents. By integrating a multi-role self-play mechanism (questioner, responder, verifier) within a single model, SPELL provides a method for generating scalable and diverse training data without requiring external human supervision. This bypasses the prohibitive costs and inherent inconsistencies associated with human labeling for long-context tasks, which are increasingly difficult as LLMs achieve higher levels of performance.

Furthermore, SPELL addresses the absence of programmatically verifiable reward signals for nuanced long-context reasoning where semantic equivalence is complex. The introduction of a self-consistent verifier, calibrated by verifiable tasks, allows for the generation of stable and semantically aware reward signals. This internal reward mechanism is critical for guiding continuous policy optimization, enabling the model to learn and refine its reasoning abilities even when explicit rule-based verification is insufficient.

The experimental results demonstrate that SPELL consistently enhances performance across a wide range of LLMs, including various architectures, sizes, and model families. Its ability to cultivate complex reasoning skills from base models, even surpassing instruction-tuned counterparts, suggests a high degree of data efficiency and practical value. The framework's superiority over traditional RL methods with static datasets, especially for more capable models, highlights the importance of a dynamic, self-evolving curriculum that adapts to the model's current competence frontier. The observed generalization to longer contexts than those used during training and the elevation of performance ceilings (as demonstrated by pass@k metrics against state-of-the-art models) indicate that SPELL contributes to a fundamental improvement in LLM long-context capabilities.

The three notable findings—that verifiable tasks can calibrate and strengthen the verifier’s assessment on non-verifiable tasks, that maintaining dynamic equilibrium among roles is critical for stable evolution, and that autonomous self-evolution becomes an indispensable strategy for advanced models—collectively point towards a paradigm shift. As LLMs approach or exceed human performance levels, external supervision becomes a limiting factor. SPELL offers a pathway for sustained advancement by shifting LLM development towards greater autonomy, where models can continuously evolve their own curricula and learn without external human intervention.

Potential impact includes accelerating the development of more capable LLMs for applications requiring deep understanding of extensive documents, such as legal analysis, scientific literature review, financial reporting, and complex question answering. While the study acknowledges limitations, such as the need for a theoretical framework for co-evolutionary dynamics, further exploration into ultra-long contexts, and some reliance on initial prompt templates, the demonstrated self-sufficiency provides a foundation for future research into fully autonomous agentic intelligence.
