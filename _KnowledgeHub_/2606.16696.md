# VENOM: Versatile Embodied Network for Omni-bodied Motion tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Osaka developed VENOM, a GPT-based model capable of full-body motion tracking across five different humanoid robots without decoupling body parts. The approach includes a novel 7705-hour multi-humanoid dataset and demonstrates comparable joint tracking accuracy to reinforcement learning experts while outperforming MLP baselines in motion stability.

## Problem

- Difficulty in achieving expressive full-body motion tracking across diverse humanoid robots from demonstration data.
- Existing cross-embodiment methods often decouple body control or are limited to simple locomotion tasks, restricting behavior range.
- Lack of large-scale, low-level control datasets for training generalist policies across varied humanoid platforms.

## Method

- Curated the **VENOM** dataset, a 7705-hour multi-humanoid dataset from five robots (Unitree G1, H1, H1 v2, Fourier N1, Booster T1), including noisy rollouts for enhanced robustness.
- Developed VENOM, a GPT-based transformer model with a ==shared central transformer block== for all humanoids, using distinct input embeddings and action heads for each embodiment.
- Trained the model using supervised learning to minimize Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between predicted and expert actions derived from the curated dataset.

## Results

- VENOM-noisy demonstrated the best motion tracking among supervised models, outperforming MLP baselines, which showed degraded performance when trained on multi-robot data.
- Achieved comparable joint tracking accuracy (RMSE approximately **0.42** to **0.58** radians) to RL experts across various behaviors and humanoids, while also showing improved motion tracking stability.
- The 95th percentile absolute root error rate for Unitree G1 was **12.3560** cm/s for VENOM-noisy, indicating better stability compared to VENOM-clean (**14.0460** cm/s) and competitive with RL Experts (**10.0030** cm/s).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - GPT-based transformer architectures demonstrate superior performance in generalizing full-body motion tracking across multiple humanoid embodiments compared to MLP baselines, which can suffer from negative transfer.
> - Incorporating noisy demonstrations during training significantly improves the robustness and overall motion tracking capability of the policy by exposing it to underexplored observation regions.
> - Supervised learning, through distillation from expert data, can achieve joint tracking accuracy comparable to RL-trained experts, highlighting its potential for complex control tasks despite lacking direct reward feedback or privileged information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper, VENOM, is situated within the emerging paradigm of generalizable cross-embodiment learning in robotics, particularly focusing on humanoid robots. The broader research landscape is characterized by efforts to develop robot generalist policies capable of generalizing across multiple modalities, embodiments, and tasks.

Existing research has explored robot-agnostic models, such as URMA and URMA v2, which demonstrate generalization capabilities and adherence to scaling laws with increased robot embodiments. Models like XHugWBC, EAGLE, and LocoGPT have shown promise in controlling humanoids for locomotion-based tasks, and some extend slightly beyond pure locomotion. However, these models often exhibit limitations in expressing a wide range of behaviors and motions due to the inherent challenges of motion retargeting across diverse humanoids. Many cross-embodiment policies achieve generalization by decoupling the control problem into separate upper and lower body control components, which can restrict the expressiveness of full-body movements.

In the domain of robot generalist policies, foundation models like Meta Motivo and BFM-Zero have been developed, typically trained on single humanoids using unsupervised reinforcement learning for tasks such as motion tracking, goal reaching, and reward optimization. Octo is another example, trained on the Open X-Embodiment dataset for flexible fine-tuning across different observation and action spaces in robot manipulation. While Unitree’s G1 humanoid is often a focus, a significant gap exists in large-scale low-level control data for other humanoid platforms, hindering the development of versatile foundation models for these robots.

Humanoid teleoperation has also seen advancements, with early works demonstrating control on platforms like Unitree H1– through large-scale imitation learning, often involving student-teacher training and decoupled body control. This evolved into full-body motion tracking on Unitree G1,–. The TWIST2 framework is a notable example in this area for its training pipeline.

Publicly available motion capture datasets like AMASS, BABEL, and OMOMO are widely used for imitation learning in humanoids. However, there is a limited number of datasets that specifically provide low-level control data for humanoids, such as MoCapAct and LocoMuJoCo. The generation of such datasets often requires dedicated controllers for each motion and humanoid, leading to high computational burdens.

VENOM positions itself to address these gaps by proposing a GPT-based multi-humanoid full-body motion tracker. Unlike many prior approaches, VENOM aims to track the entire body without requiring a split into upper and lower body control, enabling a wider range of expressive behaviors. It also contributes a novel multi-humanoid motion tracking dataset, the VENOM dataset, specifically curated to facilitate cross-embodiment full-body motion tracking. This work represents an effort towards generalizing low-level full-body motion tracking across multiple humanoids as a single, unified model, serving as a starting point for more generalized humanoid foundation models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the ongoing challenges in achieving expert-level expressive full-body motion tracking across multiple humanoid robots solely from demonstration data. Existing cross-embodiment motion tracking policies often simplify the control problem by decoupling upper and lower body control, or they are limited to locomotion-based tasks, thereby restricting the range and expressiveness of behaviors they can generate. Furthermore, there is a recognized scarcity of large-scale, low-level control data for diverse humanoid platforms, which is crucial for training generalist robot policies. The authors seek to overcome these limitations by developing a unified approach to full-body control across varying humanoid embodiments.

The key objectives of this work are explicitly outlined as follows:
1. **Dataset Curation:** To curate the "VENOM dataset," a multi-humanoid dataset comprising states, actions, and rewards collected in simulation specifically for motion tracking tasks. This addresses the lack of comprehensive low-level control data for diverse humanoids.
2. **Model Development and Demonstration:** To propose and demonstrate VENOM, a GPT-based model, capable of displaying full-body motion tracking across multiple humanoids and a variety of behaviors as a single, unified policy. This aims to eliminate the need for decoupled upper and lower body control and enable generalization across embodiments.
3. **Performance Evaluation:** To evaluate the motion tracking capabilities of VENOM against baseline models, specifically MLP architectures, and expert policies. The objective is to demonstrate that VENOM outperforms MLP baselines trained on multiple humanoid data in terms of joint motion tracking and root stability across different behavior categories.
4. **Quantification of Supervised Learning Performance:** To quantify how closely supervised learning, as employed in VENOM, can match the tracking capabilities of experts that were trained using asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning, thereby assessing the potential of imitation learning in this domain.

The central hypothesis guiding this research is that a GPT-based full-body multi-humanoid motion tracker, trained on diverse and potentially noisy humanoid motion data using supervised learning, can achieve superior motion tracking performance across various embodiments and motions compared to models trained with less diverse data (e.g., fewer embodiments, no noise, or restricted motion types). This work aims to establish a successful paradigm for foundation model training in the context of full-body humanoid motion tracking.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for VENOM involves a two-stage pipeline: first, the curation of a large-scale multi-humanoid dataset, and second, the training of a GPT-based transformer model on this dataset for full-body motion tracking.

**A. Dataset Curation (VENOM dataset):**
The VENOM dataset was curated using a pipeline similar to the MoCapAct dataset. It involved five distinct humanoid robots: Unitree G1, Unitree H1, Unitree H1 v2, Fourier N1, and Booster T1 with 29 degrees of freedom.
1. **Expert Training:** For each humanoid, expert motion trackers were trained across the entire AMASS dataset using the TWIST2’s reinforcement learning (RL) training pipeline in the IsaacGym simulator. These experts served as the source of demonstration data.
2. **Rollout Generation:** From these trained experts, successful trajectories were collected. For each motion in the AMASS dataset, one "clean" rollout and eight "noisy" rollouts were generated. Noise was intentionally introduced into the reference motion observations to enhance policy robustness. The noise ranges included 0.01 to 0.05 meters for root position, 0.1 to 0.2 radians for root orientation, 0.05 to 0.1 meters per second for root velocity, and 0.05 to 0.1 radians for joint position.
3. **Data Segmentation:** All collected rollouts, consisting of observations, actions, and rewards, were segmented into 6-second clips.
4. **Dataset Scale:** The VENOM dataset totals approximately 7705 hours of data, with 6156 hours for training and 1549 hours for validation. A specific note was made regarding Unitree H1 v2, where only 50 hours of training data and 16 hours of validation data were retained due to lower success rates of its expert in noisy conditions.

**B. Model Architecture (VENOM):**
VENOM builds upon the architectural design of LocoGPT and the autoregressive GPT training pipeline from MoCapAct, while incorporating elements from TWIST2.
1. **Core Controller:** The central component is a transformer decoder architecture, similar to a Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) model. It consists of eight transformer layers, each with eight attention heads, and an embedding dimension of 768.
2. **Input/Output Modules:** The model features multiple, humanoid-specific input embeddings and action heads. Each humanoid has its dedicated input embedding layer and output action head, while sharing the central transformer block.
 - **Input Embedding:** Token embedding layers (feedforward) process observation data `[B, T, O]` into token embeddings `[B, T, D]`, which are then added to learned position embeddings.
 - **Action Heads:** Feedforward linear layers at the output, corresponding to each humanoid, generate actions.
3. **Encoders:** To support full-body motion tracking, VENOM incorporates motion, history, and future encoders, inspired by TWIST2, to process reference motions into compact latent representations:
 - **Motion Encoder:** Encodes one step of the reference motion. It consists of a linear layer projecting input into a 60-dimensional space, flattened, and then passed through a final layer to produce a 128-dimensional latent representation.
 - **History Encoder:** Encodes ten steps of reference motion history. It uses a linear layer, followed by two 1-D convolutional layers (kernel sizes (4,2) and (2,1)), flattened, and passed through a final layer for a 128-dimensional latent representation.
 - **Future Encoder:** Encodes one step of future reference motion. It comprises a 3-layered MLP with hidden dimensions 256 and 128. Dropout regularization is applied between hidden layers.

**C. Training Procedure:**
1. **Learning Paradigm:** VENOM is trained using supervised learning, minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between predicted and expert actions.
2. **Optimization:** The model, along with individual motion tracking experts and TWIST2 baselines, was trained for 2 million steps using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1x10^-4.
3. **Input Context:** Context lengths of 50 steps (1 second) are used as motion prompts. Each prompt is a sequence of humanoid state-observations. During training, the input to the policy is updated using a sliding-window mechanism, where the oldest state is popped, and a new one is appended.
4. **Action Generation:** The action heads output a sequence of actions, from which the actions of the last step are taken and sent to the robot for control as PD targets.
5. **Data Preprocessing:** Observations are normalized using running estimates of feature-wise mean and standard deviation, and values are clipped for numerical stability. Actions are clipped between -10 and 10.
6. **Simulation Environment:** The IsaacGym simulator was used for both dataset generation and model evaluation.

**D. Baselines for Comparison:**
To evaluate VENOM's performance, several models were trained:
- **VENOM-noisy:** VENOM trained on the full dataset, including noisy rollouts.
- **VENOM-clean:** VENOM trained on the clean rollouts only.
- **VENOM-clean-reduced:** VENOM trained on a quarter of the clean dataset.
- **VENOM-noisy-G1:** VENOM trained on noisy data but only for Unitree G1 (for architectural comparison).
- **TWIST2-clean-G1:** An MLP model (architecture from TWIST2) trained on clean data for Unitree G1.
- **TWIST2-clean:** An MLP model (architecture from TWIST2) trained on clean data from all humanoids.
- **Experts:** The original RL-trained experts (from which the dataset was collected) are used as a benchmark for comparison against supervised learning approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VENOM and its baselines focused on their ability to perform full-body motion tracking across multiple humanoids, assessed through joint tracking accuracy and motion tracking stability.

**A. Scaling Across Bodies and Noise:**
The research indicated performance improvements based on three factors: architectural changes (MLP vs. GPT), increasing the number of robots in training data, and introducing noisy data.
- **Architectural Advantage:** VENOM models (GPT-based) generally outperformed TWIST2-clean (MLP-based) when trained on multi-robot data, suggesting the transformer architecture is more effective at handling diverse embodiments.
- **Data Diversity:** Increasing the number of robots in the training data was observed to enhance motion tracking capabilities (e.g., VENOM-clean outperformed VENOM-noisy-G1).
- **Robustness from Noise:** VENOM-noisy, trained with noisy rollouts, demonstrated the best motion tracking capability among all supervised models. This suggests that exposure to underexplored observation regions via noisy data contributes to improved robustness in the policy.
- **Negative Transfer:** The MLP-based TWIST2-clean model, when trained on data from multiple robots, showed a decline in motion tracking capability and struggled with robot stabilization. This indicated negative transfer, where training on diverse robots with a simpler architecture could degrade performance, especially when Unitree G1 data dominated.

**B. Motion Tracking Performance:**
Models were evaluated on Unitree G1 using 10 random motions from the VENOM dataset to assess joint tracking accuracy (Root Mean Square Tracking Error in radians) as shown in Table IV.
- VENOM models (VENOM-noisy: 0.1052 rad, VENOM-clean: 0.1059 rad, VENOM-clean-reduced: 0.1181 rad) showed comparable or better joint tracking performance compared to TWIST2-clean-G1 (0.1067 rad) and significantly outperformed TWIST2-clean (0.1686 rad).
- The overall low RMSE values for VENOM models suggest effective joint tracking.

Further, a comprehensive evaluation (Table V) across different behavior categories (athletic, crouching, dancing, hopping, locomotion) for all five humanoids measured both Joint Tracking Accuracy (RMSE) and Motion Tracking Stability (95th percentile of Absolute Root Error Rate). The success rate (percentage of motions completed) was also reported.
- **Joint Tracking Accuracy (RMSE):** VENOM-noisy generally exhibited slightly better joint tracking accuracy compared to other VENOM variants and was comparable to the RL Experts across different humanoids and behaviors. The RMSE values ranged from approximately 0.42 to 0.58 radians for VENOM-noisy across humanoids.
- **Motion Tracking Stability (95th percentile Absolute Root Error Rate):** VENOM-noisy showed lower average absolute root error rates and standard deviations in many cases compared to other supervised models, indicating better stability in root position tracking. For example, for Unitree G1, VENOM-noisy had a 95th percentile absolute root error rate of 12.3560 cm/s, compared to 14.0460 cm/s for VENOM-clean.
- **Comparison to RL Experts:** While VENOM-noisy showed strong performance among supervised models, the RL Experts consistently achieved higher motion stability and 100% success rates across all humanoids and behavior categories (e.g., Expert Unitree G1 95th percentile absolute root error rate: 10.0030 cm/s). This suggested that while supervised learning could achieve comparable joint tracking, there might be a trade-off with motion stability when directly compared to policies trained with reward feedback.
- **Dynamic Motions:** All models, including the RL experts, exhibited difficulties in consistently tracking highly dynamic motions or those involving flight phases (e.g., jumping), often leading to behaviors that prioritize maintaining ground contact for stability.
- **Supervised Learning vs. RL Experts:** Despite being trained via supervised learning and lacking access to privileged information available to the RL experts during their training, VENOM achieved comparable performance, particularly in joint tracking accuracy. This highlights the effectiveness of distillation via supervised learning in recovering a significant portion of expert capability, albeit with some compromise in overall tracking stability.

**C. Motion Generation Attempt:**
An attempt to train VENOM for autoregressive motion generation (VENOM-motion-gen and VENOM-motion-gen-mask), by removing or masking tracking-related inputs, resulted in failure to stabilize the robots. This suggests that motion generation and motion control may not scale identically with embodiment diversity, and conditioning information (like joint targets) is critical for maintaining stability and preventing drift in diverse robot/behavior settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VENOM research presents several significant contributions and has potential impacts on the field of humanoid robot learning and generalist robotics:

1. **Novel Multi-Humanoid Full-Body Motion Tracking Model:** VENOM represents a novel approach as the first GPT-based multi-humanoid full-body motion tracker that does not decouple upper and lower body control. This is a critical advancement towards unified and holistic control of complex humanoid movements, enabling a wider variety of expressive motions beyond typical locomotion-focused or segmented control schemes.
2. **Contribution of a Large-Scale Multi-Embodiment Dataset:** The curation of the VENOM dataset addresses a significant gap in the availability of large-scale, low-level control data for diverse humanoid platforms. By generating this dataset efficiently from a small number of embodiment-specific RL experts, the authors provide a valuable resource for future research in multi-embodiment learning and reduce the computational burden typically associated with such data collection.
3. **Advancement in Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** The work demonstrates the capability of a transformer-based architecture (GPT) to generalize full-body motion tracking across different humanoid embodiments. This provides empirical evidence for the effectiveness of such models in managing varying observation and action spaces inherent in cross-embodiment tasks.
4. **Insights into Supervised Learning vs. Reinforcement Learning:** The detailed comparison between VENOM (supervised learning) and RL-trained experts offers valuable insights. It quantifies that supervised learning, even without reward feedback and privileged information, can achieve joint tracking accuracy comparable to RL experts. This highlights the potential of imitation learning for complex control tasks while also revealing the inherent trade-offs, particularly in achieving the highest levels of motion stability that RL can provide. This understanding can guide future research in choosing appropriate learning paradigms.
5. **Foundation for Robot Generalist Policies:** VENOM serves as a foundational step towards developing more generalized, low-level full-body motion tracking policies for humanoids. It lays the groundwork for exploring "foundation models" in robotics that can leverage large datasets and powerful architectures to generalize across a broad spectrum of robot morphologies and tasks.
6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines several avenues for future work, including the need for prediction models that better capture long-term dependencies in dynamic motions, the potential benefits of direct reinforcement learning for motion tracking, scaling to arbitrary humanoid embodiments (robot-agnostic design), and integrating online retargeting policies to further simplify data curation. These identified directions collectively point towards a comprehensive research roadmap for advancing humanoid control.
7. **Practical Implications:** A versatile motion tracker like VENOM could have practical implications in various domains, such as teleoperation of diverse humanoid fleets, developing more realistic digital avatars, and facilitating humanoid robot deployment in real-world scenarios requiring complex and expressive behaviors.
