# Force Policy: Learning Hybrid Force-Position Control Policy under Interaction Frame for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A "Force Policy" framework improves robot manipulation in contact-rich tasks by combining a global-local vision-force control architecture with a physically grounded interaction frame. This approach leads to more precise force regulation and robust generalization across various tasks, achieving a 90% success rate on "Scrape off Sticker" compared to 50% with an alternative method.

## Problem

- Existing learning-based policies for contact-rich manipulation often use monolithic network designs, creating a trade-off between global generalization and stable local, high-frequency force control.
- Many control approaches implicitly assume or pre-define task interaction structures, limiting the transferability and interpretability of learned contact skills across diverse environments.
- Current robotic policies struggle to replicate human dexterity, where vision guides global movement while high-frequency force feedback refines local contact under uncertainty.

## Method

- **Force Policy** formalizes a physically grounded ==interaction frame (IF)== from environmental stiffness and task intent, with an adaptive strategy to recover it from observed interaction signals by classifying dominant power sources.
- Developing a ==global-local vision-force policy== that decouples a low-frequency Global Vision Policy (for high-level guidance) from a high-frequency Local Force Policy (for precise local interaction) via a dynamic router.
- Implementing a dual-policy asynchronous scheduler with ==Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)== for chunk alignment to ensure smooth, acceleration-continuous trajectory planning and reduce motion/force jerks during execution.

## Results

- Force Policy consistently outperformed vision-only and force-aware baselines across various contact-rich tasks, demonstrating superior force control precision and tracking human demonstration force profiles more accurately.
- The framework exhibited robust generalization to novel objects with varying geometries and stiffnesses, effectively combining broad visual generalization with precise local contact execution.
- The adaptive IF recovery achieved a **90%** success rate on "Scrape off Sticker" compared to **50%** for a wrench-only labeled baseline, and the asynchronous scheduler significantly reduced motion and force jerks, improving trajectory smoothness and physical robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling global, vision-driven decision-making from high-frequency local, force-governed interaction resolves the trade-off in monolithic policy designs, improving both generalization and stability.
> - Explicitly representing and recovering a physically grounded "interaction frame" offers an interpretable and transferable foundation for learning diverse contact skills, robust to unstructured environments.
> - An adaptive IF recovery method that leverages semantic reasoning to classify dominant power sources (e.g., structural stiffness vs. friction) enables more accurate and robust frame inference from real-world demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses challenges in contact-rich robotic manipulation, a domain that requires robots to interact physically with their environment. Historically, this field has relied on classical compliant control frameworks such as impedance control, admittance control, and hybrid force-position control. These methods, while principled, often necessitate manual tuning of parameters and accurate environmental models, which can limit their robustness in unstructured or uncertain settings.

The advent of learning-based methods, particularly imitation learning, has sought to overcome these limitations by enabling robots to acquire interaction strategies directly from demonstrations. Many contemporary learning-based policies integrate force sensing by treating force signals as auxiliary observations or supervision. Examples include methods that dynamically weigh visual and force information, couple visual policies with force-aware tokenizers for reactive execution, or integrate force via mixture-of-experts architectures. However, a common characteristic of many such approaches is a monolithic network design that intertwines perception, planning, and contact refinement. This entanglement can lead to a trade-off between global generalization capabilities (often vision-driven) and the stability and reactivity required for local, high-frequency force control. Some existing works learn controller parameters (e.g., admittance gains or stiffness matrices) but often assume or implicitly define the underlying interaction structure, rather than learning to represent it explicitly.

The problem of inferring control structures from demonstration data has also seen evolution, from analytical geometric modeling (e.g., Task Frame Formalization, TFF) to data-driven learning approaches. TFF provides a canonical force-motion subspace decomposition but typically assumes explicit geometry and rigid contact. Data-driven methods have explored inferring geometric constraints, contact force directions, or task-aligned frames, but often face limitations in physical interpretability or in capturing the true task structure under non-ideal conditions.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by seeking to bridge the gap between the generalization capabilities of vision-based policies and the precision of force control. It explicitly tackles the entanglement issue by proposing a global-local architecture inspired by human control mechanisms. Furthermore, it addresses the implicit nature of interaction structures by formalizing a physically grounded "interaction frame" and developing a method to recover it from demonstrations, thereby enabling the explicit representation and transfer of contact skills.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from observing human dexterity in contact-rich manipulation tasks. Humans exhibit a "global-local organization" where vision primarily guides global movement and coarse alignment, while high-frequency force feedback is utilized for local refinement and stable contact under uncertainty. Current learning-based robotic policies frequently fall short of this human capability due to two primary issues:

1. **Entangled Policy Design:** Existing learning-based policies often combine perception, planning, and contact refinement into a single, monolithic network. This design creates an inherent trade-off. Learning global generalization from visual cues and stable local interaction dynamics simultaneously within one network makes it difficult to achieve both effectively. High-frequency local control is critical for stability during contact, but scaling up models for better global generalization can interfere with this dedicated reactive loop. Force signals, when simply appended to observations, can be noisy or uninformative during free-space motion, potentially degrading global decision-making. Policies like RDP introduce a slow-fast design but maintain a dependency where the slow policy relies on the fast reactive component, limiting modularity and reusability.

2. **Implicit or Assumed Interaction Structure:** Many control-centric approaches, whether classical or learning-based, tend to assume a known task structure or learn only the parameters of a controller (e.g., admittance gains, contact forces) rather than explicitly representing the interaction structure itself. When the interaction structure is merely implicit or assumed, it becomes difficult to transfer knowledge across diverse contact skills or to adapt to variations in contact geometry and local stiffness. This limits the interpretability and generalizability of the learned behaviors. For instance, while hybrid position/force control offers an interpretable interface by separating constrained and free directions, its application often still requires a pre-defined interaction structure.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to answer two core questions:
1. **Global-local organization:** How can a robotic policy effectively realize a global-local organization, ensuring that global guidance generalizes well while local interaction remains stable and reactive?
2. **Interaction structure for control:** How can the task interaction structure be explicitly represented in a manner that allows it to transfer across diverse contact skills and is robust to unstructured environments?

The overarching objective is to develop a robotic manipulation policy that integrates vision and force feedback in a human-inspired global-local manner, explicitly models interaction structure, and demonstrates improved robustness, precise force regulation, and generalization across varied contact-rich tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed "Force Policy" addresses the identified challenges through a three-pronged approach: formalizing a physically grounded interaction frame, developing a global-local vision-force policy, and implementing a dual-policy asynchronous scheduler.

#### 3.1. A. Control Structure of Contact-Rich Tasks

1. **Interaction Frame (IF) Identification:**
 - **Physical Perspective:** The paper reformulates the interaction frame (IF) from a purely physical response, using environmental stiffness (K_env) as a proxy for local geometry. It assumes locally conservative contacts where resistance arises from physical boundaries, implying that principal stiffness axes align with local surface geometry. The IF is defined by spectrally decomposing K_env into principal stiffnesses and axes, partitioning the interaction space into a constraint subspace (U, high stiffness) and an admissible-motion subspace (T, negligible stiffness).
 - **Task Intent:** To resolve directional ambiguity and anchor the IF, "task intent" is introduced, comprising the intended motion twist (ξ\*) and interaction wrench (W\*). This intent is compatible with the physical structure (W\* ∈ U and ξ\* ∈ T). The z-axis of the IF aligns with the dominant wrench component of W\*, and the x-axis is derived by projecting ξ\* onto the plane orthogonal to the z-axis, with a fallback to a canonical reference in degenerate cases.
 - **Recovery from Interaction Signals:** For real-world demonstrations, the observed twist (ξ) and wrench (W) may not perfectly align with ideal intent due to parasitic components (Wc from ξ\*, ξc from W\*). The paper proposes an adaptive approximation strategy:
 - Leverage high-level semantic reasoning (using Gemini 3 Pro with initial visual context and task description) to classify the dominant power source (dissipative residual, e.g., friction, or structural residual, e.g., stiffness/constraints).
 - If structural residual dominates, the intended twist (ξ\*) is orthogonalized against the observed wrench (W).
 - If dissipative residual dominates, the intended wrench (W\*) is orthogonalized against the observed twist (ξ). This approach aims to provide a physically interpretable and robust IF recovery.

2. **Task Classification and Control Signal Generation:**
 - Based on the IF and its aligned signal characteristics (twist and wrench), interaction patches are classified into four canonical task modes: "Free" (negligible wrench), "Surface" (dominant normal constraint along z-axis), "Insertion" (strong force anisotropy along x-axis), and "Rotation" (rotation-dominated along z-axis with normal force).
 - These modes directly map to a hybrid control structure through a 6-DoF binary selection mask (S), where S_i=1 indicates force control and S_i=0 indicates position control along the IF-aligned axes. A reference wrench (W_ref) is also generated, specifying desired forces/torques in the force-controlled directions.

#### 3.2. B. Force Policy: A Global-Local Vision-Force Policy

The overall policy architecture is designed with a global-local decomposition:

1. **Global Vision Policy (Π_global):**
 - Operates at a low frequency (e.g., 5Hz).
 - Responsible for global perception (e.g., using 3D visual input) and high-level task progression, especially in free space.
 - Does not directly command actions during contact but provides a global visual feature (φ(I_t')) to condition the local policy.
 - Instantiated using an existing generalizable visuomotor policy (RISE-2).

2. **Local Force Policy (Π_local):**
 - Operates at a high frequency (e.g., 50Hz).
 - Handles local interaction dynamics during contact.
 - **Inputs:** Wrist-mounted camera image, history of end-effector pose and wrench feedback, and the global visual feature from Π_global.
 - **Architecture:**
 - **Multi-modal Encoder:** A lightweight ResNet-18 for wrist images (conditioned by φ(I_t') via FiLM), and a GRU for proprioceptive history (also FiLM-conditioned).
 - **Adaptive Gated Fusion:** A tri-modal adaptive gating mechanism dynamically weighs visual, proprioceptive, and global context features to create a fused embedding.
 - **Dual-Head Decoder:**
 - **Interaction Structure Head:** MLPs predict the IF pose (Σ), the reference wrench (W_ref), and the binary selection mask (S).
 - **Action Head:** A MIP head generates a chunk of future relative actions.
 - **Output:** The predicted local action (a_local) explicitly parameterizes force-based interaction by specifying the IF, selection mask, reference wrench, and desired motion chunk.

3. **Router:** An implicit router uses the predicted selection mask (S) from Π_local. If Π_local activates any force-control axes (non-zero entries in S), control authority switches from Π_global to Π_local for hybrid force-position regulation. Otherwise, control remains with Π_global.

#### 3.3. C. Dual-Policy Asynchronous Scheduler

This component manages the deployment of the dual-frequency policies to ensure smooth and stable execution:

1. **Asynchronous Inference and Execution:** Policies run asynchronously, overlapping inference with trajectory execution. Outputs from both policies are interpolated to a unified frequency (50Hz).
2. **Chunk Alignment with Dynamic Time Warping (DTW):** To prevent discontinuous jumps between action chunks due to inference latency, a model-agnostic waypoint dropout strategy based on DTW is employed. DTW aligns the predicted trajectory with the recent execution history, identifying an optimal entry index and dropping preceding waypoints. This ensures smooth, acceleration-continuous trajectory planning, which is crucial for reducing jerk and improving force-sensing fidelity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation sought to validate the effectiveness, force control precision, generalization, IF recovery accuracy, and scheduler benefits of Force Policy.

- **Effectiveness Across Diverse Tasks (Q1):** Force Policy consistently outperformed both vision-only (RISE-2, π0.5) and force-aware baselines (RDP, FoAR, ForceVLA, TA-VLA) across a suite of contact-rich tasks (Push and Flip, Plug in EV Charger, Scrape off Sticker). The largest improvements were observed in phases requiring precise and rapid force regulation, such as the insertion stage in "Plug in EV Charger" and the scraping stage in "Scrape off Sticker". The paper attributes this to Force Policy's decoupled global-local design, which insulates high-level planning from noisy force signals while enabling responsive, stable interaction via the high-frequency local force policy. Baselines often failed due to monolithic designs entangling force with global perception, low-frequency reactivity, or hierarchical coupling that could lead to erroneous actions from out-of-distribution force signals.

- **Superior Force Control (Q2):** Force Policy demonstrated more accurate force control, closely tracking the force profiles of human demonstrations. In tasks like "Scrape off Sticker (Hard)," Force Policy maintained necessary effective force magnitudes without oscillations or insufficient/excessive forces, which were common failure modes for baselines. This advantage is attributed to the explicit hybrid force-position control framework, which directly regulates interaction forces. For instance, in the "Push and Flip" task, Force Policy achieved zero pushed distance, indicating precise force regulation, while several baselines caused the heavy object to be pushed due to excessive force.

- **Generalization Ability (Q3):** Force Policy exhibited generalization to novel objects with varying colors, geometries, and stiffnesses in the "Push and Flip" task. While the vision-only backbone (RISE-2) sometimes failed on novel objects due to contact transition issues, Force Policy's combined approach reliably guided the end-effector to the target vicinity (inheriting visual generalization) and then used the local force policy to robustly establish and execute contact. This decoupling allowed Force Policy to combine broad visual generalization with local precision.

- **IF Recovery Accuracy and Interaction Quality (Q4):**
 - The proposed adaptive IF recovery method yielded more accurate interaction frames compared to analytic, power-based, wrench-only, and twist-only approaches. This was particularly evident in tasks with dominant dissipative residuals (e.g., friction during scraping), where wrench-only methods tended to recover tilted frames due to parasitic friction forces. Force Policy trained with our IF recovery strategy achieved a 90% success rate on "Scrape off Sticker," compared to 50% with wrench-only labeled IFs.
 - The high-level semantic classification for power source (using Gemini 3 Pro) showed high accuracy (100% for Push and Flip, 92% for Plug in EV Charger, 98% for Scrape off Sticker), and the policy was found to be robust to infrequent misclassifications due to the adaptive nature of the local policy.

- **Asynchronous Scheduler Benefits (Q5):** The dual-policy asynchronous scheduler significantly reduced both motion and force jerks during deployment, resulting in smoother executed trajectories (quantified by spectral arc length, SPARC). The DTW-based chunk alignment mechanism effectively attenuated signal discontinuities caused by inference latency. Supplementary experiments also indicated that this scheduler improved the stability and success rates of vision-only policies (e.g., increasing RISE-2's flip success rate from 42.5% to 62.5%), highlighting its potential as a plug-and-play module for enhancing physical robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Force Policy" framework presents a contribution to robotic manipulation research, particularly in the domain of contact-rich tasks.

- **Principled Global-Local Control Hierarchy:** The work provides a physically motivated and empirically validated architecture that decouples global, vision-driven decision-making from local, high-frequency force-governed interaction. This design addresses a fundamental trade-off in learning-based policies, demonstrating that assigning modalities to their most informative scales (vision for progress, force for stability) can lead to improved performance. This modularity could enable the integration of future advancements in vision-only policies for global guidance and specialized force controllers for local interaction.

- **Physically Grounded Interaction Frame:** The formalization and practical recovery method for the "interaction frame" offer a novel way to explicitly represent the control structure of contact-rich tasks. By anchoring spectral axes of environmental stiffness to task intent and adaptively inferring the dominant power source, the method moves beyond reliance on known geometries or simple force vector alignment. This physically grounded approach can enhance interpretability and provide a more robust basis for learning and transferring contact skills across diverse, unstructured environments.

- **Enhanced Robustness, Precision, and Generalization:** The empirical results consistently demonstrate improved robustness, more precise force regulation, and better generalization to novel objects with varying physical properties compared to several strong baselines. This indicates that the proposed architectural decomposition, combined with the explicit handling of interaction forces within a hybrid control framework, leads to more reliable and adaptable robotic behaviors in physical contact scenarios.

- **Practical Deployment with Asynchronous Scheduling:** The dual-policy asynchronous scheduler is designed to mitigate practical challenges in deploying complex learning policies, such as inference latency and motion discontinuities. Its model-agnostic, DTW-based chunk alignment strategy contributes to smoother trajectories and improved force-sensing fidelity, making the learned policies more suitable for real-world robotic systems where stable physical interaction is paramount.

The potential impact of this work includes advancing the capabilities of robots in applications requiring delicate or forceful contact, such as complex assembly, surface finishing, and human-robot collaboration in unstructured environments. While the paper acknowledges limitations regarding non-smooth contact scenarios, explicit torque control, and force-driven high-level decision-making, the proposed framework lays a foundation for future research to build upon, bridging the gap between high-level task understanding and low-level physical interaction control.
