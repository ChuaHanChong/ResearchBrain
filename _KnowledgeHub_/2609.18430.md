# StrucPhysVideo: Learning Physical Dynamics from Structured Captions and Robot Actions

> [!summary] Summary
> StrucPhysVideo is a 30B sparse-MoE video world model trained on curated physics clips with structured captions separating camera motion, materials, and timestamped entity interactions. Its TI2V variant scores 45.5% on Physics-IQ Verified, ranking first of 17 compared models. An IA2V extension adds a causal action encoder with per-block AdaLN cross-attention, then distills into a four-step causal autoregressive generator that beats DreamDojo on AgiBot rollouts.

## Problem

- Uniform clip splitting and blanket filters in web-video curation cut interactions mid-event, splice edit transitions, and discard clips whose text belongs to physical scene objects.
- Generic video captions like 'a robot handles a cup' omit which object, whether pushed or lifted, the temporal order of stages, and conflate camera motion with object motion.
- Language-conditioned video models leave execution ambiguous, since sliding and lifting reach the same goal, and bidirectional video diffusion cannot stream action-conditioned rollouts at interactive latency.

## Method

- Physical data pipeline: ==shot-boundary detection== plus ==motion-aware fixed-window selection==, quality and ==content-purity filters== with VLM text review, then Qwen3.6-27B physics verification producing four-group ==structured captions== and ==closed-set phenomenon tags==.
- **StrucPhysVideo-TI2V**: 30B ==sparse-MoE Transformer== initialized from LingBot-Video, frozen Qwen3-VL-32B joint text-image conditioning, ==first-frame-constrained flow matching==, and a curriculum shifting general-to-physics sampling from 70/30 to 20/80.
- **StrucPhysVideo-IA2V**: ==causal action encoder== over 20-D dual-arm end-effector poses feeds zero-gated per-block ==AdaLN cross-attention==; ==Causal Forcing== autoregressive training, ==causal ODE initialization==, and ==asymmetric DMD== yield a four-step streaming student.

## Results

- Physics-IQ Verified: **45.5%**, first of 17 models, **2.8** points above Cosmos3-Super Image2Video (**42.7%**) and **9.3** above best closed-source MiniMax H3 Max (**36.2%**).
- Caption ablation: physics-focused captions beat raw captions on both backbones, Wan2.2-5B reaching **33.2%** versus **31.0%** raw and **24.8%** baseline, LingBot-Video-30B **45.5%** versus **43.9%** and **37.9%**.
- On 5K AgiBot clips, the four-step IA2V student beats DreamDojo-AgiBot on every metric: PSNR **22.12** vs **20.86**, trajectory accuracy **0.833** vs **0.791**; teacher action CFG scale 2 lifts trajectory accuracy from **0.790** to **0.827**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Data curation carries most of the gain: pipeline-curated clips with raw captions add about six points, while structured physics captions add a smaller but consistent increment.
> - Guidance can be baked into a few-step causal student: training the teacher with condition dropout and distilling action-CFG trajectories strengthens action following without inference-time CFG cost.
> - Physical-plausibility benchmarks remain pixel-agreement proxies: Physics-IQ scores IoU and MSE, so higher scores show better-matching motion, not verified physical understanding or downstream policy utility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

StrucPhysVideo addresses a central problem in embodied artificial intelligence: enabling models to predict how a visual scene changes over time, particularly after physical interactions or robot actions. This problem lies at the intersection of video generation, world modeling, computer vision, robotics, and physical reasoning.

Most recent video world models generate future frames from an initial image, a text prompt, or both. Although these models can produce visually coherent videos, visual plausibility does not necessarily imply correct physical dynamics. A generated object may change position without respecting contact, gravity, support relationships, deformation, or conservation of motion. The paper therefore focuses on physical dynamics as a distinct modeling target rather than treating it as an incidental consequence of general video generation.

The work builds on several research directions. First, it uses large-scale latent video generation with Transformer architectures and flow matching, following recent diffusion and rectified-flow approaches. Second, it adopts sparse Mixture-of-Experts modeling to increase capacity while limiting per-token computation. Third, it extends image- and text-conditioned video prediction toward action-conditioned prediction, a requirement for robot world models. Finally, it uses structured language supervision to represent objects, materials, temporal actions, and camera motion more explicitly than conventional captions.

The paper is also situated within emerging benchmark efforts such as Physics-IQ Verified, which evaluates whether generated videos reproduce the timing, location, magnitude, and appearance of physical events. Its robot experiments are compared with systems including DreamDojo-AgiBot and A2World. Thus, the contribution is not a new physical simulator or an explicit symbolic physics engine; it is a data and modeling framework for improving learned visual prediction of observable physical events.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to develop video world models that better represent physical interactions and can condition future scene evolution on both language and robot actions.

The authors identify several weaknesses in existing training data. Online videos frequently contain cuts that interrupt an interaction, transitions that connect incompatible scene states, persistent blur, camera shake, overlays, and irrelevant presenter content. Uniformly splitting videos can separate the approach, contact, and post-contact phases of an interaction. These problems are especially consequential for physical learning because the model needs to observe a continuous sequence of states.

The paper also argues that ordinary captions are insufficiently specific. A caption such as “a robot handles a cup” does not identify the relevant cup, distinguish pushing from lifting, or describe the order of approaching, grasping, and placing. In multi-object scenes, object identity and temporal localization are necessary for grounding instructions and for learning state transitions.

A further motivation is that the same final state can result from different physical executions. A cup moved to another side of a table might have been slid, lifted, carried, or dropped. These trajectories differ in contact patterns and intermediate states. Robot action sequences can provide an additional constraint, allowing the model to distinguish between visually similar outcomes that arise from different commands.

The project therefore pursues three connected objectives:

1. **Curate videos containing observable and temporally continuous physical events.**
2. **Represent those events with structured captions and physical taxonomy labels.**
3. **Generate future video conditioned on initial visual observations, language, and robot actions.**

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Physical data curation and annotation

The data pipeline begins with videos collected through agentic retrieval and domain-specific providers, including physical-interaction and robotics footage. Queries are expanded using a taxonomy covering mechanics, fluid dynamics, thermal dynamics, waves and optics, material deformation, and manipulation tasks.

The curation process has three principal stages. **Spatiotemporal Video Splitting** first detects shot boundaries using PySceneDetect and TransNetV2. Candidate clips are selected within individual shots using optical-flow-based motion estimates. The method favors low motion at clip boundaries, which reduces the likelihood of cutting through an interaction, while retaining substantial motion inside the clip.

**Technical Quality Filtering** checks decodability, resolution, duration, exposure, black or solid-color frames, blur, frame repetition, and camera shake. Optical flow, Laplacian variance, Lucas–Kanade tracking, and RANSAC are used to identify defects. Smooth camera pans and tracking shots are retained, whereas persistent jitter and severe quality problems lead to rejection.

**Content Purity Filtering** removes presenter-dominated clips, screen recordings, watermarks, subtitles, and post-production overlays. Face detection and talking-head cues identify presentation-focused footage. CLIP-based screening detects screen content. OCR identifies text, which is then reviewed by a vision-language model. This review distinguishes text that belongs to physical objects, such as bottle labels or gauges, from overlays that obscure the scene.

For retained clips, 24 timestamped frames are sampled. Qwen3.6-27B performs physical verification and generates a structured caption. The caption contains four groups: global scene dynamics; camera motion and illumination; world knowledge and material properties; and entity-level temporal behaviors. The latter includes timestamped descriptions of contact, manipulation, motion, deformation, and state changes.

A second annotation call assigns closed-set tags. These include general video properties and physics-specific labels. The physics taxonomy contains five main categories and 26 leaf phenomena, with an additional “Other” category. Clips can receive multiple phenomenon labels and a designated primary label. Material classes include rigid solids, deformable solids, liquids, and granular materials. State-change labels include deformation, phase transition, and fragmentation. The annotation system also records interaction viewpoint and visible physical anomalies. The authors deliberately exclude quantities such as force, mass, temperature, and absolute speed when these cannot be estimated reliably from video.

#### 3.2. Text-image-to-video model

StrucPhysVideo-TI2V is a latent video generator that predicts future frames from a reference image and caption. A frozen Qwen3-VL-32B encoder jointly processes the text and reference image. A frozen Wan video VAE encodes the target video into latent space.

The trainable backbone is an approximately **30-billion-parameter Transformer with 48 blocks**. Each feed-forward layer uses a sparse Mixture-of-Experts module with routed experts and an always-active shared expert. Top-k routing limits computation for each token while increasing overall model capacity. The reference frame is retained as a clean latent throughout training and inference. Future latents are corrupted along a flow-matching path, and the model learns to predict the velocity field needed to recover them.

The reference latent is excluded from the denoising loss but remains available as context. During sampling, it is re-clamped after every solver update to prevent drift. The model uses joint self-attention between multimodal condition tokens and video tokens, three-axis rotary position embeddings, timestep-conditioned normalization, and Flow-UniPC sampling. The training curriculum mixes 10,000 hours of physics-focused video with 10,000 hours of general-domain video. The physics proportion increases over training from 30% to 80%, preserving broad video-generation capability while progressively emphasizing dynamics.

#### 3.3. Image-action-to-video model

StrucPhysVideo-IA2V extends the model to robot control. The input consists of an initial RGB observation and a trajectory of dual-arm end-effector commands. Each arm is represented by Cartesian position, a continuous six-dimensional rotation representation, and gripper target, producing a 20-dimensional combined action vector.

Video is sampled at 5 frames per second and actions at 15 Hz. Action windows are aligned with video-frame transitions. A four-layer causal Transformer encodes the action sequence, ensuring that each action representation depends only on current and previous commands. Per-video-frame action contexts are injected into the video backbone through adaptive normalization and gated cross-attention.

Training has three stages. First, action modules are added to a bidirectional video model. Second, autoregressive diffusion training introduces temporal causality and teacher-forced history. Third, causal ODE initialization and asymmetric distribution-matching distillation convert the model into a few-step generator. The final student can produce incremental rollouts using four denoising steps per temporal chunk.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Physics-IQ Verified performance

On the Physics-IQ Verified benchmark, StrucPhysVideo-TI2V achieved a score of **45.5%**. The benchmark contains 66 physical experiments spanning solid and fluid dynamics, thermodynamics, optics, and magnetism, evaluated across multiple viewpoints and repetitions.

The reported score exceeded Cosmos3-Super Image2Video by **2.8 percentage points**, which scored 42.7%. It was also higher than the strongest closed-source comparator listed in the paper, MiniMax H3 Max, by 9.3 percentage points. The comparison includes 17 models, although most results were taken from a benchmark snapshot rather than reproduced by the authors. The paper explicitly reproduced the LingBot-Video score, so cross-model comparisons should be interpreted in light of these differing evaluation sources.

Qualitative examples show improved preservation of scene structure and object identity. In one marble-run example, the model generated a ball that followed the track geometry and then continued along a plausible outgoing trajectory. Baseline models reportedly altered the track or failed to maintain the ball’s path. These examples illustrate the intended behavior but are not substitutes for the quantitative benchmark.

#### 4.2. Caption ablations

Caption ablations support the claim that structured physical supervision contributes beyond ordinary captions. On Wan2.2-5B, the Physics-IQ score increased from **24.8%** for the baseline to 31.0% with raw captions and 33.2% with physics-focused captions. On LingBot-Video-30B, the corresponding scores were 37.9%, 43.9%, and 45.5%.

Physics-focused captions therefore improved over raw captions by 2.2 percentage points for Wan2.2-5B and 1.6 points for LingBot-Video-30B. The result suggests that explicitly describing temporal behaviors, material properties, object participation, and camera motion can improve physical-dynamics prediction across different video backbones.

#### 4.3. Robot action-conditioned results

The IA2V model was trained on AgiBot World-Beta plus more than 500 hours of general-domain video and evaluated on 5,000 clips from AgiBot World-Alpha. Compared with DreamDojo-AgiBot, StrucPhysVideo-IA2V improved PSNR from 20.86 to 22.12, SSIM from 0.8277 to 0.8365, and reduced LPIPS from 0.1825 to 0.1602. Motion MAE decreased from 0.03509 to 0.03368.

Action and interaction metrics also improved. Trajectory accuracy increased from 0.7905 to **0.8330**, depth accuracy from 0.9736 to **0.9854**, and the interaction-quality score from 3.366 to **3.470**. Under a standardized 256-by-256, 21-frame comparison, the model again exceeded DreamDojo on the reported metrics. However, A2World used a different input configuration and did not report all metrics, limiting direct comparability.

Teacher-side action guidance was beneficial. An action CFG scale of 2 produced a strong balance, while larger scales were less effective. Small text dropout during training also improved IA2V results, despite text not being used at IA2V inference.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

StrucPhysVideo’s main significance is its integration of data curation, structured physical annotation, large-scale video modeling, and robot-action conditioning. The work treats physical learning as both a data-quality problem and a modeling problem. This is relevant because incorrect temporal segmentation or vague captions can limit physical reasoning even when the underlying generative architecture is capable.

The TI2V results indicate that structured captions can improve benchmarked physical prediction while maintaining general video-generation capabilities through curriculum mixing. The IA2V results provide evidence that the same framework can connect robot commands with predicted visual consequences, which may support planning, policy learning, simulation augmentation, and action evaluation.

Several limitations remain. The physical annotations are generated by vision-language models and may contain unsupported physical assumptions, even though the pipeline emphasizes visual evidence. The dataset is privately collected, and the paper does not report detailed final dataset sizes, annotation error rates, or inter-annotator agreement. Benchmark gains are demonstrated primarily through video similarity and VLM-based interaction metrics rather than real-world robot success. The IA2V model also operates over relatively short chunked rollouts and currently requires four denoising steps, which may still be costly for real-time control.

Future work identified by the authors includes longer-horizon streaming, one- or two-step generation, richer action spaces, and cross-embodiment generalization. A further challenge is connecting predicted video dynamics to policies that can safely act in the physical world. Consequently, the paper represents a step toward interactive visual world models, but additional validation is needed to establish whether improved video prediction translates into reliable embodied behavior.
