# UMR: Universal Manipulation Representation

> [!summary] Summary
> UMR is a unified action representation that splits manipulation into two coordinate views of one motion. World Flow captures object motion in the world frame, and Ego Trajectory gives end-effector motion relative to the current gripper pose. An SE(3) conjugation links the two. The resulting 0.5B WEPVLA policy reaches 97.5% on LIBERO and 85.7% on RLBench, and transfers zero-shot from human demonstrations to robots at 91.7%.

## Problem

- Existing policies act in embodiment-specific joint or end-effector spaces, and datasets use incompatible control semantics and coordinate frames, so pooling human and robot demonstrations does not broaden transferable skill.
- Human-to-robot transfer still depends on specialized handheld interfaces, motion retargeting, or embodiment-specific adaptation, which eases data collection but leaves human and robot action spaces incompatible.
- Object-flow and affordance representations are embodiment-agnostic but not aligned with the robot's control interface, so they need separate grasping, fitting, or decoding stages that block end-to-end joint learning.

## Method

- **Universal Manipulation Representation**: **World Flow** is an SE(3) object-motion trajectory with the grasp transform cancelled out, and **Ego Trajectory** is the same motion re-expressed in the current end-effector frame, linked by ==SE(3) conjugation==.
- **WEPVLA**: a point-cloud VLA built on frozen ==SmolVLA==, with a dual-stream ==Point Action Adapter==, a shared Point Action Expert, and ==coupled flow matching== that jointly denoises both streams but executes only the Ego Trajectory.
- Motion-aware **PriorSeg** distills a ==teacher== that uses temporal co-motion, approach, contact, and tool-sweep cues into a single-frame foreground segmenter, and a Data-Efficient Strategy edits object point clouds while preserving contact geometry.

## Results

- The **0**.5B WEPVLA reaches **85.7%** mean on **10** RLBench tasks against **82.3%** for PointACT, and lifts Sweep-to-dustpan from **59%** to **99%**, but it trails on Phone-on-base (**78** vs **99**).
- On LIBERO it averages **97.5%** with only a front camera, beating 3D-aware PointACT at **96.0%**. Ablations rise from **81.65%** for SmolVLA to **96.40%** with Ego Trajectory and **97.50%** with World Flow.
- Trained on about **10** minutes of human demonstrations per task and no robot data, it averages **91.7%** zero-shot across six real settings on Piper and Franka, against **60.8%** for HumanEgo.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Writing the same motion in two frames, a world-frame object view for transfer plus an ego-frame view for execution, lets one end-to-end policy learn from humans and robots together.
> - Expressing actions relative to the current end-effector pose gave the largest single ablation gain, which suggests re-anchoring in the ego frame matters more than world-frame supervision.
> - Raw point clouds alone slightly hurt performance; 3D input helps only after interaction-relevant foreground is selected and point features are fused with action tokens.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

People can demonstrate manipulation without a robot. A human hand's controls, however, do not directly tell a different robot how to move. Extra demonstrations are of limited use if incompatible embodiment-specific action spaces prevent a policy from learning from them together.

The authors give human and robot demonstrations a shared action description that pairs how the object should move with how the robot moves locally. They call this representation UMR, and their policy WEPVLA learns from it. In a real-world comparison, WEPVLA reports 91.7% average task success across six tabletop evaluation settings (I–VI), versus 60.8% for HumanEgo. Both policies were trained on about 10 minutes of human demonstrations per task, augmented by DES, with no robot demonstrations.

Success rates for HumanEgo (blue) and UMR (orange) under the paper's matched training-data setting. Left: settings I–VI. Right: Cross-Environment, Cross-Embodiment, and Cross-Setup aggregates (UMR 95.0%, 91.7%, 90.0%; HumanEgo 70.0%, 60.8%, 58.3%). These aggregates are overlapping subsets of task IDs. The comparison does not prove that the representation alone caused the gap.

### 2. One motion, two coordinate views

UMR has two parts. **World Flow** says how the task-relevant object moves in the world. **Ego Trajectory** says how the end effector should move relative to its current pose.

The paper assumes that the end effector keeps rigid contact with an object over an action chunk. Under that assumption, the relative end-effector motion in the world frame encodes the object's motion, because the fixed grasp transform cancels. This is the basis for World Flow, which is meant to stay consistent across grasp configurations.

Ego Trajectory expresses the corresponding end-effector motion relative to the current end-effector pose. This removes dependence on the global coordinate frame and gives an action the robot can execute locally. The authors treat the two streams as coordinate views of the same motion, linked by an $SE(3)$ conjugation, which is a rigid-transform change of coordinates. In principle, task motion learned from one demonstrator can then be expressed as locally executable motion for another.

WEPVLA is a point-cloud policy with World and Ego streams and a shared Point Action Expert. The two streams are trained and denoised jointly. Motion-aware segmentation focuses the policy on interaction-relevant foreground. At inference, the robot executes only the Ego action chunk.

The WEPVLA pipeline. Diverse views and demonstrations are aligned. World Flow and Ego Trajectory point-cloud streams pass through a Lite Point Encoder, foreground/background processing, and a shared expert. At deployment, across diverse poses, views, and embodiments, the policy outputs a denoised action command.

### 3. Does the shared description transfer?

The closest comparison is [[2605.24934|HumanEgo]], a prior method for learning robot policies from human egocentric videos. The scores reported here are HumanEgo's results in this paper's evaluation, which differs from its own benchmark. In this comparison, both methods are unified multi-task policies.

The study covers four task types: cube stacking, mug racking, trash sweeping, and towel folding. Its six evaluation settings vary factors including robot, scene, view, height, and disturbance.

For each task, the authors collect 100 human demonstrations in about 10 minutes, split into 50 nominal and 50 recovery demonstrations. DES then generates 500 additional episodes per task, for 600 training episodes per task. The 10 minutes is collection time, which differs from training time and robot execution time.

Against this setup, WEPVLA's 91.7% average compares with 60.8% for HumanEgo. The result supports the authors' interpretation that the representation helps transfer. It does not isolate the representation from every other difference between the methods.

### 4. A shared motion language

The paper's contribution is to connect object-level task motion with robot-local execution, so demonstrations do not have to be forced into one embodiment's controls. This yields zero-shot transfer in the tested tabletop settings with parallel-jaw grippers. The implementation relies on RGB-D geometry and hand-pose estimation, which can degrade with occlusion or missing depth. Broader validation with dexterous hands and more complex contact patterns remains future work.
