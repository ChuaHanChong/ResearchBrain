# GAS: Enhancing Reward-Cost Balance of Generative Model-assisted Offline Safe RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Goal-Assisted Stitching (GAS), a framework from The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, enhances generative model-assisted offline safe reinforcement learning by improving trajectory stitching and balancing reward and cost objectives. It achieves superior reward returns while maintaining safety across various constraint levels and demonstrates robust zero-shot adaptation to different safety thresholds.

## Problem

- Existing Generative Model (GM)-assisted Offline Safe Reinforcement Learning (OSRL) methods lack robust trajectory stitching capabilities, struggling to combine optimal transitions from suboptimal trajectories.
- These methods often fail to balance reward maximization with constraint satisfaction effectively, especially when faced with imbalanced or difficult-to-achieve human-specified reward-cost conditions.
- Current GM architectures concatenate target reward and cost returns without explicitly weighing their relative importance, which can lead to unsafe or overly conservative policies.

## Method

- Temporal Segmented ==Return Augmentation== (TSRA) enriches the offline dataset by generating return-to-go information for varying temporal segments, improving cross-trajectory stitching.
- Transition-level Return Relabeling fine-tunes reward and cost return-to-go values using uniform distributions, making the goal functions robust to a broader range of human-specified, potentially imbalanced, reward-cost targets.
- Goal functions, trained with expectile regression, robustly estimate optimal achievable reward and cost goals from the dataset, which then guide the policy optimization to balance reward and cost objectives.

## Results

- GAS achieved superior reward returns and ensured safety (cost satisfaction) across all evaluated tasks on Bullet-Safety-Gym and Safety-Gymnasium benchmarks, outperforming 8 baselines, especially under tight constraint settings.
- The method maintained robust zero-shot adaptation, consistently keeping cumulative costs below specified thresholds across a range of constraint levels (**10%** to **90%** of maximum costs) while flexibly achieving higher rewards as constraints loosened.
- Transition-level return relabeling and expectile regression were confirmed by ablation studies to be crucial for enhancing stitching capabilities, improving robustness to imbalanced target reward-cost inputs, and maintaining safe performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reliance on long temporal attention windows in GMs can dilute focus on immediate relevant information, hindering the ability to stitch better-than-demonstrated trajectories.
> - Expectile regression can effectively estimate higher quantiles of return distributions, helping to robustly identify and leverage optimal (high reward, low cost) transitions from suboptimal datasets.
> - Augmenting data with segmented returns and strategically relabeling transition-level returns can make OSRL models more adaptable and robust to varied or imbalanced target reward-cost specifications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated success in sequential decision-making tasks aimed at maximizing rewards through environmental interaction. However, standard RL approaches face limitations in safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving or investment portfolios, primarily due to the inherent risks of exploration and policies that solely prioritize reward maximization without explicit safety considerations.

Offline Safe Reinforcement Learning (OSRL) emerged to address these issues by enabling the learning of safe policies from pre-collected datasets, thereby mitigating the risks associated with online exploration. A central challenge in OSRL is the Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) problem, where policies may extrapolate actions beyond the training data, leading to unpredictable or unsafe behaviors. Earlier OSRL methods, which often adapted traditional RL techniques, employed strategies like Lagrangian relaxation, constraint penalties, or distribution correction to manage OOD issues. Despite these efforts, challenges persisted in effectively handling OOD problems and adapting to dynamic constraints.

More recently, Generative Model (GM)-assisted methods have gained traction in OSRL. These methods reframe the Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) as a conditional generative problem, where GMs learn to generate actions or trajectories conditioned on desired reward and cost return-to-go values. This approach offers two notable advantages: it bypasses the Bellman backup procedure, a primary source of OOD problems in traditional RL, and it provides flexibility for zero-shot adaptation to varying reward-cost objectives at test time without requiring retraining.

Despite their benefits, existing GM-assisted OSRL methods exhibit limitations. Specifically, they have been observed to lack robust "trajectory stitching" capabilities, meaning they struggle to combine optimal transitions from different suboptimal trajectories within a dataset to improve overall performance. Furthermore, these methods often lack an explicit mechanism for balancing reward maximization with constraint satisfaction, particularly when presented with human-specified reward-cost conditions that may be imbalanced or difficult to achieve simultaneously.

The presented work, "GAS: Enhancing Reward-Cost Balance of Generative Model-assisted Offline Safe RL," is situated within this context. It aims to advance GM-assisted OSRL by specifically addressing the identified challenges of insufficient stitching capabilities and the difficulty in balancing reward and cost objectives. By proposing a novel algorithm that enhances transition stitching and provides a more flexible approach to balancing reward and constraint targets, GAS contributes to improving the practical applicability and reliability of GM-assisted OSRL in safety-critical domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to develop an algorithm that enhances the stitching capabilities of Generative Model (GM)-assisted Offline Safe Reinforcement Learning (OSRL) methods and improves their ability to balance reward maximization with constraint satisfaction. These objectives are motivated by two critical limitations identified in current GM-assisted OSRL approaches:

1. **Insufficient Trajectory Stitching Capabilities:** Existing GM methods in OSRL, such as Decision Transformer (DT) and Constrained Decision Transformer (CDT), are largely based on a goal-conditioned behavior cloning paradigm. This means they learn to imitate behaviors from the dataset conditioned on desired outcomes. While this approach effectively avoids the Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) problem by bypassing Bellman backups, it inherently limits the model's ability to "stitch" together optimal transitions from diverse, potentially suboptimal, trajectories within the dataset. Traditional RL methods, which focus on immediate state conditions and utilize Bellman backups, are better at finding optimal sequences of actions by combining parts of different experiences. In contrast, GM methods often rely on temporal attention mechanisms that consider a history of past states, actions, rewards, and costs. The paper argues, with empirical support (Figure 1), that long temporal attention windows (e.g., K=10 vs. K=1 in CDT) do not significantly improve performance in OSRL, suggesting that these mechanisms may dilute focus on relevant immediate information and hinder the ability to construct better-than-demonstrated trajectories. This limitation prevents GM methods from fully leveraging suboptimal datasets to learn policies that transcend the quality of individual demonstrations.

2. **Inability to Balance Reward Maximization and Constraint Satisfaction:** In constrained settings, policies must not only achieve high rewards but also strictly adhere to safety constraints. Current GM-assisted OSRL methods face challenges in effectively balancing these dual objectives.
 - **Difficulty in Specifying Target Reward-Cost Pairs:** At test time, users must specify target reward ($\hat{R}$) and cost ($\hat{C}$) return-to-go values. Without prior knowledge, determining feasible and optimal combinations of these targets is difficult, as they often involve inherent trade-offs. Specifying overly ambitious reward targets alongside stringent cost constraints can lead to policy degradation if such conditions are not realistically achievable within the environment or dataset. The GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) structure used in CDT can only attempt to satisfy given targets, without inherently validating their feasibility or relative importance.
 - **Lack of Prioritization Mechanism:** The architecture of current GM-assisted methods, such as CDT, typically concatenates target reward and cost returns as contextual conditions without any explicit mechanism to weigh their relative importance. In safety-critical applications, constraint satisfaction often needs to be prioritized over reward maximization. The absence of such a prioritization mechanism can lead to policies that are either unsafe (violating constraints for higher rewards) or overly conservative (prioritizing safety at the expense of substantial reward loss), resulting in suboptimal performance.

To address these motivations, the proposed Goal-Assisted Stitching (GAS) algorithm aims to introduce a more flexible balance between reward and cost targets, enabling the policy to effectively stitch high-quality transitions to achieve both safe and high-performing outcomes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Goal-Assisted Stitching (GAS) algorithm is introduced to enhance transition stitching and improve the balance between reward maximization and constraint satisfaction in Generative Model-assisted Offline Safe Reinforcement Learning. GAS deviates from relying on lengthy temporal attention modules, common in transformer-based GMs, by focusing on a transition-level stitching approach guided by explicit reward and cost goal functions. The methodology comprises several interconnected components:

- **Optimal Achievable Goals:**
 The conceptual aim is to identify the maximum achievable reward return-to-go ($V^R_{t:\Gamma}$) and its corresponding cost return-to-go ($V^C_{t:\Gamma}$) for a given state, considering specified target reward and cost returns. However, directly extracting these from the dataset presents three challenges:
 1. **Standard Return Definitions:** Traditional $R_t$ and $C_t$ refer to cumulative returns from the current timestep to the trajectory's end. This overlooks the possibility that optimal transitions might occur within shorter, intermediate segments of trajectories.
 2. **Training-Test Misalignment:** Target reward-cost pairs used during testing (human-specified) might not align with the cumulative values naturally observed in training trajectories, leading to degraded performance.
 3. **"Lucky" Samples:** Simple maximization over dataset transitions can lead to overestimation due to rare, highly favorable (high reward, low cost) but potentially stochastic transitions.

- **Temporal Segmented Return Augmentation (TSRA):**
 To address the first issue, GAS augments the offline dataset by generating return-to-go information not only from the current timestep to the trajectory's end but also for shorter, varying temporal segments (from $t$ to $\Gamma$). This creates a richer dataset where transitions $(s_t, a_t)$ are associated with $(R_{t:\Gamma}, C_{t:\Gamma})$ for various $\Gamma \ge t$. This augmentation increases data volume and enables the policy to consider and stitch together optimal segments of different lengths, thereby improving cross-trajectory stitching capabilities.

- **Transition-level Return Relabeling:**
 To mitigate the misalignment between training and testing target values, GAS employs a fine-grained, transition-level return relabeling mechanism during training. For each sampled transition, the reward return-to-go ($R_{t:\Gamma}$) is relabeled from a uniform distribution around its original value, and the cost return-to-go ($C_{t:\Gamma}$) is relabeled from a uniform distribution between its original value and a maximum possible cost ($C_{max}$). These relabeled values ($\hat{R}_{t:\Gamma}, \hat{C}_{t:\Gamma}$) serve as inputs to the goal functions, making them robust to a broader range of human-specified, potentially imbalanced, reward-cost targets at test time. Crucially, these relabeled values guide the *goal functions* and not directly the policy update, preserving policy robustness.

- **Goal Functions with Expectile Regressions:**
 To address the issue of "lucky" samples and estimate optimal achievable goals robustly, GAS trains separate reward and cost goal functions ($V^R_{t:\Gamma}$ and $V^C_{t:\Gamma}$) using expectile regression. Expectile regression, a generalization of mean regression, allows optimization to focus more on high return-to-go samples (controlled by an expectile level $\alpha$, typically set above 0.5) and less on low ones, effectively estimating higher quantiles of the return distribution.
 - The **reward goal function** ($V^R_{t:\Gamma}$) is trained to output the largest reward-to-go that satisfies the constraint. Its loss function uses an advantage function $A^R_{t:\Gamma}$ which down-weights transitions violating the cost constraint or having low rewards.
 - The **cost goal function** ($V^C_{t:\Gamma}$) estimates the cost associated with the optimal reward goal. Its loss function assigns higher weights to transitions that align with higher reward goals under the given constraints. These goal functions are trained together under a unified optimization framework.

- **Goal-guided Policy Optimization:**
 Once the optimal reward and cost goals are estimated by the goal functions, they are incorporated as inputs to the policy ($\pi(a|s, \hat{R}, \hat{C}, V^R, V^C)$). The policy is then optimized using a constrained version of Advantage Weight Regression (AWR). The policy loss function encourages actions that lead to high rewards while satisfying constraints, weighted by a term derived from the expectile regression that prioritizes desirable transitions. This mechanism ensures that the policy learns to take actions that achieve the optimal targets estimated by the goal functions.

- **Dataset Reshaping:**
 The paper observes that offline datasets often exhibit an imbalanced distribution of reward-cost returns, with a high concentration of "conservative transitions" (low rewards, low costs) and a scarcity of "ideal transitions" (high rewards, low costs). To mitigate this data imbalance, which can affect training stability and efficiency, GAS reshapes the dataset during the training phase. It estimates the reward-return distribution conditioned on cost returns and constructs a new subset ($D_q$) by selecting transitions that fall within the top $q\%$ reward returns for each cost return. During training, samples are drawn from $D_q$ with probability $\epsilon$ and from the original dataset $D$ with probability $1-\epsilon$. This strategy creates a more balanced distribution, enabling the model to learn more effectively from desirable transitions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of GAS was conducted on Bullet-Safety-Gym and Safety-Gymnasium benchmarks, across 12 scenarios and compared against 8 baselines, under various constraint thresholds. Performance was measured using normalized reward and cost returns, with thresholds categorized as "tight" (10-30% of maximum cost) and "loose" (70-90% of maximum cost). The findings address three key questions:

1. **Can GAS achieve both safe and better performance with improved stitching ability?**
 - **Tight Constraint Settings:** GAS demonstrated superior performance, achieving both the best reward returns and ensuring safety (cost satisfaction) across all evaluated tasks. Traditional RL methods (CPQ, COptiDICE, WSAC, VOCE) frequently incurred severe constraint violations. Among GM methods, CDT showed some safety issues in multiple scenarios, while FISOR, though safe, exhibited significantly lower reward performance. The paper attributes GAS's superiority in tight constraints to its enhanced stitching ability, allowing it to combine safe transitions from different timesteps and trajectories.
 - **Loose Constraint Settings:** In settings where safety constraints were less stringent, GAS achieved the highest reward maximization compared to all baselines, while still maintaining safe performance. This indicates that GAS's transition stitching approach effectively identifies and combines high-reward segments without compromising safety.

2. **Can GAS preserve the zero-shot adaptation ability to different constraint thresholds?**
 - Experiments comparing GAS with CDT across a range of constraint thresholds (10% to 90% of maximum costs) showed that GAS consistently maintained cumulative costs below the specified thresholds. As the constraint threshold increased, GAS demonstrated an ability to flexibly adjust its behavior, achieving progressively higher rewards while ensuring safety. In contrast, CDT exhibited unsafe behavior in certain tight constraint scenarios (e.g., <30% of max cost) and tended to be overly conservative in others, failing to fully capitalize on looser constraints for higher rewards. This confirms GAS's robust zero-shot adaptation capability.

3. **Is GAS robust to imbalanced and human-specified target reward-cost return-to-goes?**
 - The robustness of GAS to varying and potentially imbalanced human-specified target reward-cost return-to-goes was evaluated by comparing GAS under different return relabeling levels (GAS-δ) with a variant without relabeling (GAS w/o Relabel). Results indicated that GAS consistently achieved safe performance as the target reward return-to-go increased. "GAS w/o Relabel" was observed to achieve safe performance only within a narrow range of reward targets. Increasing the hyper-parameter δ, which controls the relabeling level, was found to enhance GAS's robustness against imbalanced targets, validating the efficacy of the transition-level return relabeling mechanism.

- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Stitching Ability (Expectile Regression):** A variant of GAS without expectile regression ("GAS w/o Stitching," equivalent to $\alpha=0.5$) performed similarly to CDT, demonstrating insufficient stitching ability and suboptimal performance. This result supports the claim that expectile regression is crucial for robustly estimating optimal achievable goals and enhancing stitching. An extremely high $\alpha$ (0.99) could compromise safety in some tasks, suggesting a careful selection of $\alpha$ to avoid biases from "lucky" samples.
 - **Temporal Segmented Return Augmentation (TSRA):** The "GAS w/o TSRA" variant showed inferior performance compared to the full GAS model and suffered from constraint violations under tight thresholds. This validates the theoretical analysis that TSRA provides additional optimality guarantees by offering more flexible sub-trajectories, contributing to improved stitching and safety.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Goal-Assisted Stitching (GAS) represents a notable advancement in Generative Model-assisted Offline Safe Reinforcement Learning (OSRL). By directly addressing the limitations of existing GM methods—specifically, their inadequate trajectory stitching capabilities and challenges in balancing reward maximization with constraint satisfaction—GAS contributes to the field in several ways.

The method’s key innovations, including Temporal Segmented Return Augmentation, Transition-level Return Relabeling, and Expectile Regression-based Goal Functions, collectively enhance the learning process. This allows policies to more effectively identify and combine high-quality transitions from suboptimal datasets, leading to improved performance that can surpass the quality of individual trajectories within the training data.

The empirical results demonstrate several practical benefits of GAS. Its ability to consistently achieve safe performance under tight constraints while simultaneously maximizing rewards under looser constraints is a significant advantage. Furthermore, GAS exhibits robust zero-shot adaptation to varying safety thresholds, enabling a single trained model to operate effectively across a spectrum of safety requirements without requiring retraining. Its robustness to imbalanced and human-specified target reward-cost return-to-goes also increases its practical utility, as real-world target specifications are often imperfect or varied.

The significance of GAS extends to real-world safety-critical applications where balancing performance and strict safety is paramount. Domains such as autonomous driving, robotics, healthcare, and financial portfolio management could potentially benefit from policies that are not only high-performing but also reliably adhere to safety protocols, learned efficiently from offline data.

A potential avenue for future work acknowledged by the authors includes addressing the inherent trade-off between memory capability and stitching ability within the current attention module designs. Further research could focus on developing more advanced memory mechanisms or entirely new frameworks that overcome this limitation, potentially by combining safe and unsafe data to construct novel, safe trajectories, thereby further enhancing the robustness and performance of GM-assisted policies in complex environments.
