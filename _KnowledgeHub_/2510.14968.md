# RDD: Retrieval-Based Demonstration Decomposer for Planner Alignment in Long-Horizon Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed RDD, a retrieval-based method to automatically decompose long-horizon robotic task demonstrations into sub-tasks that align with the training data of low-level visuomotor policies. This approach consistently achieves near-expert performance on various RLBench manipulation tasks, outperforming heuristic decomposers while demonstrating high data efficiency and robust real-world application.

## Problem

- Hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) frameworks struggle with long-horizon tasks because high-level VLM planners often decompose tasks into sub-tasks unfamiliar to low-level visuomotor policies.
- Finetuning VLM planners requires pre-segmented demonstrations, which are labor-intensive to obtain via human annotation or limited by task-specific heuristic rules.
- General visual change-point detection methods can produce sub-tasks that deviate from the specific capabilities or training data of the low-level policies, degrading overall task performance.

## Method

- **RDD** formulates demonstration decomposition as an ==optimal partitioning== problem, efficiently solved by dynamic programming, achieving linear complexity for typical robotic task lengths.
- It uses a retrieval-based interval scoring function that measures the visual and temporal similarity of a proposed sub-task interval to its approximate nearest neighbor in the visuomotor policy's training dataset.
- Intervals are represented by concatenating visual embeddings of their beginning and ending frames, leveraging pre-trained visual encoders and approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS).

## Results

- RDD achieved an average success rate of **74.9%** on 13 RLBench manipulation tasks, closely matching expert performance (**75.1%**) and outperforming state-of-the-art heuristic decomposers like UVD (**71.3%**).
- The method demonstrated high data efficiency, achieving **77.9%** average success with just one demonstration per task for planner finetuning, and showed robustness across various visual encoders.
- RDD exhibited linear scalability for long demonstrations, achieving decomposition speeds of 4.3 frames per second, and delivered superior decomposition quality on real-world and out-of-distribution tasks (mIoU up to **0.706** vs. UVD's **0.506**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly aligning sub-task decompositions for VLM planner finetuning with the visual features known to the low-level visuomotor policy is crucial for improving overall task success in hierarchical robotic systems.
> - Decomposing long-horizon tasks can be efficiently and optimally solved as an interval-wise additive partitioning problem using dynamic programming, making it scalable for real-world applications.
> - Leveraging pre-trained visual encoders and efficient approximate nearest neighbor search enables an automated, training-free, and robust identification of policy-compatible sub-tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of "generalist robots" capable of performing complex, long-horizon tasks in diverse, unstructured environments is a central and challenging goal in modern robotics. Traditional robotic methods often fall short due to the inherent variability and complexity of real-world scenarios.

The current research landscape has seen a paradigm shift with the emergence of **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)** and **Large Language Models (LLMs)**, leading to **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models** specifically designed for embodied AI. Initially, single-stage VLAs showed promise for short-horizon manipulation tasks. However, long-horizon tasks, which require intricate planning, reasoning, and sequential execution, remain difficult for single-stage models.

This limitation has spurred the development of **hierarchical VLA frameworks**. In these architectures, a high-level VLM-based planner breaks down complex tasks into a sequence of simpler, short-horizon sub-tasks, providing step-by-step language instructions. These instructions then guide low-level visuomotor policies, which are specialized in executing these simpler sub-tasks. This hierarchical approach significantly improves performance, enables error correction, and facilitates human intervention.

A critical challenge within these hierarchical frameworks lies in **finetuning the VLM planner**. To train the planner to decompose tasks effectively, it requires demonstrations that are pre-segmented into sub-tasks. Historically, these segmentations have been obtained through:
1. **Human annotation:** Labor-intensive, time-consuming, expensive, and prone to human subjectivity and inconsistencies.
2. **Heuristic rules:** Often task-specific, requiring expert knowledge, and might not generalize well across tasks.
3. **General visual feature change-point detection:** Methods like UVD attempt to automate decomposition based on visual changes, but they may still produce sub-tasks that deviate significantly from the specific capabilities or training data of the low-level visuomotor policies.

This deviation is the core problem RDD addresses. If the high-level planner is finetuned on sub-tasks that are "unfamiliar" to the low-level policy (i.e., not well-represented in its training data), the planner may issue instructions that the policy struggles to execute, degrading overall task performance.

RDD's contribution is to bridge this gap by introducing an **automatic, training-free, and computationally efficient demonstration decomposition method** that explicitly **aligns the decomposed sub-tasks with the visual features present in the low-level visuomotor policy's training dataset**. This alignment ensures that the planner's output is directly compatible with the policy's learned skills, thereby optimizing the coordination between the hierarchical layers. This work represents a significant step towards more robust and scalable hierarchical robot learning systems, enhancing the practical applicability of generalist robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind RDD stems from a critical bottleneck in current hierarchical VLA frameworks: the misalignment between the sub-task decomposition strategies used for finetuning high-level planners and the actual capabilities of the low-level visuomotor policies.

The key objectives of this research are:

1. **Automate Demonstration Decomposition:** To move beyond labor-intensive human annotations and brittle, task-specific heuristic rules for segmenting long-horizon task demonstrations into sub-tasks. The goal is an automatic, training-free, and computationally efficient approach.
2. **Align Planner Finetuning Data with Visuomotor Policy Capabilities:** To ensure that the sub-tasks generated for finetuning the high-level VLM planner are visually and semantically similar to the sub-tasks on which the low-level visuomotor policy was initially trained. This "planner alignment" is crucial to prevent the planner from generating instructions for "unfamiliar" sub-tasks that the policy cannot reliably execute.
3. **Improve Overall Task Performance:** By achieving better alignment, the overarching objective is to enhance the success rate and robustness of hierarchical VLA systems in executing complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks in diverse environments.
4. **Robustness and Scalability:** To develop a decomposition method that performs consistently well across various simulation and real-world benchmarks, and that can scale to large datasets and longer demonstrations without prohibitive computational cost.
5. **Handle Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Scenarios:** To provide a mechanism for scenarios where demonstrations might contain novel sub-tasks not present in the visuomotor policy's training data, allowing for either alignment with general sub-tasks or identification for further policy training.

In essence, the authors are motivated to create a "smart" decomposition mechanism that acts as an intelligent interface between the VLM planner and the visuomotor policy, ensuring seamless execution by leveraging the policy's existing knowledge.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RDD (Retrieval-based Demonstration Decomposer) proposes a novel framework to automatically decompose demonstration videos into sub-tasks that are aligned with the training data of low-level visuomotor policies. The methodology is structured around formulating this decomposition as an optimal partitioning problem, efficiently solved by dynamic programming.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation as Optimal Partitioning

The core idea is to find an optimal partitioning strategy `P = {I_1, I_2..., I_K}` for a given demonstration sequence `S` (comprising frames `s_t`, observations `o_t`, actions `a_t`). This is formulated as:
`P* = argmax_{P ∈ Π(S)} J(P)` (Eq. 3.1)
where `Π(S)` represents all possible partitioning strategies, and `J(P)` is a scoring function that evaluates how well the proposed partition `P` aligns with the visuomotor policy's training dataset `D_train_aug`.

#### 3.2. Dynamic Programming Solver

Directly searching for `P*` is computationally intractable (`O(2^N-1)` for `N` frames). RDD leverages the property that if the scoring function `J(P)` is interval-wise additive (i.e., `J(P) = Σ_I∈P ˜J(I)` where `˜J(I)` scores a single interval), then the problem exhibits the "Principle of Optimality." This allows for an efficient solution using dynamic programming (Algorithm 1).
The algorithm finds the optimal partition with `O(N^2)` evaluations of `˜J`. Crucially, for realistic robotic tasks where sub-task durations (`L_max`) are bounded, the complexity can be further reduced to `O(N)`, making it highly scalable for long demonstrations. The algorithm also supports parallel evaluation of `˜J`.

#### 3.3. Interval Scoring Function (˜J)

The scoring function `˜J(I)` for a single interval `I` is defined as:
`˜J(I) = |I| sim(I, ANNS(V(I), D_train_aug))` (Eq. 3.3)
This function measures the similarity of a proposed interval `I` to its approximate nearest neighbor (`˜I`) found in the visuomotor policy's training dataset, weighted by the interval's duration `|I|`. This ensures that longer, well-matched intervals contribute more positively to the overall score.

#### 3.4. Interval Vector Representation (V) and Similarity Measure (sim)

- **Visual Encoder (E):** RDD uses pre-trained visual encoders (e.g., LIV, R3M, CLIP) to embed RGB image observations (`o_t`) into a compact latent vector space. The paper's main experiments utilize **LIV**, a robotics-specialized encoder.
- **Interval Representation (V(I)):** Instead of encoding every frame, `V(I)` is constructed by concatenating the visual embeddings of the **beginning (`o_b`) and ending (`o_e`) frames** of the interval `I`: `V(I) = concat(E(o_b), E(o_e))` (Eq. 3.5). This is inspired by prior work suggesting that goal states (ending frames) and initial contexts (starting frames) provide rich information about a sub-task.
- **Similarity Measure (sim):** The similarity between a proposed interval `I_ij` and its retrieved nearest neighbor `˜I_ij` is defined as:
 `sim(I_ij, ˜I_ij) = -[δ(V(I_ij), V(˜I_ij)) + α * (1 - |I_ij|/|˜I_ij|)]` (Eq. 3.6)
 This measure has two components:
 1. **Visual Similarity:** `δ(V(I_ij), V(˜I_ij))` is the angular distance between their vector representations, penalizing visual dissimilarity.
 2. **Temporal Similarity:** `α * (1 - |I_ij|/|˜I_ij|)` penalizes differences in temporal duration between the proposed and retrieved intervals, weighted by `α`.

#### 3.5. Handling Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Sub-tasks

For scenarios where demonstrations might contain sub-tasks not present in `D_train_aug`, RDD offers an alternative similarity measure:
`sim(I_ij, ˜I_ij) = -δ(V_e(I_ij), V_e(˜I_ij)) + βG(I_ij)` (Eq. 3.7)
Here, only the ending frame `V_e(I)` is used for retrieval. A second term, `βG(I_ij)`, is added, where `G(I)` evaluates how well an interval aligns with "general" sub-tasks using a heuristic change-point detection method like UVD. `β` balances the trade-off between aligning with known visuomotor sub-tasks and discovering novel, generalizable sub-tasks.

#### 3.6. Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search (ANNS)

Given the potentially vast size of `D_train_aug` (vector database), ANNS is essential for efficient retrieval. RDD employs **Annoy**, a random-projection-trees-based method, for its computational efficiency and robustness. The system can also be accelerated with GPU-based ANNS libraries like FAISS.

#### 3.7. Overall Process

1. **Database Creation:** Build a vector database of `V(I)` for all sub-task intervals in `D_train_aug` (the low-level visuomotor policy's training data).
2. **Demonstration Decomposition:** For a new demonstration `S_i` intended for planner finetuning, apply the dynamic programming solver (Algorithm 1) to find the optimal partition `P_i*`. This involves iteratively evaluating `˜J(I)` for candidate intervals `I` by:
 - Computing `V(I)`.
 - Retrieving the approximate nearest neighbor `˜I` from the database using ANNS.
 - Calculating `sim(I, ˜I)`.
3. **Planner Finetuning Data Generation:** Once `P_i*` is found, language instructions for each decomposed sub-task are generated (e.g., using Gemini-1.5-flash) to create `D_demo_aug`.
4. **Planner Finetuning:** A pre-trained VLM planner (e.g., LLaVa-based llama3-llava-next-8B) is finetuned on `D_demo_aug` using LoRA.

This systematic approach ensures that the planner learns decomposition strategies that are intrinsically compatible with the underlying visuomotor policy's learned skills.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of RDD focused on its effectiveness in decomposing demonstrations for hierarchical VLA frameworks, particularly for long-horizon robotic tasks. The key findings are:

1. **Superior Multi-Task Performance on RLBench:** RDD consistently outperformed state-of-the-art heuristic decomposers and naive strategies on 13 challenging RLBench manipulation tasks.
 - RDD achieved an average success rate of **74.9%**, closely matching the **Expert (75.1%)** performance, which serves as an upper bound (decomposition using the same task-specific heuristics as the visuomotor policy's training).
 - In contrast, **UVD** (a state-of-the-art visual feature change-point decomposer) performed similarly to **Uniform (71.4% vs. 71.3%)**, suggesting its heuristic rule often fails to align with the visuomotor policy's training data.
 - The "w/o Finetune" baseline (vanilla VLM planner) showed significantly lower performance (52.6%), highlighting the necessity of finetuning.
 - This demonstrates that explicitly aligning sub-task decomposition with the visuomotor policy's knowledge is critical for overall task success.

2. **Robustness to Visual Representations:** RDD showed strong robustness across a variety of visual encoders:
 - Robotics-specialized encoders like **LIV** and **R3M** performed well, achieving average success rates of 81.1% and 80.0%, respectively (when evaluating a subset of tasks where the visuomotor policy has decent performance, as described in footnote 2).
 - Surprisingly, **ResNet** (pre-trained on ImageNet-1k) also demonstrated strong performance (81.1%), suggesting its latent space implicitly correlates with semantic information useful for task decomposition, potentially due to its training on labeled image categories.
 - Encoders without explicit language integration during training (VC-1, VIP) generally performed worse, indicating the importance of language-aware visual features for semantic retrieval in VLA contexts.

3. **Impact of Weighting Parameters:**
 - The hyperparameter `α` (balancing visual and temporal similarity in Eq. 3.6) showed optimal performance around `α=1`. Values too low (`α=0`, no temporal alignment) or too high (over-reliance on temporal similarity) led to performance degradation. This confirms that both visual and temporal alignment are important.

4. **Data Efficiency:** RDD demonstrated high data efficiency.
 - Using just **one demonstration per task** for planner finetuning, RDD achieved an average success rate of **77.9%**, closely approaching the performance with three demonstrations (81.1%). This indicates RDD can extract meaningful decomposition rules even from limited data, likely due to the high quality and alignment of the generated keyframes.

5. **Performance on Real-world and OOD Sub-tasks:**
 - **Real-world (AgiBotWorld-Alpha):** RDD significantly outperformed UVD in decomposition quality, achieving a mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of **0.706** compared to UVD's 0.506.
 - **OOD (RoboCerebra):** When handling out-of-distribution sub-tasks, RDD (using the modified similarity measure in Eq. 3.7) consistently outperformed UVD, achieving mIoU scores up to **0.630**. This indicates RDD's ability to leverage potential similarities while also accounting for novel general sub-tasks.

6. **Speed and Scalability:**
 - Algorithm 1 (dynamic programming solver) exhibits **linear complexity O(N)** with respect to the number of frames `N` when the maximum interval length (`L_max`) is bounded, as is typical in robotics. This was empirically validated (Figure 3).
 - When integrated with GPU-accelerated ANNS libraries like FAISS, RDD can decompose demonstrations at a high speed (e.g., 4.3 fps for a 500-frame video, approximately 2 minutes), demonstrating strong scalability for large datasets.

7. **Necessity of Finetuning on Target Tasks:** Experiments showed that a planner pre-finetuned on other tasks but not the target task performed worse (77.9% avg. success) than RDD-finetuned planners (79.6%), reinforcing the need for target-task-specific finetuning.

8. **Comparison with VLM-based Decomposers:** RDD significantly outperformed a powerful VLM like **Gemini-2.5-pro** when prompted to decompose demonstrations (RDD: 74.9% vs. Gemini-2.5-pro: 72.6%). This highlights that even advanced VLMs struggle with precise, policy-aligned decomposition without RDD's specific retrieval mechanism.

**Qualitative Results:** Figure 4 visually confirmed RDD's ability to identify sub-tasks that closely match expert decompositions, even for subtle arm movements, showcasing its robustness to task-irrelevant interference, unlike UVD which often produced misaligned keyframes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Retrieval-based Demonstration Decomposer (RDD) represents a significant advancement in the field of hierarchical robot learning, particularly for long-horizon tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Planner-Policy Coordination:** RDD directly addresses a fundamental challenge in hierarchical VLAs: the misalignment between the high-level planner's decomposition logic and the low-level visuomotor policy's learned capabilities. By explicitly aligning the planner's finetuning data with the policy's existing knowledge, RDD ensures that the planner issues instructions that the robot can reliably execute. This optimized coordination leads to substantial improvements in task success rates and robustness, as demonstrated by RDD's near-expert performance.

2. **Scalable and Efficient Data Curation:** Moving away from manual annotations and brittle heuristics, RDD offers an automatic, training-free, and computationally efficient method for demonstration decomposition. This dramatically reduces the human effort and expertise required to prepare data for finetuning VLM planners. Its linear scalability for long demonstrations and compatibility with GPU-accelerated nearest neighbor search make it practical for processing large datasets in real-world robotics.

3. **Foundation for Generalist Robots:** The ability to effectively decompose complex tasks into manageable, policy-aligned sub-tasks is crucial for building truly generalist robots. RDD contributes to this goal by enabling more robust learning from diverse demonstrations and facilitating the transfer of learned skills. This could accelerate the deployment of robots in unstructured environments like homes, warehouses, and factories.

4. **Improved Data Efficiency for Planner Finetuning:** The high data efficiency shown by RDD (performing well with even a single demonstration per task) is a major advantage. This reduces the burden of collecting extensive new demonstrations for every new task, making robot learning more accessible and agile.

5. **Robustness and Adaptability:** RDD's robust performance across various visual encoders and its ability to handle out-of-distribution sub-tasks by balancing retrieval with general change-point detection make it a versatile tool. This adaptability is critical for real-world scenarios where tasks and environments can vary significantly.

6. **Insights into VLM Planner Limitations:** The comparison with VLM-based decomposers (like Gemini-2.5-pro) highlights that even powerful, general-purpose VLMs benefit significantly from specialized, retrieval-augmented mechanisms like RDD for robotics-specific tasks. This underscores the importance of domain-aware architectural designs in embodied AI.

**Potential Future Impacts:**
- **Visuomotor Policy Training:** RDD could be extended to generate new, policy-aligned sub-task training data for the visuomotor policy itself, potentially allowing the policy to learn novel skills more effectively by leveraging its existing knowledge base.
- **Specialized Sub-task Features:** Further research could explore more sophisticated sub-task interval features that capture richer temporal and semantic dynamics, beyond just start and end frames, especially for tasks requiring fine-grained temporal understanding.
- **Data Curation Integration:** RDD can be seamlessly integrated with advanced dataset curation techniques to filter out noisy or suboptimal sub-tasks from the visuomotor policy's training data, ensuring RDD aligns with only high-quality, impactful demonstrations.

**Broader Societal Impacts:**
As with any advancement in robotics and AI, RDD's capabilities carry broader implications:
- **Labor Displacement:** Increased automation through more capable robots could lead to concerns about job displacement in certain sectors.
- **Privacy and Security:** Robots operating in unstructured environments collect vast amounts of visual data, raising privacy concerns. Robust security measures are paramount to prevent misuse.
- **Ethical Deployment:** The development and deployment of increasingly autonomous systems necessitate adherence to ethical guidelines, including fairness, accountability, transparency, and safety. RDD's contribution to more reliable robot behavior can positively contribute to safety, but careful consideration of its application contexts remains vital.

In conclusion, RDD offers a promising solution to a critical problem in hierarchical robot learning, paving the way for more capable, efficient, and robust generalist robots. Its innovative approach to aligning planner and policy capabilities is a key step towards achieving advanced embodied intelligence.
