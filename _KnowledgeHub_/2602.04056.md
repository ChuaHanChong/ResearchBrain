# Modular Safety Guardrails Are Necessary for Foundation-Model-Enabled Robots in the Real World

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Texas A&M, Purdue, Amazon, and NVIDIA present a "modular safety guardrails" architecture designed for foundation model-enabled robots operating in complex, open-world environments. This framework systematically addresses action, decision, and human-centered safety through independent monitoring and intervention layers, providing non-bypassable runtime enforcement against emergent risks beyond traditional safety concerns.

## Problem

- Foundation Model (FM)-enabled robots operating in dynamic, open-world environments introduce complex safety challenges beyond traditional physical constraints.
- Existing safety mechanisms, whether embedded within FMs or as single-perspective external add-ons, are insufficient to provide comprehensive, non-bypassable runtime safety across action, decision, and human-centered dimensions.
- These approaches struggle with distribution shifts, long-tailed hazards, and the subjective, evolving nature of human interaction.

## Method

- An architectural framework called "modular safety guardrails" is proposed, featuring external modularity (decoupled from FMs) and internal modularity (specialized submodules).
- The framework comprises a Monitoring and Evaluation Layer that independently assesses risk across perception, planning, and control outputs.
- An Intervention Layer, consisting of a Decision Gate for high-level plans and an Action Gate for low-level control commands, provides non-bypassable runtime enforcement across all safety dimensions.

## Results

- The work established a comprehensive taxonomy for FM-enabled robot safety, covering action, decision, and human-centered dimensions.
- It systematically argued for the insufficiency of existing safety approaches in open-world scenarios, highlighting their vulnerabilities to distribution shifts and long-tail hazards.
- A modular safety guardrail architecture was proposed, offering systematic coverage and non-bypassable runtime assurance via a monitoring layer and two-stage intervention (Decision Gate, Action Gate), demonstrated resolving failure modes like semantic misinterpretation and unsafe motion accumulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Comprehensive safety for open-world FM-enabled robots requires addressing three distinct dimensions - action safety, decision safety, and human-centered safety - which current paradigms, including model-internal alignment and fragmented external add-ons, cannot simultaneously ensure due to structural limitations.
> - A modular architecture that separates monitoring and intervention, with distinct gates for decisions and actions, provides the necessary non-bypassable and verifiable safety authority.
> - Principled co-design opportunities, such as representation alignment and conservatism allocation, can enhance the gracefulness and efficiency of safety enforcement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of foundation models (FMs) into robotics represents a significant advancement, enabling capabilities such as open-world semantic reasoning, perception, task planning, and end-to-end visuomotor control. This has pushed robotic systems beyond controlled laboratory settings into more complex, open-ended real-world environments. However, this progress introduces new safety challenges that classical robotics safety mechanisms are not equipped to handle.

Traditional robotics safety primarily focuses on physical constraints like collision avoidance and joint limits, often assuming fixed and predefinable parameters. In contrast, FM-enabled robots operate in dynamic environments where hazards are context-dependent, specifications evolve, and interactions with humans introduce subjective safety requirements such as semantic appropriateness, intent alignment, and social norm adherence. Existing approaches, including static verification, monolithic controllers, and purely end-to-end learned policies, are identified as insufficient because they struggle with distribution shifts, rare but high-impact safety failures, and the inherent ambiguities of human interaction.

The paper critically examines two alternative views on safety placement:
1. **Safety embedded within the model:** This involves post-training alignment methods (e.g., instruction-tuning, RLHF) to make FMs intrinsically safer. While improving typical behavior, this approach lacks a non-bypassable runtime boundary, leaving systems vulnerable to unforeseen failures or distribution shifts at deployment time.
2. **Safety provided by external add-ons (single-perspective):** This encompasses various external modules that monitor or filter behavior at runtime, typically focusing on a single safety dimension:
 - **Action-level add-ons:** Control-theoretic safety filters and standards enforce physical constraints (e.g., collision avoidance). They are effective for physical safety but cannot reason about semantic or human-centered risks.
 - **Decision-level add-ons:** These detect unsafe plans before execution using learned world models, LLM-based judges, or uncertainty estimation. They target semantic appropriateness but cannot guarantee physical safety during execution and are vulnerable to FM limitations like hallucination.
 - **Human-centered add-ons:** These infer intent, learn preferences, or model trust to align robot behavior with human expectations. They enhance interaction quality but do not provide hard safety guarantees or enforcement against unsafe physical actions when misalignment occurs.

The authors argue that neither embedding safety solely within FMs nor using fragmented, single-perspective external safety add-ons can provide comprehensive protection across all safety dimensions in open-world, human-centered environments. This paper positions itself by proposing a novel modular architectural foundation that integrates comprehensive safety across the autonomy stack, directly addressing the identified shortcomings of existing methods. It advocates for an architecture that combines monitoring and intervention layers, designed with principles of external and internal modularity, to enable robust and adaptable safety enforcement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to address the emergent safety challenges introduced by the deployment of foundation model (FM)-enabled robots in real-world, open-ended environments. The authors argue for the necessity of a new architectural paradigm for safety, encapsulated in their "Core Claim":

**Core Claim:** "Under open-world deployment with long-tailed hazards and non-stationary human interaction, safety mechanisms that are either fully embedded within the autonomy model or confined to a single layer of the autonomy stack are structurally insufficient to ensure action, decision, and human-centered safety simultaneously."

The motivation for this work stems from several detailed observations regarding the limitations of current safety approaches:

- **Expanded Scope of Robotics:** FMs have enabled robots to move beyond pre-defined, constrained settings into open-world scenarios where tasks, environments, and human expectations are diverse, dynamic, and often ill-specified.
- **Novel Safety Challenges:** This expanded scope introduces new categories of safety risks that go beyond physical constraint satisfaction:
 - **Action Safety:** While classical, ensuring physical feasibility (e.g., collision avoidance, joint limits) remains critical, FMs introduce epistemic unreliability and uncertainty, which can propagate to physical actions.
 - **Decision Safety:** FMs necessitate evaluating the semantic and contextual appropriateness of a robot's plans and behaviors (e.g., not placing a hot object on wood). This requires reasoning over open-domain knowledge.
 - **Human-centered Safety:** Robot behavior must conform to human intent, norms, and expectations to be perceived as safe. This includes addressing issues of predictability, understandability, trust, and managing subjective, evolving human preferences.
- **Limitations of Existing Solutions:**
 - **Monolithic/End-to-End Solutions:** Learning a single end-to-end safety guardrail is deemed fragile due to vulnerability to distribution shifts, difficulty in capturing rare but catastrophic failures in training data, and the evolving nature of safety requirements.
 - **Single-Perspective External Add-ons:** Current external safety mechanisms typically address only one safety dimension (e.g., physical filters, semantic judges, preference learners), failing to provide integrated, comprehensive coverage. They also often rely on assumptions that break down in open-world FM-enabled contexts (e.g., predefined unsafe sets, complete safety specifications).
- **Need for Robustness and Longevity:** The open-ended nature of deployment demands safety mechanisms that are independently verifiable, updateable, composable, and non-bypassable at execution time, which current approaches largely lack.

Therefore, the key objective is to propose a modular safety guardrail architecture that specifically addresses these multifaceted challenges by providing systematic safety coverage across action, decision, and human-centered dimensions, while being robust to the intrinsic limitations of FMs and the complexities of real-world deployment. The authors aim to establish this architecture as a necessary foundation for safe, real-world physical AI deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is an architectural framework termed "modular safety guardrails." This framework is designed to provide comprehensive and adaptable safety enforcement for FM-enabled robotic systems by separating safety concerns from the core autonomy stack.

The core principles of this architecture are:
- **External Modularity:** The guardrail operates independently of the upstream FMs it monitors, minimizing shared parameters, training objectives, or statistical coupling. This allows it to function as an auditable and verifiable safety authority, distinct from the guarded FM's potential uncertainties and failure modes.
- **Internal Modularity:** The guardrail itself is decomposed into specialized submodules, each targeting distinct failure modes and time-scale requirements. These submodules have explicit interfaces, non-overlapping responsibilities, and can be independently tested, replaced, or updated.

The modular safety guardrail architecture consists of two primary layers (Figure 2 in the paper):

#### 3.1. Monitoring and Evaluation Layer

This layer is responsible for independently assessing risk across the entire autonomy stack's outputs (perception, planning, control). Its function is to detect potential safety violations *before* they propagate downstream.
- **Information Flow:** It observes execution-relevant outputs from the perception, planning, and control modules through decoupled channels.
- **Risk Assessment:** It generates risk signals across all three safety dimensions:
 - **Action Safety:** Flags violations of physical constraints such as joint limits, collision margins, or dynamic feasibility (e.g., via risk-aware control-theoretic monitors).
 - **Decision Safety:** Checks semantic consistency and contextual appropriateness, including FM-specific issues like hallucination or adversarial vulnerability (e.g., using FM-based evaluators, critic agents, or uncertainty quantification).
 - **Human-centered Safety:** Monitors predictability, preference alignment, and trust indicators (e.g., via intent inference, preference learning).
- **Representation:** It interfaces with explicit, execution-relevant representations (e.g., regions of interest, candidate plans, trajectories, control commands) rather than relying on internal latent embeddings of FMs, unless explicitly exposed.

#### 3.2. Intervention Layer

This layer provides non-bypassable, execution-time authority to mitigate risks detected by the Monitoring and Evaluation Layer when they exceed acceptable thresholds. It consists of two complementary submodules:
- **Decision Gate:**
 - **Function:** Operates at the planning level, screening candidate plans generated by FMs.
 - **Target:** Primarily addresses violations of decision safety and human-centered safety.
 - **Action:** Blocks unsafe plans and triggers replanning or requests user clarification. It is explicitly decoupled from plan generation to maintain a clear safety authority boundary. By intervening upstream, it prevents the action gate from having to compensate for fundamentally inappropriate high-level intent.
- **Action Gate:**
 - **Function:** Operates at the execution level, enforcing physical safety by constraining or modifying low-level control commands just before they are applied to the robot.
 - **Target:** Enforces action safety.
 - **Action:** Employs mechanisms like shielding, trajectory adjustment, or projection onto safe sets. It acts as the last line of defense, ensuring that all executed commands remain within physical bounds even under disturbances, tracking errors, or residual upstream failures, regardless of how the plans were generated.

#### 3.3. Co-Design Opportunities

Beyond modular enforcement, the architecture enables principled co-design opportunities to make safety enforcement more efficient, less conservative, and graceful:
- **Representation Alignment (Between Layers):** Focuses on how safety-relevant information from the Monitoring Layer is represented for the Intervention Layer. The goal is for monitoring outputs to preserve uncertainty and risk structure in an "actionable" format (e.g., spatially grounded uncertainty sets instead of scalar scores). This allows the Intervention Layer to apply precise, directional, or local constraints.
- **Conservatism Allocation (Between Modules):** Concerns the coordination of strictness between the decision gate and the action gate. Instead of independent, stacked conservatism, co-design enables conditional approval of plans by the decision gate, provided the action gate can certify feasibility of maintaining safety margins in real-time. This ensures that conservatism is applied where most precisely enforceable, preventing unnecessary plan rejections or stoppages.

This methodology asserts that by implementing these modular layers and embracing co-design principles, FM-enabled robots can achieve systematic safety coverage across physical, semantic, and human-interaction contexts in complex, open-world deployments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper's main "findings" are not empirical data but rather the architectural solution and its proposed characteristics that address the identified safety challenges. The core result is the theoretical argument and proposed framework for why modular safety guardrails are necessary and how they systematically provide safety for FM-enabled robots.

Key elements of this "finding" include:

- **Characterization of FM-enabled Robot Safety:** The paper defines a comprehensive taxonomy of safety dimensions:
 - **Action Safety:** Physical feasibility and constraint compliance.
 - **Decision Safety:** Semantic and contextual appropriateness.
 - **Human-centered Safety:** Conformance to human intent, norms, and expectations.
 This characterization highlights the multi-faceted nature of safety beyond purely physical constraints, which is crucial for FM-enabled robots operating in human environments.

- **Identification of Insufficiency in Existing Approaches:** The paper systematically analyzes why existing safety paradigms (model-internal safety, single-perspective external add-ons) are inadequate for open-world FM-enabled robotics. This includes their vulnerability to distribution shift, inability to handle long-tail hazards, lack of comprehensive coverage across safety dimensions, and failure to establish non-bypassable runtime enforcement. This analysis establishes the fundamental gap that the proposed architecture aims to fill.

- **Proposition of Modular Safety Guardrails as a Necessary Architecture:** The central finding is the argument that architectural modularity—both external (decoupling safety from FMs) and internal (decomposing safety into specialized submodules)—is necessary for safe and reliable deployment. This architecture is presented as a "non-bypassable, execution-time safety architecture."

- **Detailed Architecture of Modular Safety Guardrails:** The paper presents the specific design of the two-layer system:
 - **Monitoring and Evaluation Layer:** Functions as an independent risk assessor across perception, planning, and control outputs, generating risk signals for all three safety dimensions.
 - **Intervention Layer:** Comprises a **Decision Gate** (screening high-level plans for semantic and human-centered safety) and an **Action Gate** (enforcing physical safety on low-level control commands). This two-gate design addresses risks at different semantic levels and time scales, providing both upstream rejection and last-resort physical enforcement.

- **Enforceable Safety Assurance:** The proposed architecture yields safety assurances through:
 - **Non-bypassability:** All execution-relevant proposals pass through the intervention layer.
 - **External Modularity:** Enables independent auditing and verification.
 - **Internal Modularity:** Allows heterogeneous safety mechanisms to be verified, updated, and composed. This systematic coverage across safety dimensions differentiates it from prior work.

- **Identification of Co-Design Opportunities:** The architecture also opens up avenues for "co-design" to enhance safety enforcement:
 - **Representation Alignment:** Improving how monitoring outputs are represented (e.g., spatially grounded uncertainty ellipsoids instead of scalar scores) enables more precise and less conservative interventions by the Intervention Layer.
 - **Conservatism Allocation:** Coordinating the strictness between the decision and action gates prevents excessive conservatism and allows for more graceful, task-preserving interventions by allocating conservatism to the module that can enforce it most precisely.

- **Illustrative Deployment Examples:** The paper provides concrete examples (language-instructed household robot, open-vocabulary mobile manipulation, dexterous manipulation) that demonstrate how the modular guardrail architecture interfaces with different FM configurations. These examples serve as existence proofs, illustrating how the architecture can resolve complex failure modes (e.g., misidentified objects, semantic misinterpretation, or unsafe motion accumulation) that single-perspective or model-internal safety solutions cannot. For instance, in the household robot example, representation alignment and conservatism allocation allow the robot to safely reroute with increased clearance for a hot pan near a child, rather than defaulting to a brittle stop.

In summary, the paper's core finding is the conceptualization and architectural justification of a modular safety guardrail system as a necessary, comprehensive, and adaptable approach to enable the safe deployment of FM-enabled robots in real-world, human-centric environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance of this work lies in its comprehensive theoretical framework for addressing the emergent safety challenges posed by the integration of foundation models (FMs) into real-world robotics. By proposing modular safety guardrails, the authors aim to provide an architectural foundation that could fundamentally reshape how safety is conceived, designed, and implemented in advanced robotic systems.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

- **Enabling Safer Real-World Deployment:** The primary impact is the potential to accelerate the safe deployment of FM-enabled robots in unstructured, human-centered environments. By providing a structured, verifiable, and non-bypassable safety layer, the architecture offers a mechanism to mitigate the inherent uncertainties and potential failure modes of FMs, making these systems more trustworthy for public interaction.
- **Comprehensive Safety Coverage:** This work moves beyond traditional physical safety to systematically address decision safety (semantic and contextual appropriateness) and human-centered safety (intent alignment, social norms). This holistic approach is crucial for robotics applications that involve complex human interaction and open-ended tasks, potentially reducing unintended behaviors and enhancing user acceptance.
- **Addressing Limitations of Current Approaches:** The proposed modularity directly tackles the brittleness of end-to-end learning solutions and the fragmented nature of single-perspective external safety add-ons. It offers a framework for building robust safety systems that can adapt to evolving requirements and distribution shifts, unlike static or monolithic approaches.
- **Promoting Independent Verification and Auditing:** The principle of external modularity, which decouples the guardrail from the FM autonomy stack, is critical for establishing an independent safety authority. This enables easier auditing, verification, and certification of safety mechanisms, which is vital for regulatory compliance and public trust in autonomous systems.
- **Fostering an Ecosystem of Safety Modules:** The call for a broader ecosystem of composable guardrail modules encourages specialized development for different failure modes, uncertainty sources, and interaction contexts. This could lead to a shared safety stack and standardized interfaces, reducing redundant effort and promoting best practices across the robotics community.
- **Advancing Co-Design Principles:** The emphasis on co-design opportunities (representation alignment and conservatism allocation) highlights a path toward more intelligent and less conservative safety enforcement. This could lead to more efficient interventions that maintain task progress while ensuring safety, rather than defaulting to disruptive stops or overly strict constraints. This shift from simple filtering to informed negotiation between safety layers is a notable advancement.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The paper serves as a "call to action" for the research community, identifying concrete directions for future work, including the development of comprehensive safety benchmarks and metrics for quantifying "operational independence" between FMs and guardrails. This positions the paper as a foundational piece that defines a new research agenda for embodied AI safety.

**Potential limitations and challenges, as acknowledged by the authors, also define areas of future impact:**

- **Computational Overhead:** FM-based guardrails introduce latency, which needs to be addressed through asynchronous design and fallback mechanisms. Research into efficient, real-time safety evaluation is crucial.
- **Independence of FM-based Guardrails:** The effectiveness relies on the guardrail's independence from the monitored FM. When guardrails themselves use FMs, ensuring true "operational independence" (e.g., uncorrelated failure modes) is a significant challenge requiring new metrics and research.

Despite these challenges, the work provides a well-reasoned argument and a clear architectural blueprint, offering a pathway toward more reliable, adaptable, and human-centric safety for the next generation of robotic systems.
