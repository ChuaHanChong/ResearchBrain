# Data Efficient Behavior Cloning for Fine Manipulation via Continuity-based Corrective Labels

> [!summary] Summary
> This work gives the first real-world validation of CCIL, a data-augmentation framework that fights covariate shift in behavior cloning by fitting a locally Lipschitz dynamics model to demonstrations and back-tracking corrective state-action labels that steer the robot toward expert states. A practical instantiation adds spectral-normalized dynamics training and Jacobian-based label filtering, lifting chopstick fine-manipulation success rates from 23% to 83% on cube grasping.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning from offline demonstrations suffers compounding errors and covariate shift, pushing robots into out-of-distribution states that cause task failure.
- Existing remedies demand interactive experts, simulators, large sub-optimal datasets, or task-specific domain knowledge, so they do not apply under offline-demonstration-only assumptions.
- Corrective-label augmentation was validated only in simulation; its local Lipschitz continuity assumption seemed doubtful for contact-rich manipulation, and parameter-tuning guidance was absent.

## Method

- ==Spectral-normalization regularization== trains the dynamics model, imposing a loose upper bound on the ==Lipschitz constant== while letting local continuity vary freely across state-action regions.
- ==BackTrack label generation==, inspired by ==Backward Euler==, sets the synthetic state to the expert state minus the predicted state change while reusing the expert action.
- ==Jacobian-based label filtering== replaces the fixed neighborhood threshold: reject a label when local ==Jacobian spectral norm== times label distance exceeds a quantile-chosen error bound.

## Results

- On a 7-DOF chopstick robot, corrective labels raised success from **23%** to **83%** (cube, 100 trajectories), **58%** to **72%** (gear insertion), and **17%** to **48%** (coin), across 48 trials per agent.
- Gains concentrate in low-data regimes and shrink as demonstrations grow; two-proportion z-tests confirm significance at **p < 0.05** for both grasping tasks.
- Label-quantile ablation shows intermediate quantiles win: using all labels matches or underperforms behavior cloning, and contact-cluster labels alone degrade success versus free-space labels.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Contact-rich manipulation still contains enough locally smooth sub-space that continuity-based augmentation works, despite global dynamics discontinuities at contact events.
> - Filtering augmented labels by a per-point estimated error bound matters more than the enforced Lipschitz constraint; unconstrained dynamics models already show small local coefficients.
> - Corrective labels trade error against coverage: conservative labels stay too close to demonstrations to expand support, aggressive ones inject wrong dynamics and hurt.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL) represents a paradigm for robots to acquire complex behaviors by mimicking expert demonstrations. Behavior cloning (BC) is a foundational method within IL, where a policy is directly learned from expert state-action pairs. While scalable with large datasets, the application of IL, particularly BC, in real-world robotics is frequently hampered by two primary challenges: covariate shift and the scarcity of expert demonstrations.

Covariate shift arises when a robot deviates from the expert's demonstrated trajectory due to sensor noise, joint errors, or inherent system uncertainties. These deviations can lead the agent into states that are out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to the training data, resulting in compounding errors and potential task failure. Addressing covariate shift often necessitates additional resources such as interactive expert feedback, high-fidelity simulators, or large volumes of sub-optimal data, which are not always available or practical in real-world robotic deployments.

The Continuity-based Corrective Labels for Imitation Learning (CCIL) framework was proposed as a data augmentation strategy to mitigate covariate shift. CCIL operates under the assumption of local Lipschitz continuity in system dynamics, allowing it to learn a dynamics model from demonstrations and generate corrective labels that guide the agent back towards expert states. While CCIL has demonstrated promising results in various simulation environments, its empirical validation in real-world robotic applications, especially in tasks involving complex contact dynamics and fine manipulation, has been limited. Such tasks are inherently challenging due to their precision requirements, susceptibility to minor errors, and the presence of discontinuities in dynamics caused by physical interactions.

This work addresses this gap by providing empirical evidence for CCIL's effectiveness in real-world fine manipulation. It investigates whether the fundamental assumption of local Lipschitz continuity holds sufficiently in contact-rich manipulation tasks and offers practical guidance for implementing CCIL on physical robots, specifically focusing on data efficiency in low-data regimes and robustness to hyperparameter choices. The paper positions itself as a practical application and validation of an existing theoretical framework in a domain where its assumptions are particularly challenged.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to provide the first empirical validation of the Continuity-based Corrective Labels for Imitation Learning (CCIL) framework in real-world fine manipulation tasks. This overarching goal is motivated by the significant challenges faced by imitation learning in practical robotic applications, specifically covariate shift and data scarcity, and the unverified applicability of CCIL's underlying assumptions in complex physical environments.

The specific motivations and objectives can be detailed as follows:

- **Empirical Validation of CCIL in Real-World Fine Manipulation:** Despite CCIL's reported success in simulation, there has been a lack of empirical evidence supporting its efficacy on physical robots. Real-world manipulation tasks, particularly fine manipulation, involve complex contact dynamics that can introduce discontinuities in system dynamics, potentially challenging CCIL's core assumption of local Lipschitz continuity. The research aims to determine if CCIL can effectively improve imitation learning performance in such challenging, contact-rich scenarios.
- **Assessment of Local Lipschitz Continuity in Real-World Dynamics:** CCIL fundamentally relies on the presence of local Lipschitz continuity in the system dynamics. A key motivation is to investigate whether real-world fine manipulation tasks, which inherently involve physical contact and potential discontinuities, exhibit sufficient local smoothness for CCIL to be successfully applied. This involves analyzing the local Lipschitz coefficients of learned dynamics models in these domains.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity in Low-Data Regimes:** Real-world robotic demonstrations are often costly and time-consuming to collect, leading to limited data availability. Imitation learning agents trained on scarce data are particularly susceptible to covariate shift. A major objective is to evaluate CCIL's ability to provide significant performance improvements in low-data regimes, thereby enhancing data efficiency and making imitation learning more practical for tasks where expert demonstrations are sparse.
- **Providing Practical Guidelines for CCIL Implementation:** Deploying and tuning imitation learning algorithms on physical robots can be complex and expensive due to the need for numerous real-world trials. There is a lack of understanding regarding the optimal design choices and hyperparameter settings for CCIL in practical robotic domains. This work aims to conduct extensive ablation studies to explore the impact of key CCIL design parameters—specifically the Lipschitz constraint during dynamics model training and the label rejection threshold for generated corrective labels—on performance. The goal is to derive practical insights and guidelines to simplify the application and tuning of CCIL for roboticists.
- **Demonstrating CCIL's Practicality for Alleviating Compounding Errors:** Ultimately, the research seeks to demonstrate that CCIL offers a practical and effective method for mitigating compounding errors in imitation learning on physical robots. This includes showing that CCIL can generate high-quality corrective labels that guide the agent back to expert states, even in domains with mixed local Lipschitz continuity, and that judicious filtering of these labels is beneficial.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need to bridge the gap between simulation-based theoretical benefits of CCIL and its unverified practical utility in real-world, data-constrained, and dynamically complex robotic fine manipulation tasks, while also offering actionable guidance for its implementation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a methodology centered on extending the Continuity-based Corrective Labels for Imitation Learning (CCIL) framework to real-world fine manipulation tasks and conducting extensive empirical validation and ablation studies.

**A. Imitation Learning Framework**
The core imitation learning algorithm used is Behavior Cloning (BC), where a policy $\pi$ is learned from expert demonstrations $D^* = \{(s^*_j, a^*_j, s^*_{j+1})\}_j$ by minimizing a policy loss function $L(a_i, \pi(s_i))$ over the combined dataset of expert and generated corrective labels.

The robot's action $a$ is represented as a tuple $(x, q, c)$, corresponding to the end-effector's Cartesian position, orientation (quaternion), and chopstick angle. The action loss function used is:
$L(a, \hat{a}) = \alpha_1 \|x - \hat{x}\|^2 + \alpha_2 \theta^2 + \alpha_3 (c - \hat{c})^2$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the target and predicted orientations, and $\alpha_1=10, \alpha_2=1, \alpha_3=10$ are weights.

**B. Continuity-based Corrective Labels for Imitation Learning (CCIL)**
CCIL aims to mitigate covariate shift by generating corrective labels $(s^G_t, a^G_t)$ that guide the agent back towards expert states $s^*_t$. This process involves two main steps:

1. **Training a Dynamics Model:** Since the true dynamics $f$ are unknown, CCIL first trains a dynamics model $\hat{f}$ from the expert demonstrations. The objective is to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between predicted state changes and observed state changes:
 $MSE = E_{(s^*_t, a^*_t, s^*_{t+1}) \sim D^*} [\hat{f}(s^*_t, a^*_t) + s^*_t - s^*_{t+1}]$.
 Crucially, CCIL leverages the concept of local Lipschitz continuity. To encourage the learned neural network dynamics model $\hat{f}$ to exhibit this property, the training process includes spectral normalization regularization. This modifies the weight matrices $W_i$ of the neural network layers by scaling them with respect to a desired Lipschitz constant $K$, such that $W_i \leftarrow \frac{W_i}{\max(\|W_i\|_2, K^{-n})} \cdot K^{-n}$, where $n$ is the number of layers. The local Lipschitz constant at any state-action $(s, a)$ is approximated by the spectral norm of the Jacobian matrix, $K(s, a) \approx \|J_{\hat{f}}(s, a)\|_2$.

2. **Generating and Filtering Corrective Labels:** For each expert transition $(s^*_t, a^*_t, s^*_{t+1})$, a corrective label $(s^G_t, a^G_t)$ is generated. The BackTrack label generator is used:
 $s^G_t \leftarrow s^*_t - \hat{f}(s^*_t, a^*_t)$
 $a^G_t \leftarrow a^*_t$
 This aims to find a state $s^G_t$ from which taking action $a^*_t$ would lead back to $s^*_t$ according to the learned dynamics.

 A critical component of this work is the *refined label filtering process*. Unlike previous CCIL formulations that filter labels based solely on a fixed distance threshold $\delta$ (i.e., $\|s^G_t - s^*_t\| > \delta$), this work proposes filtering based on an estimated error bound for each generated label. This bound depends on the training error $\epsilon$, the local Lipschitz coefficients of the learned dynamics $\hat{f}$ ($K_1$), and the true dynamics $f$ ($K_2$). Assuming $\epsilon \approx 0$ and $K_2 \propto K_1$, a label is rejected if:
 $\|J_{\hat{f}}(s^*_t, a^*_t)\|_2 \cdot \|s^G_t - s^*_t\| \geq \sigma$, where $\sigma$ is a hyperparameter representing the acceptable label error bound. In practice, $\sigma$ is chosen as a quantile of the distribution of error bounds computed for all generated labels. This method allows for a "trust region" around expert data that varies dynamically based on the local continuity properties.

**C. Experimental Setup**

- **Hardware:** Experiments were conducted on a 7-DOF HEBI robot equipped with a chopstick end-effector (Fig. 2a). The robot operates with a hierarchical controller, receiving 6-DOF end-effector pose targets plus 1-DOF for gripper control at 20Hz. A motion capture system provided perception.
- **Tasks:** Three fine manipulation tasks were selected, requiring millimeter-level precision:
 - **GraspCube:** Grasping and lifting a 1cm cube.
 - **GearInsertion:** Inserting a Lego gear into a hole (0.2mm tolerance).
 - **GraspCoin:** Grasping and lifting a thin, slippery metal coin.
- **Data Collection:** Demonstrations were collected via a mix of heuristic controllers (GraspCube: 500 trajectories, GearInsertion: 100 trajectories) and human teleoperation (GraspCoin: 200 successful trajectories). Failed demonstrations were filtered out. Initial object positions varied.
- **Parameter Tuning:**
 - **Lipschitz Constraint (K):** An initial unconstrained model was trained, and its local Lipschitz coefficient distribution analyzed. A tighter constraint was chosen if limited local continuity was observed.
 - **Label Rejection Threshold ($\sigma$):** Corrective labels were generated without filtering, their error bound distribution analyzed, and $\sigma$ was set to filter out a specific quantile of outliers or long tails.
- **Evaluation:** Each agent's success rate was tested across 48 trials (16 fixed initial conditions, 3 trials each). Success was a binary variable. Two-proportion z-tests were performed to determine the statistical significance of performance differences ($p < 0.05$).

**D. Hypotheses**
The experimental design specifically aimed to test four hypotheses:
- **H1:** CCIL improves imitation learning policies in real-world fine-manipulation tasks, particularly in low-data regimes.
- **H2:** Real-world tasks with complex contacts can still exhibit sufficient local Lipschitz continuity for CCIL's assumptions.
- **H3:** CCIL's performance is sensitive to the label rejection hyperparameter.
- **H4:** CCIL's performance is relatively robust to variations in the local Lipschitz constraint enforced during dynamics model training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive experiments across three fine manipulation tasks, training 36 agents for primary evaluation and an additional 33 for ablation studies. The findings address the core hypotheses and provide empirical insights into CCIL's performance in real-world robotic scenarios.

**A. Corrective Labels' Improvement to Imitation Learning (H1)**
The results indicate that CCIL generally boosts the performance of behavior cloning agents in real-world fine manipulation tasks, with particular effectiveness in low-data regimes.

- **Overall Performance Boost:** CCIL-trained agents demonstrated an increase in success rate across all three tasks compared to baseline behavior cloning. For GraspCube, success rate increased from 23% to 83% (with 100 trajectories). For GearInsertion, it improved from 6% to 17% (with 20 trajectories) and from 58% to 72% (with 100 trajectories). For GraspCoin, it rose from 17% to 48% (with 200 trajectories).
- **Statistical Significance in Low-Data Regimes:** The performance improvements observed were statistically significant ($p < 0.05$) for the GraspCube and GraspCoin tasks. For GearInsertion, significance was observed at higher data volumes (100 trajectories) and very low data (20 trajectories), but not in intermediate ranges. This suggests that CCIL is most beneficial when expert demonstrations are limited, where covariate shift poses a greater challenge.

**B. CCIL's Assumptions on Local Lipschitz Continuity (H2)**
The experiments provided evidence that real-world fine manipulation tasks exhibit sufficient local Lipschitz continuity to allow for the application of CCIL, even in the presence of contact-rich dynamics.

- **Learned Dynamics Model Properties:** When training dynamics models, a loose upper bound on the Lipschitz constraint resulted in local Lipschitz coefficients that converged to those of an unconstrained model (Fig. 3b). This indicates that the environments possess inherent local continuity that the learned models are capable of fitting, even without strict enforcement.
- **Correlation of Continuity with Real-World Regimes:** An analysis of generated corrective labels for the GraspCube task revealed two distinct clusters based on their local Lipschitz coefficients and label distance (Fig. 4a). The "blue" cluster (lower local Lipschitz coefficients, smaller label distance) primarily corresponded to free-space robot movements, while the "green" cluster (higher local Lipschitz coefficients, larger label distance) was associated with states involving contact with the cube (Fig. 4b).
- **Impact of Label Quality:** Policies trained using only the "blue" (free-space, low-error) labels showed an improved success rate, demonstrating the benefit of high-quality corrective labels. Conversely, incorporating "green" (contact-rich, higher-error) labels alone, or both sets of labels, did not yield the same level of improvement, and in some cases, diminished performance (Fig. 4c). This highlights the importance of filtering generated labels based on their estimated error.

**C. Impact of the Quality of Generated Labels (H3)**
The efficacy of CCIL was found to be highly sensitive to the label rejection threshold, which controls the quality of generated labels incorporated into the training dataset.

- **Harm from Low-Quality Labels:** Including all generated labels (i.e., a high label rejection quantile) could lead to policy performance that was similar to or worse than behavior cloning without CCIL (Fig. 5). This is attributed to the presence of high-error labels, which, if incorrect under the true dynamics, can negatively impact policy learning.
- **Balance Between Error and Usefulness:** Optimal CCIL performance was achieved at intermediate label rejection quantiles (e.g., around 0.2 and 0.8 for GraspCube, Fig. 5). This suggests a need to balance the trustworthiness of labels (lower error bounds, typically closer to expert data) with their potential to expand data support (labels further from expert data but potentially with higher error). Filtering out extreme outliers while retaining a reasonable number of beneficial corrective labels proved crucial.

**D. Impact of Continuity Constraint (H4)**
CCIL's performance demonstrated relative robustness to variations in the Lipschitz constraint enforced during the training of the dynamics model.

- **Robustness to Constraint Level:** Across different Lipschitz constraints, there consistently existed a label rejection quantile that led to a statistically significant performance boost over behavior cloning (Fig. 6). This implies that CCIL can function effectively even with a range of Lipschitz constraints.
- **Effectiveness with Unconstrained Models:** Notably, CCIL still performed well with an "unconstrained" dynamics model (infinite Lipschitz constraint) at certain label rejection quantiles (e.g., 20% and 80%). This finding is supported by the observation that even unconstrained models can exhibit sufficient local continuity (Fig. 3b), suggesting that explicit enforcement of a Lipschitz constraint may not always be strictly necessary in environments with inherent smoothness.

These results collectively indicate that CCIL is a viable framework for real-world fine manipulation, provided that attention is paid to the quality of generated labels through appropriate filtering.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of robotic imitation learning by providing the first empirical validation of the Continuity-based Corrective Labels for Imitation Learning (CCIL) framework on real-world fine manipulation tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Bridging the Simulation-to-Reality Gap for CCIL:** Prior work on CCIL primarily demonstrated its effectiveness in simulation. This paper's empirical validation on physical robots for complex fine manipulation tasks, which involve inherently discontinuous contact dynamics, provides crucial evidence that CCIL's theoretical assumptions, particularly local Lipschitz continuity, are sufficiently applicable in real-world scenarios. This strengthens the case for CCIL as a practical method beyond simulated environments.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robotic Learning:** A persistent challenge in deploying imitation learning on physical robots is the high cost and difficulty of collecting large volumes of expert demonstrations. The findings demonstrate CCIL's significant performance boost, especially in low-data regimes, for precision-demanding tasks like grasping and insertion. This indicates that CCIL can enhance data efficiency, potentially reducing the amount of expert data required to train robust policies, thereby making imitation learning more accessible and scalable for new robotic applications.
- **Providing Practical Guidelines for Implementation:** The extensive ablation studies offer practical insights into tuning key hyperparameters for CCIL. The finding that optimal performance is highly sensitive to the *label rejection threshold* (which controls the quality of generated corrective labels) and relatively robust to the *Lipschitz constraint* for dynamics model training is valuable. This guidance simplifies the deployment of CCIL by informing design choices and reducing the need for costly, exhaustive real-world hyperparameter sweeps. The observation that unconstrained dynamics models can still yield effective results under CCIL further simplifies its application.
- **Enhanced Robustness to Covariate Shift:** By effectively generating corrective labels that guide the robot back towards expert states, CCIL directly addresses the problem of covariate shift. This capability is critical for improving the robustness and reliability of learned policies when encountering out-of-distribution states during execution, leading to fewer task failures and more dependable robotic behavior.
- **Enabling Learning in Contact-Rich Environments:** The empirical demonstration that CCIL can operate effectively in contact-rich manipulation tasks, despite the inherent discontinuities, opens avenues for its application in a broader range of robotic domains, including assembly, surgical robotics, and assistive robotics, where precise physical interaction is paramount.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a robust baseline and validation methodology for CCIL on real robots. It naturally paves the way for future research directions, such as extending CCIL to high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., images), exploring different policy architectures (e.g., diffusion policies), and investigating more advanced techniques for dynamics modeling and label generation under realistic contact constraints.

Overall, this research validates CCIL as a practical and effective tool for making imitation learning more robust and data-efficient in real-world fine manipulation, addressing critical barriers to the widespread adoption of learning-based control in robotics.
