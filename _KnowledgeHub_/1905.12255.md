# Stay on the Path: Instruction Fidelity in Vision-and-Language Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces a new metric, Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS), and a more challenging dataset, Room-for-Room (R4R), to evaluate instruction fidelity in Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN). It demonstrates that training agents with fidelity-oriented rewards on R4R significantly improves their adherence to natural language instructions, with CLS scores increasing by approximately 70% compared to goal-oriented training.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) metrics, focused on goal completion (e.g., Success Rate, SPL), fail to adequately measure an agent's true fidelity to the entire natural language instruction and the reference path.
- The foundational Room-to-Room (R2R) dataset uses 'direct-to-goal shortest paths,' which inadvertently allows agents to achieve high scores by reaching the goal efficiently rather than genuinely following potentially winding instructions.
- It is often ambiguous whether VLN agents genuinely understand and follow language instructions throughout the navigation task, or if they exploit dataset biases and visual cues for efficient goal-seeking.

## Method

- Proposed **Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS)**, a novel metric designed to explicitly measure instruction fidelity by incorporating ==path coverage== and ==soft penalties== for deviations from the reference path.
- Introduced **Room-for-Room (R4R)**, a more challenging dataset created by concatenating existing R2R paths to generate longer, 'twistier' trajectories that decouple goal completion from strict instruction following.
- Trained a strong baseline agent (==Reinforced Cross-Modal Matching, RCM==) using both traditional goal-oriented rewards and a new ==fidelity-oriented reward== function that incorporates the proposed CLS metric.

## Results

- On the R4R dataset, the fidelity-oriented agent achieved a CLS score of **34.6%** on the Validation Unseen split, substantially outperforming the goal-oriented agent's **20.4%**.
- Ablation studies on R4R showed the fidelity-oriented agent's CLS score significantly degraded (from **34.6%** to **25.3%**) when provided with only the last five tokens of instructions, demonstrating its reliance on full language input, whereas the goal-oriented agent's CLS remained largely unchanged.
- All model variants demonstrated significantly lower performance on R4R compared to R2R, confirming R4R's effectiveness as a more challenging benchmark for evaluating instruction following capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing VLN metrics and datasets like R2R are largely insensitive to instruction fidelity, as agents can achieve high performance by simply reaching the goal, often without genuinely interpreting complex instructions.
> - Agents explicitly rewarded for instruction fidelity using the CLS metric learn to rely more heavily on the full natural language instructions, demonstrating deeper language grounding compared to goal-oriented agents.
> - The R4R dataset successfully serves as a more robust benchmark, revealing significant performance gaps and highlighting the true challenge of multi-step, sequential instruction following in VLN.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the rapidly evolving field of **Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN)**, a challenging embodied AI task that requires an agent to interpret natural language instructions and visual scenes to navigate through an environment to reach a specified goal. VLN builds upon advancements in connecting language to other modalities, such as image and video captioning, visual question answering (VQA), and visual dialogue, but distinguishes itself by additionally demanding active planning, movement, and dynamic response to sensory inputs in a 3D environment.

The paper focuses specifically on VLN within photo-realistic simulations, particularly the widely used **Room-to-Room (R2R) dataset** (Anderson et al., 2018b). Since its introduction, R2R has spurred significant progress, with models like Speaker-Follower (Fried et al., 2018) and Reinforced Cross-modal Matching (RCM) (Wang et al., 2019) demonstrating increasingly capable navigation. However, as the field matured, a critical issue emerged: despite impressive goal completion rates, it remained unclear how much of this success was attributable to genuine language understanding and instruction following versus agents merely exploiting dataset biases or using visual cues to take the most direct path to a goal.

Previous works had hinted at these limitations. For instance, Thomason et al. (2019) showed that R2R agents could perform surprisingly well even without language input, suggesting an over-reliance on visual cues and environmental structure. Similarly, while augmented instruction data (Fried et al., 2018) improved performance, subsequent analysis (Huang et al., 2019) revealed that many of these augmented instructions had low fidelity to the paths, implying that existing metrics were not sensitive enough to instruction adherence. Anderson et al. (2018a) also proposed the Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) metric to penalize long, inefficient paths, but even SPL primarily focuses on goal-reaching efficiency rather than explicit instruction following throughout the entire journey.

Jain et al.'s work directly addresses these shortcomings. It identifies a crucial gap: the dominance of goal-completion-centric metrics and the "direct-to-goal shortest path" nature of the R2R dataset itself. By introducing a new metric, **Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS)**, and a more challenging dataset, **Room-for-Room (R4R)**, this paper provides a robust framework for evaluating an agent's true fidelity to natural language instructions. This work pushes the VLN community towards developing agents that not only reach the goal but do so by genuinely understanding and following the *path prescribed by the language*, thereby deepening the "L" in VLN and laying the groundwork for more reliable and interpretable embodied AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research stem from a critical observation: despite significant progress in Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN), the true extent of language understanding by navigation agents remains ambiguous due to inherent limitations in existing datasets and evaluation metrics.

**Objective 1: Highlight and Rectify Shortcomings of Current VLN Metrics.**
- **Motivation:** Existing metrics, predominantly focused on goal completion (e.g., Success Rate, Navigation Error, Success weighted by Path Length – SPL), fail to adequately measure an agent's fidelity to the entire natural language instruction and the corresponding reference path. An agent might reach the destination by taking a direct, uninstructed shortcut, yet still achieve a high score under these metrics. This masks whether the agent genuinely *understands* and *follows* the language or simply finds an efficient way to the goal, potentially exploiting environmental biases. The authors argue that such a focus undervalues the "language understanding" component of VLN.

**Objective 2: Propose a Novel Metric for Instruction Fidelity.**
- **Motivation:** To address the deficiencies of existing metrics, the authors propose **Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS)**. The motivation is to create a metric that explicitly penalizes deviations from the reference path, rewards adherence to the full instruction sequence, provides soft penalties for minor deviations, and ensures a unique optimal score for a perfect path match. CLS aims to provide a more holistic evaluation of an agent's ability to "stay on the path."

**Objective 3: Develop a More Challenging Dataset for Evaluating Instruction Following.**
- **Motivation:** The **Room-to-Room (R2R)** dataset, while foundational, suffers from a critical design flaw: all reference paths are "direct-to-goal shortest paths." This characteristic inadvertently aligns goal completion with path conformity, making it difficult to distinguish agents that genuinely follow instructions from those that simply navigate efficiently to a goal. To overcome this, the authors introduce **Room-for-Room (R4R)**, an extension of R2R. The motivation for R4R is to create longer, "twistier" paths by concatenating existing R2R paths, thereby decoupling goal completion from instruction fidelity and making true instruction following more essential for success.

**Objective 4: Demonstrate the Efficacy of Fidelity-Oriented Training and Evaluation.**
- **Motivation:** With the new CLS metric and R4R dataset, the authors aim to demonstrate that agents explicitly rewarded for instruction fidelity (using CLS as a reward signal) will learn to follow natural language instructions more accurately than agents trained solely on goal-oriented rewards. This objective seeks to validate the utility of their proposed metric and dataset in driving research towards agents with deeper and more reliable language understanding for navigation tasks. Furthermore, through ablation studies, they aim to show that fidelity-oriented agents truly rely on the *full* language instructions, unlike goal-oriented agents that might achieve similar performance with only partial instructions.

In essence, the core motivation is to ensure that VLN agents are evaluated and trained not just on *where* they end up, but *how* they get there, fostering the development of truly language-grounded navigation capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach, combining critical analysis of existing evaluation paradigms, the development of new tools (a metric and a dataset), and empirical validation through agent training and experimentation.

#### 3.1. Critique of Existing Navigation Metrics

The authors begin by outlining five desiderata for ideal VLN metrics: (1) Path similarity measure, (2) Soft penalties for deviations, (3) Unique optimum for exact matches, (4) Scale invariance, and (5) Computational tractability. They then evaluate commonly used metrics against these desiderata:
- **Path Length (PL), Navigation Error (NE), Oracle Navigation Error (ONE), Success Rate (SR), Oracle Success Rate (OSR):** These are criticized for being primarily goal-oriented, ignoring the intermediate steps of the reference path, and thus violating Desideratum (1).
- **Success weighted by Path Length (SPL):** While an improvement by penalizing long paths, it still focuses on the shortest path to the *goal* rather than the reference path itself, violating Desideratum (1) and (3).
- **Success weighted by Edit Distance (SED):** This metric moves in the right direction by considering path similarity through Levenshtein distance of action sequences. It satisfies Desiderata (1), (3), (4), and (5). However, its main limitation is the "hard penalty" nature of edit distance, treating any difference as equally costly regardless of magnitude (e.g., a slight turn deviation is penalized as much as a 180-degree turn in a different direction), thus failing Desideratum (2) for soft penalties.

#### 3.2. Proposed Metric: Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS)

To overcome the limitations of existing metrics, the authors propose CLS, defined as the product of **Path Coverage (PC)** and **Length Score (LS)**: `CLS(P, R) = PC(P, R) · LS(P, R)`.
- **Path Coverage (PC):** This term measures how well the agent's predicted path `P` covers the reference path `R`. It is calculated as the average exponential decay of distances from each node in `R` to its nearest node in `P`. The exponential decay ensures *soft penalties* (Desideratum 2), where small deviations are penalized less severely than large ones. PC also directly considers *all nodes* in both paths, satisfying Desideratum (1).
- **Length Score (LS):** This term penalizes the predicted path's length (`PL(P)`) against an "expected optimal length" (`EPL(P, R)`). `EPL` is calculated as `PC(P, R) · PL(R)`, meaning if an agent only covers half of the reference path, its optimal length is expected to be half of the reference path's total length. LS penalizes paths that are too short (didn't cover enough) or too long (inefficient for the covered portion).
CLS is shown to satisfy all five desiderata, providing a comprehensive and robust measure of instruction fidelity.

#### 3.3. Proposed Dataset: Room-for-Room (R4R)

Recognizing the limitations of R2R's "shortest-to-goal" paths, the authors introduce R4R through a data augmentation strategy:
- **R2R Limitations:** R2R paths are short (4-6 edges) and always the shortest distance between start and goal. This means agents can succeed by simply navigating to the goal, not necessarily following the specific, possibly winding, instructions.
- **R4R Construction:** R4R is created by concatenating pairs of existing R2R paths and their corresponding instructions. Two paths A and B are joined if the goal of A is within a threshold distance of the start of B. A shortest path `C` is inserted to bridge any gap. This process generates significantly longer and more "twisty" paths, making instruction fidelity critical, as the direct-to-goal path for an R4R trajectory might be very different from the combined reference path. The combined instructions are also used. R4R thus provides a more challenging and realistic benchmark for instruction following.

#### 3.4. Agent Model and Training

The authors re-implement the **Reinforced Cross-Modal Matching (RCM) agent** from Wang et al. (2019), a strong baseline for VLN tasks.
- **Architecture:** The agent utilizes an LSTM-based navigator, a language encoder (GloVe embeddings + bidirectional RNN), and a visual encoder (CNN features from panoramic views + orientation features, pooled using attention). An action predictor uses a bilinear dot product with attention to language and visual context to select navigable directions.
- **Training Strategy:** Training consists of two phases:
 1. **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** An initial warm-up phase where the agent learns from expert demonstrations by maximizing the likelihood of ground-truth actions.
 2. **REINFORCE Policy Gradient Updates:** After BC, the agent is fine-tuned using policy gradients, with rewards received for sampled action sequences. The authors employ an interleaved training strategy where BC and policy gradient batches are mixed, with the BC contribution decaying over time.
- **Reward Functions:**
 - **Goal-oriented reward:** A dense reward that gives a positive signal for getting closer to the final target location at each step and a bonus for successfully reaching the goal at the end of the episode (Equation 11).
 - **Fidelity-oriented reward:** A sparse reward that combines the goal success bonus with the **CLS score** of the entire predicted path at the end of the episode (Equation 12). This explicitly incentivizes following the *entire* reference path.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Experiments compare goal-oriented and fidelity-oriented agents on both the original R2R and the new R4R datasets, across validation seen and unseen environments. Ablation studies are performed where agents are evaluated with only the last 5 tokens of instructions or no instructions at all, to assess their reliance on the full language input. Performance is measured using both standard metrics (PL, NE, SR, SPL) and the proposed CLS.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on both the original R2R and the newly constructed R4R datasets, using goal-oriented and fidelity-oriented reward functions, yielded several key insights:

1. **R2R Dataset's Insensitivity to Instruction Fidelity:**
 - On the R2R dataset, there were no significant performance differences between agents trained with goal-oriented rewards and those trained with fidelity-oriented rewards (Table 3). This supports the authors' hypothesis that R2R's direct-to-goal shortest paths implicitly align goal completion with path conformity. Consequently, an agent optimized purely for reaching the goal often *appears* to be following instructions, even if it's not explicitly learning language fidelity.
 - Ablation studies on R2R showed that providing only the last five tokens of instructions accounted for a significant portion of the performance difference between full instructions and no instructions. This highlights that for R2R, the crucial part of language understanding often boils down to identifying the correct *stopping point* rather than truly following the entire described journey.

2. **R4R Dataset as a More Challenging and Revealing Benchmark:**
 - Overall scores for all model variants were significantly lower on R4R compared to R2R (Table 4). This confirms that R4R, with its longer and twistier concatenated paths, presents a considerably greater challenge for navigation agents, necessitating more robust instruction following.

3. **Fidelity-Oriented Agents Outperform on Instruction Fidelity in R4R:**
 - On the R4R dataset, the agent trained with the **fidelity-oriented reward (incorporating CLS)** dramatically outperformed the goal-oriented agent in terms of **CLS score** (e.g., 34.6% vs. 20.4% on R4R Validation Unseen). This is the most critical finding, demonstrating that explicitly rewarding for path conformity using the CLS metric successfully guides agent learning towards better instruction following.
 - Notably, the fidelity-oriented agent also achieved a lower navigation error (8.08m vs. 8.45m on R4R Validation Unseen), suggesting that better instruction fidelity can also lead to more accurate goal reaching, even if not directly optimized for it in a shortest-path sense.

4. **CLS-Rewarded Agents Exhibit Greater Reliance on Language:**
 - The ablation studies on R4R further reinforced the benefits of fidelity-oriented training. When instructions were removed or reduced to the last five tokens:
 - The goal-oriented agent's CLS performance barely degraded (20.4% with full instructions vs. 20.4% with last 5 tokens on R4R Validation Unseen), indicating it did not rely heavily on the *full* language instructions for its (already low) path conformity.
 - In contrast, the fidelity-oriented agent's CLS score significantly dropped (34.6% to 25.3% with last 5 tokens, and further to 23.0% with no instructions on R4R Validation Unseen). This demonstrates that the fidelity-oriented agent had genuinely learned to utilize and rely on the *entire* natural language instruction for successful navigation, thereby keeping the "L" in VLN prominent.

5. **Limitations of SPL Highlighted by R4R:**
 - The results show that metrics like SPL can be misleading in scenarios with non-shortest-path instructions. For instance, on R4R, the goal-oriented agent sometimes achieved a higher SPL score than the fidelity-oriented agent (e.g., 10.2% vs. 7.7% on R4R Validation Unseen), even when its instruction fidelity (CLS) was vastly inferior. This crucial observation, also illustrated in Figure 1, underscores that SPL primarily measures efficient goal arrival, not adherence to a specified, potentially winding, path.

In summary, the research successfully demonstrates that current VLN evaluation practices on R2R underestimate the importance of genuine language understanding. By introducing R4R and CLS, the authors provide a more robust framework that reveals the true challenge of instruction following and incentivizes agents to learn a deeper, more faithful interpretation of natural language navigation commands.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a substantial contribution to the field of Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) by addressing fundamental limitations in current evaluation methodologies and datasets. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Reframing VLN Evaluation for True Language Understanding:** The paper's most immediate and impactful contribution is the critical re-evaluation of how VLN agents are assessed. By clearly articulating the shortcomings of goal-completion-centric metrics and the "shortest-to-goal" bias of datasets like R2R, it compels the community to focus on *instruction fidelity* rather than just *goal attainment*. This shift is crucial for developing agents that truly "understand" language, moving beyond merely exploiting environmental shortcuts or biases.

2. **Introducing a Robust Metric (CLS):** The proposed Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS) provides a scientifically sound and practical metric for evaluating instruction fidelity. By incorporating soft penalties, path coverage, and length adherence, CLS offers a nuanced and comprehensive measure that aligns with intuitive notions of "staying on the path." This metric is likely to become a new standard for evaluating VLN agents, particularly as the field matures and seeks to build more reliable and human-interpretable systems.

3. **Creating a More Challenging and Realistic Dataset (R4R):** The Room-for-Room (R4R) dataset, derived from R2R but featuring longer, twistier, and semantically richer paths, offers a significantly more challenging benchmark. This new dataset is instrumental in revealing the true gap between current agent capabilities and human-level instruction following. It provides a fertile ground for developing novel architectures and training strategies that specifically address the complexities of multi-step, sequential language instructions.

4. **Guiding Future Research and Development:** By demonstrating that agents explicitly rewarded for instruction fidelity (via CLS) learn to rely more heavily on language, the paper provides a clear roadmap for future research. It encourages the development of models that prioritize robust language grounding and sequential decision-making over mere goal-seeking. The substantial "headroom" identified on R4R (low CLS scores even for the best agents) indicates that this area remains ripe for innovation.

5. **Practical Relevance for Real-World Robotics:** The emphasis on instruction fidelity has profound practical implications. In real-world scenarios, a robot following instructions must do so precisely, not just efficiently. As the authors eloquently state, "going straight to the goal could be literally deadly to the robot or agent" in applications like search-and-rescue, navigating around traps, or hazardous environments. This work lays the groundwork for creating safer, more dependable, and more trustworthy autonomous systems that can execute human commands with high precision and adherence.

6. **Influence from Google Research:** Given its origin from Google Research, a leading AI powerhouse, this work is poised for significant adoption and influence within the academic and industrial research communities. The involvement of prominent researchers like Ashish Vaswani and Jason Baldridge further solidifies its credibility and potential to shape the trajectory of VLN research.

In conclusion, "Stay on the Path" is a seminal paper that critically assesses and provides concrete solutions to a fundamental problem in VLN. By offering a better toolkit for evaluation (CLS) and a more challenging environment (R4R), it significantly advances the field's capacity to develop agents that genuinely understand and faithfully execute natural language navigation instructions, paving the way for more intelligent and reliable embodied AI.
