# TurboVLA: Real-Time Vision-Language-Action Model at 32 Hz on an RTX 4090 with <1 GB VRAM

> [!summary] Summary
> TURBOVLA introduces an efficient vision-language-action model that achieves real-time robotic manipulation at 32 Hz with under 1 GB VRAM on an RTX 4090, directly mapping visual and linguistic features to actions. This approach matches or surpasses the manipulation performance of larger, LLM-centric systems, significantly lowering hardware requirements for deployment.

## Problem

- Existing LLM-centric Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models exhibit high computational and memory overhead due to large language models, leading to substantial inference latency.
- The high latency prevents real-time robotic execution, limiting control frequency and hindering responsive interaction and high-throughput manipulation.
- High VRAM usage and large model sizes in LLM-centric VLAs constrain deployment to high-end GPUs or cloud infrastructure, increasing costs and limiting edge device deployment.

## Method

- **TurboVLA** reformulates the VLA paradigm to a direct V + L A (Vision + Language Action) mapping, bypassing large language models as central mediators.
- It employs compact modality-specific encoders (==DINOv3== for vision, ==BERT== for text) and a lightweight ==bidirectional vision-language interaction== module to fuse features efficiently.
- A ==non-autoregressive continuous action chunk decoder==, inspired by ==ACT==, predicts entire sequences of robot actions in a single forward pass, minimizing decoding costs.

## Results

- TurboVLA achieved an inference latency of **31.2 ms** (**32 Hz**) and used only **0.9 GB** VRAM on an RTX 4090 with a model size of **0.2 billion** parameters.
- It demonstrated competitive manipulation success rates of **97.7%** on LIBERO and **60.2%** on RoboTwin 2.0, matching or outperforming larger, less efficient LLM-centric VLA models.
- Real-world experiments on an AgileX Piper platform validated its practical deployability and superior performance compared to π0.5 under identical conditions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective execution-level robotic control and language-conditioned manipulation do not necessitate the broad reasoning and generative capabilities of a large language model at every step.
> - Direct, lightweight, and bidirectional cross-modal interaction between visual and linguistic features is crucial for efficiently constructing control-oriented representations and outperforms one-way or no interaction.
> - The combination of compact encoders, efficient cross-modal fusion, and non-autoregressive action decoding enables high-frequency, real-time VLA performance with significantly reduced computational and memory footprints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models has gained prominence for enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks conditioned by natural language instructions. Current VLA systems frequently adopt an "LLM-centric" architecture, characterized by a V → L → A (Vision → Language Model → Action) pathway. In this paradigm, visual observations are typically projected into the representation space of a large language model (LLM), combined with the task instruction, processed by the LLM, and then decoded into robot actions. This design facilitates the transfer of broad semantic knowledge from large-scale pretraining, supporting open-vocabulary understanding, semantic generalization, and high-level reasoning in robotic control.

Despite these advantages, the LLM-centric approach introduces significant computational and memory overhead. The involvement of large language models, often comprising billions of parameters, results in high inference latency and substantial GPU memory consumption. This poses a challenge for real-time robotic execution, which is critical for responsive interaction, high-throughput manipulation, and deployment on resource-constrained platforms, such as edge devices or consumer-grade hardware. Previous efforts to improve VLA efficiency have focused on action-side redesigns (e.g., continuous action experts, parallel decoding) or backbone-side optimizations (e.g., quantization, pruning, distillation). While these methods can accelerate action generation or reduce some computational load, they often retain the large multimodal backbone, thus perpetuating a core bottleneck.

TurboVLA addresses this specific limitation by proposing a direct V + L → A mapping, fundamentally re-architecting the VLA pipeline. Instead of a large language model acting as the central interface, TurboVLA directly fuses visual and linguistic features through lightweight, bidirectional interaction, constructing control-oriented representations without the expansive computational demands of an LLM. This work positions itself as an alternative to the prevailing LLM-centric paradigm, suggesting that execution-level control can be effective without relying on the extensive reasoning and generative capabilities of a large language model at every step. It aligns with a complementary line of research exploring more direct language interfaces for robot control, where textual instructions condition perception and control rather than serving as prompts for generative language modeling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient VLA model capable of real-time, language-conditioned robotic manipulation while significantly reducing computational and memory resource requirements. The authors aim to establish a VLA paradigm that provides competitive manipulation performance but operates at high frequencies with minimal hardware demands, making it suitable for practical deployment on resource-constrained platforms.

The motivation for this work stems from identified limitations in prevailing LLM-centric VLA architectures:
- **Computational and Memory Bottlenecks:** Existing VLA models typically use large language models as a central processing unit for multimodal inputs. This design involves substantial computation and memory overhead for every policy invocation, due to the billions of parameters in these LLMs and the need to process visual observations through them.
- **Constraints on Real-time Execution:** The high inference latency associated with LLM processing creates a significant bottleneck for real-time robotic execution. Responsive interaction and high-frequency control are often essential for robust and agile manipulation, particularly in dynamic environments or when interacting with humans. The limited control frequency (e.g., 11 Hz for LLM-centric models compared to 32 Hz for TurboVLA) hinders practical application.
- **High Deployment Costs:** The considerable VRAM requirements and large model sizes of LLM-centric VLAs necessitate high-end GPUs or remote server infrastructure, increasing deployment costs and reliance on network connectivity. This limits their deployment on local, consumer-grade hardware or edge devices where low latency and reliability are paramount.
- **Over-specification for Execution-level Control:** The authors observe that while language is essential for instruction-conditioned manipulation, execution-level control may not require the broad reasoning and generative capacity of a general-purpose LLM. Once an instruction specifies a skill, the policy primarily needs to integrate visual evidence with the instruction to guide action, rather than performing open-ended language generation or complex task decomposition. A lightweight text encoder and compact cross-modal interaction might suffice for this purpose.

Therefore, the authors are motivated to design a "simple, elegant and efficient VLA that directly maps vision and language to actions for execution-level manipulation, without centering on a large language model." This involves exploring an alternative architecture that prioritizes efficiency and real-time performance without compromising manipulation capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TurboVLA reformulates the conventional V → L → A pathway into a direct V + L → A mapping. The core design principles involve using compact modality-specific encoders, lightweight bidirectional vision-language interaction, and a non-autoregressive continuous action chunk decoder.

The architecture of TurboVLA comprises three main stages, as illustrated in Figure 3(a):

#### 3.1. Multimodal Feature Encoding

To reduce overhead and provide sufficient instruction understanding for execution-level tasks, TurboVLA employs compact, modality-specific encoders:
- **Visual Encoder:** DINOv3 is utilized as the visual backbone. For each camera observation $I^{(i)}_n$, the image encoder extracts spatial visual features. These features are then projected into a shared hidden dimension $d$, and augmented with positional embeddings $E^{(i)}_{pos}$ (to preserve spatial structure within a view) and camera-view embeddings $e^{(i)}_{view}$ (to identify the source camera). Features from multiple camera streams (K) are concatenated to form $Z^v_n$.
- **Lightweight Text Encoder:** BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) is used to encode task instructions $x$. The text encoder generates token-level instruction features $f_{text}(x)$, which are then projected into the policy dimension $d$ to form $Z_l \in R^{N_l \times d}$. The complete token sequence is retained to provide fine-grained information about objects, attributes, and spatial relations for visual conditioning.
- **Robot State Encoding:** The current robot state $s_n$ is encoded separately by a lightweight projection network $f_{state}$ into $Z^s_n$. These state features are directly fed into the action decoder, allowing the vision-language interaction module to focus solely on task-conditioned scene understanding.

This stage replaces the high-dimensional LLM interface with compact feature sequences, reducing intermediate activation memory and attention costs while preserving essential control information.

#### 3.2. Vision-Language Interaction Module

This module is designed to directly exchange information between visual and language features, circumventing the need for an LLM as a central mediator (Figure 3(b)). It comprises N bidirectional cross-modal layers (`FusionLayer`), which progressively update both visual and instruction feature streams.
- Each `FusionLayer` takes the current visual features $V^{\ell-1}_n$ and instruction features $L^{\ell-1}_n$ as input.
- It performs layer normalization, followed by bidirectional cross-attention:
 - **Visual-to-Instruction Cross-Attention:** Injects scene context into the instruction stream by using instruction features as queries and visual features as keys/values.
 - **Instruction-to-Visual Cross-Attention:** Conditions visual features on task semantics by using visual features as queries and instruction features as keys/values.
- Modality-specific feed-forward networks and residual connections are applied after attention.
- After N such layers, the updated streams $V^N_n$ and $L^N_n$ are concatenated to form the fused multimodal representation $Z^n_{vl}$.
This direct interaction allows the instruction to modulate relevant visual features, and the visual context to refine the instruction representation, efficiently constructing task-specific multimodal information for action prediction.

#### 3.3. Continuous Action Chunk Prediction

A lightweight transformer decoder, inspired by ACT (Zhao et al., 2023), is used to generate robot actions.
- The decoder takes the fused multimodal representation $Z^n_{vl}$, the robot state features $Z^s_n$, and a set of $H$ learnable action queries $Q_a = [q_1..., q_H]$ as input.
- It predicts a complete sequence of $H$ continuous actions, an "action chunk" $\hat{A}_n$, in a single forward pass. This non-autoregressive approach avoids the sequential decoding cost of token-based action generation.
- The model is trained using behavior cloning with an $\ell_1$ loss function, matching the predicted action chunks to expert action chunks $A^{*}_{n}$. No auxiliary language-modeling objective is required.

By integrating these components, TurboVLA establishes an efficient V + L → A execution pathway that significantly reduces model size, inference latency, and GPU memory usage compared to LLM-centric designs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates TurboVLA across simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboTwin 2.0) and real-world robotic tasks, focusing on manipulation performance, inference latency, and resource efficiency.

#### 4.1. Performance and Efficiency on LIBERO (Single-Arm Manipulation)

On the LIBERO benchmark (Table 1), TurboVLA demonstrates competitive manipulation performance while achieving significantly higher efficiency:
- **Manipulation Success:** TurboVLA achieved an average success rate of 97.7% across the LIBERO suites. This performance matches or marginally exceeds several "Capability-oriented VLAs" (e.g., π0.5 at 96.9%, VLA-JEPA at 97.2%) and outperforms "Acceleration-oriented VLAs" and "Lightweight VLAs."
- **Model Scale:** It contains only 0.2 billion parameters, approximately 6% of π0.5's parameter count (3.4B).
- **Inference Latency:** TurboVLA achieved an inference latency of 31.2 ms, enabling action updates at 32 Hz. This is substantially faster than π0.5 (93.6 ms) and other acceleration-oriented VLAs like DDVLA (60.8 ms) and OpenVLA-OFT (112.2 ms).
- **VRAM Usage:** It required only 0.9 GB of inference VRAM, which is significantly lower than competing methods that often demand several gigabytes (e.g., π0.5 uses 12.8 GB, VLA-JEPA uses 5.3 GB).
These results indicate that a direct V+L→A paradigm can achieve strong execution-level manipulation without the substantial computational and memory footprint of an LLM-centric design.

#### 4.2. Performance on RoboTwin 2.0 (Bimanual Manipulation)

For multi-task bimanual manipulation on RoboTwin 2.0 (Table 2), TurboVLA maintained its performance and efficiency advantages:
- **Manipulation Success:** It achieved an average success rate of 60.2% across 50 bimanual tasks.
- **Efficiency:** This was accomplished with 0.4 billion parameters (using a DINOv3 ViT-L configuration) and an inference latency of 43.4 ms.
- **Comparison:** TurboVLA outperformed both π0.5 (57.0% success at 95.6 ms) and StarVLA-α (50.3% success at 74.9 ms) on this benchmark.

#### 4.3. Real-World Evaluation

Real-world experiments on an AgileX Piper platform (Figure 4) further validated TurboVLA's practical deployability:
- **Task Success:** TurboVLA achieved success rates of 92.5% for "grab roller," 80% for "move playing card away," 90% for "press stapler," and 87.5% for "stack three bowls."
- **Comparison:** It consistently outperformed π0.5 under the same platform, training data, and evaluation protocol.
The real-world results corroborate the model's ability to operate effectively under real sensory noise and closed-loop execution, leveraging its architectural efficiency for practical deployment.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies provided insights into TurboVLA's design choices:
- **Semantic Language Conditioning (Table 3):** Removing language conditioning reduced average success significantly to 70.8% (e.g., LIBERO-Goal dropped to 11.6%), confirming the necessity of language for disambiguating behaviors. Using task-ID embeddings improved performance but still lagged behind semantic instructions, indicating the value of natural language's rich semantics.
- **Instruction Encoder (Table 4):** Testing with T5-small and SigLIP-Base text encoders showed competitive performance (97.1% and 95.5% average success, respectively), suggesting that lightweight text encoders are sufficient and the architecture is not tied to a specific text representation.
- **Vision-Language Interaction Design (Table 5):** Bidirectional interaction yielded the highest average success (97.7%), outperforming no interaction (direct concatenation, 95.2%), and asymmetric one-way cross-attention variants (96.1% for language querying visual, 96.5% for visual querying language). This supports the importance of mutually conditioning visual and linguistic features.
- **Interaction Depth (N) (Table 6):** Increasing interaction layers from N=2 (93.5%) to N=6 (97.7%) improved performance, while N=8 resulted in a slight degradation (96.6%), establishing N=6 as an effective balance.
- **Action Horizon (H) (Figure 6):** An action horizon of H=12 achieved the peak performance (97.7%), with shorter (H=8, 96.4%) and longer (H=15, 95.6%) horizons showing slightly reduced success.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TurboVLA research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by challenging the prevalent LLM-centric paradigm for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The work demonstrates that high-performance, real-time robotic control, conditioned by natural language, can be achieved without relying on a large language model as the central interface between perception and action.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Enabling Ubiquitous Robotic Deployment:** By achieving real-time action updates (32 Hz) with exceptionally low inference latency (31.2 ms), minimal VRAM usage (<1 GB), and a compact parameter count (0.2B), TurboVLA significantly lowers the hardware barrier for deploying sophisticated language-conditioned manipulation policies. This makes advanced robotic capabilities accessible on resource-constrained platforms, such as edge devices, embedded systems, or consumer-grade GPUs, without the need for expensive cloud infrastructure or high-end robotics hardware. This could accelerate the adoption of intelligent robots in various applications, from industrial settings to personal assistive robotics.
- **Redefining VLA Architectural Design:** The paper introduces a compelling alternative to the dominant V → L → A pathway by proposing a direct V + L → A mapping. This architectural shift suggests that for execution-level control, the comprehensive generative and reasoning capabilities of an LLM may be over-specified and computationally inefficient. This insight can guide future research in designing more purpose-built and efficient VLA models, encouraging the community to critically re-evaluate the role and placement of large language models within robotic control pipelines.
- **Advancing Performance-Efficiency Trade-offs:** TurboVLA establishes a new performance-efficiency frontier. It demonstrates that efficiency gains do not necessitate a compromise in manipulation task success; instead, it matches or surpasses the performance of substantially larger and more resource-intensive VLA models. This highlights the importance of optimizing for both capabilities and deployment metrics, encouraging a broader evaluation framework beyond mere task completion rates.
- **Foundation for Hierarchical Robotic Systems:** While TurboVLA is designed for concrete execution-level instructions, the authors acknowledge that complex semantic understanding and high-level task planning may still benefit from LLMs. The research lays the groundwork for future hierarchical robotic systems that could combine the efficient execution pathway of TurboVLA with the planning and reasoning capabilities of LLMs at a higher abstraction level, leading to systems that are both intelligent and efficient across diverse task complexities.
