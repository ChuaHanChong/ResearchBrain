# Visual Foresight: Model-Based Deep Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Based Robotic Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley and Google introduced "Visual Foresight," a model-based deep reinforcement learning framework that autonomously learns to predict future raw sensory observations for robotic control. This approach enables a single trained model to perform a wide range of manipulation tasks, including those with novel rigid and deformable objects, achieving a 75% success rate across 16 diverse scenarios.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning (RL) struggles with broad generalization to novel tasks and objects when applied to real-world robotics.
- Real-world robotic manipulation tasks often lack ground truth reward signals or low-dimensional state representations, which are difficult to engineer.
- Collecting large, diverse datasets for robotic learning is labor-intensive due to the need for human intervention for resets and reward labeling.

## Method

- An unsupervised data collection phase where a robot autonomously explores the environment by executing random actions and recording raw sensory observations.
- A ==Skip Connection Neural Advection== (**SNA**) recurrent neural network is trained to predict future pixel-level observations given current images and action sequences.
- A **Visual MPC** (Model Predictive Control) framework at test time uses the trained predictive model to plan actions via a sampling-based optimization (==Cross-Entropy Method==, CEM) to achieve user-defined goals.

## Results

- The occlusion-aware SNA video prediction model achieved an average improvement of **10.6 	0.82 pixels** in target object movement compared to the DNA model, demonstrating enhanced robustness.
- Visual MPC with the registration-based cost function attained a **66%** success rate on long-distance pushing tasks, significantly outperforming OpenCV tracking (**45%**) and predictor propagation (**20%**).
- A single Visual MPC model generalized to **75%** of 16 diverse manipulation tasks, including novel rigid and deformable objects, substantially exceeding a hand-engineered baseline's **18.75%** success rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning to predict future raw sensory observations provides sufficient self-supervision for a robot to acquire useful knowledge about world dynamics without explicit reward signals.
> - Incorporating temporal skip connections within the video prediction model (SNA) is crucial for robustly handling occlusions and maintaining accurate predictions of object motion.
> - Flexible goal specification methods (pixel distance, registration-based, meta-learned classifiers) enable a single predictive model to address a broad range of manipulation tasks, from precise placement to abstract relational goals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated success in learning complex skills from raw sensory inputs, but its application to real-world robotics, particularly in terms of broad generalization to novel tasks and objects, remains an ongoing challenge. Traditional RL often relies on explicit reward signals or low-dimensional state representations, which are typically unavailable or difficult to engineer in diverse, unstructured real-world environments. When deployed in limited settings, RL algorithms tend to learn skills that are specific to those narrow conditions, hindering generalization.

This work addresses the problem of learning generalizable robotic behaviors from high-dimensional sensory inputs (e.g., camera images) with minimal external supervision. It positions itself within the domain of model-based reinforcement learning, where an agent learns a predictive model of its environment to inform its actions. While model-based RL is known for its efficiency compared to model-free methods, existing work has often focused on low-dimensional state spaces, synthetic images, or individual robotic skills without extensive evaluation on generalization in the real world. Prior research in action-conditioned video prediction has explored synthetic video games or unstructured real-world videos, but its application to real-world robotic control with an emphasis on generalization to unseen objects and tasks has been less explored.

Furthermore, this research relates to self-supervised robotic learning, where large-scale, unattended data collection is used to acquire specific skills like grasping or obstacle avoidance. However, many of these approaches still rely on a success measure, event indicator, or reward function during data collection. This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on learning predictive models that support a variety of manipulation skills without requiring such explicit success signals during the data collection phase, instead deriving supervision solely from the raw stream of sensory observations. By developing a self-supervised model that predicts future raw sensory observations, the authors aim to overcome the limitations of sparse reward signals and generalize across diverse tasks and objects, moving towards more broadly applicable robotic intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a deep reinforcement learning method that is practical for real-world robotics tasks, specifically robotic manipulation, and capable of effective generalization to novel tasks and previously unseen objects. This objective is motivated by several challenges inherent in deploying deep RL in real-world scenarios:

1. **Lack of Ground Truth Reward Signals:** In complex, real-world robotic manipulation tasks, designing or providing precise reward signals for every possible task and scenario is often impractical or impossible. The absence of such signals necessitates alternative forms of supervision.
2. **High-Dimensional Sensory Inputs:** Robots in real-world environments receive high-dimensional sensory data, such as camera images. While rich in information, these inputs are challenging for learning algorithms that require processing and interpretation for effective control.
3. **Limited Generalization:** Deep RL algorithms often specialize in tasks encountered during training, struggling to generalize to new tasks, environments, or objects not previously seen. This limits their broad applicability and requires extensive retraining for each new scenario.
4. **Data Collection Challenges:** Collecting large, diverse datasets for robotic learning in structured environments is labor-intensive, requiring human intervention for resets, reward labeling, and safety. A method enabling autonomous and unsupervised data collection is crucial for scaling up learning in diverse real-world settings.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a self-supervised, model-based approach centered on sensory prediction. The core idea is that by learning to predict future raw sensory observations (e.g., camera images) based on current observations and actions, a robot can acquire useful knowledge about the world's dynamics without external rewards. This predictive model then forms the basis for planning actions to achieve user-defined goals.

Specific objectives derived from this motivation include:
- Developing a general framework, termed "visual MPC," for deep RL that can learn behaviors in diverse, open-world environments.
- Designing deep neural network architectures capable of accurately predicting pixel-level observations, particularly in the presence of occlusions and with novel objects.
- Proposing and evaluating practical methods for specifying and evaluating progress towards goals directly from pixel predictions, given the high-dimensional nature of the outputs. These methods include designated pixel positions, goal images, and classifier-based goal definitions.
- Demonstrating the system's ability to manipulate a range of objects, including both rigid and deformable types, and to solve various user-defined tasks using a single trained model, thereby showcasing broad generalization.
- Integrating and extending prior work to create a unified system capable of complex, multi-objective tasks and robust operation in the face of perturbations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Visual Foresight system operates through a three-phase model-based reinforcement learning approach: unsupervised data collection, predictive model training, and planning-based control at test time.

#### 3.1. Unsupervised Data Collection

A robot autonomously collects interaction data by executing random actions sampled from a pre-specified distribution. This distribution is designed to allow the robot to explore relevant parts of the state space, and for certain complex tasks like grasping or folding, a simple "palmar reflex" primitive is incorporated. This primitive causes the gripper to close when its wrist height is below a threshold, increasing the probability of encountering grasping or folding scenarios. Crucially, data is collected without any reward signal or environment resets, enabling automated and unattended data collection in diverse settings.

#### 3.2. Predictive Model Training

A video prediction model, a recurrent neural network (RNN) denoted `g_θ`, is trained on the autonomously collected data. This model takes a current image and a sequence of actions as input and generates a corresponding sequence of future image frames.
- **Architecture:** The model is based on a transformation-based approach, leveraging a 2D flow field (`ˆF_t+1←t`) to warp previous images and generate future frames. This design aims to make prediction easier by reusing object appearances and background scenes.
- **Pixel Motion Prediction:** The transformation-based nature of the model allows it to automatically predict the 2D motion of individual pixels by applying the same warping transformations to distributions over pixel locations.
- **Skip Connection Neural Advection (SNA) Model:** To enhance robustness to occlusions (e.g., by the robot arm), a Skip Connection Neural Advection (SNA) model is introduced. This model transforms pixels not only from the immediately preceding generated image but also from other prior images in the sequence, including the initial real image (`I_0`). These transformed images are then combined via learned masks to form the predicted frame. A simplified version transforms only from the previous and initial image, with the initial image directly used for background.
- **Loss Function:** The model is trained using gradient descent to minimize an `L2` image reconstruction loss.
- **Architectural Improvements:** The SNA variant incorporates improvements such as instance normalization and ReLU activations after convolutional layers, instance normalization on LSTM pre-activations, and the use of average pooling for downsampling and bilinear interpolation for upsampling to avoid artifacts. Actions are concatenated to the inputs of all convolutional layers.

#### 3.3. Test-Time Control (Visual Model Predictive Control - MPC)

At test time, a sampling-based, gradient-free optimization procedure, specifically the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), is employed to find the sequence of actions that minimizes a specified cost function over a planning horizon `T`.
- **Iterative Replanning:** To correct for model inaccuracies and adapt to real-world dynamics, actions are iteratively replanned at each real-world time step, following the MPC framework.
- **Action Sampling:** CEM iteratively samples action sequences, evaluates their predicted cost using the video prediction model, and refits a Gaussian distribution to the top-performing samples. To promote stability and commitment to prior plans, the sampling distribution for the first iteration of CEM in a new real-world time step is centered around the best action sequence found in the previous step, with reduced variance for earlier actions.

#### 3.4. Goal Specification and Cost Functions

The paper proposes three distinct methods for defining goals and evaluating progress:
- **Pixel Distance Cost:** A user designates one or more pixels on objects and specifies their desired goal positions. The cost is calculated as the sum of the expected Euclidean distances between the predicted future locations of these designated pixels and their respective goal positions. This provides a smooth objective encouraging movement towards the goal over multiple steps.
- **Registration-Based Cost:** This method improves pixel distance tracking by incorporating a self-supervised registration network (`R`). This network, trained on the same unsupervised data as the video prediction model, registers the current image to both the initial image (`I_0`) and a user-provided goal image (`I_g`). This allows for dynamic updates of the target object's current position (`d_0,t`, `d_g,t`) in the image. The cost combines contributions from registration to `I_0` and `I_g`, weighted by their estimated local registration success (based on photometric distance), enhancing robustness to large movements or failures in one registration pathway.
- **Classifier-Based Cost Functions:** For more abstract tasks where absolute positions may not matter (e.g., relative object arrangement), a meta-learned goal classifier is used. This classifier (`f(o)`) is trained using Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) and Concept Acquisition through Meta-Learning (CAML) to infer task goals from only a few positive examples of success provided by the user. The classifier outputs a probability of success, which is then converted into a cost (e.g., thresholded probability), allowing the planner to explore diverse goal states.

#### 3.5. Multi-View Visual MPC

To enable tasks requiring 3D spatial reasoning, the system uses two cameras from different angles. The registration method and planning costs are applied independently per view and then combined via weighted averaging, with weights derived from the registration network's success estimates (Equation 9).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides both qualitative and quantitative evidence for the effectiveness and generalization capabilities of Visual MPC.

#### 4.1. Generalization to Diverse Tasks and Objects

Qualitative examples (Figures 1 and 12) demonstrate Visual MPC's ability to solve a wide array of complex tasks using a single model. These include:
- Multi-object manipulation: Placing an object onto a plate without displacing the plate.
- Object rearrangement: Moving objects to specific locations.
- Relative object rearrangement: Positioning objects relative to each other.
- 3D object positioning: Lifting and placing objects in specific 3D space, which requires multiple camera views.
- Obstacle avoidance: Moving an object around a designated obstacle.
- Deformable object manipulation: Tasks such as wrapping a towel around an object or folding a pair of shorts. The model, trained on towel videos, generalized to shorts without prior exposure.

#### 4.2. Comparison of Video Prediction Architectures (SNA vs. DNA)

A quantitative comparison assessed the performance of the occlusion-aware SNA model against the DNA model (without temporal skip connections) on a multi-objective pushing task that involved pushing one object while keeping another stationary. The stationary object could be occluded by the gripper.
- **Result:** The SNA model achieved an average improvement of 10.6 ± 0.82 pixels (in a 64x64 image) in moving the target object towards the goal, significantly outperforming the DNA model which showed only 0.83 ± 0.25 pixels improvement (Table 1). This indicates that temporal skip connections in SNA are effective in handling occlusions and maintaining accurate predictions of object positions.

#### 4.3. Evaluation of Registration-Based Cost Functions

Experiments focused on the importance of updating the model's belief about target object locations, particularly for long-horizon tasks, and the effectiveness of the self-supervised registration network.
- **Robustness to Perturbations:** Qualitative examples (Figures 15, 16, and supplementary video) illustrate the system's ability to recover from unexpected events, such as an object being knocked out of the robot's grasp or an object behaving unexpectedly, by replanning and making new grasp attempts or correcting pushes.
- **Long vs. Short Horizon Tasks:** In a pushing experiment without gripper control, three methods were compared: Visual MPC with the registration network, Visual MPC with off-the-shelf OpenCV tracking, and Visual MPC using the video prediction model's own prior predictions (predictor propagation).
 - **Result:** For long-distance tasks (30cm initial distance), Visual MPC with the registration network achieved a 66% success rate, outperforming OpenCV tracking (45%) and predictor propagation (20%) (Table 2). For short-distance tasks (15cm), all methods performed comparably (83% success). This highlights the critical role of robust object tracking via the learned registration network for successful execution of long-horizon tasks.

#### 4.4. Evaluation of Classifier-Based Cost Functions

This section evaluated the meta-learned classifier for abstract tasks compared to pixel distance and latent space distance (DSAE autoencoder) metrics.
- **Result:** The meta-learned classifier demonstrated substantially higher success rates for tasks involving relative object arrangements, including scenarios with distractor objects, across 20 tasks with novel objects (Figure 11). Distance-based metrics struggled to infer the goal of these abstract tasks. This confirms the classifier's ability to capture task invariances (e.g., arm position, exact absolute location) and learn from few examples.

#### 4.5. Multi-Task Performance against Baseline

A quantitative comparison was conducted between Visual MPC and a hand-engineered, open-loop baseline controller across 16 diverse tasks (10 hard object pushing/grasping, 6 cloth folding).
- **Result:** Visual MPC achieved a 75% success rate, significantly outperforming the calibrated camera baseline, which succeeded in only 18.75% of trials (Table 3). This indicates that the implicit understanding of physics embedded in the video prediction models is crucial for performing a diverse range of manipulation tasks, going beyond simple end-effector movement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of deep reinforcement learning for robotics, particularly concerning generalization and real-world applicability. The key significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Generalization:** The work demonstrates that a single, self-supervised model can generalize to a wide array of complex manipulation tasks, including those involving novel rigid and deformable objects, and various objectives (pushing, grasping, placing, folding, obstacle avoidance). This addresses a critical limitation of many current RL approaches that often exhibit poor generalization beyond their training distributions.
2. **Reduced Supervision Requirements:** By leveraging self-supervision from raw visual prediction, the method significantly reduces the need for human-engineered reward functions, low-dimensional state estimators, or extensive manual labeling for each new task. This enables more autonomous and scalable data collection in real-world environments.
3. **Practical Real-World Application:** The development of "visual MPC" provides a practical framework for deploying deep RL on physical robots. The ability to collect data autonomously and operate without continuous human supervision makes it suitable for diverse, open-world robotic applications.
4. **Flexible Goal Specification:** The introduction of multiple goal specification methods—designated pixels, goal images, and meta-learned classifiers from few-shot examples—offers flexibility for users to define tasks ranging from precise object placement to abstract relational goals. This broadens the types of tasks that can be addressed by a single system.
5. **Robustness to Real-World Challenges:** The incorporation of the Skip Connection Neural Advection (SNA) model allows the system to handle occlusions, while the registration-based cost function provides robust object tracking and enables recovery from unexpected perturbations or initial failures during execution. This contributes to the system's reliability in dynamic real-world settings.
6. **Unified Framework for Diverse Skills:** The capacity to integrate various manipulation primitives (pushing, grasping, folding) and operate on both rigid and deformable objects within a single framework represents a step towards more general-purpose robotic agents.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The developed framework, models, and comprehensive simulation environment serve as a foundation for future research in long-horizon planning, handling partial observability, and improving the precision and dexterity of robotic manipulation, as acknowledged in the paper's limitations and future directions.

Despite limitations such as requiring visible target objects and current restriction to medium-duration tasks, this work significantly contributes to the development of intelligent robotic systems capable of learning and adapting to unseen scenarios in complex environments.
