# WAM-Nav: Asymmetric Latent World-Action Modeling for Unified Visual Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> WAM-Nav, an asymmetric latent world-action model, unifies visual navigation tasks by jointly generating actions and predicting short-horizon latent visual foresight. The model achieves a 15.7% SR improvement over NavDP for Image-Goal navigation, along with better performance across Point-Goal and No-Goal tasks in zero-shot simulation and real-world robot deployment.

## Problem

- Reactive navigation policies directly mapping observations to actions often lack anticipatory reasoning, leading to suboptimal paths and collisions in complex, cluttered environments.
- Existing visual imagination or world-model-based strategies that decouple scene prediction from policy learning introduce computational latency and accumulate errors over sequential steps.
- Most navigation systems are single-task, requiring substantial redesign and retraining to adapt to different goal specifications such as Image-Goal, Point-Goal, or No-Goal exploration.

## Method

- A ==Latent== World-Action Model (**WAM-Nav**) is introduced, jointly modeling long-horizon action trajectories and short-horizon latent visual foresight using a shared ==Diffusion== ==Transformer==.
- Unified Goal Alignment processes diverse goal inputs (Image, Point, No-Goal) into both visual semantic and geometric queries, enabling a single policy for multi-task navigation.
- Dual-Stream Contextual Conditioning (DSCC) fuses goal-modulated visual memory with trajectory-aware motion history to generate kinematically smooth and geometrically collision-free paths.

## Results

- WAM-Nav achieved superior zero-shot generalization in simulation, demonstrating a **15.7%** SR improvement over NavDP for Image-Goal navigation (**50.2%** SR / **48.2%** SPL) and consistent gains for Point-Goal and No-Goal exploration.
- The model exhibited competitive computational efficiency with 0.7 TFLOPs per decision and an inference latency of 0.26s, making it significantly faster than previous complex world models.
- Real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot yielded an average **85%** task success rate across diverse environments, confirming strong zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment generalization compared to baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An asymmetric horizon design, with a long planning horizon for actions (24 steps) and a short prediction horizon for latent visual foresight (1 step), effectively balances proactive reasoning with efficiency, avoiding error accumulation from large viewpoint changes.
> - Jointly modeling visual foresight and actions within a shared generative framework, specifically a Diffusion Transformer, facilitates dynamic exchange of spatiotemporal constraints, leading to improved trajectory quality.
> - Integrating ego-motion history (kinematic momentum) with visual observations through dual-stream conditioning significantly enhances trajectory stability and geometric safety in diverse and cluttered environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied visual navigation, which involves guiding agents through environments using visual observations, has transitioned from traditional decoupled mapping-and-planning methods to learning-based intelligent approaches. Within this landscape, two dominant paradigms have emerged:

1. **Reactive End-to-End Policies**: These methods directly map visual observations to executable actions. Examples include GNM, ViNT, NoMaD, and NavDP. While offering simplicity, these reactive policies typically lack explicit predictive reasoning regarding future perceptual consequences, making them susceptible to local optima and collisions in complex, cluttered environments. Their reliance on instantaneous perception limits proactive obstacle avoidance.

2. **Visual Imagination and World-Model-Based Strategies**: These approaches aim to endow agents with predictive reasoning capabilities by generating or evaluating future states.
 - **Modular Decoupled Approaches**: These systems typically separate scene prediction from policy learning. They can involve generating future visual subgoals, which are then used by inverse dynamics models to infer actions (e.g., NavigateDiff, NWM), or generating candidate paths that are subsequently scored by a world model imagining future visual states (e.g., Pathdreamer, NWM). A primary limitation of these modular designs is the introduction of substantial computational latency and compounding errors due to the sequential and separate training processes, often leading to delayed responses and inconsistent trajectories.
 - **World-Action Models (WAMs)**: While WAMs have shown promise in robot manipulation by jointly modeling visual-action capabilities, their autoregressive generation paradigm for navigation remains challenging. Large viewpoint changes inherent in navigation can lead to significant real-time performance limitations and accumulated prediction errors during multi-step visual rollouts.

Furthermore, most existing navigation methods are designed for a single type of goal specification (e.g., Image-Goal or Point-Goal), necessitating redesign and retraining for different tasks. Although efforts like NavDP have supported multiple goal types, their unimodal alignment designs can result in imbalanced performance across tasks.

WAM-Nav addresses these limitations by introducing a novel approach that integrates the benefits of predictive foresight with efficient, unified policy learning. It aims to overcome the reactive nature of end-to-end policies, mitigate the computational overhead and error accumulation of modular imagination, adapt the WAM paradigm for navigation efficiency, and provide a single policy capable of balanced performance across diverse navigation goals.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of WAM-Nav are rooted in addressing the identified shortcomings of existing visual navigation paradigms:

1. **Enhance Predictive Reasoning for Proactive Obstacle Avoidance**: A key motivation is to move beyond the reactive nature of many current end-to-end policies. These policies, which directly map observations to actions, often lack anticipatory reasoning, making them prone to collisions and getting stuck in local optima within cluttered environments. WAM-Nav aims to imbue agents with the ability to foresee future environmental states and consequences of their actions, enabling proactive decision-making.

2. **Mitigate Error Accumulation and Inference Latency in Modular Foresight Systems**: While visual imagination offers predictive foresight, conventional modular approaches that decouple scene prediction from policy learning frequently suffer from error accumulation over sequential prediction steps and increased inference latency. This separation can lead to misaligned state-action mappings, compounding control errors, and responses that are not real-time. WAM-Nav seeks to address these issues by jointly modeling action generation and visual foresight within a unified framework, reducing the overhead and improving the consistency of predictions.

3. **Adapt World-Action Modeling for Efficient and Robust Navigation**: Existing World-Action Models have demonstrated joint visual-action modeling capabilities, particularly in robot manipulation. However, their autoregressive generation paradigms are not well-suited for navigation, which involves significant ego-motion and large viewpoint changes. Such scenarios can exacerbate prediction errors and hinder real-time performance. WAM-Nav aims to overcome these challenges by adopting an asymmetric joint diffusion strategy that provides reliable short-horizon visual foresight while maintaining long-horizon action continuity without compromising inference efficiency.

4. **Develop a Unified Policy for Diverse Navigation Tasks with Balanced Performance**: Most existing navigation policies are specialized for a single type of goal (e.g., Image-Goal, Point-Goal, or No-Goal exploration). Adapting these policies to new task specifications typically requires significant redesign, data collection, and retraining. Even multi-task approaches have exhibited imbalanced performance. WAM-Nav seeks to develop a single, unified policy capable of handling Image-Goal, Point-Goal, and No-Goal exploration naturally, ensuring balanced and robust performance across these varied task types without architectural switching. This objective is driven by the desire for greater versatility and practical applicability of navigation agents.

In essence, WAM-Nav is motivated by the goal of enabling embodied agents to make robust and foresighted navigation decisions under complex constraints and diverse task specifications, all while maintaining inference efficiency suitable for real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

WAM-Nav operates as a Latent World-Action Model (WAM) designed for embodied visual navigation, jointly learning action generation and latent visual foresight within a unified framework. The architecture is composed of three primary components: Unified Goal Alignment, Dual-Stream Contextual Conditioning, and Asymmetric Action-Foresight Generation, all orchestrated by a shared Diffusion Transformer (DiT).

#### 3.1. Unified Goal Alignment (UGA)

To ensure balanced performance across Image-Goal, Point-Goal, and No-Goal exploration tasks within a single policy, WAM-Nav employs a unified goal alignment mechanism. Unlike approaches that convert all tasks to a single format, WAM-Nav retains modality-specific goal information.
A goal input `g` (which can be an image, relative coordinates, or a masked zero-state for exploration) is first processed by a modality-specific feature extractor `E_ϕ(·)` to produce a base embedding `e_g`. This `e_g` is then projected into two complementary goal embeddings:
- **Visual Semantic Query (`g_V`):** An embedding intended for visual memory retrieval. For image goals, `E_ϕ` is a Vision Transformer; for point goals, `e_g` uses sinusoidal positional encoding; and for no-goal, it's a masked zero-state.
- **Geometric Query (`g_G`):** An embedding providing trajectory-level directional guidance.
These projections `ψ_V(·)` and `ψ_G(·)` are learnable linear mappings. This dual representation preserves the expressiveness of each goal modality while offering a unified interface for downstream components.

#### 3.2. Dual-Stream Contextual Conditioning (DSCC)

The DSCC module constructs a spatiotemporal context by fusing historical visual observations with the agent's ego-motion history. This fusion ensures that generated trajectories are both geometrically collision-free and kinematically smooth.

- **Goal-Modulated Visual Memory:** Historical RGB observations `O_t` (from time `t-k+1` to `t`) are processed by a frozen DINO-v2 backbone, yielding a visual memory tensor `V`. The visual semantic query `g_V` computes relevance scores (`α`) for each visual patch token within `V` via a scaled dot-product. These scores residually update `V` to `~V`, selectively emphasizing spatial cues relevant to the target.

- **Trajectory-Aware Motion History:** To incorporate kinematic momentum, the agent's executed pose sequence `S_t` is maintained. Absolute poses are converted into coordinate-invariant relative displacements and heading changes (`~S_t`) with respect to the current egocentric frame (Algorithm 1 details this process). This `~S_t` sequence is then embedded and processed by a causal Transformer encoder `H` to capture temporal kinematic profiles. The geometric query `g_G` queries `H` via cross-attention to extract a consolidated kinematic vector `o_kin`, which summarizes the agent’s historical motion continuity relative to its target directionality.

- **Condition Fusion via Cross-Attention:** The `o_kin` vector biases a set of learnable query embeddings `Q_c` and, along with the goal-modulated visual memory `~V`, is fed into a multi-layer Transformer Decoder. This decoder structurally integrates the streams, yielding a unified conditioning context `C`. This fusion mechanism leverages kinematic momentum to guide visual spatial retrieval, ensuring generated actions adhere to both physical smoothness and geometric safety.

#### 3.3. Asymmetric Action-Foresight Generation (AAFG)

Conditioned on the unified context `C`, WAM-Nav jointly models a long-horizon action trajectory `A_t` (over `H_act` steps) and a short-horizon latent visual foresight sequence `Z_{t+1:t+H_vis}` (over `H_vis` steps, where `H_vis ≤ H_act`). Future visual states `o_i` are compressed into compact latent grids `z_i` using a pre-trained Stable Diffusion VAE.

The generative process is implemented using a shared Diffusion Transformer (DiT) and trained with a flow-matching strategy. This strategy models continuous probability paths by mapping Gaussian priors to the empirical data manifold. At any flow time `τ`, interpolated states `A_τ` and `Z_τ` are defined, with corresponding target velocity fields `u_A` and `u_Z`.

- **Shared DiT Architecture:** Noised `A_τ` and `Z_τ` sequences are tokenized, concatenated, and processed through multiple DiT blocks. Crucially, within each block, these heterogeneous tokens undergo shared self-attention, enabling dynamic exchange of spatiotemporal constraints between physical action paths and latent visual representations. Both streams cross-attend to the conditioning context `C`, and the timestep `τ` is modulated via adaptive LayerNorm (adaLN) layers to regress the joint velocity fields (`ˆu_A`, `ˆu_Z`). This shared parameterization ensures that latent foresight acts as a perception-grounded constraint on action generation, penalizing action-scene inconsistency.

- **Asymmetric Design Rationale:** The design is asymmetric to address the challenges of navigation. Long `H_act` preserves trajectory continuity, while short `H_vis` provides reliable near-future geometric constraints. This avoids the inference latency and accumulated visual errors that long autoregressive visual rollouts (common in manipulation WAMs) would introduce under large egocentric viewpoint changes in navigation.

#### 3.4. Model Training and Inference

WAM-Nav is trained end-to-end with a joint objective function (`L_total`) comprising:
- Flow-matching velocity regression (L2 loss) for `ˆu_A` and `ˆu_Z`.
- A symmetric contrastive InfoNCE loss (`L_align`) for multi-modal goal alignment, maximizing mutual information between cross-space projections of equivalent objectives.
During training, the DINOv2 visual backbone and Stable Diffusion VAE are frozen, while the goal image encoder, fusion decoder, causal motion encoder, and shared DiT are trained from scratch.

For online deployment, WAM-Nav employs a receding-horizon control loop. Conditioned on `C`, the policy samples 16 candidate trajectories. The first sampled trajectory is selected for execution, consistent with prior generative navigation policies like NAVDP. The flow-matching ODE solver runs for 10 Euler integration steps to balance quality and real-time responsiveness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Extensive zero-shot deployments were conducted in simulation and real-world environments to evaluate WAM-Nav's effectiveness across five research questions.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

WAM-Nav was trained on the large-scale visual navigation dataset VLN-N1 (over 400 hours of collision-free trajectories) and evaluated in a zero-shot setting on an IsaacSim benchmark using a ClearPath Dingo robot. Evaluation environments included ClutterScenes (with easy and hard obstacle distributions) and InternScenes (home and commercial layouts), totaling 6,000 episodes. Metrics included Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) for Image-Goal and Point-Goal navigation, and explored Area and Episode Time for No-Goal exploration.

- **Q1: Zero-shot Generalization and Balanced Performance:** WAM-Nav demonstrated competitive average performance across all three task settings (Table 3). On Image-Goal navigation, it achieved 50.2% SR and 48.2% SPL, representing a 15.7% SR improvement over NavDP. For Point-Goal navigation, it achieved 80.4% SR and 78.0% SPL, a 3.3% SR improvement over NavDP. In No-Goal exploration, it explored an average of 171.1 m². These results indicate strong zero-shot robustness across diverse obstacle layouts and semantic complexities, with balanced performance across goal types, supporting the effectiveness of its unified goal alignment.

- **Q2: Navigation in Cluttered Environments:** Qualitative comparisons (Figure 3) with NavDP on the Image-Goal task revealed that WAM-Nav exhibits smoother and more proactive obstacle avoidance. Unlike NavDP, which often reacted to obstacles only upon approach, WAM-Nav utilized its short-horizon latent foresight to anticipate near-future geometric constraints while maintaining long-horizon action continuity. The decoded visual foresights were consistent with ground-truth observations, confirming the reliability of latent foresight as guidance.

- **Q3: Computational Efficiency:** Despite incorporating latent visual foresight, WAM-Nav maintained efficiency for online navigation (Table 1). Its inference latency was 0.26 seconds, a modest overhead compared to NavDP (0.16 s) but significantly faster than NWM (1.43 s). The model's trainable parameters (129.2M) were comparable to NavDP (136.4M), and it required lower per-decision computation (0.7 TFLOPs vs. NavDP's 1.3 TFLOPs and NWM's 8.3 TFLOPs). This validates the efficiency of the asymmetric short-horizon foresight for real-time applications.

- **Q4: Component Contributions (Ablation Studies):**
 - **Trajectory Conditioning and Latent Foresight (Table 4):** Latent prediction alone improved SR from 42.1% to 45.7%, indicating its role in anticipating local obstacle constraints. Motion trajectories alone improved performance in ClutterScenes but degraded in complex InternScenes, suggesting kinematic history improves smoothness but not necessarily perceptual foresight in varied semantic contexts. Combining both (DSCC) yielded the best overall performance (50.2% SR / 48.2% SPL), demonstrating that ego-motion history stabilizes trajectories while latent foresight provides necessary geometric constraints.
 - **Unified Goal Alignment (Table 6):** Using both visual (`g_V`) and geometric (`g_G`) queries provided the strongest No-Goal exploration performance and maintained competitive results for Image-Goal and Point-Goal tasks, supporting their complementary nature for a unified multi-task policy.
 - **Memory Window Size, DSCC Fusion, Action-Foresight Architecture (Table 5):** An optimal memory window size of `k=8` was identified. Explicit goal injection was confirmed as necessary for effective conditioning. The fully shared DiT architecture outperformed decoupled and partially shared variants, indicating that direct regularization of action generation by latent foresight through shared representations is most effective.
 - **Asymmetric Horizon Design (Table 7):** Optimal performance was achieved with a short visual horizon (`H_vis = 1`), while longer visual horizons progressively degraded performance. This supports the design choice that, in navigation, reliable near-future geometric constraints from short-horizon prediction are more beneficial than long autoregressive visual rollouts prone to error accumulation.

#### 4.2. Deployment on Real World

- **Q5: Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** WAM-Nav was deployed in a zero-shot manner on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot equipped with an Intel RealSense D455 camera (Figure 4a). Evaluation across diverse indoor and outdoor environments (meeting room, warehouse, lobby, parking lot) yielded an average 85% task success rate. Compared to NavDP, WAM-Nav achieved higher success rates in real-world scenarios (Figure 4b). This indicates that latent foresight and motion-aware conditioning improve obstacle avoidance and trajectory stability beyond simulation, validating effective zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. Further generalization tests (Table 8) showed WAM-Nav consistently outperforming NavDP across different embodiments (wheeled Dingo, G1, H2 humanoid robots) in simulation, indicating learned embodiment-agnostic representations. The performance gap between WAM-Nav and NavDP also increased with task difficulty (Table 9), highlighting the benefit of predictive feedback for long-horizon navigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

WAM-Nav represents a notable advancement in embodied visual navigation by integrating predictive foresight into a unified, efficient policy. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Proactive Navigation**: By jointly modeling action generation and latent visual foresight, WAM-Nav enables proactive obstacle avoidance and more robust navigation decisions, particularly in cluttered and complex environments. This moves beyond the limitations of purely reactive policies, contributing to safer and more reliable autonomous agents.

2. **Unified Multi-Task Capability**: The unified goal alignment mechanism allows a single policy to handle Image-Goal, Point-Goal, and No-Goal exploration tasks with balanced performance. This reduces the need for specialized models, simplifying development, deployment, and making navigation systems more versatile across different application scenarios.

3. **Efficiency without Sacrificing Foresight**: The asymmetric action-foresight generation, coupled with a shared Diffusion Transformer, achieves predictive reasoning with minimal inference latency and computational cost. This addresses a critical bottleneck in prior world-model-based approaches, making the system suitable for real-time operation on resource-constrained physical robots.

4. **Strong Zero-Shot Generalization and Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The demonstrated ability to generalize across diverse simulation environments (ClutterScenes, InternScenes) and transfer effectively to real-world physical robots (Unitree G1) in a zero-shot manner is significant. This indicates that WAM-Nav learns robust, transferable representations and policies, accelerating the deployment of learned navigation skills from simulation to reality. The observed cross-embodiment generalization further underscores its potential for broad applicability.

5. **Foundation for Future Embodied AI**: The paradigm of jointly learning actions and latent world models, particularly with asymmetric horizons, offers a promising direction for developing more intelligent and anticipatory embodied AI agents. This work could inspire further research into integrated perception-action-prediction systems for complex robotic tasks.

Despite these advancements, the paper acknowledges two primary limitations that also suggest future research directions:
- **Perception of Near-Field Obstacles**: The fixed camera height and limited field of view can restrict the perception of obstacles immediately in front of the robot, potentially leading to inaccurate trajectory predictions or delayed avoidance. Future work could explore adaptive viewpoint control to dynamically adjust perception.
- **Lack of Embodiment Modeling**: The current policy does not explicitly account for the robot's full body shape, which can lead to collisions between the robot's body and obstacles even if the camera's trajectory appears clear. Future research could focus on embodiment-aware training with diverse robot morphologies to improve navigation robustness.
