# How Well Can Reasoning Models Identify and Recover from Unhelpful Thoughts?

> [!summary] Summary
> Yang et al. investigate large language models' capacity for self-reevaluation by testing their ability to identify and recover from various injected "unhelpful thoughts." The research finds a substantial gap between thought recognition and effective recovery, particularly noting an inverse scaling trend where larger models demonstrate reduced robustness to certain unhelpful thought injections, impacting their reliability and safety.

## Problem

- The true effectiveness and generality of LLMs' meta-cognitive abilities, such as self-reevaluation and self-correction within chains of thought, remained underexplored.
- It was unclear how well LLMs could identify and effectively recover from various types of internal "unhelpful thoughts," ranging from uninformative ramblings to incorrect or misdirecting reasoning paths.
- Understanding the limitations in self-reevaluation is crucial for assessing potential vulnerabilities in reasoning models, particularly concerning adversarial attacks through thought injection and interactions with external tools.

## Method

- A two-part evaluation framework was developed, assessing models' ability to *identify* unhelpful thoughts via binary classification and their capacity to *recover* from them by measuring task performance after injection.
- Four distinct categories of ==unhelpful thoughts== (uninformative rambling, irrelevant, misdirecting, incorrect) were generated and programmatically ==injected into LLMs' thinking processes== across five diverse reasoning datasets.
- Experiments were conducted on multiple sizes of three LLM families (DeepSeek R1-Distill, s1.1, EXAONE Deep), including tests with explicit recovery cues and a novel =="attack-in-thought" jailbreak== scenario to examine practical vulnerabilities.

## Results

- Models demonstrated high accuracy in identifying unhelpful thoughts (e.g., near-perfect for irrelevant thoughts) but showed severe performance drops (e.g., up to **70%** decrease in accuracy) across all unhelpful thought types during recovery.
- For short irrelevant thoughts, larger models like DeepSeek R1-Distill 70B exhibited significantly lower recovery performance compared to smaller models (e.g., 7B), a trend consistently observed across three model families.
- Explicit cues to encourage recovery, such as additional instructions or "aha moment" triggers, provided only negligible to modest improvements, failing to restore baseline performance after thought injection.
- The inverse scaling trend for recovery translated to jailbreak scenarios, where smaller models were more robust to "attack-in-thought" injections (harmful content within internal thought processes) than larger models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - There is a significant discrepancy between LLMs' strong ability to *identify* unhelpful thoughts when explicitly prompted and their struggle to *recover* from them when those thoughts are injected into their reasoning process.
> - The observed "meta-cognitive" awareness in LLMs is often local, leading to self-reevaluation within an unhelpful trajectory rather than a global re-grounding of reasoning to the original problem.
> - Larger models exhibit an unexpected non/inverse-scaling trend for recovery from short irrelevant thoughts, performing worse than smaller models and suggesting increased brittleness in specific adversarial contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

The paper by Yang et al. investigates a critical aspect of advanced reasoning models: their capacity for self-reevaluation. While contemporary LLMs demonstrate remarkable problem-solving through intricate chains of thought, often featuring explicit reflection and self-correction, the true effectiveness and generality of this "meta-cognitive" ability remain underexplored. This study systematically probes how well models can identify and recover from various types of "unhelpful thoughts" – ranging from uninformative ramblings to incorrect or misdirecting reasoning paths. The findings reveal significant limitations in recovery, particularly highlighting inverse scaling trends where larger models struggle more with certain unhelpful thought injections, with important implications for both reasoning capabilities and model safety against adversarial attacks.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the rapidly evolving field of large language models (LLMs), specifically focusing on their emergent reasoning capabilities and meta-cognitive behaviors. Recent advancements have seen models like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1 exhibit unprecedented performance on complex reasoning tasks, largely attributed to their ability to generate "chains of thought" that involve reflection, backtracking, and self-validation—often termed "aha moments." This capacity for internal self-reevaluation has been lauded as a key driver of their success and a form of "meta-cognitive" behavior, drawing parallels to human critical thinking.

The existing literature extensively explores methods for explicitly supervising LLMs to develop self-reevaluation abilities (e.g., Weng et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023). However, this paper identifies a crucial gap: while models *demonstrate* self-reevaluation, the *effectiveness* and *generality* of this intrinsic capability, especially when faced with misleading or unhelpful internal thoughts, remained largely unquantified. Previous research has touched upon models' ability to identify mistakes or question their own reasoning, but a systematic investigation into their recovery mechanisms in the face of diverse unhelpful internal states was lacking.

Furthermore, the paper connects its findings to the critical issue of LLM safety and robustness, particularly in the context of "inverse scaling" phenomena (McKenzie et al., 2023), where larger models sometimes perform worse on specific tasks than smaller ones. This directly informs the ongoing efforts to develop secure and reliable AI systems, especially as LLMs increasingly integrate "tool-use" for web search or code execution within their reasoning processes. Such external interactions could inadvertently introduce "unhelpful thoughts" in the form of malicious or misleading information, making the study of thought injection highly relevant to real-world deployment challenges. By probing the limits of LLMs' meta-cognitive abilities, this research offers insights crucial for advancing both the cognitive understanding and practical safety of next-generation AI.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of reasoning models' self-reevaluation capabilities. Specifically, the authors aim to determine how well these models can **identify** and **recover from** various types of unhelpful thoughts during their problem-solving processes.

The primary motivations behind this investigation are multi-faceted:

- **Unpacking Emergent Abilities:** Large reasoning models have shown a remarkable, often emergent, capacity for reflection, backtracking, and self-validation within their chains of thought. While impressive, the true *robustness* and *generality* of this "meta-cognitive" behavior are not well understood. The authors are motivated to move beyond anecdotal observations to a systematic, empirical assessment of these abilities.
- **Assessing "Meta-Cognitive" Awareness:** The term "meta-cognitive" is frequently used to describe LLM self-correction. The paper seeks to rigorously test whether this is a superficial behavior or indicates a deeper, more general awareness that enables models to effectively steer their reasoning away from unproductive or incorrect paths.
- **Identifying Vulnerabilities:** Understanding the limitations in self-reevaluation is crucial for identifying potential vulnerabilities in current reasoning models. If models struggle to recover from internal misdirection, it indicates a fundamental weakness that could be exploited or lead to unreliable behavior.
- **Implications for Safety and Robustness:** A significant motivation is the growing concern about the safety of LLMs. The authors specifically hypothesize that a model's inability to recover from unhelpful thoughts could be a pathway for adversarial attacks, such as "jailbreaking," especially when external information (e.g., via tool-use like web searches) can be injected into a model's internal thinking process.
- **Guiding Future Development:** By pinpointing specific types of unhelpful thoughts that models struggle with, the research aims to provide actionable insights for developing more robust, reliable, and safer large reasoning models with genuinely effective self-reevaluation mechanisms.

To achieve these objectives, the study focuses on four distinct categories of unhelpful thoughts: uninformative rambling, irrelevant thoughts (solving a different question), misdirecting thoughts (solving a slightly different question), and incorrect thoughts (solving the given question with errors).

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous evaluation framework centered on two main assessments: **identification** and **recovery** from unhelpful thoughts.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Framework

The authors define a model's output as comprising a "thinking process" (long chains of thought encapsulated by special tokens like `<think>...</think>`) and a "response" (the final answer). A "thought" is any segment of this thinking process.

1. **Identification Task:** Models are presented with a reasoning problem and an unhelpful thought, then asked to classify whether the thought is helpful or not in solving the *given* problem. This is framed as a binary classification task.
2. **Recovery Task:** Unhelpful thoughts are directly *injected* into the model's thinking process (pre-filling the `<think>` block without an end-of-thought token), forcing the model to continue its reasoning from that point. The model's ability to recover is measured by comparing its task performance (e.g., accuracy) with and without this thought injection.

#### 4.2. Types of Unhelpful Thoughts

Four distinct categories of unhelpful thoughts were generated to represent different levels of detection difficulty:

- **Uninformative Rambling:** Short, generic phrases that mimic a thinking process but contain no problem-specific information. These were manually created based on common LLM verbal fillers.
- **Irrelevant Thoughts:** Complete thoughts that solve a *different*, unrelated question. These were generated by collecting actual LLM thoughts from other problems and injecting them into the current problem context.
- **Misdirecting Thoughts:** Thoughts that solve a question *similar to but slightly different* from the original. These were created by using a powerful LLM (o4-mini) to subtly modify original questions, then having R1-Distill models generate solutions for these modified questions.
- **Incorrect Thoughts:** Thoughts that attempt to solve the *correct* question but contain errors leading to a wrong answer. These were sourced from a less capable R1-Distill 1.5B model, which is prone to making mistakes.

To study the impact of thought length, shorter variants (10%, 33%, 66% of original length) were created for irrelevant and misdirecting thoughts.

#### 4.3. Experimental Setup

- **Evaluation Datasets:** The study used five diverse reasoning datasets: AIME 24 and MATH-500 (mathematics), ARC Challenge and GPQA Diamond (science), and HumanEval (coding). These datasets require non-trivial reasoning.
- **Reasoning Models:** The primary models tested were five sizes of DeepSeek R1-Distill (7B, 8B, 14B, 32B, 70B). Additional experiments used s1.1 (7B, 14B, 32B) and EXAONE Deep (2.4B, 7.8B, 32B) models to confirm generalizability.
- **Inference:** Experiments were run using `lm-evaluation-harness` with `vllm`, a temperature of 0.6, and specified maximum token generation limits.

#### 4.4. Specific Experiments

1. **Identification Experiment (Section 4):**
 - Models were given clear instructions for each thought type (e.g., "does it solve the given question or a different one?").
 - A specific prompting strategy was used to encourage binary classification ("yes"/"no") rather than directly solving the problem, by forcing the model to start its thinking with a specific meta-cognitive phrase.

2. **Recovery Experiment (Section 5):**
 - Unhelpful thoughts were directly prepended to the model's generation process within the `<think>` block.
 - Performance was measured by the standard task-specific accuracy, comparing baseline (no injection) against injection scenarios.
 - Further tests explored the impact of explicit cues: (1) an additional instruction to actively "spot and correct mistakes" and (2) appending an "aha moment" trigger ("But wait, let me think again.") at the end of the injected thought.
 - Manual inspection of success and failure cases provided qualitative insights into model behavior. An attention weight analysis (Appendix A.4) was conducted to visualize how models attend to the user query during recovery.

3. **Jailbreak Implications Experiment (Section 6):**
 - This experiment tested how the observed vulnerabilities translate to adversarial scenarios.
 - **Attack-in-thought:** A harmless reasoning question was posed, but a harmful question (from HarmBench) and a short jailbreak prompt were *injected into the thinking process*. This mimics the short irrelevant thought injection.
 - **Attack-in-input:** A standard jailbreak setup where the harmful question and prompt were part of the *user input*.
 - A judge model (o4-mini) classified model responses as "harmful," "refused," or "harmless" (for attack-in-thought), and "harmful" or "refused" (for attack-in-input). Attack robustness was defined as the ratio of non-harmful responses.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings that shed light on the limitations of current reasoning models' self-reevaluation capabilities.

#### 5.1. Identification vs. Recovery Discrepancy

While models, particularly larger ones, demonstrated high accuracy in *identifying* unhelpful thoughts when explicitly asked (e.g., near-perfect for irrelevant thoughts), this ability did *not* translate to effective *recovery* when those same thoughts were injected into their ongoing reasoning process. This discrepancy suggests a significant gap between passive recognition of an unhelpful thought and actively rerouting reasoning to correct course. Performance drops were observed across all unhelpful thought types upon injection, most severely for irrelevant, incorrect, and full misdirecting thoughts.

#### 5.2. Non/Inverse-Scaling Trends in Recovery

A striking and counter-intuitive finding was the emergence of **non/inverse-scaling trends** for recovery from *short irrelevant thoughts*. Larger models (e.g., DeepSeek R1-Distill 70B) exhibited *lower* recovery performance compared to smaller models (e.g., 7B). This trend was consistently observed across three different model families (R1-Distill, s1.1, EXAONE Deep) and various evaluation datasets, indicating a general brittleness of larger models to such injections. Smaller models were found to recover better when the irrelevant thoughts were shorter.

#### 5.3. Limited Meta-Cognitive Awareness

Manual inspection of model behaviors revealed that despite sometimes triggering "aha moments" during unhelpful thought injection, these moments often led to *local self-reevaluation* within the injected, irrelevant line of reasoning, rather than a global realization that the entire premise was misdirected. Models tended to "naively continue" the injected thought's reasoning trajectory, demonstrating that their self-reevaluation abilities are far from a general, context-aware "meta-cognitive" awareness. Attention analysis further suggested that models often fail to recover because they do not adequately ground their reasoning back to the original user query, especially for larger models in failure cases.

#### 5.4. Ineffectiveness of Explicit Cues

Attempts to improve recovery using explicit cues proved largely insufficient. Providing an additional instruction to "spot and correct mistakes" yielded negligible performance gains. While appending an "aha moment" trigger ("But wait, let me think again.") did offer some improvements, especially for incorrect and full misdirecting thoughts, the absolute performance still remained significantly below the baseline where no thoughts were injected. This indicates that once a model commits to an unhelpful reasoning trajectory, external prompts are not enough to fully override it.

#### 5.5. Jailbreak Vulnerabilities

The observed non/inverse-scaling trend directly translated to **jailbreak scenarios**. In "attack-in-thought" experiments, where harmful content was injected as an irrelevant thought, smaller models were found to be *more robust* and less susceptible to generating harmful responses compared to larger models. This underscores a novel safety concern: the increased capacity of larger models might make them more brittle to certain types of internal adversarial manipulation. Interestingly, robustness against "attack-in-thought" (which showed inverse scaling) did not correlate with robustness against "attack-in-input" (which showed normal scaling), suggesting distinct vulnerabilities depending on the attack vector.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers profound insights into the foundational capabilities and vulnerabilities of state-of-the-art large reasoning models, carrying significant implications for both theoretical understanding and practical deployment.

#### 6.1. Fundamental Understanding of LLM Reasoning

The study critically challenges the assumption that LLMs possess a general "meta-cognitive" awareness. By demonstrating a stark disparity between models' ability to *identify* unhelpful thoughts and their struggle to *recover* from them, it reveals a fundamental limitation in their self-reevaluation mechanisms. This suggests that current LLM "aha moments" might be more akin to local error-checking rather than a holistic contextual re-grounding, indicating that simply scaling up models does not necessarily lead to more robust or truly "meta-cognitive" reasoning.

#### 6.2. Enhanced Safety and Robustness Considerations

The discovery of non/inverse-scaling trends, where larger models are *more brittle* to short irrelevant thought injections, introduces a crucial safety concern. This vulnerability is particularly salient given the increasing integration of "tool-use" (e.g., web search, code execution) into LLM thinking processes. External, potentially untrusted, inputs from these tools could inadvertently or maliciously introduce "unhelpful thoughts," creating novel attack vectors that were previously unconsidered. The jailbreak experiments validate this, showing that harmful irrelevant thought injection can bypass safety mechanisms, and surprisingly, smaller models are more robust in this specific context. This calls for a re-evaluation of current safety paradigms, urging researchers to consider thought-based vulnerabilities alongside traditional input-based attacks.

#### 6.3. Guiding Future LLM Development

The findings provide actionable directions for future research and development of more robust LLMs. Instead of solely focusing on improving error identification, efforts should concentrate on enhancing models' *recovery mechanisms* and their ability to re-ground their reasoning to the original query, especially after encountering internal deviations. Understanding *why* larger models exhibit inverse scaling for short irrelevant thoughts is a critical open question that could unlock new architectural or training paradigms. The research strongly advocates for developing models with more sophisticated contextual awareness and mechanisms for systematic re-orientation when their reasoning trajectory becomes misaligned.

#### 6.4. Practical Implications for Deployable AI

For practitioners deploying LLMs, this work highlights the need for careful consideration of how external information sources might interact with a model's internal reasoning. It suggests that even seemingly benign external inputs could inadvertently steer a model off course or, worse, enable malicious actors to manipulate its behavior through "thought injection." Developing robust filtering or verification layers for information introduced during tool-use, and strengthening models' inherent ability to scrutinize the relevance and correctness of *all* internal thoughts, will be crucial for building truly safe and reliable AI systems.

In conclusion, "How Well Can Reasoning Models Identify and Recover from Unhelpful Thoughts?" is a significant contribution that exposes a critical weakness in the meta-cognitive capabilities of modern LLMs. Its findings underscore the need for targeted advancements in self-reevaluation to build reasoning models that are not only powerful but also genuinely robust, reliable, and safe against increasingly sophisticated forms of manipulation.
