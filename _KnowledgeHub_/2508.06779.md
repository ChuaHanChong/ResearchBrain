# Learning a Vision-Based Footstep Planner for Hierarchical Walking Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania's GRASP Laboratory developed a vision-based hierarchical control framework, integrating a depth camera for elevation mapping with a reinforcement learning footstep planner and a low-level operational space controller for bipedal robots. This system achieved superior velocity tracking and higher success rates in simulation compared to a model predictive control baseline and demonstrated successful, albeit limited, transfer to hardware on challenging terrains.

## Problem

- Existing vision-based locomotion controllers for bipedal robots are often fragile to noise and limited by simplified terrain assumptions, struggling in unstructured environments.
- Real-time planning for bipedal locomotion is computationally challenging due to the high dimensionality of full multi-body dynamics, necessitating simplified models.
- Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation face significant challenges when transferring to physical robots due to the "sim-to-real gap" in dynamics and sensor characteristics.

## Method

- A hierarchical control framework utilizes a single depth camera for elevation mapping, an RL policy for high-level 3D footstep planning using an ALIP model, and a low-level ==Operational Space Controller (OSC)== for joint control.
- The RL policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with an asymmetric actor-critic, symmetric mirror loss, extensive domain randomization, and curriculum learning in a Drake simulation environment.
- Strategies like elevation map drift correction, along with randomizing dynamics parameters and observable states, are employed to facilitate sim-to-real transfer to the Cassie bipedal robot.

## Results

- In simulation, RL policies consistently outperformed an MPC baseline, achieving better velocity tracking MSE (e.g., **0.0209** m²/s² for Joint RL vs. **0.0726** m²/s² for MPC on ascending stairs) and significantly higher success rates (e.g., **99.5%** for Joint RL vs. **5%** for MPC on descending slopes with μ=**0.4**).
- RL policies demonstrated improved recovery from collisions, with significantly lower failure rates following a collision (Joint policy: **27.71%**, ALIP policy: **38.88%**) compared to the MPC baseline (**52.68%**).
- The learned policies successfully transferred to the Cassie robot, performing robustly on flat and mildly challenging outdoor terrains, though stair climbing performance on hardware was limited to a maximum of four steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - While effective, the ALIP reduced-order model has limitations in handling highly discontinuous terrains like stairs, where incorporating additional joint position inputs significantly improves policy performance.
> - RL policies can learn robust behaviors such as collision recovery and implicit obstacle avoidance without explicit reward terms, outperforming model-based approaches in handling unexpected events.
> - Hierarchical control architectures can introduce complexities in sim-to-real transfer, as learned high-level policies may generate plans that push low-level controllers into suboptimal operational regimes on hardware, leading to degraded performance.
