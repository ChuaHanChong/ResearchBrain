# Learning a Vision-Based Footstep Planner for Hierarchical Walking Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania's GRASP Laboratory developed a vision-based hierarchical control framework, integrating a depth camera for elevation mapping with a reinforcement learning footstep planner and a low-level operational space controller for bipedal robots. This system achieved superior velocity tracking and higher success rates in simulation compared to a model predictive control baseline and demonstrated successful, albeit limited, transfer to hardware on challenging terrains.

## Problem

- Existing vision-based locomotion controllers for bipedal robots are often fragile to noise and limited by simplified terrain assumptions, struggling in unstructured environments.
- Real-time planning for bipedal locomotion is computationally challenging due to the high dimensionality of full multi-body dynamics, necessitating simplified models.
- Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation face significant challenges when transferring to physical robots due to the "sim-to-real gap" in dynamics and sensor characteristics.

## Method

- A hierarchical control framework utilizes a single depth camera for elevation mapping, an RL policy for high-level 3D footstep planning using an ALIP model, and a low-level ==Operational Space Controller (OSC)== for joint control.
- The RL policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with an asymmetric actor-critic, symmetric mirror loss, extensive domain randomization, and curriculum learning in a Drake simulation environment.
- Strategies like elevation map drift correction, along with randomizing dynamics parameters and observable states, are employed to facilitate sim-to-real transfer to the Cassie bipedal robot.

## Results

- In simulation, RL policies consistently outperformed an MPC baseline, achieving better velocity tracking MSE (e.g., **0.0209** m²/s² for Joint RL vs. **0.0726** m²/s² for MPC on ascending stairs) and significantly higher success rates (e.g., **99.5%** for Joint RL vs. **5%** for MPC on descending slopes with μ=**0.4**).
- RL policies demonstrated improved recovery from collisions, with significantly lower failure rates following a collision (Joint policy: **27.71%**, ALIP policy: **38.88%**) compared to the MPC baseline (**52.68%**).
- The learned policies successfully transferred to the Cassie robot, performing robustly on flat and mildly challenging outdoor terrains, though stair climbing performance on hardware was limited to a maximum of four steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - While effective, the ALIP reduced-order model has limitations in handling highly discontinuous terrains like stairs, where incorporating additional joint position inputs significantly improves policy performance.
> - RL policies can learn robust behaviors such as collision recovery and implicit obstacle avoidance without explicit reward terms, outperforming model-based approaches in handling unexpected events.
> - Hierarchical control architectures can introduce complexities in sim-to-real transfer, as learned high-level policies may generate plans that push low-level controllers into suboptimal operational regimes on hardware, leading to degraded performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bipedal robot locomotion in unstructured and challenging terrains is a significant research area with broad applications, including search and rescue and disaster response. Achieving robust and adaptive locomotion in such environments necessitates the seamless integration of proprioceptive feedback and visual perception, akin to human walking behavior where surroundings are continuously evaluated for safe footholds while planning subsequent steps.

Historically, vision-based locomotion controllers often employ a modular framework encompassing perception, planning, and control. The perception module processes visual data to construct an environmental map, which then informs a high-level planner for foot placements or motion trajectories. Finally, a low-level controller translates these plans into actuator commands. While this pipeline has shown success in structured environments, it frequently relies on handcrafted visual features and model-based strategies. These approaches can be susceptible to noise and are often limited by simplified terrain assumptions, such as piecewise-planar surfaces, leading to fragility in real-world, complex settings.

Reduced-order models (ROMs), such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP) and its variants like the Angular Momentum Linear Inverted Pendulum (ALIP), have been instrumental in making planning for bipedal locomotion tractable. These simplified representations capture key dynamics, offering computationally efficient and interpretable formulations for real-time motion planning and control. Traditionally used in model-based control (e.g., with Model Predictive Control, MPC), ROMs have also found utility in more recent reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks.

Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) have significantly propelled the integration of visual perception and locomotion control. DRL enables both end-to-end control policies and hybrid control architectures that can operate directly on raw or minimally processed visual inputs. This reduces the dependence on manual feature engineering, enhancing flexibility and generalization. However, a primary challenge in DRL-based robotics remains the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation struggle to transfer directly to physical hardware due to modeling errors and environmental discrepancies. Domain randomization has emerged as a prevalent technique to mitigate this gap by randomizing simulated environments to expose policies to a broader distribution of conditions.

This work contributes to this landscape by proposing a vision-based hierarchical control framework that integrates an RL-based high-level footstep planner with a low-level operational space controller. It leverages the benefits of ROMs for state representation and RL for robust, adaptable planning, while directly addressing the challenges of vision integration, real-time performance, and sim-to-real transfer for bipedal robots navigating challenging, unstructured environments. The paper specifically investigates the use of the ALIP model as a low-dimensional state representation within an RL framework and its implications for hierarchical control and sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and adaptable vision-based control framework that enables bipedal robots to navigate challenging, unstructured terrains by integrating real-time visual perception with dynamic footstep planning.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in existing bipedal locomotion control systems:
- **Fragility of Current Vision-Based Frameworks:** Existing vision-based locomotion controllers often depend on manually designed visual pipelines or handcrafted visual features. These approaches are frequently fragile in real-world scenarios, susceptible to noise, and limited to simplified terrain assumptions (e.g., piecewise-planar surfaces). This fragility complicates real-time footstep planning in genuinely unstructured environments.
- **Computational Complexity of Full-Order Models:** While optimal control problems can embed full multi-body dynamics, the high dimensionality of bipedal robots makes such formulations computationally impractical for real-time optimization. This necessitates the use of simplified reduced-order models (ROMs), but their integration with robust vision-based strategies, especially for uneven terrain and noisy visual inputs, remains a challenge.
- **Challenges in Reinforcement Learning Deployment:** Although reinforcement learning (RL) has shown promise in learning robust locomotion controllers and integrating visual perception, policies learned in simulation often face significant difficulties when directly transferred to physical robots due to discrepancies between simulated and real-world dynamics and sensor characteristics (the "sim-to-real gap").
- **Need for Real-Time and Interpretable Solutions:** There is a need for control architectures that can generate real-time footstep plans and offer a degree of interpretability, particularly when integrating visual information.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines three main contributions:
- **A vision-based hierarchical controller:** This controller uses a single depth camera and a reduced-order model (ALIP) to enable efficient and interpretable 3D footstep planning through reinforcement learning. This aims to overcome the fragility of manual pipelines and leverage RL's ability to learn robust mappings.
- **Hardware validation of the full pipeline:** The framework is validated on both structured and unstructured terrains using the Cassie robot, with benchmarking against a model predictive control (MPC) baseline in simulation. This objective addresses the sim-to-real transfer challenge and demonstrates real-world applicability.
- **An analysis of the ALIP model and hierarchical control structure:** The research investigates the capabilities and limitations of using the ALIP model as a policy input and the impact of the hierarchical control structure on performance, especially regarding complex terrain and sim-to-real transfer. This aims to provide insights into the design choices for future bipedal locomotion systems.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to advance the state-of-the-art in bipedal robot navigation, making robots more capable of operating autonomously in complex, real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a vision-based hierarchical control framework for bipedal locomotion, integrating a high-level reinforcement learning (RL) footstep planner with a low-level Operational Space Controller (OSC). The approach can be broken down into perception, planning, control, and sim-to-real transfer strategies.

**A. Overall Architecture:**
The system operates hierarchically:
1. **Perception Module:** Generates an elevation map from depth camera data.
2. **High-Level Footstep Planner (RL Policy):** Uses the elevation map, robot state (ALIP model), and desired velocity commands to output 3D footstep locations.
3. **Low-Level Controller (OSC):** Tracks spline trajectories derived from the generated footsteps to control robot joints.

**B. Perception Module:**
- **Sensor:** An Intel RealSense D455 depth camera, mounted on the robot's pelvis and pointed downwards, provides point-cloud data.
- **Processing:** Point clouds are pre-processed to mask out the robot's legs. These are then fed into a robot-centric elevation mapping framework.
- **Elevation Map:** The map is updated at 30 Hz. It is cropped to a 64x64 grid with a resolution of 0.025 meters per cell. Unknown heights are filled via nearest-value interpolation, and a median filter smooths the values.
- **Input to RL:** The processed elevation map is concatenated with a grid of potential footstep locations. This grid is centered on a desired footstep position (u_dx, u_dy) derived from a periodic ALIP trajectory based on the velocity command and a constant stance width.
- **State Estimation:** Robot state, including the mass-normalized ALIP state, is estimated by a contact-aided invariant Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) using simulated or real sensor measurements.

**C. Reinforcement Learning for Footstep Planning (High-Level Controller):**
- **Inputs:** The RL policy receives the desired velocity (v_des), the mass-normalized ALIP state (s_ALIP), and an encoded elevation map (M_elevation).
- **Outputs:** The policy outputs 3D foot placement coordinates [p_x, p_y, p_z] in the local stance frame. Task-space actions are used for improved sample efficiency.
- **Policy Architecture:** A two-component neural network:
 - A **residual network** processes the encoded elevation map to produce a latent representation.
 - This latent representation is combined with the observation vector [v_des, s_ALIP] and fed into a **Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network**, which generates the actions.
 - Gait timing information is concatenated with the actions to ensure temporal consistency.
 - Actor and critic networks do not share layers.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function is a sum of positive reward terms and negative penalty terms, constrained to be non-negative (r = min(w^T r - w^T p, 0)).
 - **Reward Terms:** Encourage desired behavior, including tracking desired forward and lateral velocities (r_vx, r_vy), aligning footstep heights with terrain elevation (r_z), promoting pelvis stability (r_φ), encouraging action continuity (r_at), and regulating actions close to LQR-derived optimal footsteps (r_reg).
 - **Penalty Terms:** Discourage undesirable behavior, such as deviations in swing foot trajectory tracking (p_track), excessive torque usage (p_τ), stepping near terrain edges (p_edge), and front foot collisions with the terrain (p_collision). Specific penalties for edge and collision are critical for hardware stability.
- **Policy Training:**
 - **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used in a Drake simulation environment.
 - **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** The critic has access to privileged information (ground truth height map, joint positions, pelvis pose) to improve value estimation and training speed.
 - **Symmetric Mirror Loss:** A mirror loss is added to the PPO objective to promote symmetric gait and discourage asymmetric footsteps.
 - **Randomization:** Agent is initialized in randomized poses and swing phases. Commanded velocities are sampled, with backward walking restricted to flat terrain.
 - **Terrain Categories:** Training occurs across six terrain types: flat, flat with obstacles, blocks, stairs, stairs with slopes, and slopes (parameters detailed in Table III).
 - **Episode Termination:** Episodes terminate early if foot-to-pelvis distance is too small (fall), swing foot tracking error is too large, or self-collision occurs.

**D. Task-Space Objectives for Whole-Body QP (Low-Level Controller):**
- **Method:** An inverse-dynamics operational-space control Quadratic Program (QP) translates footstep plans into joint commands.
- **Swing Foot Trajectory:** A single-segment polynomial spline is generated for each new footstep target. This QP ensures continuity of desired position, velocity, and acceleration, minimizes swing foot acceleration, and maintains ground clearance.
- **CoM Control:** Center of mass (CoM) height is controlled to a virtual plane defined by the ALIP model for the current and upcoming stance foot.
- **Other Controls:** Swing foot angle is controlled parallel to this plane. Pelvis pitch and roll are controlled to zero, swing leg yaw to zero, and pelvis yaw rate matches operator commands (zero during training).

**E. Sim-to-Real Transfer Strategies:**
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive randomization of dynamics parameters (PD gains, joint damping, link mass, ALIP state noise, friction coefficient) and observable states (elevation map shifts and uniform noise, point cloud biases, communication delays, external pelvis forces) is applied during training (Table IV).
- **Curriculum Learning:**
 - **Initial Phase:** Training starts on flat terrain, low stairs (max 10 cm), and slopes without domain randomization to establish basic stability.
 - **Second Phase:** After initial convergence, all terrain types and full domain randomization are introduced. The probability of flat terrain is kept low (10%) to prevent forgetting negative x-velocity commands.
- **Elevation Map Drift Correction:** To counteract vertical drift in the state estimator caused by foot impacts, a correction strategy is applied. Before each map update, the vertical offset between the current stance foot position and the corresponding elevation in the map is calculated and used to correct the map's height.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed framework through extensive simulation experiments and hardware validation on the Cassie bipedal robot.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
- **Controller Comparison (Table V):** Two RL policies were evaluated: ALIP (using ALIP state) and Joint (using ALIP state + joint positions). These were benchmarked against a vision-based ALIP Model Predictive Control (MPC) baseline from prior work.
 - **Velocity Tracking:** Both RL policies consistently outperformed the MPC baseline in terms of Mean Squared Error (MSE) of velocity tracking across all evaluated terrain types (ascending/descending stairs and slopes) and friction coefficients (0.4, 0.8, 1.1). For example, on ascending stairs with μ=0.8, ALIP RL had an MSE of 0.0514 m²/s² and Joint RL had 0.0209 m²/s², while MPC had 0.0726 m²/s².
 - **Success Rates:** RL policies achieved higher success rates than MPC for most terrain types, particularly on stairs and challenging slope descents. On descending slopes with μ=0.4, ALIP RL had 82.5% success, Joint RL had 99.5%, while MPC only managed 5%.
 - **ALIP vs. Joint Policy:** The Joint policy generally demonstrated better velocity tracking performance compared to the ALIP policy across all terrains, while maintaining comparable high success rates.
- **Collision Evaluation:**
 - **Frequency:** The MPC baseline exhibited a significantly higher frequency of hard collisions (forces exceeding 1000 N) per episode (average 0.4833) compared to the RL policies (ALIP: 0.1467, Joint: 0.11).
 - **Failure Rate Post-Collision:** RL policies showed improved recovery capabilities. The failure rate following a collision for MPC was 52.68%, whereas ALIP and Joint policies had considerably lower failure rates of 38.88% and 27.71%, respectively. These recovery behaviors were observed to transfer to hardware.
- **Obstacle Avoidance (Fig. 6):** The RL policies learned obstacle avoidance behaviors directly from the training environment without explicit safety or obstacle avoidance reward terms. The robot could navigate around obstacles while maintaining forward motion. However, it was noted that the policy could sometimes get stuck between obstacles, leading to in-place stepping.

**B. Hardware Experiments (Fig. 8):**
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The policy successfully transferred to hardware despite discrepancies such as a bent right bar and a 1 kg lighter custom battery compared to the nominal URDF model.
- **Performance on Flat Terrains:** The controller performed successfully on both indoor and outdoor flat-like terrains with perception in the loop.
- **Performance on Stairs:** The controller showed limited performance when ascending stairs. The ALIP policy achieved a maximum of two steps before failure, while the Joint policy performed slightly better, managing up to four steps. This indicates a degradation of performance on highly discontinuous terrains in the real world.

**C. Analysis:**
- **ALIP as a Policy Input:**
 - **Stair Climbing:** In extended simulation evaluations (200 episodes, 50s duration) for stair-climbing, the Joint policy significantly outperformed the ALIP policy, averaging 10.024 steps before failure compared to ALIP's 6.534 steps. This suggests limitations of the ALIP model in handling substantial height variations inherent in stair negotiation.
 - **Feature Attribution (Fig. 7):** Saliency maps and integrated gradients indicated that the influence of joint position inputs increased, while the contribution of the ALIP state decreased, with increasing terrain complexity (e.g., from flat to stairs) in the Joint policy. This supports the observation that more expressive inputs beyond the ALIP model are necessary for challenging discontinuous environments.
- **Hierarchical Control (Table VI):**
 - **OSC Tracking Performance:** An L2 ratio metric was used to quantify the tracking performance of the Operational Space Controller (OSC) using reference trajectories from both MPC and Joint footstep planners, in both simulation and hardware.
 - **Sim vs. Real Discrepancy:** While both planners yielded relatively low and comparable L2 ratios in simulation, the RL planner consistently resulted in *higher* L2 ratios across all output components (swing foot position/velocity, CoM position/velocity) in hardware. For instance, the L2 ratio for swing foot ẏ in hardware was 1.3454 for RL compared to 0.1747 in simulation, an error difference of 1.1707. For MPC, this difference was 0.8368 (1.0785 real vs. 0.2417 sim).
 - **Implication:** This suggests that in the real world, the RL policy generates footstep positions that push the low-level OSC into operational regimes where its performance degrades more significantly compared to the MPC planner, which produces trajectories that remain within the OSC's more effective operating range. The sim-to-real gap further exacerbates this degradation in OSC performance for the RL policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards enabling bipedal robots to autonomously and robustly navigate complex, unstructured terrains, an area critical for applications such as search and rescue and disaster response.

The significance of this work lies in several key areas:
- **Advancement in Vision-Based Locomotion:** The development of a vision-based hierarchical control framework that successfully integrates a single depth camera for elevation mapping with an RL footstep planner represents an advance over previous methods that relied on fragile, handcrafted visual pipelines or limited proprioceptive feedback. This makes bipedal robots more capable of real-time footstep planning in diverse and challenging environments.
- **Effective Integration of RL and Reduced-Order Models:** The study demonstrates the utility of combining reinforcement learning for high-level decision-making with a reduced-order model (ALIP) for state representation. This approach effectively balances problem complexity, training efficiency, and interpretability by using a low-dimensional yet informative encoding of robot dynamics.
- **Rigorous Hardware Validation:** The successful transfer and partial validation of the learned policy on the underactuated bipedal robot Cassie in real-world outdoor environments is a crucial outcome. This hardware demonstration provides concrete evidence of the framework's practical applicability and its robustness against real-world uncertainties and discrepancies not fully captured in simulation.
- **Detailed Analysis of Hierarchical Control Challenges:** The analytical evaluation provides valuable insights into the limitations of reduced-order models (specifically ALIP) in highly discontinuous terrains like stairs. It also illuminates the complexities introduced by hierarchical control architectures during sim-to-real transfer, particularly how a learned high-level policy can drive a low-level controller into less optimal operating regimes in hardware. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the challenges in designing and deploying complex robotic control systems.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The identified limitations concerning the ALIP model's representational capacity for complex terrains and the difficulties in aligning learned high-level policies with low-level controllers provide clear directions for future research. This includes exploring more expressive reduced-order models and developing strategies to improve the compatibility and robustness of hierarchical control layers across the sim-to-real gap.

The potential impact of this research is in enhancing the autonomy and operational range of bipedal robots, enabling them to perform tasks in environments currently inaccessible or hazardous for human operators. By demonstrating improved robustness, real-time visual awareness, and a methodical approach to sim-to-real transfer, this work contributes to the foundational knowledge necessary for deploying agile, dynamic legged robots in practical, unstructured settings.
