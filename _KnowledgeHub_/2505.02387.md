# RM-R1: Reward Modeling as Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and collaborators present RM-R1, a new class of Reasoning Reward Models (REASRMS) that integrate deep, interpretable reasoning into the reward assignment process. This approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on three major reward model benchmarks by employing a two-stage training pipeline (reasoning distillation and reinforcement learning) and a structured "Chain-of-Rubrics" mechanism, providing transparent and coherent rationales for judgments.

## Problem

- Scalar-based Reward Models (ScalarRMs) are opaque, lacking interpretability or explicit reasoning steps, limiting their utility in complex, reasoning-intensive tasks.
- Generative Reward Models (GenRMs) offer free-form textual judgments but often provide superficial, unstructured, and less reliable reasoning, leading to suboptimal performance compared to ScalarRMs.
- Accurate human preference judgments in real-world scenarios require deep cognitive processes like criteria inference, trade-off analysis, and consequence simulation, which existing reward models do not fully embody.

## Method

- Introduces ==Reasoning Reward Models (REASRMS)==, a new class of generative reward models designed to integrate deep, interpretable reasoning into the reward assignment process.
- Proposes a ==two-stage training pipeline==: an initial Reasoning Distillation stage to bootstrap the model's ability to generate structured reasoning traces, followed by Reinforcement Learning (RL) using GRPO to refine generalization and critical thinking.
- Develops a ==Chain-of-Rubrics (CoR)== rollout mechanism via a sophisticated system prompt that guides the model to adapt its reasoning strategy based on task type (e.g., self-solve for reasoning tasks, generate tailored criteria for chat tasks) prior to making a judgment.

## Results

- RM-R1 models achieve state-of-the-art performance across RewardBench, RM-Bench, and RMB benchmarks, outperforming larger open-weight models and even proprietary models by up to **4.9%**.
- On the reasoning-intensive RM-Bench, RM-R1-DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-32B sets new state-of-the-art accuracy with **91.8%** in math and **74.1%** in code.
- Ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of the two-stage training (distillation + RL), Chain-of-Rubrics prompting, and explicit query categorization, demonstrating that these components are crucial for superior performance and interpretability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating robust, structured reasoning capabilities into reward modeling significantly enhances both RM's interpretability and its performance, particularly in complex scenarios.
> - A two-stage training pipeline combining reasoning distillation with subsequent reinforcement learning is highly effective for training high-performing Reasoning Reward Models (REASRMS), allowing for data efficiency and generalization.
> - Structured prompting mechanisms like Chain-of-Rubrics and explicit task categorization are crucial for enabling models to proactively generate effective reasoning traces and adapt evaluation strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reward models (RMs) are integral to the post-training alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human preferences, particularly through reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF). Existing reward modeling approaches typically fall into two categories: scalar-based reward models (ScalarRMs) and generative reward models (GenRMs).

ScalarRMs treat reward modeling as a classification task, assigning a numerical score to a response. While often effective and direct, these models are generally opaque, lacking transparency regarding their decision-making process. This characteristic can limit their utility in complex, reasoning-intensive tasks where intermediate justification is beneficial.

GenRMs, conversely, leverage the generative capabilities of LLMs to produce free-form textual judgments. While these models offer increased transparency, prior research suggests that the reasoning they provide is frequently superficial or inadequate for reliable judgment, leading to suboptimal performance compared to their scalar counterparts.

This paper addresses a perceived gap in current reward modeling: the insufficient integration of deep, interpretable reasoning into the reward assignment process. It observes that accurate human preference judgments inherently involve multifaceted cognitive processes, such as inferring evaluation criteria, managing trade-offs, and predicting consequences, all of which require extensive reasoning. Existing methods, including process supervision and generative verifiers, have explored aspects of reasoning or step-level evaluation, but this work posits that a systematic approach to training RMs specifically for comprehensive reasoning is needed.

The paper aims to introduce a new class of models, Reasoning Reward Models (REASRMS), which explicitly formulate reward modeling as a reasoning task. By emphasizing the generation of long and coherent reasoning chains, this work seeks to enhance both the interpretability and performance of reward models, moving beyond simple outcome prediction or superficial generative explanations. It positions itself as a systematic empirical investigation into training paradigms that can effectively leverage and integrate reasoning capabilities into reward models, aiming to overcome the limitations of both ScalarRMs and prior GenRMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central question driving this research is: "Can we cast reward modeling as a reasoning task?" The authors are motivated by several observations regarding the limitations of current reward modeling paradigms and the nature of human judgment:

- **Critical Role of Reward Models:** Reward models are essential for aligning LLMs with human preferences, serving as scalable proxies for human evaluators in RLHF pipelines. The quality of these reward signals directly impacts the alignment of the final LLM.
- **Limitations of Existing RM Categories:**
 - **Scalar-based RMs (ScalarRMs)** are efficient but inherently opaque. They provide a numerical score without any explanation of *why* a particular score was assigned, hindering transparency and limiting their ability to handle nuanced or complex preference tasks that demand justification.
 - **Generative RMs (GenRMs)** offer textual feedback and greater transparency. However, the reasoning provided by current GenRMs is often reported as superficial or unhelpful, leading to performance that may not consistently match or exceed ScalarRMs. This suggests that simply generating text is not equivalent to deep, reliable reasoning.
- **Nature of Human Preference Judgment:** Real-world decision-making and preference judgments, particularly in complex domains, necessitate deep cognitive processes. These include inferring latent evaluation criteria, weighing multiple conflicting criteria, and simulating potential outcomes. Such tasks inherently require extensive reasoning beyond simple pattern matching or direct classification. The paper provides an illustrative example where judging a conversational response (e.g., to a user expressing self-doubt) requires understanding emotional harm and psychological safety, which an off-the-shelf instruct model might miss by over-fitting to superficial patterns.
- **Hypothesis on Reasoning Integration:** Inspired by the advancements in chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning for complex tasks, the authors hypothesize that explicitly integrating long and coherent reasoning chains into the reward modeling process will significantly improve both the interpretability of the RM's decisions and its overall performance.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

- To introduce and validate a new class of generative reward models, Reasoning Reward Models (REASRMS), which explicitly frame reward modeling as a reasoning task.
- To propose and implement a reasoning-oriented training pipeline, culminating in a family of models termed RM-R1. This pipeline aims to effectively instill and leverage reasoning capabilities in LLMs for reward assignment.
- To design and implement a "Chain-of-Rubrics (CoR)" mechanism within RM-R1. This mechanism enables the model to self-generate sample-level evaluation rubrics or solutions (for math/code tasks) and then evaluate candidate responses against these generated criteria.
- To empirically demonstrate that RM-R1 models achieve superior performance on standard reward model benchmarks, surpassing existing state-of-the-art open-weight and proprietary models.
- To conduct a thorough analysis of the training pipeline, including ablation studies, scaling effects, and case studies, to understand the critical components contributing to the success of REASRMS.

Ultimately, the goal is to develop reward models that not only provide accurate preference judgments but also offer transparent, verifiable, and context-dependent justifications for those judgments, mirroring human evaluative processes more closely.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Reasoning Reward Models (REASRMS) and details a two-stage training pipeline for RM-R1, starting from an instruction-tuned language model. The methodology emphasizes the integration of explicit, structured reasoning into the reward modeling process.

#### 3.1. Task Definition

The generative reward modeling task is defined as taking a prompt (`x`), two responses (`y_a`, `y_b`), and a ground truth preferred label (`l`), and generating a textual judgment (`j`). This judgment `j` includes the model's prediction of the preferred response (`l̂`). The overall objective is to maximize the probability that the generated `l̂` matches the ground truth `l`.

#### 3.2. Training Pipeline for RM-R1

The training of RM-R1 consists of two sequential stages:

- **Stage 1: Reasoning Distillation**
 - **Initialization**: The process begins with an off-the-shelf instruction-tuned model (e.g., Qwen-2.5-14B-Instruct).
 - **Data Synthesis**: A subset of preference data (`D_sub`) is selected. For each sample (`x`, `y_a`, `y_b`, `l`), an "oracle" model (e.g., Claude-3.7-Sonnet, OpenAI-O3) is prompted to generate a high-quality, structured reasoning trace (`r^(i)`) that justifies the selection of the preferred response (`y_l^(i)`). This involves a two-stage correction process where Claude-3.7-Sonnet generates initial traces, and OpenAI-O3 corrects any inaccuracies, particularly on harder chat tasks.
 - **Distillation Dataset Creation**: The generated reasoning trace is concatenated with the chosen label to form `y_trace^(i)`. The distillation dataset (`D_distill`) consists of pairs `(x, y_trace^(i))`.
 - **Objective**: The instruction-tuned model is fine-tuned to minimize the negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss of generating these `y_trace^(i)` sequences, given the prompt and responses. This stage aims to bootstrap the model with the ability to produce consistent and structured reasoning. A relatively small dataset (approximately 8.7K examples) is used for distillation.

- **Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning (RL)**
 - **Motivation**: Distilled models can overfit to specific patterns in the training data, limiting generalization. The RL stage is introduced to enhance the model's critical thinking and generalization abilities for reasoning-based rewarding.
 - **Policy Formulation**: The reward model `r_θ(j | x, y_a, y_b)` is treated as a policy model.
 - **Optimization Algorithm**: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (a variant of PPO) is used. GRPO optimizes the policy by maximizing an objective that incorporates the reward for the generated judgment (`R(x, j)`) and a KL-divergence regularization term against a reference reward model (`r_ref`). The `r_ref` is typically the model checkpoint obtained after the distillation stage.
 - **Reward Design**: A simplified, correctness-based reward function is used: `R(x, j|y_a, y_b) = +1` if the extracted judgment (`l̂`) matches the ground truth (`l`), and `-1` otherwise. Format rewards were explored but found to have no significant impact.

#### 3.3. Chain-of-Rubrics (CoR) Rollout Mechanism

To effectively elicit the reasoning capabilities during inference and RL training, a specialized system prompt (shown in Figure 3 of the paper) is designed for RM-R1. This prompt guides the model through a structured reasoning process:

- **Task Categorization**: The model is instructed to first classify the input sample into one of two categories:
 - **`<type> Reasoning </type>`**: For tasks involving math, coding, domain knowledge, multi-step inference, or logical deduction.
 - **`<type> Chat </type>`**: For open-ended conversations, factual questions, stylistic rewrites, safety questions, or general helpfulness requests without deep reasoning.
- **Type-Specific Rollout Strategy**:
 - **For Reasoning Tasks**: The model is prompted to:
 1. Solve the client's question itself, presenting the solution within `<solution>` tags.
 2. Evaluate the two chatbot responses based on correctness, completeness, and reasoning quality, referencing its own solution.
 3. Include its evaluation within `<eval>` tags, using specific tags for quotes or summaries from each chatbot.
 4. Conclude with a final judgment in the format `<answer>[[A]]</answer>` or `<answer>[[B]]</answer>`.
 - **For Chat Tasks**: The model is prompted to:
 1. Generate evaluation criteria (rubric) tailored to the question and context, enclosed in `<rubric>` tags.
 2. Assign weights to each rubric item.
 3. Include a justification (`<justify>`) within the `<rubric>` for the chosen criteria and weights.
 4. Compare both chatbot responses against this rubric.
 5. Provide its evaluation within `<eval>` tags, using quotes/summaries.
 6. Conclude with a final judgment in the format `<answer>[[A]]</answer>` or `<answer>[[B]]</answer>`.

This CoR mechanism enables RM-R1 to tailor its evaluative approach based on the perceived task type, applying rubric-based evaluation for chat tasks and correctness-first judgment for reasoning tasks.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmarks**: RM-R1 is evaluated on three benchmarks: RewardBench, RM-Bench (the most reasoning-intensive), and RMB (comprehensive helpfulness and harmlessness).
- **Training Data**: A cleaned subset of Skywork Reward Preference 80K (excluding data with spurious correlations), 8K examples from Code-Preference-Pairs, and the full Math-DPO-10K dataset are used.
- **Baselines**: RM-R1 is compared against ScalarRMs (e.g., INF-ORM-Llama3.1-70B), GenRMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-sonnet), and other REASRMS (e.g., JudgeLRM, DeepSeek-GRM).
- **Model Variants**: RM-R1 models are trained based on Qwen-2.5-Instruct or DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen, ranging from 7B to 32B parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of RM-R1 models yielded several findings regarding performance, training efficacy, scaling behavior, and interpretability.

#### 4.1. State-of-the-Art Performance

RM-R1 models demonstrated superior average performance across RewardBench, RM-Bench, and RMB.
- On average, RM-R1-DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-14B surpassed leading existing reward models, including INF-ORM-Llama3.1-70B, Nemotron-4-340B-Reward, and GPT-4o, by up to 4.9%, despite being significantly smaller.
- The 32B variants, RM-R1-Qwen-Instruct-32B and RM-R1-DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-32B, further extended this performance lead.
- This performance highlights the potential of REASRMS, particularly in contrast to previous GenRMs that often showed suboptimal performance compared to ScalarRMs. RM-R1 models consistently outperformed top-tier ScalarRMs.

#### 4.2. Effective Training towards Reasoning for Reward Modeling

The specialized reasoning-oriented training pipeline contributed to substantial performance gains.
- RM-R1-Qwen-Instruct-14B consistently outperformed Self-taught-evaluator-llama-3.1-70B, a model five times its size.
- On RM-Bench, identified as the most reasoning-intensive benchmark, RM-R1 models showed significant improvements, exceeding the top-performing baseline by up to 8.7%. RM-R1-DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-32B achieved new state-of-the-art results with 91.8% accuracy in math (compared to 73% for previous best) and 74.1% in code (compared to 63%).
- The Instruct-based RM-R1 models demonstrated data efficiency, achieving competitive performance with only 8.7K examples used for distillation, in contrast to larger datasets (e.g., 800K for DeepSeek-Distilled).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies of Training Recipes

An ablation study (Table 2) investigated the contribution of different training components using the Qwen-2.5-Instruct-32B model:
- **Cold Start RL Alone**: This approach, using only rule-based rewards for correctness, provided a slight improvement on hard chat and reasoning tasks but did not close the performance gap compared to the full RM-R1.
- **Chain-of-Rubrics (CoR) Prompting**: Incorporating self-generated rubrics for judgment (even without task categorization) improved overall performance, particularly for chat and safety tasks.
- **Query Categorization (QC)**: Explicitly categorizing tasks into "Chat" or "Reasoning" within the CoR prompt further enhanced reasoning performance, indicating that clearer task guidance is beneficial.
- **Distillation + RL + Rubrics + QC (RM-R1)**: Seeding the model with high-quality reasoning traces via distillation before RL training yielded the strongest results across all task categories (chat, chat hard, safety, reasoning), confirming the necessity of a warm start for robust reasoning.

#### 4.4. Scaling Effects

The analysis explored how performance varied with model size and inference-time compute:
- **Model Sizes (Figure 4a)**: RM-R1 exhibited a clear scaling law; larger models (7B, 14B, 32B) not only achieved better absolute performance but also showed greater relative performance *gains* from the reasoning training procedure. This suggests that the training effectively leverages the inherent reasoning capabilities of larger models.
- **Inference-time Computation (Figure 4b)**: Performance consistently improved as the allowed token budget during inference increased (from 512 to 8192 tokens). This finding underscores the benefits of longer reasoning chains in reward modeling.

#### 4.5. Effectiveness of Reasoning Training

A comparison of reasoning-based training methods against answer-only (SFT) approaches (Table 3) showed that:
- Reasoning-based training (Instruct + Distilled + SFT, and RM-R1) consistently outperformed SFT-only approaches (Instruct + SFT) across benchmarks, even when trained on the same amount of data.
- High-quality distillation alone (Instruct + Distilled), applied to a small data subset, provided notable gains, highlighting the value of structured intermediate reasoning even before full RL training.

#### 4.6. Training Dynamics

Observing the training dynamics (Figure 5) of the Qwen-2.5-14B-Instruct model under RL:
- **Cold Start RL**: The model gradually increased response length as it learned to reason, but training became unstable towards the end, with a sharp drop in the reward curve, suggesting potential overfitting.
- **Warm Start RL (after distillation)**: The model started with stronger reasoning abilities, exhibiting longer responses initially. It then produced more concise traces before gradually increasing length again, with a smooth and consistently rising reward curve, indicating more stable and efficient learning.

#### 4.7. Case Study

A qualitative case study (Table 4) demonstrated the benefits of RM-R1's reasoning capabilities:
- **Transparent Judging Process**: RM-R1 generated interpretable and coherent reasoning traces, explicitly articulating its rationale for preferring one response over another.
- **High-Quality, Question-Dependent Rubrics**: RM-R1 accurately understood the context of the question (e.g., medical information) and generated appropriate rubrics (e.g., prioritizing "accuracy"). In contrast, a cold-start RL model focused on superficial features like "relevance" or "comprehensiveness" without considering factual correctness.
- **Faithful Adherence to Rubrics and Content-Based Judgment**: RM-R1 consistently applied its self-generated rubrics, identifying inaccuracies in candidate responses based on factual content rather than surface-level presentation, leading to structured and verifiable judgments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "RM-R1: REWARD MODELING AS REASONING" carries several significant implications and potential impacts for the field of large language models and AI alignment:

- **Reconceptualization of Reward Modeling**: The work fundamentally redefines reward modeling from primarily a prediction or classification task to an explicit reasoning process. By formally integrating chain-of-thought-style reasoning and structured evaluation rubrics, RM-R1 demonstrates that reward models can not only provide judgments but also offer transparent justifications for them. This shift moves reward modeling closer to human-like evaluative cognition.
- **Enhanced LLM Alignment**: More accurate, reliable, and interpretable reward signals directly contribute to improved alignment of LLMs with complex human preferences. By enabling RMs to conduct deep reasoning, RM-R1 can provide better guidance to LLMs during reinforcement learning, potentially leading to models that are more helpful, harmless, and honest in nuanced situations.
- **Improved Interpretability and Trust**: The explicit reasoning traces generated by RM-R1, including self-generated rubrics and step-by-step evaluations, enhance the interpretability of reward judgments. This transparency can increase trust in automated evaluations, aid in debugging LLM behaviors, and facilitate a deeper understanding of *why* certain responses are preferred over others. This contrasts with opaque scalar reward models or GenRMs with superficial rationales.
- **New State-of-the-Art for Generative Reward Models**: RM-R1 establishes new performance benchmarks for generative reward models, consistently outperforming much larger open-weight models and even proprietary systems like GPT-4o. This achievement validates the viability of reasoning-enhanced generative RMs, challenging previous notions that generative RMs were inherently suboptimal compared to scalar RMs.
- **Validated Training Paradigm**: The systematic empirical study and ablation analyses provide insights into effective training strategies for REASRMS. The findings underscore the importance of both reasoning-oriented distillation (for a warm start) and reinforcement learning (for generalization and critical thinking), along with structured inference mechanisms like Chain-of-Rubrics and query categorization. This offers a robust blueprint for future research and development in reward model training.
- **Implications for Resource Allocation**: The observed scaling laws with model size and inference-time compute suggest that reasoning capabilities are indeed beneficial and scalable for reward modeling. The ability of REASRMS to dynamically allocate more compute for complex problems, as envisioned, could lead to more efficient and adaptive reward systems. While long reasoning chains may increase latency, the paper suggests that architectural considerations (e.g., parallel processing) can mitigate practical overheads.
- **Future Research Directions**: The work opens several avenues for future research, including:
 - **Active Preference Collection**: Using REASRMS to intelligently query human preferences only when the current rubric set is insufficient for novel samples.
 - **Multimodal/Agentic Reward Modeling**: Extending the reasoning-based approach to scenarios involving multiple modalities or complex agentic interactions.
 - **Efficiency Improvements**: Further optimizing the computational overhead associated with generating long reasoning chains, building on the foundation established by this work.

In summary, this research advances the field of AI alignment by introducing a novel, interpretable, and high-performing class of reward models that explicitly leverage reasoning. By doing so, it contributes to the development of more sophisticated and trustworthy LLMs.
