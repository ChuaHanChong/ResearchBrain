# MV-WAM: Manifold-Aware World Action Model with Value Augmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> MV-WAM introduces a manifold-aware World Action Model that explicitly addresses the geometric mismatch between visual observations and robot actions to enhance generalization in robotic manipulation. It achieved 55.7% success on out-of-distribution tasks in simulation, outperforming the strongest World Action Model baseline by 29.3%, and a mean success rate of 77.5% in real-world tasks, surpassing baselines like π0 by 35%.

## Problem

- Existing World Action Models (WAMs) for robotic manipulation exhibit a significant 'generalization gap' in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios despite strong in-domain performance.
- There is a structural mismatch between the intrinsically heterogeneous representational manifolds of high-dimensional visual observations and low-dimensional, precision-critical robot actions.
- Joint optimization of these incompatible modalities under a single objective compromises action learning, leading to brittle action representations that fail under distribution shifts.

## Method

- **MV-WAM** employs a novel end-to-end ==Diffusion Transformer (DiT)== based framework with a ==dual-branch expert architecture== that unifies video world modeling, robotic action prediction, and value estimation.
- It utilizes ==manifold-aware dual prediction objectives==, applying distinct ==flow-matching== transformation functions (velocity-prediction for video, x0-prediction for action/value) tailored to each modality's intrinsic geometric properties.
- A ==progress-valued regulation== mechanism incorporates ==Monte Carlo value estimation== and a value-guided rollback protocol for autonomous detection and recovery from execution deviations during online inference.

## Results

- In simulation (RoboTwin 2.0), MV-WAM achieved an **84.0%** success rate in in-domain tasks and **55.7%** in out-of-distribution (Random) scenarios, outperforming the strongest WAM baseline (HALO) by **29.3%** in Random conditions.
- It demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization capabilities, achieving **54.0%** success on unseen OOD tasks, and further improved to **74.6%** with 10-shot adaptation.
- In real-world experiments on a dual-arm robot, MV-WAM achieved a mean success rate of **77.5%**, significantly surpassing baselines like (**42.5%**) and RDT (**32.5%**), demonstrating effective transfer of simulation benefits to physical deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The generalization gap in WAMs stems from a fundamental 'structural mismatch' between the distinct geometric manifolds of visual observations and robot actions, where visual complexity can overshadow action learning.
> - Tailored, modality-specific optimization objectives and a structured causal attention mask are crucial for learning robust action representations, preventing cross-modal interference, and improving out-of-distribution generalization.
> - Integrating a progress-value estimation and a rollback mechanism allows for real-time error detection and self-correction, enabling more reliable and autonomous execution in dynamic and uncertain environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in embodied robotics: achieving robust and generalizable manipulation across diverse and unseen environments. The field has seen advancements from two main paradigms: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs).

VLA models, building on large-scale vision-language pretraining, have significantly improved robotic manipulation in semantic and task-level generalization. However, they have shown limitations in visual and environmental generalization, particularly in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, because policies trained on limited demonstration data often fail to capture the visual diversity of real-world deployment.

WAMs extend the VLA paradigm by integrating large-scale action-free video data. This approach aims to leverage the richer visual diversity available in video data to broaden visual generalization beyond what curated demonstrations can offer. Existing WAMs vary in how they couple video priors with policies, ranging from decoupled approaches where video models and policies interact via generated visual predictions or latent representations, to fully unified approaches that tokenize all signals into a single stream, and Mixture-of-Transformers (MoTs) designs that use modality-specific experts while sharing global attention. While WAMs have demonstrated improvements over non-WAM baselines, particularly in in-domain performance, the generalization gains to OOD scenarios have not kept pace, resulting in a persistent "generalization gap." The paper notes that improvements from video priors often disproportionately benefit visual prediction rather than action robustness, which is the primary target for WAMs.

This research identifies a core issue underlying this generalization gap: a structural mismatch between the intrinsically heterogeneous representational manifolds of visual observations and robot actions. Visual data is high-dimensional and densely structured, while robot actions are low-dimensional and precision-critical. Joint optimization of these incompatible modalities, especially when visual tokens dominate the objective, compromises action learning and leads to brittle action representations under distribution shifts. MV-WAM is presented as a novel framework that seeks to address this structural manifold mismatch directly, aiming to improve action generalization by treating these modalities as geometrically distinct during the learning process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to bridge the generalization gap observed in existing World Action Models (WAMs) for robotic manipulation. While current WAMs show strong performance on in-domain tasks, their gains do not extend proportionally to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, indicating a lack of robust generalization.

The motivation for MV-WAM stems from a detailed analysis of this generalization gap, which the authors attribute to a "structural mismatch between visual and action modalities."
- **Heterogeneous Manifolds**: Visual observations and robot actions are inherently different in their characteristics and thus reside on distinct representational manifolds. Visual data is high-dimensional and characterized by spatial and photometric regularities, implying a high-curvature manifold. In contrast, robot actions are low-dimensional, temporally structured, and require high precision in control, suggesting a smoother, low-curvature manifold.
- **Compromised Joint Optimization**: When these intrinsically heterogeneous manifolds are subjected to a single, shared optimization objective, the learning process for actions is compromised. The sheer volume and complexity of visual tokens tend to dominate the shared objective, leading to under-trained and brittle action representations. This issue is masked in in-domain scenarios by strong cross-modal correlations but becomes apparent under distribution shifts, where visual perturbations can propagate through shared parameters and degrade action prediction.
- **Formal Analysis**: The paper empirically verifies this manifold asymmetry through t-SNE analysis, showing action representations are more sensitive to distribution shifts than visual ones, and through manifold curvature estimates (κ_v = 3.8 ± 0.6 vs. κ_a = 1.3 ± 0.2, p < 0.001), confirming a significant geometric discrepancy. A formal analysis, referencing generalization bounds for Lipschitz functions on Riemannian manifolds, explains that a manifold-agnostic objective inflates the effective curvature experienced by the action branch from its native κ_a to κ_v. This leads to a looser generalization bound for the action modality, directly manifesting as degraded robustness under distribution shift.

Given this motivation, the key objectives for MV-WAM are:
1. **Develop a unified end-to-end framework**: This framework should jointly model visual prediction (future imaginations), robot action generation, and value estimation within a single architecture.
2. **Introduce a manifold-aware optimization scheme**: This scheme must explicitly account for the structural heterogeneity across modalities, ensuring each modality is supervised according to the intrinsic structure of its target manifold to eliminate cross-modal optimization interference and gradient imbalance.
3. **Implement a cross-modality causal mask**: This mask should hierarchically ground action tokens in predicted visual frames and value function tokens in both visual and action contexts, thus conditioning action generation on the predicted visual future while preserving modality-specific structure.
4. **Incorporate a progress-value regulation mechanism**: This mechanism should estimate task completion and detect misalignment between predicted frames and generated actions during execution, enabling autonomous identification and recovery from execution deviations through value-guided rollback.

By addressing these objectives, MV-WAM aims to enable action-free video to serve as a direct source of policy generalization and achieve robust, generalizable robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MV-WAM (Manifold-Aware World Action Model with Value Augmentation) is an end-to-end framework that unifies video world modeling, robotic action prediction, and value estimation. It is designed to address the generalization gap by explicitly accounting for the manifold mismatch between visual and action modalities.

#### 3.1. Novel End-to-End Framework

MV-WAM is built upon a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone, extended into a manifold-aware multi-objective Mixture-of-Transformers (MoTs) architecture.

- **Dual Branch Expert Architecture**:
 - **Video Expert**: Composed of 30 Transformer blocks, initialized from the large-scale pretrained video generative model WoW-1.3B. It employs a spatiotemporal Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to compress raw visual observations into latent representations, which are then processed by the DiT backbone for video diffusion modeling. Task instructions, encoded by a umT5 text encoder, are injected into this expert via cross-attention.
 - **Action-Value Expert**: Also comprises 30 DiT blocks, but is trained from scratch. It takes multi-view visual states, encoded by SigLIP2, as a visual condition. Language embeddings are injected into odd Transformer layers, and SigLIP2 visual embeddings into even layers. Within this expert, action tokens and state tokens share a unified encoder-decoder for action generation, while value tokens are processed by an independent encoder-decoder for separate value estimation.
- **Temporal Alignment**: To ensure precise temporal coherence between action tokens and video latents, the Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) scaling factor for the Action-Value Expert is set to 1/4 of that used in the Video Expert.
- **Structured Causal Attention Mask**: This mask regulates information flow to preserve modality-specific priors and ensure appropriate conditioning:
 - Video tokens are restricted to self-attention only within the visual stream, maintaining high-fidelity video generation.
 - Action and state tokens can attend to both video context and other action tokens, grounding actions in visual predictions.
 - Value tokens attend jointly to visual, action, and state tokens, enabling a holistic assessment of task progress and visual-action alignment. This asymmetric masking ensures actions are conditioned on visual predictions while video generation remains unaffected by action signals.

#### 3.2. Manifold-Aware Dual Prediction Objectives

Instead of a single objective, MV-WAM employs distinct flow-matching transformation functions for each modality, tailored to their intrinsic geometric properties.
- **Video Expert Objective (L_v)**: For the high-curvature visual manifold, the Video Expert directly predicts the velocity field, following standard flow matching:
 $L_v = E_{\tau,z_0,z_1} \|\pi_{v\theta}(z_\tau, \tau, c) - (z_1 - z_0)\|_2^2$, where $z_0$ are clean video latents, $z_1$ is noise, and $z_\tau$ is the interpolated latent.
- **Action-Value Expert Objective (L_a)**: For the low-curvature manifolds of action and value tokens, the objective is equivalent to directly regressing the clean latent. For actions, this is:
 $L_a = E_{\tau,a_0,a_1} \|\pi_{a\theta}(a_\tau, \tau, c) - a_0\|_2^2$. This modified x0-prediction avoids the $1/t^2$ reweighting factor found in some velocity-space x0-losses, which could cause instability when jointly optimized with video generation.
The entire framework is optimized using a joint loss: $L = \lambda_v L_v + \lambda_a L_a$, with balancing weights $\lambda_v = \lambda_a = 1$. During inference, the Video Expert denoises latents from the predicted velocity field, while the Action-Value Expert directly outputs the optimized action representation.

#### 3.3. Progress-Valued Regulation

This mechanism provides explicit feedback on task progress and detects visual-action misalignment during inference, enabling error recovery.
- **Monte Carlo Value Estimation**: Value tokens are trained to estimate task value $c$ using Monte Carlo (MC) returns. For each state-action pair in a trajectory, the value target $\hat{c}(s_t, a_t)$ is the empirical discounted return accumulated from the current step to the end of the trajectory. This converts sparse task outcomes into a dense progress signal.
- **Value-Guided Rollback for Online Execution**: During closed-loop execution, the predicted value token acts as a dynamic progress monitor. A rolling buffer tracks the peak value $c_{peak}$ achieved. If the newly predicted value $\hat{c}_t$ drops significantly below this peak ($\hat{c}_t < c_{peak} - \delta$, where $\delta$ is a task-aware threshold), the current action chunk is flagged as potentially erroneous. The policy then triggers a rollback-and-resample protocol: execution reverts to the cached checkpoint corresponding to $c_{peak}$, and a new action chunk is resampled from this restored state using the inherent stochasticity of diffusion. This self-correcting loop prevents error accumulation and guides the robot toward high-progress outcomes.

#### 3.4. Training Configuration

The MV-WAM architecture contains 1.9B parameters (excluding external encoders). A large-scale pre-training corpus comprising approximately 7,500 hours of diverse robotic manipulation data (EgoDex, Agibot, RoboMind, RoboCoin, Open X-Embodiment, and private datasets) is used.
- **Two-stage training procedure**:
 1. **Video Expert pre-training**: The Video Expert (30 Transformer blocks, initialized from WoW-1.3B) is pre-trained for 20k steps.
 2. **Joint vision-action fine-tuning**: The full model, including the pre-trained Video Expert and a 30-block Action-Value Expert trained from scratch, is fine-tuned for 15k steps. The Action-Value Expert operates at a lower hidden dimension (768) and is projected to the video-token space (1536 dimensions) only within shared self-attention layers to reduce computational cost. The model achieves an inference speed of 17.9 actions per second on a single A800 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of MV-WAM was evaluated through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world settings, including comparisons against existing VLA and WAM baselines, zero-shot and few-shot generalization tests, and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments on RoboTwin 2.0

- **Benchmark**: RoboTwin 2.0, involving 50 manipulation tasks on a dual-arm robot in both "Clean" (in-domain) and "Random" (out-of-distribution with perturbed lighting, backgrounds, object configurations) settings.
- **Results (Table 1)**:
 - MV-WAM achieved a mean success rate of 84.0% on Clean scenarios and 55.7% on Random scenarios.
 - It outperformed the strongest VLA baseline (π0) by substantial margins (84.0% vs. 46.4% on Clean; 55.7% vs. 16.3% on Random).
 - Compared to other WAM baselines, MV-WAM surpassed HALO, the strongest competitor, by 3.5% on Clean and 29.3% on Random.
 - MV-WAM exhibited a notably smaller relative drop in performance from Clean to Random (33.7%) compared to HALO (67.2%), BagelVLA (72.8%), and Fast-WAM (91.2%). This indicates improved robustness to distribution shifts.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Generalization

- **Setup**: MV-WAM was trained on 40 tasks and evaluated on 10 unseen OOD tasks under 0-shot, 1-shot, 5-shot, and 10-shot conditions.
- **Results (Table 2)**:
 - In the zero-shot setting, MV-WAM achieved success rates of 55.6% on Clean and 54.0% on Random tasks, demonstrating inherent generalization capabilities to novel tasks without specific supervision.
 - Performance increased with more demonstrations, reaching 77.1% on Clean and 74.6% on Random at 10-shot, indicating efficient adaptation to new tasks.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **Manifold-Aware Prediction Targets (Figure 3a)**:
 - The optimal configuration, using x0-prediction for the action branch and velocity-prediction for the video branch, achieved 84.0% Clean / 55.7% Random.
 - Applying a single unified objective (either noise-prediction or flow-prediction) to both modalities resulted in substantial performance collapse under Random conditions (15.0% and 6.0% respectively). This supports the hypothesis that tailored prediction targets are critical for action robustness.
- **Denoising Steps (Figure 3b)**:
 - Performance generally improved with more denoising steps. However, gains diminished, with 3 steps recovering over 96% of the 5-step Clean performance while reducing computational cost by 40%. Even with a single step, competitive success rates were maintained (66.5% Clean / 36.4% Random), indicating robust jointly trained representations.
- **Progress-Value Tokens (Figure 3c)**:
 - Value tokens consistently improved performance over a baseline without value prediction (83.4% Clean / 54.5% Random).
 - Performance varied marginally across different rollback threshold (δ) values, indicating the robustness of the rollback mechanism to this hyperparameter (δ=0.20 was adopted as default).

#### 4.4. Real-World Experiments

- **Platform and Tasks**: Deployed on a TienKung dual-arm robot for four daily manipulation tasks of varying difficulty (Pick Backbag & Coffee, Drop Cloth, Pick Cloth, Fold Cloth).
- **Results (Table 3)**:
 - MV-WAM achieved a mean success rate of 77.5%, significantly outperforming π0 (42.5%) and RDT (32.5%).
 - It achieved a 100% success rate on structured tasks like Drop Cloth and Pick Cloth.
 - For Fold Cloth, the most demanding task requiring precise deformable manipulation, MV-WAM achieved 20%, whereas π0 achieved 10% and RDT achieved 0%.
 - These results demonstrate that the performance advantages observed in simulation effectively transfer to physical deployment, yielding more robust manipulation policies in diverse real-world scenarios, particularly for tasks requiring fine-grained contact.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in MV-WAM highlights a critical aspect of generalization in embodied robotics: the inherent geometric differences between distinct modalities like visual observations and robot actions. By identifying and formally analyzing the "manifold mismatch" between these modalities, the paper posits that robust policy generalization requires not merely deeper integration but a more principled geometric alignment during the learning process.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Addressing the Generalization Gap**: MV-WAM provides a framework that effectively mitigates the long-standing generalization gap in World Action Models. By designing modality-aware optimization objectives and a structured causal mask, it ensures that learning is tailored to the intrinsic geometry of each modality, leading to more robust action representations under distribution shifts.
- **State-of-the-Art Generalization**: The extensive experiments demonstrate MV-WAM's ability to achieve high success rates across diverse simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, including challenging out-of-distribution and zero-shot scenarios. The significantly reduced performance drop in randomized environments compared to baselines indicates a substantial improvement in generalization capability.
- **Efficient Design**: The paper suggests that these generalization gains can be achieved with a relatively efficient architecture (1.9B parameters), implying that principled design, rather than solely model scale, is a key driver for generalization.
- **Enhanced Autonomy and Recovery**: The introduction of the progress-valued regulation mechanism with value-guided rollback is a notable advancement. This mechanism allows the policy to autonomously detect and recover from execution deviations in real-time, which is crucial for reliable deployment in unconstrained environments and for long-horizon tasks.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancing Deployable Robotics**: The improved robustness and generalization capabilities of MV-WAM offer a pathway towards more reliable and widely deployable robotic manipulation systems. Robots could adapt more effectively to variations in visual appearance, lighting, backgrounds, and object configurations found in real-world settings.
- **Reduced Development Costs**: Generalizable policies can significantly reduce the engineering effort and data collection costs typically required to adapt robots to new environments or tasks, making robotic assistance more accessible and scalable.
- **Broadening Robotic Applications**: Such systems have the potential to enhance productivity and quality of life by enabling robots to perform complex and repetitive tasks in various domains, including manufacturing, logistics, healthcare support, and domestic services, with greater reliability and less human intervention.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- The scalability of MV-WAM's proposed mechanisms has not been validated at larger model scales, leaving it an open question for future research.
- Monte Carlo value estimates, while useful, can be noisy under sparse reward conditions, which may limit the reliability of the rollback mechanism in very long-horizon tasks.
- Qualitative failure analysis revealed areas for improvement in high-level visual understanding for embodiment-aware planning and low-level action precision for contact-sensitive scenarios.

The broader impact section also highlights important societal considerations, including safety risks, labor market effects, and accountability challenges associated with deploying capable manipulation systems. These concerns necessitate rigorous safety evaluation, human oversight, clear operational boundaries, and robust monitoring mechanisms for future real-world deployments.
