# Can Large Language Models Invent Algorithms to Improve Themselves?: Algorithm Discovery for Recursive Self-Improvement through Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Self-Developing enables Large Language Models to autonomously discover and refine algorithms for their own improvement, specifically focusing on model merging. This approach allowed an LLM to invent algorithms that outperformed human-designed methods, achieving a 6% accuracy improvement over the seed model on GSM8k and demonstrating strong transferability to out-of-domain models with a 7.4% gain over human-optimized baselines.

## Problem

- Large Language Model (LLM) improvement is largely constrained by human experts manually designing and testing new architectures, training methodologies, and fine-tuning strategies.
- Existing LLM self-improvement methods, such as self-generated data or iterative refinement, do not enable the LLM to invent the underlying improvement algorithms themselves.
- Many current LLM self-improvement techniques depend on more powerful external models (e.g., GPT-4) to guide the improvement process, limiting autonomous development.

## Method

- The team developed ==Self-Developing==, an iterative, reinforcement learning-based framework where an =='algorithm factory' LLM== generates model-improving algorithms.
- Algorithms are produced as ==executable Python code== for a specific task (model merging) and evaluated on performance metrics to generate preference data.
- The algorithm factory LLM is continuously refined using ==Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)== based on the performance of its generated algorithms, creating a ==recursive self-improvement loop==.

## Results

- LLM-discovered algorithms consistently outperformed the seed model, achieving a **6%** accuracy improvement on GSM8k and an **8%** improvement on MATH.
- The best LLM-discovered algorithm for GSM8k surpassed human-designed methods like Task Arithmetic and TIES Merging by over **4%** (e.g., **76.1%** vs **71.9%**).
- Discovered algorithms showed strong transferability to out-of-domain models, yielding **78.8%** accuracy on GSM8k, outperforming a human-optimized baseline by **7.4%** (**71.4%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models (LLMs) can autonomously invent and refine complex algorithms, even surpassing human-designed methods for specific tasks like model merging.
> - Iterative refinement of the algorithm-generating LLM using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) significantly enhances the quality and performance of the discovered algorithms over time.
> - Algorithms discovered by the framework demonstrate strong generalization capabilities, maintaining high performance even when applied to out-of-domain models not used during their generation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to remarkable capabilities across various domains, yet their ongoing improvement remains largely contingent on human ingenuity. This paper, "Can Large Language Models Invent Algorithms to Improve Themselves?", directly addresses a critical limitation in the current LLM development paradigm: the human-constrained exploration of *model-improving algorithms*.

Historically, LLM advancements have been driven by human experts who design architectures, training methodologies, and fine-tuning strategies. While recent research has explored various forms of LLM self-improvement—such as fine-tuning with self-generated data (e.g., Yuan et al., 2024; Gülçehre et al., 2023), self-play (e.g., Tu et al., 2024), or iterative refinement based on environmental feedback (e.g., Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023)—a fundamental constraint persists: these methods do not empower the LLM to *invent* the underlying improvement *strategies* or *algorithms* themselves. The exploration of *how* to improve LLMs is still limited by human knowledge and imagination.

This paper's proposed framework, **Self-Developing**, aims to bridge this gap by enabling LLMs to autonomously discover, implement, and refine their own improvement algorithms. This represents a significant departure from existing self-improvement paradigms. Unlike methods that generate data for fine-tuning or plan actions based on feedback, Self-Developing targets the meta-level task of algorithm discovery itself.

The work builds upon the growing field of LLM-driven code generation (e.g., Jiang et al., 2024), which has seen success in various applications, including scientific discovery (e.g., AlphaEvolve by Novikov et al., 2025). Some prior work has also touched upon LLM-driven algorithm discovery, such as discovering loss functions for preference optimization (Lu et al., 2024) or improving code that makes calls to LLMs (Zelikman et al., 2023). However, the authors clearly distinguish Self-Developing from these predecessors by highlighting two crucial limitations in existing methods:
1. **No generator improvement:** Previous approaches typically do not recursively improve the algorithm generator itself.
2. **External dependency:** Many methods rely on more powerful external models (e.g., GPT-4) to guide the improvement process.

Self-Developing explicitly addresses both limitations. It introduces a recursive loop where the "algorithm factory" (the LLM generating the algorithms) is continuously refined through Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), learning from the performance of its self-generated algorithms. Furthermore, it operates without dependence on external, stronger teacher models, relying solely on the seed model's inherent capabilities and performance on a target task.

By focusing on the practical application of model merging—a technique for combining specialized models to enhance overall performance—the paper demonstrates the viability and effectiveness of autonomously discovered algorithms in a concrete and relevant domain. In essence, this research pushes the boundaries of LLM autonomy, moving towards a future where AI systems can not only solve complex problems but also fundamentally evolve their own problem-solving methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to empower Large Language Models (LLMs) to autonomously discover, implement, and refine their own improvement algorithms, thereby transcending the inherent limitations imposed by human-designed methodologies. The authors pose a provocative question that encapsulates their core ambition: "Could we empower LLMs to autonomously discover and develop algorithms to improve themselves?"

The key motivations driving this work are multifaceted:

- **Breaking Human-Centric Constraints:** Current LLM development is fundamentally constrained by human knowledge and imagination. Researchers manually design and test improvement strategies, which limits the exploration of the vast algorithmic space. The primary motivation is to break free from this human bottleneck, allowing LLMs to explore and invent novel, high-performance algorithms that might lie beyond current human conceptualizations. The analogy to AlphaGo's "Move 37" (Silver et al., 2016)—a highly effective, non-intuitive move discovered by AI—underscores the potential for superhuman algorithmic creativity.

- **Automating LLM Development:** The process of continually improving LLMs is resource-intensive and requires significant human expertise. By enabling LLMs to autonomously develop their own improvement methods, the framework aims to automate a critical part of the LLM development process, reducing the need for constant human intervention and potentially accelerating the pace of AI advancement.

- **Achieving Recursive Self-Improvement:** The ultimate vision is to create a self-sustaining cycle where LLMs can not only solve given tasks but also autonomously evolve the very methodologies they use to enhance their capabilities. This concept of recursive self-improvement is central to the long-term goal of artificial general intelligence (AGI). The paper aims to demonstrate a crucial step towards this by showing that an LLM can devise and implement self-improvement strategies using only its inherent capabilities and knowledge, without relying on superior external models.

- **Practical Demonstration through Model Merging:** To provide a concrete and measurable demonstration of this ambitious goal, the authors focus on "model merging algorithms." This specific application is chosen because it is a practical technique for combining the strengths of specialized LLMs (e.g., models fine-tuned on different datasets) to create a more robust and capable general model. The challenge is not just to apply existing merging techniques, but for the LLM to *invent* new and more effective ones. The success of the framework is defined by the autonomously generated algorithms leading to models that demonstrate higher performance on specified tasks compared to the initial seed model and existing human-designed methods.

In summary, the core objective is to shift the paradigm of LLM improvement from a human-driven design process to an autonomous, AI-driven discovery process, paving the way for truly self-evolving AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The **Self-Developing** framework proposed in this paper implements an iterative, reinforcement learning-based approach to enable LLMs to autonomously discover, implement, and refine their own improvement algorithms. The core idea revolves around the symbiotic improvement of two components: a "seed model" (M0), which is the target of improvement, and an "algorithm factory" (πg_t), which is an LLM responsible for generating the improvement algorithms. The framework operates through a recursive improvement cycle, as detailed below:

1. **Algorithm Factory Initialization (Step 1):**
 - At the start of the process, the algorithm factory (πg_1) is initialized by cloning the seed model (M0). This means the LLM that will generate algorithms initially possesses the same capabilities as the model it aims to improve.

2. **Algorithm Generation (Step 2):**
 - In each iteration *t*, the current algorithm factory (πg_t) takes a carefully crafted prompt as input. This prompt encourages the LLM to generate novel model-improving algorithms.
 - The algorithms are generated as executable Python code. A crucial design choice is to focus on "model merging algorithms," which combine multiple input models (specifically, their "task vectors" – the weight differences from the seed model) into a single, improved model. An example Python function demonstrating a simple weighted sum of task vectors is provided as a one-shot example in the prompt to guide the LLM's output format and conceptual scope.
 - The LLM generates a batch of *N* candidate algorithms (e.g., 3000 in experiments).

3. **Algorithm Evaluation (Step 3):**
 - The generated Python code snippets are first filtered for executability (e.g., removing syntactically incorrect code or those leading to timeouts).
 - For each valid algorithm, it is applied to the original seed model (M0) and a set of predefined "merge candidate models" to produce a new, potentially improved model. The algorithms always operate on M0, ensuring a consistent baseline for improvement.
 - These newly created models are then evaluated on a development set of the target task (e.g., GSM8k or MATH). The performance score of each model quantifies the effectiveness of the algorithm that generated it.

4. **Algorithm Factory Refinement (Step 4):**
 - Based on the evaluation scores from Step 3, "preference data" is created. This involves identifying "high-performing" algorithms (those whose generated models achieved scores above a certain percentile, *pw*%) and "low-performing" algorithms (those below a certain percentile, *pl*%).
 - This preference data (pairs of preferred vs. rejected algorithms for a given prompt) is then used to fine-tune the algorithm factory (πg_t) using **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**. DPO is a reinforcement learning technique that directly optimizes a policy to align with human (or, in this case, empirically derived) preferences, without needing an explicit reward model.
 - To aid learning, the top *k* performing algorithms from *all previous iterations* are also included in the high-performing set, ensuring the factory learns from historical successes.
 - The fine-tuning updates πg_t to create πg_(t+1), which is expected to generate even better algorithms in the next iteration. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is used for efficient fine-tuning.

5. **Iterative Improvement (Step 5):**
 - The entire cycle (generation, evaluation, refinement) is repeated for a fixed number of iterations.
 - A **temperature decay schedule** is introduced for the LLM's generation process. Initially, a higher temperature promotes exploration and diverse algorithm generation. As iterations progress, the temperature gradually decreases, shifting the focus towards exploitation and refinement of more effective strategies based on accumulated learning. This balances the trade-off between exploring novel ideas and refining promising ones.

The framework's strength lies in its closed-loop nature, allowing the LLM to learn directly from the success and failure of its own algorithmic creations, driving a recursive self-improvement process without external human feedback or reliance on superior external models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted with the Self-Developing framework yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the capacity of LLMs to autonomously discover and refine model-improving algorithms, even outperforming human-designed counterparts.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Tasks:** Mathematical reasoning benchmarks GSM8k and MATH, widely used in LLM self-improvement research. Development sets were used for algorithm evaluation within iterations, and a separate test set for final evaluation.
- **Models:** The seed model (M0) was `openchat-3.5-1210`, a fine-tuned Mistral-7B variant known for its code generation capabilities. Three other Mistral-7B based models (`Abel-7B-002`, `OpenHermes-2.5-Mistral-7B`, `SciPhi-Mistral-7B-32k`) served as merge candidates, selected for their specialization in mathematics, code, and scientific domains, respectively.
- **Process:** 3 iterations, each generating 3000 algorithms. DPO trained the algorithm factory using the top 3% and bottom 10% algorithms, with temperature decay across iterations (initial T=1.2, decay β=0.2).
- **Baselines:** Established human-designed model merging algorithms: Task Arithmetic, TIES Merging, and Model Stock. These baselines were optimized for performance on the development set before final test evaluation.

**Key Findings:**

1. **LLMs Can Self-Improve Through Discovered Algorithms (Q1):**
 - The results unequivocally show that models generated using LLM-discovered algorithms consistently outperformed the initial seed model.
 - On GSM8k, the best LLM-discovered algorithm achieved 76.1% accuracy, a significant **6% improvement** over the seed model's 70.1%.
 - On MATH, accuracy jumped from the seed model's 0.5% to 8.5% with the best discovered algorithm.
 - This confirms the framework's ability to enable LLMs to enhance their own performance autonomously, without relying on stronger teacher models like GPT-4.

2. **Discovered Algorithms Surpass Human-Designed Ones (Q2):**
 - A standout finding is that Self-Developing successfully invented algorithms that outperformed well-established human-designed techniques.
 - For GSM8k, LLM-discovered algorithms (76.1%) **outperformed Task Arithmetic (71.9%) by 4.2%** and TIES Merging (71.8%) by 4.3%.
 - On MATH, the LLM's best algorithm (8.5%) matched Task Arithmetic and slightly surpassed TIES Merging (8.4%).
 - This demonstrates that LLMs can indeed transcend human limitations in algorithmic design, finding more effective solutions for specific tasks.

3. **Training the Algorithm Factory Improves Algorithm Quality (Q3):**
 - The iterative refinement of the algorithm factory through DPO proved crucial for enhancing the quality of generated algorithms.
 - Performance on MATH showed a clear progression across iterations: 7.0% (Iter 1), 7.5% (Iter 2), and 8.5% (Iter 3) for the best models from each iteration.
 - Analysis of the distribution of algorithm performance (Figure 4) revealed a significant shift from low-performing algorithms dominating early iterations to a higher proportion of high-performing ones as learning progressed. This validates the effectiveness of the DPO-based training loop.

4. **Evolution and Nature of Discovered Algorithms (Q4):**
 - The discovered algorithms demonstrated a flexible combination of techniques, evolving in complexity over iterations.
 - **Iteration 1:** Initial discoveries included weighted mixture strategies combining weighted averages with element-wise maximum and minimum operations (e.g., Figure 5). The LLM also proposed conceptual elements like Gaussian blur, showing creative exploration.
 - **Iteration 3:** Algorithms evolved to incorporate more sophisticated concepts, such as adaptive weighting mechanisms based on custom distance metrics that consider both magnitude and direction of weight vectors (e.g., Figure 6).
 - Analysis of generated code comments (Appendix C, Table 5) showed diverse ideas explored in early iterations, including concepts from geometry (similarity, distance, norm, angle), statistics (Gaussian, entropy), and machine learning (clustering, factorization). This highlights the LLM's ability to draw from a broad knowledge base in its algorithmic search.

5. **Strong Transferability of Discovered Algorithms:**
 - A remarkable finding was the strong generalization capability of the LLM-discovered algorithms to *out-of-domain* models (new merge candidates not used during algorithm generation).
 - When applied to a new set of Mistral-7B based models (`WizardMath-7B-V1.1`, `BioMistral-7B`, `Starling-LM-7B-alpha`), many LLM-discovered algorithms maintained high performance. On GSM8k, they achieved 78.8% accuracy, surpassing the **Task Arithmetic (71.4%) that was specifically *optimized* for these new models by 7.4%**.
 - This indicates that the framework discovers robust, generalizable merging strategies, rather than merely fitting to the original set of candidate models. Some algorithms even showed effective cross-task performance, suggesting broader applicability.

In summary, the results provide compelling evidence that LLMs can autonomously discover, implement, and iteratively refine novel, high-performing improvement algorithms that not only enhance their own capabilities but also surpass human-designed benchmarks, demonstrating strong generalization properties.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The **Self-Developing** framework proposed by Ishibashi, Yano, and Oyamada marks a profound shift in the paradigm of Large Language Model (LLM) improvement, moving from human-dependent design to autonomous, AI-driven algorithmic discovery. Its significance and potential impact are far-reaching:

1. **Paving the Way for True AI Self-Evolution:** This research represents a crucial step towards realizing the long-held vision of recursive self-improvement in artificial intelligence. By demonstrating that LLMs can invent and refine the *methodologies* for their own advancement, rather than merely executing human-defined instructions or learning from human-curated data, Self-Developing pushes the boundaries of AI autonomy. This capability could lead to a future where AI systems evolve with minimal human intervention, continuously enhancing their intelligence and problem-solving abilities.

2. **Unlocking Novel Algorithmic Solutions:** The human imagination, while vast, is inherently limited. As demonstrated by the framework, LLMs can discover algorithms that outperform human-designed counterparts and integrate diverse techniques in ways that might not be intuitively obvious to human researchers. This capability opens the door for genuinely novel optimization techniques and AI methodologies to be uncovered, potentially accelerating breakthroughs in various scientific and engineering domains. The discovery of adaptive weighting based on custom distance metrics for model merging, for instance, showcases this creative capacity.

3. **Automating and Accelerating LLM Development:** The current lifecycle of LLM development—from pre-training to fine-tuning, alignment, and optimization—is highly labor-intensive and requires significant human expertise. By automating the discovery and refinement of improvement algorithms, Self-Developing offers a pathway to reduce this human bottleneck. This could lead to faster iteration cycles, more efficient resource utilization, and a dramatic acceleration in the pace of LLM innovation, making advanced AI capabilities more accessible and adaptable.

4. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalizability:** The demonstrated strong transferability of the discovered algorithms to out-of-domain models is particularly impactful. It suggests that Self-Developing doesn't just find brittle, overfitting solutions, but rather uncovers robust, generalizable algorithmic principles. This means that once an LLM has learned to invent effective improvement strategies, these strategies might be broadly applicable across similar models or even different tasks, reducing the need for extensive re-optimization and human fine-tuning for new applications.

5. **Implications for Artificial General Intelligence (AGI):** The ability of an AI to improve its own learning and problem-solving processes is a cornerstone of the AGI concept. This research offers a concrete, demonstrable mechanism for such meta-learning, showing how an LLM can improve its "algorithm factory" to generate better "products" (improved models). This proof-of-concept is a vital contribution to the foundational research necessary for building truly intelligent, adaptive, and autonomous AI systems.

While the current study focuses on mathematical reasoning tasks and model merging, the underlying principle of an LLM recursively refining its own improvement algorithms holds immense potential for broader applications, from natural language understanding and generation to scientific discovery, robotics, and complex system optimization. The limitations acknowledged by the authors (scope of tasks) are common in foundational research, and the demonstrated capability nonetheless presents a compelling vision for the future of AI development.
