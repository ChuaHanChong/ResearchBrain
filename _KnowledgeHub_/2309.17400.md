# Directly Fine-Tuning Diffusion Models on Differentiable Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> A new direct gradient-based fine-tuning method, DRaFT, optimizes diffusion models with differentiable rewards, offering significantly enhanced sample efficiency compared to reinforcement learning methods. It enables models to align with diverse objectives, including aesthetic quality and object presence, while providing novel control mechanisms for blending learned styles.

## Problem

- Pre-trained diffusion models often fail to align with specific human preferences, aesthetic standards, or complex user-defined objectives, as they are primarily trained to imitate data distribution.
- Existing alignment methods for diffusion models, particularly those based on reinforcement learning, are highly sample-inefficient and computationally expensive, hindering scalability and broad application.
- Prior gradient-based fine-tuning approaches either optimize only initial noise samples for each generation or rely on limited one-step gradient estimation, which are less efficient or effective than direct model parameter optimization.

## Method

- **DRaFT** directly backpropagates the ==gradient of a differentiable reward function== through the entire multi-step diffusion sampling process to fine-tune the model's parameters.
- It leverages ==Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)== and ==gradient checkpointing== to manage the computational and memory demands, making full backpropagation feasible for large diffusion models.
- The method introduces **DRaFT-K** for ==truncated backpropagation== and **DRaFT-LV** for ==low-variance gradient estimation==, further improving training efficiency and stability.

## Results

- DRaFT variants demonstrated superior sample efficiency, achieving strong performance (e.g., maximizing LAION Aesthetics scores) over **200x** faster than reinforcement learning baselines like DDPO.
- Fine-tuned models showed substantial improvements in aesthetic quality and human preference alignment, achieving **state-of-the-art** performance on benchmarks like **HPSv2**, and generalized well to unseen prompts.
- The approach successfully applied to diverse objectives, including controlling image compressibility, enabling or disabling specific object presence using object detectors, and generating diffusion adversarial examples.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct gradient-based optimization using differentiable rewards achieves substantially higher sample efficiency and faster convergence compared to reinforcement learning approaches for diffusion model alignment.
> - Truncating backpropagation to the last few sampling steps (DRaFT-K) can lead to better performance and training stability than full backpropagation through the entire diffusion chain, suggesting issues with exploding gradients for very long sequences.
> - LoRA parameters offer unique benefits for fine-tuning diffusion models, including the ability to scale the effect of fine-tuning and linearly combine multiple learned styles, enhancing model control and customization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper directly addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly advancing field of generative AI, particularly with diffusion models: **aligning model outputs with specific desired behaviors or human preferences**.

**Context of Diffusion Models:** Diffusion models have revolutionized generative modeling for continuous data, achieving unprecedented quality in generating images, videos, and audio. However, models trained solely to replicate their vast training datasets (e.g., diverse images from the web) often fall short of generating content that aligns with nuanced human aesthetic preferences, specific stylistic requirements, or other user-defined objectives.

**The "Alignment Problem":** This gap between raw data distribution modeling and desired behavior is known as the "alignment problem." In the language modeling community, this has been famously tackled by methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which has been instrumental in fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) to be more helpful, harmless, and follow instructions.

**Existing Approaches for Diffusion Model Alignment:**
- **Classifier-Free Guidance:** A technique commonly employed in text-to-image models to improve text-image alignment during inference by linearly combining conditional and unconditional score estimates. While effective, it's an inference-time adjustment, not a training-time fine-tuning method for arbitrary preferences.
- **Fine-tuning on Curated Datasets:** Training diffusion models on specialized, human-curated datasets (e.g., LAION Aesthetics) to improve specific qualities like aesthetic appeal. This is effective but requires extensive data collection and labeling, and often doesn't allow for dynamic, on-the-fly optimization to new rewards.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Diffusion Models:** Inspired by RLHF in NLP, several recent works have attempted to apply RL to fine-tune diffusion models based on human preference models or other rewards. Papers by Lee et al. (2023), Wu et al. (2023b), Dong et al. (2023), Black et al. (2023), and Fan et al. (2023) fall into this category.
 - **Limitations of RL:** A crucial limitation of these RL-based approaches highlighted by the authors is their **sample inefficiency**. They require a vast number of reward function queries and are difficult to scale to generally improve generation style across diverse prompts. This inefficiency stems from the need to sample entire trajectories and use policy gradient methods, which discard a lot of information compared to analytical gradients.
- **Gradient-Based Methods (Prior Work):**
 - **Guidance (general):** While valuable, guidance typically applies the reward function to *noisy* intermediate latents or predicted clean images, which can lead to out-of-distribution issues for the guidance model.
 - **Backpropagation through sampling for *other* tasks:** Watson et al. (2022) differentiated through sampling to learn sampler hyperparameters for efficiency. Fan & Lee (2023) considered it for speeding up sampling but focused on RL due to memory concerns. These works did not focus on optimizing arbitrary differentiable rewards for model parameters.
 - **Direct Optimization of Diffusion Latents (DOODL) (Wallace et al., 2023):** This method *does* backpropagate through sampling to optimize differentiable rewards, but it optimizes the *initial noise sample* ($x_T$) rather than the model parameters. This means optimization must be repeated for every single generation, making inference very slow.
 - **Reward Feedback Learning (ReFL) (Xu et al., 2023):** This is the closest prior work to DRaFT. ReFL uses reward function gradients to fine-tune a diffusion model by evaluating the reward on a *one-step predicted clean image* from a randomly chosen intermediate denoising step. While effective, DRaFT aims to improve upon its efficiency and simplicity.

**DRaFT's Contribution to the Landscape:**
DRaFT carves out a significant niche by proposing a *direct, efficient, gradient-based method* that leverages the differentiability of many reward functions. It addresses the sample inefficiency of RL methods and the limitations of previous gradient-based approaches by:
1. Performing **full backpropagation through the entire sampling chain** (when memory permits).
2. Employing **LoRA** and **gradient checkpointing** to make this computationally feasible.
3. Introducing **truncated backpropagation (DRaFT-K)** and **low-variance gradient estimation (DRaFT-LV)** to further enhance efficiency and stability.
4. Critically, it applies the reward function *only to the final generated image*, avoiding issues with evaluating rewards on noisy intermediate latents.

This positions DRaFT as a powerful and practical tool that could become as crucial for aligning diffusion models as RLHF has become for LLMs, especially given the prevalence of differentiable reward functions in practice.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a **simple, efficient, and effective method for fine-tuning diffusion models to maximize differentiable reward functions**. The authors identify a significant gap in current diffusion model training paradigms and propose DRaFT as a solution.

The key motivations driving this work are:

1. **Bridging the Gap Between Training Data and Desired Behavior:** Diffusion models, despite their impressive generative capabilities, are often trained to merely imitate the statistical distribution of their training data. This intrinsic objective frequently diverges from practical desired behaviors, such as generating aesthetically pleasing images, adhering to specific stylistic guidelines, or satisfying complex user preferences (e.g., ensuring objects are present or absent). The motivation is to enable models to reliably produce outputs that align with human intent and subjective quality metrics.

2. **Addressing the Inefficiency of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Diffusion Alignment:** While RL-based methods, inspired by successes in LLM alignment (RLHF), have been proposed for diffusion models, the authors highlight their significant **sample inefficiency**. These methods require a large number of reward queries and are computationally expensive, making them challenging to scale for broad application and diverse objectives. The authors are motivated to find a more computationally tractable and faster-converging alternative.

3. **Leveraging Differentiable Rewards Directly:** Many practical reward functions, such as aesthetic classifiers, image-text alignment scores (e.g., CLIP-based scores), object detectors, or even custom computational metrics (e.g., image compressibility), are inherently differentiable or can be implemented in a differentiable manner. The authors are motivated by the premise that if a reward function is differentiable, its gradients can be directly exploited for optimization, offering a more efficient path than gradient-free (or gradient-estimated) RL methods.

4. **Enabling General and Flexible Fine-Tuning:** The goal is to create a framework that is not confined to a narrow set of objectives but can be applied broadly across various differentiable rewards. This includes:
 - Improving aesthetic quality and alignment with human preferences.
 - Controlling object presence or absence.
 - Modifying physical image properties (e.g., compressibility).
 - Generating images for specific adversarial tasks.

5. **Achieving Practical Scalability and Controllability:** The authors aim for a method that can be scaled to large datasets and models, offering practical benefits like reduced memory footprint and faster training times. Furthermore, they seek to introduce novel control mechanisms, such as scaling and combining fine-tuning effects, that enhance the utility and creative potential of the adapted models.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a robust, efficient, and versatile gradient-based fine-tuning solution for diffusion models that harnesses the power of differentiable rewards, thereby overcoming the limitations of current alignment strategies and pushing the boundaries of controllable generative AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of DRaFT's methodology lies in directly backpropagating the gradient of a differentiable reward function through the entire diffusion sampling process. This contrasts with previous RL-based methods that rely on less efficient policy gradient techniques or supervised fine-tuning. The authors meticulously develop several variants to optimize this direct gradient approach for efficiency and performance.

#### 3.1. Direct Reward Fine-Tuning (DRaFT) - The Foundational Idea

The objective is to fine-tune the parameters $\theta$ of a pre-trained diffusion model to maximize a differentiable reward function $r$:
$J(\theta) = E_{c \sim p_c, x_T \sim N(0,I)} [r(\text{sample}(\theta, c, x_T), c)]$
where $\text{sample}(\theta, c, x_T)$ represents the full T-step diffusion sampling process. The approach involves computing $\nabla_{\theta} r(\text{sample}(\theta, c, x_T), c)$ and using gradient ascent.

**Challenges and Solutions for Full Backpropagation:**
Backpropagating through the entire sampling chain (which involves multiple sequential applications of the denoising UNet) is computationally intensive and memory-demanding, similar to backpropagation through time (BPTT) in recurrent neural networks. DRaFT tackles this with two key techniques:

- **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA):**
 - Instead of fine-tuning all parameters of the large pre-trained UNet, LoRA freezes the original weights and injects small, low-rank matrices into select layers (e.g., feedforward and cross-attention). Only these low-rank matrices are trained.
 - **Benefits:** Drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, leading to significant memory savings, faster training, and smaller checkpoint sizes. Crucially, it enables convenient scaling (multiplying LoRA weights by a scalar to interpolate between original and fine-tuned models) and compositionality (linearly combining LoRA weights from different fine-tuned models).
- **Gradient Checkpointing:**
 - To manage the memory footprint during backpropagation, gradient checkpointing stores only a subset of activations (e.g., the input latent for each denoising step) and recomputes the necessary intermediate activations during the backward pass.
 - **Benefits:** Reduces peak memory consumption at the cost of increased computation time, making full backpropagation feasible for deep computational graphs like the diffusion sampling chain.

#### 3.2. DRaFT-K: Truncated Backpropagation

Empirically, the authors found that full backpropagation could lead to optimization difficulties, partly due to exploding gradients. To address this and improve efficiency, they introduce DRaFT-K:
- **Concept:** Backpropagation is truncated to only the *last K* sampling steps. A `stop_grad` operation is applied at step T-K, preventing gradients from flowing further back in time.
- **Benefits:** Reduces the computational cost of the backward pass (fewer UNet backprops). Surprisingly, it also *improves training efficiency and final performance* for smaller K, indicating that full backpropagation through the entire 50-step chain can be unstable. For very small K (e.g., K=1), gradient checkpointing may not even be necessary.

#### 3.3. DRaFT-LV: Low-Variance Gradient Estimation for K=1

For the specific and highly efficient case of DRaFT-1 (backpropagating only through the very last sampling step), the authors propose DRaFT-LV to further boost efficiency by reducing gradient variance:
- **Concept:** Instead of a single noise sample for the last denoising step, multiple noise samples (n examples) are used. The reward gradient is then averaged over these multiple examples.
- **Benefits:** Provides lower-variance gradient estimates, leading to more stable and faster convergence. Despite adding 'n' additional forward and backward passes, the overall overhead is minimal compared to the full T-step sampling process, resulting in roughly 2x faster training than DRaFT-1 for typical 'n' values like 2.

#### 3.4. General Reward Fine-Tuning Algorithm

The paper unifies DRaFT, DRaFT-K, DRaFT-LV, and previous work like ReFL into a single conceptual algorithm (Algorithm 1). This framework highlights how these methods differ mainly in the placement and conditions of `stop_grad` operations and the number of steps considered for gradient computation. For instance, vanilla DRaFT sets K=T, ReFL uses `stop_grad` at a random intermediate timestep and breaks early, and DRaFT-LV refines DRaFT-1 with variance reduction.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup and Implementation Details

- **Base Model:** Stable Diffusion 1.4 (a latent diffusion model). Fine-tuning involves backpropagation through both the latent space sampling and the VAE decoder.
- **Sampler:** DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) with 50 steps.
- **Guidance:** Classifier-free guidance with a weight of 7.5.
- **Optimization:** AdamW optimizer with LoRA parameters. Base model weights converted to bfloat16 for memory efficiency, while LoRA parameters are float32.
- **Reward Functions Tested:** LAION Aesthetics Classifier, Human Preference Score v2 (HPSv2), PickScore, differentiable JPEG compressibility/incompressibility, OWL-ViT object detector for object addition/removal, and a ResNet-50 classifier for adversarial examples.

This comprehensive methodology, combining direct gradient computation with advanced efficiency techniques, forms a powerful and versatile framework for fine-tuning diffusion models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate DRaFT's superior efficiency and versatility across a wide array of differentiable reward functions.

#### 4.1. Superior Sample Efficiency and Performance

- **Outperforming RL Baselines:** DRaFT variants, particularly DRaFT-LV, consistently achieved strong performance with significantly fewer reward queries compared to reinforcement learning methods like DDPO (Black et al., 2023). For instance, DRaFT maximized LAION Aesthetics scores >200x faster than DDPO.
- **Efficiency vs. ReFL:** DRaFT-LV proved to be approximately 2x faster than ReFL (Xu et al., 2023), a prior gradient-based method. DRaFT-1 also slightly outperformed ReFL while being conceptually simpler.

#### 4.2. Impact of Truncated Backpropagation (DRaFT-K)

- **Unexpected Benefit of Smaller K:** Contrary to intuition, truncating backpropagation to fewer steps (smaller K) led to *better* sample efficiency and higher final reward values. Full backpropagation (DRaFT-50) often resulted in slower optimization and lower maximum reward.
- **Gradient Explosion:** Analysis showed that gradient norms explode as K increases (Figure 8), suggesting optimization difficulties for longer backpropagation chains. Gradient clipping helped mitigate this but didn't fully resolve the performance gap.
- **Generalization Across Time:** Despite backpropagating only through the last few steps, the learned LoRA parameters influenced the entire sampling chain. Images improved when LoRA-adapted parameters were applied for many steps, even early in sampling, demonstrating that the adaptation is not confined to the end of the trajectory.

#### 4.3. Fine-tuning for Aesthetic Quality and Human Preferences

- **Aesthetic Improvement:** DRaFT substantially improved aesthetic quality scores (e.g., LAION Aesthetic Classifier). Qualitatively, fine-tuned models generated more detailed and stylized images (Figure 1, Figure 5).
- **Human Preference Alignment:** Achieved state-of-the-art performance on HPSv2, a benchmark for human preference in text-to-image generation, outperforming various other generative models and fine-tuning baselines (Figure 4).
- **Qualitative Differences:** Fine-tuning with different human preference rewards (Aesthetic, HPSv2, PickScore) led to distinct stylistic outcomes, indicating the method's sensitivity and effectiveness in capturing specific preferences (Figure 5, Left).

#### 4.4. Generalization Capabilities

- **Prompt Generalization:** Models fine-tuned on a limited set of prompts (e.g., 45 animal prompts) demonstrated excellent generalization to entirely new prompt subjects while retaining the learned style (Figure 6).
- **Reward Generalization:** Fine-tuning on one human preference reward (e.g., PickScore) transferred reasonably well when evaluated on another (HPSv2), showcasing robustness.

#### 4.5. Benefits of LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)

- **Scaling LoRA Weights:** Multiplying LoRA parameters by a scalar allowed smooth interpolation between the original pre-trained model and the fine-tuned model, providing a convenient control knob for the strength of the adaptation (Figure 7, Left). This also proved effective in mitigating reward hacking.
- **Mixing LoRA Weights:** Linearly combining LoRA parameters from models independently fine-tuned for different rewards (e.g., PickScore and HPSv2) resulted in images that blended the characteristics of both rewards, offering fine-grained stylistic control without additional training (Figure 7, Right).

#### 4.6. Versatility Across Diverse Reward Functions

- **Compressibility/Incompressibility:** Successfully fine-tuned models to generate images that were either highly compressible (simple, low-frequency) or highly incompressible (complex, high-frequency) using a differentiable JPEG loss (Figure 11).
- **Object Detection and Removal/Addition:** Utilized OWL-ViT object detector to train models to explicitly exclude (e.g., people from a farmer's market scene) or include (e.g., strawberries in a fruit bowl) specific objects (Figure 12).
- **Adversarial Examples:** Demonstrated the ability to generate "diffusion adversarial examples" where an image prompted for one class (e.g., "mouse") was classified as a different target class (e.g., "cat") by a pre-trained ResNet-50, revealing texture biases (Figure 13).

#### 4.7. Reward Hacking and Over-optimization

- The paper openly acknowledges that aggressive fine-tuning can lead to "reward hacking," where models optimize for the reward in unintended ways, resulting in a collapse of diversity or overly stylized outputs that might not be subjectively preferred despite high scores (Figure 3, Right; Appendix B.8).
- Among various mitigation strategies explored (LoRA scaling, early stopping, KL regularization), LoRA scaling proved most effective at improving generalization and reducing overfitting to the reward function.

Overall, the findings establish DRaFT as a highly effective and efficient method for aligning diffusion models with differentiable rewards, offering significant advantages over prior approaches and opening new avenues for controllable generative AI.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DRaFT represents a significant step forward in the field of generative AI, particularly for diffusion models, with broad implications and potential impact:

1. **Enabling Efficient and Direct Reward Alignment:** The most immediate and significant impact of DRaFT is providing a **highly efficient, gradient-based alternative to reinforcement learning for fine-tuning diffusion models on differentiable rewards**. By directly leveraging reward gradients and using techniques like LoRA and gradient checkpointing, DRaFT bypasses the sample inefficiency that has plagued RL-based approaches in this domain. This efficiency drastically reduces the computational resources and time required to align generative models, accelerating research and development.

2. **Broad Applicability to Diverse Objectives:** The demonstrated success across a wide range of reward functions – from subjective aesthetics and human preferences to objective criteria like image compressibility, object presence, and adversarial properties – highlights DRaFT's versatility. This means the method can be applied to many real-world use cases where specific, measurable characteristics of generated content are desired, extending far beyond simple stylistic adjustments.

3. **Enhanced Controllability and Customization:** The benefits of LoRA, specifically **scaling** and **mixing** fine-tuned weights, offer unprecedented levels of control over the style and content of generated images. Users can smoothly interpolate between the base model and fine-tuned styles, or combine multiple learned preferences, opening doors for highly personalized and nuanced generative applications that are difficult to achieve with prompt engineering alone. This empowers creators with a more flexible toolkit for tailoring AI-generated content.

4. **Accelerating Reward Model Development:** The efficiency of DRaFT also has a crucial indirect impact on the development of better reward models. By quickly and effectively optimizing diffusion models to a given reward, DRaFT can rapidly expose instances of "reward hacking" or unintended behaviors. This rapid feedback loop is invaluable for researchers to identify weaknesses in their reward functions and iterate on designing more robust and human-aligned preference models. Just as RLHF revealed limitations in initial LLM reward models, DRaFT can play a similar role for image generation.

5. **Unifying Framework for Gradient-Based Fine-Tuning:** The paper's contribution of a unifying framework that encompasses DRaFT, its variants, and prior work like ReFL provides a clearer understanding of the design space for gradient-based fine-tuning algorithms. The insights into gradient explosion with full backpropagation and the effectiveness of truncated backpropagation (DRaFT-K) offer valuable guidance for future algorithm design.

6. **Potential for Broader Impact Across Modalities:** While demonstrated on text-to-image models, the principles of DRaFT are not limited to images. The methodology could logically extend to other continuous data modalities generated by diffusion models, such as video, audio, or 3D assets, further broadening its scientific and practical impact.

In summary, DRaFT's significance lies in making preference alignment for diffusion models substantially more practical and powerful. By offering an efficient, direct, and versatile gradient-based solution, it has the potential to become a cornerstone technique for developing higher-quality, safer, and more user-aligned generative AI systems, mirroring the transformative role of RLHF in the large language model ecosystem.
