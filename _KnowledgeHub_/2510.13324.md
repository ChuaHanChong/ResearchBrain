# Tactile-Conditioned Diffusion Policy for Force-Aware Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Technical University of Darmstadt developed the Force-Aware Robotic Manipulation (FARM) framework, which integrates high-dimensional tactile force distributions into a diffusion policy's action space to enable explicit force control in robotic manipulation. This system achieved a 100% success rate on dynamic screw-tightening tasks and superior performance in grape picking and plant insertion compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Robots often fail in contact-rich manipulation due to a lack of precise force control, leading to object damage or slippage.
- Current imitation learning methods primarily use visuotactile feedback as observations, not for explicit control of gripper forces.
- Replicating human-like dexterity in regulating grasp forces based on touch remains a core challenge in robotics.

## Method

- Introduced the FARM framework, a diffusion policy that explicitly predicts robot pose, grip width, and target grip force, using high-dimensional tactile force distributions as observations.
- Utilizes a robot-free data collection process with a human-held UMI gripper, and deploys policies on a geometrically identical, open-source Actuated UMI gripper.
- Employs a dual-mode gripper control system that intelligently switches between position control for free-space movements and closed-loop force control during object contact.

## Results

- FARM achieved a 100% success rate in the dynamically challenging screw-tightening task, significantly surpassing all baselines.
- The framework demonstrated high success (95%) in static force tasks such as Plant Insertion and Grape Picking, comparable to or exceeding force-aware baselines.
- Quantitative evaluation using Wasserstein-1 distance showed FARM's superior force matching to human demonstrations, particularly in screw tightening, with a W1 distance of 0.7538 N.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly incorporating high-dimensional tactile force distributions as both observations and actions enables robots to actively regulate and mimic human-like grasp forces.
> - Robot-free data collection with a geometrically matched demonstration and deployment gripper simplifies policy transfer and accelerates the development of force-aware skills.
> - A dual-mode gripper control strategy, switching between position and force control, enhances robustness and adaptability during various phases of manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical and long-standing challenge in robotics: enabling robots to perform contact-rich manipulation tasks with human-like dexterity, particularly when handling fragile or deformable objects. Humans effortlessly regulate grasp forces through touch, an ability largely missing in most robotic systems. While tactile sensing has emerged as a key component for robust manipulation, its effective integration for *direct force control* in robots remains a significant hurdle.

Current imitation learning (IL) approaches, which leverage human demonstrations to teach robots new skills, often treat visuotactile feedback merely as an additional observation modality. In these frameworks (e.g., 3D-ViTac, GelFusion, TactileAloha, MimicTouch, ViTaMIn, FreeTacMan), tactile information might help resolve visual occlusions or detect contact states, but the applied forces are typically an indirect consequence of gripper kinematic commands. This leads to a lack of precise force regulation, making it difficult for robots to adapt to varying object properties or unexpected contact dynamics. Some works, like those by Ablett et al. and DexForce, do integrate force information from kinesthetic teaching but primarily convert these forces into position targets for impedance control, still treating force as an *observation* influencing position, rather than an *explicit action*. Furthermore, many of these methods rely on robot-in-the-loop data collection, which can limit generalization across different robot embodiments.

A smaller subset of research has begun to explore integrating tactile signals directly into the action space. Xu et al.'s TactAR, for instance, uses a reactive diffusion policy for arm force control, but separates planning from reactive refinement and focuses on arm-level forces rather than direct grip force. Adeniji et al.'s Feel-the-Force framework does predict grasping forces alongside poses, but relies on specific calibrations, manual annotations, and a restrictive, synchronized control scheme that can be slow.

FARM distinguishes itself within this landscape by:
1. **Directly integrating high-dimensional tactile data (force distributions) into both the *observation space* and the *action space* of a diffusion policy.** This allows the policy to explicitly predict and control target grip forces, moving beyond merely conditioning actions on tactile observations.
2. **Employing a robot-free data collection paradigm** using an adapted handheld Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) gripper. This mitigates the "embodiment gap" by allowing human experts to directly demonstrate force-aware manipulation without being constrained by robot kinematics or dynamics during data acquisition.
3. **Developing an open-source, actuated gripper (Actuated UMI)** whose geometry precisely matches the handheld demonstration gripper, enabling direct transfer of learned policies without retargeting.
4. **Implementing a dual-mode control strategy** that intelligently switches between grip width position control and closed-loop force control, providing both precision in free space and robust force regulation during contact.

By addressing these gaps, FARM significantly advances the field of visuotactile imitation learning, paving the way for more dexterous, adaptable, and robust robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind FARM stems from the observation that **humans naturally regulate grasp forces through touch**, a capability crucial for handling objects of varying fragility and deformability. In contrast, robotic manipulation often struggles with contact-rich tasks due to the difficulty in precisely controlling applied forces, leading to common failures like slippage or object damage. Existing imitation learning techniques fall short by treating tactile feedback predominantly as an auxiliary observation, thus leaving gripper forces as an uncontrolled side-effect of kinematic commands.

Given this background, the primary objective of this work is to **develop an imitation learning framework that explicitly incorporates high-dimensional tactile feedback into the robotic action space**, thereby enabling truly force-aware manipulation. This framework, termed Force-Aware Robotic Manipulation (FARM), aims to empower robots to not only perceive tactile interactions but also to directly target and regulate the contact forces they produce.

Specific objectives contributing to this overarching goal include:
- **Integrating a physically grounded, high-dimensional force profile** (estimated shear and normal force distributions from a GelSight Mini sensor via FEATS) as a rich observation modality.
- **Defining a novel action space** for a diffusion policy that jointly predicts robot pose, target grip width, and, crucially, *target grip force*. This enables the policy to generate temporally consistent, force-aware action sequences.
- **Designing and building the Actuated UMI gripper** as an open-source platform. This hardware development facilitates the direct transfer of policies learned from human demonstrations (collected with a geometrically matched handheld UMI) to a robotic manipulator, bypassing complex retargeting challenges.
- **Developing a robust dual-mode gripper control strategy** for deployment, allowing seamless transitions between position control (for free-space movements and grip width) and closed-loop force control (during object contact).
- **Empirically evaluating and ablating the contributions** of different tactile representations and control modes across a variety of real-world manipulation tasks with distinct force requirements (high-force, low-force, and dynamic force adaptation).

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between sophisticated tactile sensing capabilities and direct, intelligent force control in robots, moving beyond indirect conditioning to active, force-aware action generation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FARM framework is built upon a sophisticated interplay of custom hardware, a dedicated data collection pipeline, an extended diffusion policy architecture, and a novel dual-mode gripper control strategy.

**A. Gripper Hardware:**
The approach utilizes two custom-designed grippers with matching geometries:
1. **Adapted Hand-held UMI Gripper:** Used for human demonstration data collection. It modifies the original UMI gripper by replacing the GoPro with an Intel RealSense D405 camera (for in-hand RGB and grip width measurement via ArUco markers) and rigid fingers. One finger features a GelSight Mini tactile sensor, while the other has a matching gel pad. OptiTrack markers provide precise pose tracking.
2. **Actuated UMI Gripper:** Designed for robotic deployment on a Franka Research 3 robot. This custom-built gripper is powered by a DYNAMIXEL XL430-W250-T motor, using a belt-driven mechanism for synchronous finger actuation. Its geometry, sensor placement (GelSight Mini, Intel RealSense D405, OptiTrack, ArUco), and kinematics precisely mirror the handheld version, enabling direct policy transfer without the need for retargeting. The design files and control software are open-sourced.

**B. Data Collection:**
A robot-free data collection paradigm is employed using the adapted hand-held UMI gripper, allowing an expert to directly demonstrate tasks. This method captures both gripper kinematics and high-resolution tactile feedback. Synchronized data streams are recorded at 25 Hz (synchronized to GelSight Mini images):
- **RGB images:** From the Intel RealSense D405 (848x480x3).
- **Grip width:** Measured by tracking ArUco markers on the fingers via the in-hand camera.
- **Gripper pose:** Tracked via OptiTrack markers (3D position, 6D rotation).
- **GelSight Mini tactile images:** High-resolution raw tactile data (320x240x3).
- **Force distribution estimates:** Computed from each GelSight Mini image using a pre-trained and fixed Finite Element Analysis for Tactile Sensing (FEATS) model. FEATS outputs physically grounded estimates of shear (x and y) and normal (z) contact forces.

**C. FARM Diffusion Policy:**
The core of FARM is an imitation learning policy based on the **Diffusion Policy framework**, specifically using its 1D temporal Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) variant with Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) conditioning. The policy's strength lies in its **extended observation and action spaces**:
- **Observation Space (at time *t*):**
 - Downsampled in-hand RGB image (96x96x3).
 - Current grip width.
 - **High-dimensional tactile feedback:** A 3-channel image encoding the full force distributions (shear x, shear y, normal z) from FEATS (scaled to 96x96x3).
 - **Scalar tactile feedback:** The total normal force (integrated from the distribution).
 - Gripper pose (3D position, 6D rotation).
- **Action Space:**
 - Absolute target end-effector pose.
 - Target grip width (w_d).
 - **Target grip force (F_d_z).**

Separate ResNet-18 encoders process the in-hand RGB and tactile force images. The diffusion policy predicts action trajectories over a fixed prediction horizon of 32 steps, with an action execution horizon of 16 steps, and uses two recent observations for context. The policy is trained using a mean squared error loss on the noise added during the denoising process, leveraging the LeRobot implementation with modifications.

**D. Policy Deployment and Gripper Control:**
For deployment on the Actuated UMI gripper, a **dual-mode control strategy** is implemented to handle both free-space and contact phases:
1. **Force Control Mode:** Activated when both the policy's `target force` (F_d_z) and the `estimated contact force` (ˆF_z, from FEATS) are below a threshold of -0.5 N, indicating confident object contact. In this mode, a PID controller adjusts the gripper's position command to regulate the actual force to match the target force.
2. **Position Control Mode:** Active otherwise (no contact, or releasing). The `target grip width` (w_d) predicted by the policy is directly sent as a position command to the DYNAMIXEL motor's internal PD controller.

This scheme ensures stability and responsiveness. The diffusion policy runs at 7 Hz, while the lower-level force and position controllers operate at higher rates (e.g., 25 Hz for force control, synchronized with GelSight Mini and FEATS). Two crucial calibrations are performed: hand-eye calibration (OptiTrack world frame to robot base) and a linear mapping from ArUco-measured grip width to motor position.

**E. Baselines:**
To isolate the contributions of FARM's design choices, it is compared against three baselines, all implemented as diffusion policies:
1. **Force-Aware Baseline:** Uses scalar total normal force (from FEATS) as the sole tactile input. Action space includes target grip force and uses the dual-mode controller.
2. **Tactile-Aware Baseline:** Uses raw GelSight Mini images as tactile input. Action space includes target pose and grip width, but *excludes* target grip force (no explicit force control).
3. **Vision-Only Baseline:** No tactile feedback. Action space includes target pose and a binary gripper state (open/closed).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on a real Franka Research 3 robot across three distinct tasks, each testing different force requirements: Plant Insertion (high, static force), Grape Picking (low, static force), and Screw Tightening (dynamic, adaptive force). For each task, 30 human demonstrations were collected, and policies were evaluated over 20 rollouts.

**1. Importance of Explicit Force Signals for Task Success:**
Comparing the force-aware, tactile-aware, and vision-only baselines demonstrated the general superiority of incorporating force information:
- **Plant Insertion:** Force-aware (95% success) outperformed Vision-only (85%) and Tactile-aware (65%). Explicit force control ensured sufficient grip, while the tactile-aware policy often slipped due to insufficient force.
- **Grape Picking:** Force-aware (85%) was significantly better than Tactile-aware (60%) and Vision-only (0%). Precise force control was crucial to avoid crushing the grape, which vision-only consistently failed to do.
- **Screw Tightening:** Tactile-aware (40%) surprisingly outperformed Force-aware (10%), both being superior to Vision-only (0%). This suggests that for complex contact tasks, the higher-dimensional information in raw tactile images (Tactile-aware) is more informative than a single scalar force value (Force-aware), even without explicit force control. The vision-only baseline failed due to the lack of tactile feedback needed to sense screw resistance.

These results highlight that explicit force incorporation is highly beneficial for tasks requiring consistent static forces (high or low). However, for dynamically changing contact, scalar force alone may be insufficient, pointing to the need for richer representations.

**2. Impact of High-Dimensional (Image-Based) Force-Distribution Information:**
FARM, which leverages high-dimensional force distributions as observations, showed significant advantages, particularly in complex tasks:
- **Plant Insertion & Grape Picking:** FARM achieved 95% success rates for both, comparable to or slightly better than the force-aware baseline. This indicates that for tasks with relatively static force demands, scalar force signals can be sufficiently informative.
- **Screw Tightening:** FARM achieved a 100% success rate, dramatically outperforming all baselines (Force-aware 10%, Tactile-aware 40%, Vision-only 0%). The ability of FARM to utilize image-based shear and normal force distributions allowed for more precise control, enabling proper Allen key alignment and accurate detection of screw tightness. Baselines struggled with maintaining engagement (Force-aware) or interpreting complex contact dynamics from raw images (Tactile-aware).

This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that high-dimensional force-distribution information is crucial for tasks requiring nuanced, multi-dimensional, and dynamic force interactions, providing superior control and contact state awareness.

**3. Comparison in the Force Domain for Screw Tightening (Wasserstein-1 Distance):**
Quantitative analysis using the Wasserstein-1 distance (W1) confirmed FARM's superior force matching during the screw tightening task:
- **FARM:** Achieved a W1 distance of 0.7538 N, indicating a very close alignment between applied forces during rollouts and human demonstrations.
- **Force-Aware Baseline:** Had a W1 distance of 1.6580 N, showing reasonable but less precise force matching. It could produce similar forces but lacked contact state information for success.
- **Tactile-Aware Baseline:** Exhibited a W1 distance of 4.3801 N, applying consistently insufficient forces.
- **Vision-Only Baseline:** Recorded the highest W1 distance of 5.0515 N, consistently applying excessive forces.

These results validate that by incorporating force distributions into both observation and action spaces, FARM enables the robot to apply forces that are not only effective but also closely mimic the complex, time-varying force profiles demonstrated by humans.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FARM framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation and imitation learning, with substantial implications for future research and real-world applications.

**1. Enabling Truly Force-Aware Manipulation:** The most critical impact is FARM's ability to move beyond treating tactile sensing as an auxiliary observation to directly integrating high-dimensional force profiles into the action space. This paradigm shift empowers robots to actively regulate grip forces, a capability essential for delicate, contact-rich tasks previously out of reach for many learning-based systems. This direct control over force allows for more dexterous, adaptable, and robust interaction with objects.

**2. Bridging the Reality Gap with Robot-Free Data Collection:** The use of an adapted handheld UMI gripper for human demonstrations, coupled with the geometrically matched Actuated UMI for deployment, offers an elegant solution to the perennial "reality gap." By allowing human experts to demonstrate skills in a natural, unconstrained manner while collecting robot-embodied data, FARM simplifies the data acquisition process and enables direct policy transfer, significantly reducing the complexity and cost of training robots for new tasks.

**3. Open-Source Contribution to the Robotics Community:** The decision to open-source the design files and control software for the Actuated UMI gripper, alongside the codebase, is a valuable contribution. This facilitates reproducibility, encourages adoption, and provides a robust platform for further research in tactile-aware manipulation, accelerating innovation across the community.

**4. Enhanced Performance in Complex Tasks:** The experimental results, particularly the 100% success rate in the dynamically challenging screw-tightening task, demonstrate FARM's superior performance in scenarios where nuanced, multi-dimensional force interactions are critical. This showcases its potential for applications requiring fine motor skills and adaptive contact control, such as assembly, surgical robotics, or handling diverse consumer goods.

**5. Robustness through Dual-Mode Control:** The implementation of a dual-mode control strategy (switching between position and force control) provides a robust and intelligent way to manage gripper behavior in both free-space and contact conditions. This ensures stable performance and adaptability, addressing a common challenge in deploying learning-based policies on physical robots.

**Future Directions:** The paper also outlines promising avenues for future work, including generalizing the method to bimanual manipulation, incorporating anthropomorphic hands, and employing flow-matching objectives for enhanced reactivity. These directions suggest that FARM is not a singular solution but a foundational framework that can be extended to tackle even more complex and human-like manipulation challenges.

In summary, FARM pushes the boundaries of visuotactile imitation learning by tightly coupling advanced tactile sensing with explicit force-based action generation. Its methodological innovations and demonstrated performance promise a future where robots can interact with the physical world with unprecedented dexterity and intelligence, opening up new possibilities in manufacturing, service robotics, and beyond.
