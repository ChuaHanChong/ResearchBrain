# RoboPianist: Dexterous Piano Playing with Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> ROBOPIANIST, a deep reinforcement learning system from UC Berkeley and Google DeepMind, enables simulated anthropomorphic robot hands to perform complex piano pieces. The system achieves an average F1 score of 0.79 across 12 musical etudes, significantly outperforming a model-based predictive control baseline which scored 0.43.

## Problem

- Existing dexterous manipulation benchmarks offer a narrow definition of dexterity, often focusing on single goal states rather than continuous, high-precision tasks.
- Replicating human-like spatial and temporal precision, complex finger coordination, and long-term planning in high-dimensional robotic control, especially for bi-manual tasks, remains challenging.
- Prior robotic piano-playing efforts were generally limited in complexity, often using specialized hardware or simplified tasks with only a few notes or simple chords.

## Method

- The **ROBOPIANIST** system, built in ==MuJoCo==, leverages two anthropomorphic ==Shadow Dexterous Hands== and a full-size digital piano to model piano playing as a ==finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP)==.
- It employs a tailored ==reward function== combining ==key press rewards==, ==finger proximity incentives== based on human fingering priors from the **ROBOPIANIST-REPERTOIRE-150** dataset, and an ==energy expenditure penalty==.
- Policy optimization uses ==DroQ== (a regularized ==Soft-Actor-Critic (SAC)== variant), incorporating a ==lookahead horizon== in the observation space to enable anticipatory planning for future notes.

## Results

- Specialist ROBOPIANIST policies achieved an average **F1 score of 0.79** on the ROBOPIANIST-ETUDE-12 benchmark, demonstrating a substantial performance lead over the MPC baseline which averaged **0.43**.
- The learned policies exhibited skilled piano playing behaviors, including precise bi-manual control across the keyboard, simultaneous striking for chords, and rapid alternation for trills.
- Multi-task Behavioral Cloning showed improved generalization on unseen pieces as the number of training songs increased, suggesting a pathway for learning a diverse repertoire, although zero-shot performance did not match specialist policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Piano playing serves as a robust and challenging benchmark for robot dexterity, demanding high spatial-temporal precision, complex multi-finger coordination, and long-term planning beyond typical manipulation tasks.
> - The integration of human priors (fingering labels) and a lookahead mechanism in the observation space is crucial for deep reinforcement learning agents to overcome exploration challenges and learn complex motor skills in high-dimensional action spaces.
> - While specialist RL policies can achieve high performance on individual pieces, multi-task RL currently struggles to generalize effectively or show positive transfer across a diverse repertoire of songs without substantial degradation in performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses the challenge of replicating human-like dexterity in robotic hands, a long-standing open problem in robotics and a significant area of research in high-dimensional control. Previous research in dexterous manipulation has seen progress with learning-based approaches, particularly in tasks such as in-hand object re-orientation and manipulation of simply-articulated objects using lower-dimensional systems or single robot arms. However, the authors argue that these tasks represent a narrow definition of dexterity when compared to human capabilities. Many existing benchmarks are characterized by a single goal state or termination condition, which can limit the complexity of learned behaviors and sometimes result in unnatural motions. Bi-manual manipulation with high-dimensional hands remains particularly rare in the literature.

Within the specific domain of robotic piano playing, prior efforts have focused on specialized hardware designs or customized controllers. Some works have utilized dexterous hands with inverse kinematics and offline trajectory planning, or reinforcement learning on miniature pianos with a single four-fingered hand. These earlier robotic piano tasks, however, were generally limited in complexity, often involving only a few successive notes or simple chords.

ROBOPIANIST seeks to bridge this gap by introducing piano playing as a complex, high-dimensional control task that demands a broader definition of dexterity, including spatial and temporal precision, complex finger coordination, and long-term planning. It differentiates itself from existing dexterous manipulation benchmarks by requiring continuous accuracy over an entire musical piece, rather than merely achieving a specific final state. Unlike character control tasks that often optimize for motion progress or terminal goals, piano playing necessitates both objective melodic and rhythmic accuracy, as well as subjective musicality, which translates into challenging stylistic constraint satisfaction for robotic policies. This work aims to push the boundaries of bi-manual dexterous control by providing a more complex and human-like benchmark for evaluating advanced control policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable simulated anthropomorphic robot hands to learn and perform a wide repertoire of piano pieces using deep reinforcement learning. This goal is motivated by the desire to address the limitations of existing dexterous manipulation benchmarks and challenge current robotic control methods with tasks that demand a more comprehensive and human-like level of dexterity.

Specifically, the authors identify three key desiderata for high-dimensional control policies that piano playing inherently requires:
1. **Spatial and Temporal Precision**: The robot must accurately press the correct piano keys at precisely the right times, mirroring the demanding accuracy required from a human pianist. This involves precise control over finger positions and timing.
2. **Coordination**: The system must achieve multiple simultaneous goals, such as different fingers on each hand striking various notes without collision, while also managing the interplay between the two hands. This necessitates intricate multi-limb and multi-finger coordination.
3. **Planning**: The robot needs to plan its actions in anticipation of future notes, positioning its hands and fingers to facilitate reaching subsequent keys within given spatial and temporal constraints. This implies a longer-term horizon for decision-making beyond immediate note presses.

The authors propose that successfully addressing these challenges in a piano-playing context will foster the development of artificial embodied intelligence exhibiting precision and agility comparable to the human motor control system. They specifically aim to demonstrate that deep reinforcement learning, combined with careful system design and human priors, can synthesize policies capable of playing a diverse repertoire of musical pieces, thereby surpassing the capabilities of traditional model-based optimization methods in this complex domain. Furthermore, the motivation extends to providing an open-sourced environment and benchmark to facilitate future research in high-dimensional control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces ROBOPIANIST, an end-to-end system for learning piano playing. The approach combines a sophisticated simulation environment, a curated musical dataset with human priors, a tailored reinforcement learning framework, and specific design considerations to overcome the challenges of high-dimensional control.

**Simulation Environment:**
The system is built within the open-source MuJoCo physics engine. It features a full-size digital piano with 88 keys, each modeled as a joint with a linear spring that generates sound when depressed beyond a certain range (0.5° of maximum range). A sustain pedal mechanism is also implemented. The robotic manipulators consist of two anthropomorphic Shadow Dexterous Hands, closely reproducing human hand kinematics. Each hand has 22 actuators, resulting in a total of 44 degrees of freedom, and they are partially underactuated.

**Musical Representation and Evaluation:**
Musical pieces are represented using the MIDI standard, converted into a time-indexed piano roll. This roll serves as the goal trajectory, indicating which keys must be pressed at each time step.
Evaluation of agent proficiency uses standard metrics: precision, recall, and F1 score. These are computed by comparing the real-time state of the piano keys (active/inactive) with the ground-truth goal state, averaged over time steps. The F1 score, combining precision and recall, is the primary metric, ranging from 0 to 1, and is reported to correlate with qualitative performance.

**MDP Formulation and Observation Space:**
Piano playing is modeled as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP).
- **State Space (S):** Includes proprioceptive information (hand and forearm joint positions, forearm Cartesian position) and piano key joint positions.
- **Goal State Information:** Crucially, the observation space includes a vector of key goal states from the piano roll, stacked for a "lookahead horizon" (L). This allows the agent to plan for future notes. A discrete vector indicating which fingers should be used for each note (human priors) is also included.
- **Action Space (A):** Consists of 45 dimensions, including target joint angles for both hands and a scalar value for the sustain pedal. The agent predicts target joint angles at 20Hz, which are then converted to torques by PD controllers running at 500Hz.

**Reward Design:**
The reward function is central to learning performance and incorporates several components:
1. **Key Press Reward:** Rewards the agent proportionally to how depressed the *active* keys are and provides a constant penalty if any *inactive* keys are pressed hard enough to produce sound. This dense reward signal aids exploration.
2. **Finger Proximity Reward:** Encourages active fingers to move spatially close to their assigned target keys, based on fingering labels, to further guide exploration.
3. **Energy Expenditure Penalty:** Minimizes the absolute sum of joint torques multiplied by joint velocities, promoting efficient and less erratic movements.
The total reward is a weighted sum of these components.

**Human Priors (Fingering Labels) and Dataset:**
To mitigate exploration challenges in the high-dimensional action space, fingering labels (which finger to use for each note) are incorporated into the reward formulation. These labels were derived from annotations in the PIG dataset, forming a corpus of 150 annotated MIDI files called ROBOPIANIST-REPERTOIRE-150. This dataset comprises pieces of varying difficulty and length from diverse Western composers.

**System Design Considerations:**
- **Lookahead Horizon (L):** Including future goal states in the observation space allows the policy to plan and position hands for upcoming notes.
- **Constraining Action Space:** Degrees of freedom in the Shadow Hand that are not strictly necessary for piano playing or do not exist in human hands were explored for disablement, and the thumb's joint range was reduced. This was found to speed up learning, though full action space could achieve similar performance with more training.

**Policy Optimization and Baselines:**
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** The primary method uses DroQ, a regularized variant of Soft-Actor-Critic (SAC).
- **Model-based Baseline:** Online predictive control (MPC) with Predictive Sampling, leveraging the physics engine as a dynamics model, was used for comparison. This baseline was run at one-tenth of real-time speed to allow sufficient search time.
- **Multi-task Learning:** Both multi-task RL (training a single agent on multiple songs simultaneously) and multi-task Behavioral Cloning (BC, distilling specialist RL policies into a single policy) were investigated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a comprehensive evaluation of the ROBOPIANIST system, focusing on its ability to learn individual pieces, generalize across multiple songs, and the impact of various design choices.

#### 4.1. Specialist Policy Learning

- **Performance Comparison:** ROBOPIANIST, using the DroQ RL algorithm, significantly outperformed the MPC baseline. On the ROBOPIANIST-ETUDE-12 subset of tasks (12 songs), the ROBOPIANIST agent achieved an average F1 score of 0.79, while the MPC baseline averaged 0.43. This performance gap was consistent across the full ROBOPIANIST-REPERTOIRE-150. The authors hypothesize that MPC struggled due to the computational demands of searching a large action space within a limited time budget.
- **Qualitative Behaviors:** The learned policies demonstrated skilled piano behaviors, including simultaneous control of both hands to reach notes at opposite ends of the keyboard, precise and simultaneous striking of note triplets to play chords, and rapid alternation between adjacent notes to play trills.

#### 4.2. Multi-Song Policy Learning

- **Multitask RL Training:** Training a single RL agent to play multiple songs simultaneously proved challenging. As the number of training songs increased, the F1 score on individual songs (e.g., "Für Elise") decreased significantly. The agents also failed to demonstrate positive transfer or zero-shot generalization to unseen songs in ROBOPIANIST-ETUDE-12, indicating that multitask RL struggled to generalize in this context.
- **Multitask Behavioral Cloning (BC):** Distilling specialist RL policies into a single multitask policy using Behavioral Cloning showed improved generalization compared to multitask RL. As the number of training songs increased, the test F1 scores on unseen pieces also increased, suggesting that the BC model began to generalize. However, the zero-shot performance on the test set remained considerably lower than that of the specialist RL policies. The study also found that larger model sizes could better capture the complexity of multiple songs but were prone to overfitting with insufficient data.

#### 4.3. Further Analysis of Hyperparameters

- **Control Frequency and Lookahead Horizon:** There was an observed interplay between these parameters. A control frequency of 20Hz (0.05 seconds) was identified as optimal. Decreasing the control timestep (e.g., to 100Hz) drastically reduced performance by increasing the effective task horizon and complicating exploration. Conversely, a control frequency of 10Hz led to coarser MIDI discretization and negatively impacted note timing. Performance generally improved with increased lookahead horizon up to a certain point, as it allowed for better planning.
- **Discount Factor:** The discount factor in the RL reward function had a significant impact, especially for more complex songs. Discount factors in the range of 0.84 to 0.92 yielded the best performance. Higher discount factors (e.g., 0.96, 0.99) often resulted in more conservative policies that might skip note segments, leading to lower F1 scores, particularly for faster-paced pieces. Lower discount factors encouraged more exploratory behavior early in training, leading to faster learning of correct note striking.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ROBOPIANIST project represents a significant contribution to the fields of robotic dexterity and high-dimensional control. By framing piano playing as a control challenge, it introduces a novel and complex benchmark that goes beyond the traditional manipulation tasks, demanding a higher degree of spatial-temporal precision, multi-finger coordination, and long-term planning from robotic policies.

The development of the ROBOPIANIST simulation environment, along with the ROBOPIANIST-REPERTOIRE-150 dataset of annotated musical pieces, provides a valuable open-source platform for the research community. This benchmark offers graded difficulties and interpretable evaluation metrics (F1 score), enabling rigorous and reproducible comparisons of new algorithms.

The main findings demonstrate that deep reinforcement learning, when appropriately engineered with careful system design (e.g., tailored reward functions, human priors in the form of fingering labels, and lookahead observations), can achieve high levels of dexterity in complex bi-manual tasks that challenge traditional model-based approaches. The qualitative results showcasing simultaneous two-hand control, chord playing, and trills provide empirical evidence of the system's capabilities.

Furthermore, the work highlights the current limitations and open challenges in multi-task reinforcement learning, particularly the difficulty of training a single policy to generalize across a diverse repertoire of complex tasks and the absence of positive transfer in the RL setting. While behavioral cloning showed more promise for generalization in multi-task scenarios, it did not achieve expert-level performance in a zero-shot context. These findings point towards the need for further research in robust multi-task learning and generalization for high-dimensional control.

Future directions suggested by the authors include integrating human motion priors (e.g., from video demonstrations), exploring zero-shot generalization to new musical pieces, and incorporating multimodal data such as sound and touch feedback to enrich the learning experience. Overall, ROBOPIANIST establishes a challenging and realistic testbed that can drive advancements in robot learning, contributing to the broader goal of developing more agile and intelligent robotic systems.
