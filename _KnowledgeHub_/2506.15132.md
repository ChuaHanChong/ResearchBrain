# Booster Gym: An End-to-End Reinforcement Learning Framework for Humanoid Robot Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Booster Gym is presented as an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework for humanoid robot locomotion, utilizing an asymmetric actor-critic PPO architecture and comprehensive domain randomization to bridge the sim-to-real gap. It achieves zero-shot policy transfer to a physical humanoid robot, showcasing robust omnidirectional walking, adaptability to various terrains, and resistance to external disturbances.

## Problem

- Bridging the "sim-to-real gap" for reinforcement learning policies to enable robust zero-shot transfer from simulation to physical humanoid robots.
- The absence of an integrated, end-to-end framework for training and deploying RL-based humanoid locomotion, forcing researchers to assemble various disparate components.
- Challenges in simulating and controlling humanoid robots with parallel mechanical structures, as many physics engines are optimized for serial chains.

## Method

- Developed "**Booster Gym**," an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework using an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and PPO for policy optimization.
- Incorporated comprehensive domain randomization during training, covering robot dynamics, actuator properties, environmental factors, external disturbances, and communication latency, to address the sim-to-real gap.
- Designed a Python-based deployment pipeline that includes a series-parallel conversion module within the SDK to support complex mechanical structures like parallel linkages.

## Results

- The Booster T1 humanoid robot demonstrated successful omnidirectional walking capabilities, accurately tracking linear and angular velocity commands.
- Policies trained with the framework enabled stable locomotion on various terrains, including a 10-degree slope, and exhibited strong resistance to external impulsive and continuous disturbances.
- Achieved successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, with joint trajectories from Isaac Gym closely mirroring real-world robot behavior after accounting for quantitatively measured real-world parameters in domain randomization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The combination of an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and PPO allows for robust policy learning leveraging privileged simulation data while maintaining real-world deployability with limited observations.
> - Comprehensive and quantitatively informed domain randomization, including specific latency and parallel structure handling, is key to achieving effective zero-shot sim-to-real policy transfer.
> - An integrated, open-source framework can significantly accelerate humanoid robot locomotion research by consolidating diverse components and reducing infrastructure setup burden.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot locomotion control has seen a significant shift with the advent of learning-based methods, particularly reinforcement learning (RL). Traditionally, humanoid locomotion relied on model-based approaches such as Whole-Body Control (WBC) and Model Predictive Control (MPC). While effective in some scenarios, these methods often necessitate extensive tuning of analytical models, exhibit sub-optimal performance with external disturbances or modeling inaccuracies, and are computationally intensive for real-time operation on robots, sometimes requiring compromises in precision or offloaded computations.

Reinforcement learning has emerged as an alternative, allowing robots to acquire control policies through interaction with simulated environments, thereby minimizing modeling assumptions. This has facilitated the development of robust and adaptive locomotion controllers capable of handling complex tasks like dynamic standing and navigating challenging terrains.

A critical challenge in RL for robotics is the "sim-to-real gap," which refers to the discrepancy between idealized simulation environments and the uncertainties of the real world, including sensory noise, actuator dynamics, and environmental variations. To address this, several techniques have been developed:
- **Domain randomization:** Training policies across a diverse range of simulated parameters (dynamics, sensor noise, environments) to enhance robustness and generalization to the real world. However, excessive randomization can sometimes lead to conservative policies.
- **Teacher-student distillation and Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA):** Multi-stage approaches where an expert policy trained with privileged simulation information distills its knowledge into a deployable student policy that relies on proprioceptive feedback and infers latent parameters for real-time adaptation.
- **End-to-end frameworks:** More recent advancements that achieve performance comparable to privileged expert policies while maintaining real-world deployability, often integrating real-world data to refine models and reduce reliance on extensive randomization.

In parallel with algorithmic advancements, the computational demands of deep RL have driven the development of high-performance simulation tools. Traditional CPU-based simulators like MuJoCo and Webots offer precision but are limited in parallelization. GPU-accelerated simulators, such as NVIDIA Isaac Gym, have revolutionized training by enabling massive parallelization of physics simulations, reward and observation calculations, and neural network training entirely on the GPU. This significantly accelerates training by minimizing data transfer overhead and supporting thousands of parallel environments. Other GPU-accelerated simulators, like Isaac Lab, MuJoCo Playground, and Genesis, are also emerging, further expanding the capabilities of robot simulation.

This work positions itself within this landscape by focusing on creating a comprehensive, end-to-end code framework that addresses the sim-to-real gap for humanoid robot locomotion. It leverages NVIDIA Isaac Gym for efficient training due to its widespread adoption and suitability for humanoid locomotion tasks, despite some acknowledged limitations in its PhysX engine. The framework incorporates extensive domain randomization, drawing inspiration from existing open-source projects like IsaacGymEnvs and humanoid-gym, and explicitly tackles challenges such as parallel mechanical structures common in humanoid robot design. The paper aims to provide a practical and accessible tool for the robotics community, bridging the gap between theoretical RL advancements and successful real-world humanoid deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and disseminate a comprehensive, end-to-end reinforcement learning framework, termed "Booster Gym," designed to facilitate the training and deployment of locomotion policies for humanoid robots. This framework aims to cover the entire pipeline from simulation-based policy training to zero-shot transfer and execution on physical robots.

The motivation for this work arises from several challenges inherent in applying reinforcement learning to real-world humanoid robotics:
- **Complexity of Sim-to-Real Transfer:** While reinforcement learning has simplified the design and training of motion policies in simulation, transferring these policies to physical robots remains complex. This is due to discrepancies between simulation and reality, including the intricate dynamics of humanoid robots, the presence of sensory noise, and hardware-specific limitations that are difficult to perfectly model in simulation. Successful real-world deployment necessitates training procedures that enhance the robustness and generalizability of learned policies.
- **Lack of Integrated Solutions:** Despite significant progress in specific RL training methods and domain randomization techniques, there is a perceived need for a comprehensive, integrated code framework that encompasses all necessary implementation details from training to deployment. Researchers often have to manage various disparate components, which can impede efficient development and iteration.
- **Handling Parallel Structures:** Humanoid robots frequently incorporate parallel mechanical structures, particularly in areas like ankles, to reduce inertia and improve dynamic performance. These structures pose a specific challenge for simulation environments and control algorithms, as many GPU-based physics engines are primarily designed for serial kinematic chains. An effective framework needs to address this conversion for seamless deployment.
- **Accelerating Development:** By providing an open-source, user-friendly framework with customizable interfaces for RL algorithms, reward functions, and physical parameters, the authors aim to lower the entry barrier for researchers. This is intended to accelerate the development of advanced humanoid robot capabilities, allowing the community to focus more on algorithmic innovation rather than infrastructure setup.
- **Validation on a Physical Platform:** A key motivation is to validate the framework's effectiveness through empirical demonstration on a real humanoid robot, the Booster T1. This ensures that the proposed solutions for sim-to-real transfer are practical and enable robust capabilities such as omnidirectional walking, disturbance resistance, and terrain adaptability in physical environments.

In essence, the project is motivated by the desire to streamline the development process for RL-based humanoid locomotion, making it more accessible and robust for real-world applications by providing a consolidated, validated, and open-source software tool.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in Booster Gym is based on a reinforcement learning framework, leveraging an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), with a strong emphasis on bridging the sim-to-real gap through comprehensive domain randomization and a robust deployment pipeline.

#### 3.1. Reinforcement Learning Formulation

The robot control problem is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), defined by `M = ⟨S, A, p, r, O, γ⟩`, where `S` is the state space, `A` the action space, `p` the state transition function, `r` the reward function, `O` the observation space, and `γ` the discount factor. The agent learns a policy `π(a_t|o_t)` to map partial observations to action distributions, aiming to maximize the expected discounted sum of future rewards.

An **asymmetric actor-critic (AAC)** architecture is adopted, where the actor network receives partial observations `o_t` (consistent with real-world sensor data), while the critic network receives the full state `s_t` (privileged information available only in simulation) to improve value estimation. **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** is used as the RL algorithm to optimize the policy, employing a clipped surrogate loss function to constrain policy updates and an entropy regularization term to encourage exploration.

#### 3.2. Problem Setup

- **Observation Space:** The actor's partial observation `o_t` includes proprioceptive feedback (base angular velocity, gravity vector, joint positions, joint velocities), the previous action `a_{t-1}`, and velocity commands (`v_x, v_y, ω_yaw`) for omnidirectional walking. A gait cycle `(cos(2πf t), sin(2πf t))` is incorporated to guide the learning of periodic gaits, which is set to zero for standing. The critic's observation `s_t` includes all actor observations without noise, plus privileged information such as body mass, center of mass, base linear velocity, base height, push force, and push torque (Table I).
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs joint position offsets `a_t`. These offsets are added to default joint positions `q_0` to determine desired joint positions `q_des = q_0 + a_t`. A PD controller, running at a higher frequency on the motor driver, converts `q_des` into torque commands `τ_des = k_p(q_des - q) - k_d ˙q`, with zero desired joint velocities. The policy operates at 50 Hz.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function is a weighted summation of various components (Table II), categorized into:
 - **Tracking rewards:** Torso velocity tracking (x, y, yaw). Curriculum learning is used to progressively increase velocity commands, and commands are reset randomly, including a "stand still" option for gait transitions.
 - **Gait rewards:** Encouraging leg movement according to a reference gait cycle. Foot height is used instead of contact forces for swing phase detection due to Isaac Gym's simplified collision estimation. Penalties are applied for foot slip, feet yaw/roll, and feet distance.
 - **Regularization rewards:** Penalties for base height, orientation, torque, torque tiredness, power consumption, linear/angular velocities, joint velocities/accelerations, base acceleration, action rate, joint position limits, and collisions. A survival reward and a penalty for early termination are also included.
- **Episode Design:** Episodes have a maximum duration of 1500 steps (30 seconds). Early termination conditions (e.g., excessively low base height, high base velocity, robot falling) are implemented to prevent exploration of ineffective states, resetting the environment without subsequent rewards.

#### 3.3. Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap

To facilitate zero-shot transfer from simulation to the real world, the framework implements comprehensive **domain randomization**:
- **Robot Body Dynamics:** Randomization of mass and Center of Mass (CoM) positions for trunk and other links, along with observation noise, to account for variations in physical properties.
- **Actuator Characteristics:** Randomization of joint stiffness, damping, friction, and communication delays to mimic real-world actuator behaviors.
- **Environmental Conditions:** Simulation of diverse terrains and randomization of contact properties (friction, compliance, restitution).
- **External Disturbances:** Random application of external kicks and pushes (sudden velocity changes or applied forces) to enhance robustness against unexpected real-world impacts.
The framework also quantitatively measures real-world parameters, such as communication latency (9-12 ms round-trip) and policy inference time (<1 ms), and incorporates corresponding latency randomization (0-20 ms) during training to align simulation with real-world temporal characteristics.

#### 3.4. Deployment

A Python-based deployment framework is developed, including a pre-trained policy for real-world hardware implementation.
- **Efficient Execution:** Trained policies are exported in a Just-In-Time (JIT) compiled format for efficient execution on the robot's onboard CPU at 50 Hz. The policy outputs joint position targets, which are fed to the motor's built-in PD controller with fixed gains and zero desired joint velocities.
- **Hardware Abstraction:** The Booster Robotics SDK is provided, built on Data Distribution Service (DDS) middleware, offering a Publisher-Subscriber communication model. This SDK provides a unified API for low-level motor control and sensor data access (IMU, joint encoders).
- **Parallel Structure Handling:** A series-parallel conversion module is integrated into the SDK to address closed kinematic chains, common in humanoid ankle designs. This module allows policies to be trained with a computationally efficient virtual serial structure in simulation and then seamlessly deployed on parallel mechanisms. It calculates virtual series joint feedback using the robot's kinematic model and converts policy outputs from the series to the parallel structure using a transposed Jacobian matrix and a PD controller, thereby reducing the sim-to-real gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the Booster Gym framework was evaluated through real-world deployment on the Booster T1 humanoid robot across various locomotion tasks.

#### 4.1. Omnidirectional Walking Performance

The robot demonstrated successful omnidirectional walking capabilities. It was able to execute forward, backward, sideways, and rotational movements, effectively tracking linear velocity commands in the x and y directions, and angular velocity commands in the yaw direction. This included both single-direction and mixed-direction commands, as well as commands that varied over time (Figure 3). These results indicate the framework's capacity to facilitate fundamental locomotion behaviors.

#### 4.2. Walking on Diverse Terrains and Under Disturbances

The trained policy exhibited robustness and adaptability across varied environmental conditions and in response to external perturbations.
- **Terrain Adaptability:** The robot maintained stable locomotion on a range of surfaces, including grass, stone pathways, soil, asphalt, concrete, and tiled floors. Notably, the policy enabled stable forward, backward, and turning motions on a 10-degree slope, adapting its step transitions without explicit terrain perception (Figure 4, Figure 5).
- **Disturbance Resistance:** The policy demonstrated resilience to external disturbances. In tests involving an impulsive impact (a 10 kg weight dropped from 1 m while the robot was stepping in place), the robot experienced significant torso displacement but rapidly adjusted its foot placement to regain stability within a few steps (Figure 6). Additionally, the robot maintained stability and resumed motion promptly when subjected to continuous external forces during forward movement.

#### 4.3. Sim-to-Real Transfer

The investigation into sim-to-real transfer involved quantifying the dynamics gap between simulation and physical deployment.
- **Zero-Shot Transfer Validation:** Comparative analysis of joint position-velocity trajectories during walking (0.5 m/s forward velocity) in Isaac Gym, MuJoCo, and the real world indicated that the designed domain randomization effectively covered real-world properties. The trajectories from Isaac Gym training closely matched those observed during real-world deployment, demonstrating successful zero-shot policy transfer (Figure 7).
- **Cross-Simulation Verification:** Results from MuJoCo indicated that this environment provided a close approximation of real-world dynamics, suggesting its utility as a lightweight and consistent platform for rapid policy validation.
- **Quantified Parameter Alignment:** Critical real-world parameters, such as communication latency and policy inference time, were quantitatively measured to refine domain randomization. Round-trip communication latency from command transmission to initial observed joint position change was measured at 9-12 ms, with policy inference time under 1 ms. These findings informed the inclusion of sensor-to-actuator latency randomization (0-20 ms) in simulation training, further enhancing the alignment between simulated and real-world temporal characteristics (Figure 8).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Booster Gym framework represents a contribution to the field of humanoid robot locomotion control, particularly in addressing the practical challenges of applying reinforcement learning in real-world scenarios.

One significant aspect of this work is the provision of a **complete, end-to-end solution** for training and deploying RL-based locomotion policies. By integrating various components—from simulation environment configuration and algorithm selection (AAC with PPO) to comprehensive domain randomization and a robust deployment pipeline—the framework streamlines a process that often requires significant integration effort from researchers. This consolidation is designed to reduce the implementation burden, allowing researchers to allocate more resources to algorithmic development rather than infrastructure setup.

The framework's focus on **bridging the sim-to-real gap** through systematic and comprehensive domain randomization is another key impact. By randomizing robot body dynamics, actuator characteristics, environmental conditions, and external disturbances, the policies are trained to be more robust and generalizable. The quantitative analysis of real-world parameters like communication latency and its incorporation into the simulation further refines this approach, leading to effective zero-shot transfer capabilities as demonstrated on the Booster T1 robot. This capability is crucial for advancing humanoid robotics, where real-world training is often prohibitively expensive and risky.

The successful demonstration of **omnidirectional walking, terrain adaptability, and disturbance resistance** on a physical humanoid robot validates the framework's practical utility. These capabilities are fundamental for real-world applications of humanoid robots in diverse and unstructured environments. The framework's ability to handle parallel mechanical structures through a series-parallel conversion module in the SDK addresses a specific hardware challenge, enhancing its applicability to a broader range of humanoid designs.

Finally, by releasing Booster Gym as an **open-source resource**, the authors aim to facilitate community-driven innovation. This open-source approach has the potential to lower the barrier to entry for researchers and developers interested in humanoid robot locomotion, accelerate the iterative development of customized locomotion strategies, and foster collaboration within the robotics community. This could lead to a more rapid advancement of robust and versatile humanoid robot capabilities beyond the specific examples presented in the paper.
