# Experiential Reflective Learning for Self-Improving LLM Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Experiential Reflective Learning (ERL) allows large language model (LLM) agents to adapt to new environments by generating reusable heuristics from single-attempt task executions. This method injects relevant learned guidelines into the agent's prompt, resulting in a 56.1% success rate on Gaia2, a 7.8% improvement over the ReAct baseline, and increased reliability by up to 10.6%.

## Problem

- General-purpose LLM agents often struggle to adapt to specialized environments with unfamiliar tools and domain-specific conventions.
- Current agents frequently do not effectively leverage past interactions, approaching each new task from scratch and leading to redundant failures.
- Prior experiential learning methods for LLM agents often require multiple task retries, incur scalability issues with growing context, or lose granular details through aggregation.

## Method

- The framework generates ==structured heuristics== from single-attempt task experiences (successes or failures), each containing an analysis and a 'Learned Guideline' with explicit trigger conditions and actionable advice.
- These generated heuristics are stored in a ==persistent heuristic pool==, forming the agent's experiential memory.
- When facing a new task, the system uses an LLM to retrieve and score the top-k most relevant heuristics (based on similarity, diversity, and informativeness) and injects them into the agent's ==system prompt== to guide its execution.

## Results

- ERL achieved a **56.1%** overall success rate on the Gaia2 benchmark, representing a **7.8%** improvement over the ReAct baseline (48.3%) and a **5.2%** improvement over the strongest prior method, ExpeL (50.9%).
- The framework significantly enhanced agent reliability, increasing the pass^3 metric (succeeding on all three runs) by **8.3%** on Execution tasks and **10.6%** on Search tasks.
- Raw trajectories used as few-shot demonstrations failed to improve performance over the baseline, while heuristics consistently outperformed them, offering up to a **23.8%** gain in specific task types for comparable context lengths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distilled, structured heuristics provide a more effective and generalizable learning signal for agents compared to raw task trajectories or few-shot demonstrations.
> - LLM-based retrieval of relevant heuristics, considering both task similarity and guideline content, is crucial for performance and outperforms simpler embedding-based or random selection.
> - Heuristics derived from failed tasks are particularly effective for 'Search' tasks, while those from successful tasks are more beneficial for 'Execution' tasks, suggesting a complementary role for different types of experiences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous agents powered by large language models (LLMs) has enabled significant advancements in complex reasoning and multi-step problem-solving. However, these general-purpose agents frequently encounter challenges in adapting to novel environments, which may feature unfamiliar tools, domain-specific conventions, and unique operational contexts. Traditional adaptation methods, such as fine-tuning, are often resource-intensive, impractical for closed-source models, and do not inherently support continuous, on-the-fly learning from new experiences.

This limitation has spurred interest in experiential memory systems, which aim to facilitate parameter-free agent improvement by leveraging accumulated experience. Prior research in this area includes methods like ExpeL (Zhao et al., 2024), which extracts insights by comparing successful and failed trajectories, often requiring multiple task retries. ExpeL’s approach of concatenating all extracted insights into every test prompt can lead to scalability issues as the knowledge base grows. Another method, AutoGuide (Fu et al., 2024), generates context-aware guidelines by contrasting trajectory pairs and performs guideline retrieval at every agent turn. However, AutoGuide incurs substantial overhead due to continuous retrieval and may lack guidance when no stored context matches the current state. Both ExpeL and AutoGuide depend on multiple rollouts per task to construct contrastive trajectory pairs, a requirement that may not be feasible in real-world deployment scenarios where tasks cannot be repeatedly retried.

Experiential Reflective Learning (ERL) addresses these identified limitations by proposing an efficient self-improvement framework. It distinguishes itself by generating reusable heuristics from single-attempt trajectories, eliminating the need for curated training sets or repeated task execution. Furthermore, ERL aims to preserve granular trajectory details within its heuristics, which existing cross-task aggregation methods might otherwise lose. By focusing on selective retrieval and concise, transferable guidance, ERL positions itself as an advancement in enabling LLM agents to adapt more effectively and efficiently to new environments through continuous, parameter-free learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a self-improvement framework, Experiential Reflective Learning (ERL), that enables large language model (LLM) agents to adapt rapidly and efficiently to specialized environments. This framework aims to allow agents to leverage past interactions and learn from accumulated experience, thereby overcoming a significant limitation where agents approach each new task as if from scratch, regardless of prior engagements.

The motivation behind ERL stems from several identified challenges in current LLM agent deployment:
- **Lack of Environment Adaptation**: General-purpose LLM agents frequently struggle to adapt to new environments characterized by unfamiliar tools and domain-specific conventions. Their inherent design often leads to suboptimal performance or failure in such specialized contexts.
- **Inefficiency of Existing Adaptation Methods**: While fine-tuning offers a way to adapt models, it is resource-intensive, particularly for large models, and often unfeasible for proprietary, closed-source LLMs. Furthermore, fine-tuning does not inherently support continuous learning in dynamic environments.
- **Limitations of Prior Experiential Learning Approaches**: Previous methods designed for parameter-free improvement from experience, such as ExpeL and AutoGuide, present their own set of constraints:
 - **Requirement for Multiple Retries**: Many existing methods necessitate multiple task retries (e.g., successful and failed trajectories) to extract reusable insights. This assumption often breaks down in practical agent deployments where tasks cannot be repeatedly executed.
 - **Scalability Issues**: Approaches that concatenate all accumulated insights into every prompt can lead to context window saturation and poor scalability as the agent gathers more experience.
 - **High Overhead**: Some methods involve continuous context identification and guideline retrieval at every agent turn, incurring substantial computational overhead.
 - **Loss of Granular Detail**: Existing methods sometimes aggregate knowledge across tasks, which may lead to a loss of specific, actionable insights embedded within individual trajectories.

ERL is specifically motivated to address these gaps by:
- **Enabling Rapid Adaptation**: By generating and retrieving actionable heuristics, ERL aims to provide task-specific guidance that facilitates quicker and more effective adaptation to new scenarios.
- **Efficient Knowledge Extraction**: ERL focuses on extracting heuristics from single-attempt trajectories, significantly reducing the data collection burden and making the framework applicable in real-world settings where task retries are limited or impossible.
- **Providing Transferable Abstractions**: The framework seeks to distill complex trajectories into concise, structured heuristics that capture transferable lessons, rather than relying on raw, context-heavy demonstrations.
- **Improving Reliability**: Beyond just increasing success rates, a key motivation is to enhance the consistency and stability of agent performance across multiple runs for the same task.

In essence, ERL aims to empower LLM agents with a practical, efficient, and scalable mechanism for continuous self-improvement through reflection on past experiences, thereby enhancing their robustness and adaptability in diverse operational environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Experiential Reflective Learning (ERL) framework is composed of two primary interactive components: heuristic generation from task experience and retrieval-augmented execution for new tasks.

#### 3.1. Heuristic Generation

The process begins as an agent interacts with an environment and completes tasks. The environment provides a binary feedback signal indicating success or failure for each task. Each interaction constitutes an "experience," comprising the task description, the detailed execution trajectory (including reasoning steps, tool calls, and their outputs), and the outcome signal.

Upon completion of each task, the agent engages in a self-reflection process, which is guided by an LLM. This reflection analyzes the accumulated experience to generate a structured "heuristic." Each heuristic contains two main parts:
1. **Analysis**: This section identifies the root causes contributing to the success or failure of the task. For instance, it might pinpoint a specific logical error, a misuse of a tool, or an effective decision point.
2. **Learned Guideline**: This is an actionable principle derived from the analysis, designed to be generalizable across tasks. Guidelines include explicit trigger conditions (e.g., "When sending emails to calendar attendees...") and recommended actions (e.g., "...first resolve names to email addresses via the Contacts tool before calling the email API"). These heuristics are generated from single-attempt trajectories, making the process efficient and suitable for scenarios where repeated execution is not feasible. The generated heuristics are then stored in a persistent pool for future use.

#### 3.2. Retrieval-Augmented Execution

When the agent encounters a new task, it leverages the accumulated pool of heuristics to guide its execution. This component involves the following steps:
1. **Task Analysis**: An LLM first analyzes the new task, decomposing it into potential sub-tasks and anticipated action steps.
2. **Heuristic Scoring and Retrieval**: The LLM then scores each stored heuristic for its relevance to the current task. This selection process considers:
 - **Similarity**: How closely the stored task description matches the new task.
 - **Diversity**: Ensuring a range of experiences are considered to cover various potential lessons.
 - **Informativeness**: The quality and applicability of the guideline content within the heuristic.
3. **Context Injection**: The top-k most relevant heuristics are then selected and injected into the agent's system prompt. This provides task-specific advice directly within the agent's working context, rather than overwhelming it with the entire heuristic pool. This injection augments the agent's baseline reasoning process (e.g., a ReAct loop) without requiring modifications to its core logic. The paper indicates k=20 as the default number of retrieved heuristics and uses GPT-5.2 as the LLM for retrieval.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

ERL was evaluated on the Gaia2 benchmark (Froger et al., 2025), which assesses long-horizon agentic capabilities within a simulated mobile environment featuring 12 applications and 101 tools. The evaluation focused on the Search and Execution splits of Gaia2.
- **Baseline**: The default ReAct agent scaffold of Gaia2 served as the baseline.
- **Agent Backbone**: GPT-5-mini was used as the LLM backbone for all methods to ensure a consistent comparison.
- **Evaluation Universes**: Gaia2's structure includes isolated "universes" with identical tools but disjoint information. ERL accumulated heuristics on 8 training universes (112 execution / 132 search tasks) and was evaluated on 2 held-out test universes (48 execution / 28 search tasks) to prevent knowledge contamination.
- **Comparison Methods**: ExpeL (Zhao et al., 2024) and AutoGuide (Fu et al., 2024) were implemented for comparison, with their specifics detailed in the paper's appendix, including the use of Reflexion for trajectory generation in both.
- **Additional Evaluations**: The study also included comparisons against few-shot prompting with raw trajectories, investigations into random and embedding-based heuristic retrieval, and ablations on learning specifically from failures or successes. An evaluation on the τ^2-bench (Barres et al., 2025) for dual-control environments was also performed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the efficacy and characteristics of the Experiential Reflective Learning (ERL) framework.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance on Gaia2

ERL achieved a 56.1% overall success rate on the Gaia2 benchmark, which represents a +7.8% improvement over the ReAct baseline (48.3%) and a +5.2% improvement over ExpeL (50.9%), the strongest prior experiential learning method tested. These gains were observed consistently across different task types: +8.3% on Execution tasks (51.4% vs. 43.1% baseline) and +7.1% on Search tasks (60.7% vs. 53.6% baseline). In contrast, prior methods like AutoGuide showed uneven performance, excelling in Search tasks (61.9%) but underperforming the baseline in Execution tasks (39.6%). Few-shot prompting with raw trajectory demonstrations yielded no improvement over the baseline, resulting in a 46.4% success rate.

#### 4.2. Agent Reliability

ERL demonstrated substantial improvements in agent reliability. The passˆ3 metric, which measures the fraction of tasks successfully completed across all three runs, showed gains of +8.3% in Execution and +10.6% in Search compared to the baseline. This indicates that ERL contributes to more stable and consistent agent performance. While pass@3 (success in at least one of three runs) also improved, the smaller gains suggest that ERL primarily enhances the consistency of task completion rather than significantly increasing the number of newly solvable scenarios.

#### 4.3. Generalization of Heuristics vs. Raw Trajectories

Heuristics were found to generalize more effectively than raw trajectories. Few-shot prompting using raw trajectories as in-context demonstrations failed to improve performance over the baseline. This finding suggests that raw trajectories may lack the distilled, actionable insights necessary for agents to generalize to new scenarios. Conversely, ERL’s heuristics, which are concise and strategic principles, consistently outperformed raw trajectories across various token budgets, providing a superior learning signal and enabling more experiences to fit within fixed context windows.

#### 4.4. Importance of Retrieval Quality

The quality of heuristic retrieval was shown to be critical. ERL’s LLM-based selection mechanism, retrieving k=20 heuristics, achieved 56.1% success, outperforming both random selection (best random configuration at 53.8%) and embedding-based retrieval using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (53.3%). Random selection performance exhibited a non-monotonic pattern, peaking between 40-60 heuristics before declining, which supports the necessity of a selective retrieval mechanism. The LLM-based retrieval considers task similarity, error patterns, and guideline content, leading to more effective guidance.

#### 4.5. Impact of Learning from Failures vs. Successes

The origin of heuristics (failure-derived vs. success-derived) had a task-dependent impact on performance. Failure-derived heuristics substantially outperformed success-derived ones overall (58.9% vs. 49.9%). This effect was particularly pronounced in Search tasks, where failure heuristics yielded a +14.3% improvement over the baseline by providing negative constraints that help prune ineffective strategies. In contrast, success-derived heuristics performed best on Execution tasks, achieving a +9.0% improvement by reinforcing proven action sequences. The study concludes that for practical deployment with unknown task distributions, retrieving from both sources offers a reliable compromise.

#### 4.6. Token Count and Cost Analysis

An analysis of token usage and API costs (using OpenAI API with GPT-5-mini and GPT-5.2) revealed that ERL incurred an approximate 40% overall increase in API costs compared to the ReAct baseline for a full Gaia2 test set evaluation. This increase was primarily due to the nearly doubled input tokens during scenario rollout (+85%), as retrieved heuristics (∼20k tokens) were appended to the base system prompt (∼12k tokens) at every turn. However, prompt caching helped absorb a larger share of input tokens under ERL (88% vs. 82% for baseline), mitigating the cost increase for rollout to a moderate +15%. The additional costs for heuristic generation and retrieval contributed to the overall increase.

#### 4.7. Iterative ERL Evaluation

An iterative variant of ERL, where heuristics are added to the pool and immediately used for subsequent tasks within the same batch, was also evaluated. While iterative ERL achieved higher success rates on the source tasks during accumulation (44.6% Execution, 59.9% Search) compared to standard ERL (42.0% Execution, 53.8% Search), it showed lower generalization performance on the held-out test tasks (-5.4% overall success rate). Possible explanations include that guidance in iterative ERL may lead agents to encounter narrower failure modes, resulting in heuristics that lack the breadth for novel situations, or that heuristics from guided trajectories are less transferable as they reflect behavior shaped by prior guidance rather than independent discovery.

#### 4.8. τ^2-bench Evaluation

On the τ^2-bench, ERL improved the overall success rate over the baseline (0.380 vs. 0.367), showing gains on single-control domains like Airline and Retail. On these domains, ERL also improved passˆ3, corroborating the Gaia2 finding that it enhances consistency. However, on the dual-control Telecom domain, ERL exhibited a slight drop in success rate and passˆ3, while pass@3 improved. This divergence suggests limitations in ERL's ability to capture complex user coordination strategies in less predictable, dual-control environments, indicating a potential area for future work.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Experiential Reflective Learning (ERL) framework represents a contribution to the field of LLM agent self-improvement, particularly concerning adaptation to new environments. The key findings demonstrate its potential to enhance the performance and reliability of autonomous agents without requiring parameter updates to the underlying LLM.

The primary significance lies in ERL's ability to extract and leverage "heuristics" from single-attempt experiences. This moves beyond prior methods that often rely on costly multi-retry scenarios or extensive curated datasets, making ERL more applicable to real-world deployment where task retries may be limited. By distilling complex trajectories into concise, transferable guidelines, ERL provides a more efficient and scalable form of in-context learning compared to direct few-shot prompting with raw trajectories. This distinction is crucial for maintaining performance within the context window constraints of LLMs while still benefiting from accumulated experience.

The improved task completion reliability, as evidenced by significant gains in the passˆ3 metric, suggests that ERL not only helps agents solve more tasks but also enables them to do so more consistently. This enhanced stability is valuable for applications requiring dependable agent performance. The research also highlights the critical role of intelligent, LLM-based retrieval in selecting relevant heuristics, demonstrating that quality of guidance is more important than sheer quantity. Furthermore, the differentiated impact of learning from failures versus successes provides insights into tailoring experiential learning strategies based on task characteristics, potentially leading to more specialized and effective guidance.

The potential impact of ERL extends to various domains where autonomous agents operate in dynamic or specialized environments. It offers a practical pathway for:
- **Continuous Learning in Production**: Agents could continuously learn and refine their strategies in live environments, adapting to evolving tools, conventions, and user interactions without requiring frequent re-training or fine-tuning of the base model.
- **Reduced Development Costs**: By enabling parameter-free adaptation, ERL could reduce the computational and engineering resources typically associated with adapting LLMs to new tasks or domains.
- **Enhanced Robustness**: Agents guided by ERL's heuristics may exhibit increased resilience to novel situations and error conditions by leveraging lessons from past mistakes and successes.
- **Explainable Agent Behavior**: The structured nature of heuristics, with explicit analyses, triggers, and actions, could also contribute to more interpretable agent decision-making.

While the increased API costs due to larger context windows are noted, future work on more compact heuristic representations could mitigate this. The limitations identified in dual-control environments also point towards important research directions for enhancing agent coordination strategies. Overall, ERL provides a foundational framework for building more adaptive, reliable, and continuously self-improving LLM agents.
