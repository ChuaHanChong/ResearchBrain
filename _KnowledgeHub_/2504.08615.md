# Tactile sensing enables vertical obstacle negotiation for elongate many-legged robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Elongate many-legged robots from Georgia Institute of Technology successfully negotiate vertical obstacles up to 25 cm (five times their center height) by integrating a novel tactile antenna and foot contact sensors with a feedback control framework. This enables robust 3D navigation in complex, confined environments.

## Problem

- Elongate many-legged robots face limitations in negotiating significant vertical obstacles beyond planar terrains due to their morphology and operational challenges.
- Traditional perception methods like vision are often impractical for these robots in confined, low-light environments, or due to body oscillation, leading to poor data quality.
- Specialized adhesion mechanisms are not suitable for the general rugged terrains these robots are expected to traverse, and complex 3D motion planning for high-degree-of-freedom systems is challenging.

## Method

- Developed a novel ==tactile sensing system== featuring a custom-designed ==tactile antenna== on the robot's head and binary limb contact sensors embedded in each leg tip.
- Implemented a ==feedback control framework== that uses input from the tactile antenna (estimated obstacle height and position) and foot contact sensors (segment duty factors) to dynamically adjust vertical body undulation.
- The control framework employs a **Head Controller** for initial obstacle negotiation by raising or pitching the head, and a **Pitch-Down Controller** that brings subsequent floating body segments onto the obstacle.

## Results

- The robot successfully climbed box-shaped obstacles up to **20 cm** and bundled-cylinder obstacles up to **25 cm** (five times its center height), significantly exceeding the **10 cm** limit of open-loop control.
- The system demonstrated robust climbing on diverse and challenging terrains, including rapidly changing curvatures, multiple consecutive obstacles, and shifting, unstable elements.
- The robot successfully completed an inspection task within a **3-meter-long, 0.2-meter-radius** pipe filled with debris, showcasing its utility in challenging, confined 3D environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simple, low-bandwidth tactile sensing provides sufficient information for complex 3D behaviors like climbing in high-degree-of-freedom, many-legged systems, overcoming limitations of vision in challenging environments.
> - Integrating "mechanical intelligence" (robot physical structure) with basic feedback controllers enables robust and predictable climbing performance in unstructured and dynamic environments.
> - The proposed feedback control strategy, leveraging both head-mounted and leg-based tactile feedback, effectively guides the robot over obstacles much taller than itself.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research, particularly in the realm of legged locomotion, has made significant strides in navigating complex terrains. Quadrupedal robots, for instance, have achieved impressive parkour feats often relying heavily on advanced vision systems to reconstruct environmental geometry for sophisticated motion planning. Similarly, specialized robots utilizing adhesion mechanisms (vacuum, magnetic, bio-inspired claws) have demonstrated robust climbing on vertical walls and trees.

However, this paper identifies critical gaps when attempting to transfer these successes to **elongate many-legged robots**, which are distinct in their morphology and operational challenges. The authors highlight three key limitations:
- **Vision Quality:** Many-legged robots often operate in confined, low-light environments, and their elongated bodies experience substantial orientation oscillation during locomotion, leading to poor camera data quality. Furthermore, low camera placement limits comprehensive terrain information.
- **Adhesion Design:** Adhesive foot designs, while effective for specific surfaces, are impractical for general rugged terrains that many-legged robots are expected to traverse.
- **3D Motion Planning Complexity:** With over 25 degrees of freedom (DoF), these systems are inherently complex. Developing robust whole-body control strategies without clear biological analogues for 3D many-legged locomotion is particularly challenging.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing an **alternative paradigm: tactile sensing**. Instead of relying on high-bandwidth vision or specialized adhesion, the authors argue for the efficacy of low-bandwidth tactile feedback. This aligns with a growing trend in robotics towards more robust and computationally efficient perception methods, especially in unstructured or challenging environments where traditional vision might fail. The use of bio-inspired tactile antennas and integration of tactile sensing in snake and hexapod robots provides precedents, but this paper specifically applies and extends these concepts to the unique challenges of elongate, high-DoF, many-legged systems for significant vertical obstacle negotiation. It fills a crucial void in enabling this class of robots to move beyond predominantly 2D movement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable **elongate many-legged robots to reliably negotiate significant vertical obstacles**, thereby expanding their operational range beyond planar or mildly rugged terrains. The authors are motivated by the observation that despite their proven ability to traverse challenging 2D environments (e.g., pine straw, rocks, mud), recent field tests reveal a critical need for 3D behaviors like climbing or ascending steep slopes. Real-world obstacles often encountered by mid-sized robots (e.g., rocks, wreckage, pipes) are considerably taller than the robots themselves, demanding robust vertical mobility.

Specific objectives include:
1. **Developing a novel tactile sensing system:** To enable the robot to probe obstacles and gather crucial short-range information about their structure, acting as a robust alternative to problematic vision-based systems.
2. **Designing a feedback control framework:** To integrate the tactile sensory input (from both the antenna and foot contact sensors) and dynamically adjust the robot's vertical body undulation for effective climbing, overcoming the limitations of open-loop control.
3. **Demonstrating robust and predictable climbing performance:** Validating the proposed system's capabilities through rigorous laboratory and outdoor experiments across a variety of complex obstacles, including those with rapidly changing curvatures and unstable elements.
4. **Advancing the understanding of 3D motion planning for high-DoF systems:** By providing insights into "what to sense" and "how to respond" using simple, low-bandwidth feedback for many-legged robots.

Ultimately, the motivation is to pave the way for future highly capable, low-profile many-legged robots that can autonomously navigate diverse and complex 3D environments for applications like inspection, search-and-rescue, or exploration in confined, unstructured spaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combination of hardware development, control algorithm design, and extensive experimental validation.

**Robot Platform:** The study utilizes a mid-sized, elongate many-legged robot, approximately 5-10 cm in height and 1-2 kg in weight, characterized by its high static stability and redundancy. The robot's locomotion is based on **wave templates**, which prescribe sinusoidal traveling waves for leg stepping, horizontal body undulation, and vertical body undulation, allowing for coordinated limb and body movements crucial for forward motion on rough terrain.

**Tactile Sensory Systems:**
1. **Tactile Antenna:** A custom-designed antenna is mounted on the robot's head. It incorporates two Force Sensing Resistors (FSRs) to detect contact forces (0-10 N). The antenna's tip is connected via springs to allow deformation upon contact, preventing jamming. It provides binary contact states: "Hit" (analog value > 300, ~1 N force) or "Void" (value < 300). The antenna oscillates vertically, allowing the robot to probe the environment. The Z-position of the antenna tip, relative to the head joint, is calculated using rigid transformation based on known segment lengths and joint angles. By combining antenna contact history (H) and Z-position history (Z), the system estimates `Z_max` (average of highest three Z-positions of positive hits) and `Z_min` (average of lowest three Z-positions of negative hits).
2. **Binary Limb Contact Sensing System:** Capacitive sensors (MPR121) are embedded in the tip of each leg to detect foot-ground interaction. These provide binary contact states ("Contact" or "No contact") by classifying analog values (below 50 indicates contact). This system is used to compute the "duty factor" for each robot segment, indicating the percentage of time a segment's legs are in contact with the ground.

**Control Framework for Climbing:**
The authors first analyze the limitations of an **open-loop controller**, which relies solely on the predefined wave templates. While effective for obstacles up to twice the robot's height (10 cm) by raising the belly with vertical body undulation, it fails for higher obstacles.

To overcome this, a **feedback control framework** is developed, which independently controls the vertical motion of specific body joints:
1. **Head Controller (Raise-Up Phase):** This controller primarily manages the vertical joint angle of the first (head) segment. Inspired by wall-following algorithms, it uses the `Z_max` and `Z_min` values from the antenna to adjust the head's pitch motion.
 - If `|Z_max|` is larger (indicating an obstacle ahead requiring upward motion), the head segment is raised using a proportional (P) controller (`K_p1 * (Z_max + a)`).
 - If `|Z_min|` is larger (indicating a need to pitch down onto the obstacle), the head pitches downward (`K_p2 * (|Z_min| - a)`).
 - If no hit is detected, implying descent, the head is hard-coded to pitch down by a constant value (`θ_0`) to facilitate landing.
 This ensures the head is quickly positioned atop the obstacle and then maintains a constant distance from its contour.

2. **Pitch-Down Controller (Drag Phase):** Once the head segment is on the obstacle, subsequent segments may float, reducing thrust. To accelerate their transition onto the obstacle, this controller uses duty factor data from the foot contact sensors.
 - Segments with a duty factor below a threshold (0.2) are identified as "floating."
 - The floating segment with the *smallest index* (i.e., closest to the obstacle's edge) is targeted.
 - The vertical joint immediately ahead of this floating segment is commanded to pitch downward with a periodic motion (`A_p * sin(4t) + θ_p`).
 - A priority rule is established: if the head controller and pitch-down controller both attempt to control the head segment (index 1), the pitch-down controller takes precedence.

**Theoretical Maximum Climbing Capacity:** The authors analyze two physical bounds for climbing box-shaped obstacles:
- **Dimensional Bound (`b_1`):** Determined by the robot's physical dimensions, specifically the distance from the head joint to the center of mass of the front mechanism (`L_c`) or to the farthest front legs (`L_h`), plus an offset (`h_0`) for belly height. This relates to the ability of the robot to "hook" onto an obstacle.
- **Mechanical Bound (`b_2`):** Related to the robot's ability to overcome gravity during the "drag" phase, where it essentially moves up an incline. This is constrained by the friction angle (μ) between the legs and the surface and the robot's length (`L`). The estimated limit for their robot based on these bounds is approximately 24 cm.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted in both laboratory and outdoor settings, demonstrate significant advancements in the robot's climbing capabilities.

- **Improved Climbing Height:** While the open-loop control could only manage obstacles up to 10 cm, the feedback controller significantly increased this.
 - The robot successfully climbed **20 cm box-shaped obstacles**, which is just below its calculated theoretical limit of 24 cm.
 - It also climbed **25 cm bundled-cylinder obstacles** (five times its center height), exceeding the box-shaped limit due to the cylinders' inherent slope (around 60 degrees), which effectively raises the `h_0` in the mechanical bound equation.
- **Robustness to Obstacle Type and Curvature:** The robot demonstrated robust climbing on:
 - **Bundled-cylinder obstacles:** These feature rapidly changing curvatures, validating the controller's ability to adapt to complex geometries.
 - **Multiple consecutive obstacles:** The robot successfully traversed two obstacles (15 cm and 13 cm high, 0.5 m apart) covered with metal beams, demonstrating handling of challenging transitions.
 - **Shifting, unstable obstacles:** It navigated a pile of randomly placed, shifting cylinders (up to 20 cm high), showcasing resilience to dynamic and unpredictable terrain.
- **Outdoor Performance Validation:** The climbing controller proved robust in complex outdoor environments:
 - Successfully climbed obstacles up to **20 cm (four times its height)** in confined spaces with rocks, mud, and leaves.
 - Completed an "inspection" task within a **3-meter-long, 0.2-meter-radius pipe** filled with robot-sized rocks, leaves, and pine straw in 80 seconds, showcasing its utility in challenging, confined 3D environments.
- **Effectiveness of Tactile Feedback:** Detailed tracking of antenna data (`Z_max`, `Z_min`) and segment duty factors confirmed that the proposed control mechanisms (head raising/pitching down based on antenna input, and localized pitch-down motion for floating segments based on duty factor) actively contributed to the successful negotiation of obstacles. The sequential shifting of floating segment indices confirmed the controller's adaptive response.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the capabilities of elongate many-legged robots, offering an innovative approach to 3D motion planning that contrasts with traditional vision-centric methods.

- **Validation of Tactile Sensing for 3D Navigation:** The paper conclusively demonstrates that simple, low-bandwidth tactile sensors can provide sufficient information for complex 3D behaviors like climbing in high-DoF, many-legged systems. This is particularly impactful for applications in cluttered, low-light, or otherwise visually challenging environments where cameras are ineffective or computationally expensive.
- **Enhancing Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** By leveraging "mechanical intelligence" and simple feedback controllers, the robot exhibits predictable and robust climbing performance in highly unstructured and dynamic environments. This reduces reliance on complex, high-computation perception and planning, making the robot more resilient to real-world variability and less prone to failures from sensor noise or environmental ambiguities.
- **Expanding Application Domains:** The demonstrated ability to climb significant vertical obstacles and traverse confined spaces (like pipes) opens up new application possibilities for these low-profile, many-legged robots. Potential areas include:
 - **Pipe inspection:** Especially in complex or debris-filled pipelines.
 - **Search and rescue:** Navigating rubble and confined spaces where humans or other robots cannot easily access.
 - **Exploration:** In environments with varying terrain heights and limited visibility.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The work highlights the value of bio-inspired, mechanically intelligent designs. It also explicitly lays out future research directions, such as:
 - Extending antenna capabilities for obstacle avoidance or ceiling detection.
 - Developing more sophisticated, non-linear control policies, potentially through reinforcement learning, to optimize climbing speed.
 - Investigating the integration of adhesion mechanisms to further enhance climbing capacity.
 - Improving the physical robustness of the antenna design (e.g., using TPU for compliance).

In summary, this paper effectively demonstrates an alternative, robust solution for environment perception and response in legged robots, significantly advancing the practical utility of elongate many-legged systems in challenging 3D environments.
