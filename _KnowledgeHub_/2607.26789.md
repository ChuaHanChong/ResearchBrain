# CheckVLA: Execution-Time Verification with Action-Conditioned World Model for Long-Horizon Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, CheckVLA, introduces execution-time verification for Vision-Language-Action policies, using an action-conditioned world model to monitor action chunk execution for deviations. This system dynamically triggers latency-aware repairs, leading to a 36.1% average success rate on RoboCasa365 mobile manipulation tasks, an 8.5 percentage point increase over periodic replanning.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies often use open-loop "action chunks" for long-horizon mobile manipulation, which are prone to errors from unobserved deviations during execution.
- Existing commit-time policy confidence mechanisms cannot react to deviations occurring after an action chunk is dispatched.
- Observation-only anomaly monitors struggle to differentiate between expected changes resulting from robot actions and genuine, unexpected anomalies without action-conditioned context.

## Method

- **CheckVLA** introduces a closed-loop execution framework featuring an ==action-conditioned world model== that predicts future observations based on committed actions.
- A ==conformally calibrated risk trigger== aggregates prediction-observation discrepancies into a risk score to decide when to intervene, offering statistical guarantees on false alarms.
- ==Latency-aware suffix rewriting== generates new action sequences during interventions, incorporating a hard prefix for continuity and risk-adaptive guidance for proportional correction.

## Results

- CheckVLA achieved a **36.1%** average success rate on RoboCasa365, outperforming invocation-matched periodic replanning by **8.5 percentage points**.
- The action-conditioned verifier demonstrated a timely recall of **77.9%** at a **5%** Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER), substantially higher than observation-only (**48.6%**) or action-shuffled (**37.9%**) variants.
- Analysis showed **25.0%** of episodes had low policy uncertainty but high world-model risk, concentrating **48.4%** of all failures, with **76.0%** of these being timely recoverable.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action chunks can be treated as testable predictions, allowing for feedback mechanisms during otherwise open-loop execution to detect in-chunk failures.
> - Action conditioning is critical for the world model to accurately discern unexpected deviations from expected changes, significantly improving detection over observation-only methods.
> - Execution-time verification provides a complementary layer of robustness to VLA policies, addressing "confidently-wrong" scenarios where policies are confident but the world state has diverged.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Long-horizon mobile manipulation, which combines navigation with complex physical interactions, is a challenging area in embodied artificial intelligence. Contemporary Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are increasingly employed for such tasks, often operating by executing predetermined "action chunks." These chunks represent sequences of actions issued without receiving new high-level visual input, a practice known as open-loop execution. This chunking strategy can amortize inference costs and enhance temporal consistency.

However, a fundamental limitation of open-loop chunking is its vulnerability to unobserved deviations. If an unexpected event occurs after an action chunk is committed—such as an object slipping or the robot's base being displaced—the subsequent actions in the chunk continue to execute based on an invalidated premise, potentially propagating and compounding errors. Current commit-time policy confidence mechanisms cannot react to deviations that manifest after dispatch, as they lack access to updated observations. Furthermore, existing post-dispatch monitoring systems that rely solely on observation-only anomaly scores may struggle to differentiate between expected changes resulting from the robot's actions and genuinely anomalous, unexplained changes, due to the absence of an action-conditioned reference.

The broader research landscape includes several approaches to address these issues:
- **Commit-time uncertainty estimation** provides insights into policy confidence but cannot use post-dispatch observations without re-invoking the policy.
- **Observation-based monitors** analyze incoming visual data but often lack the context of what *should* happen given the robot's actions.
- **Adaptive execution and asynchronous continuity methods** focus on when to refresh an action chunk or how to maintain seamless execution during replanning, but typically do not integrate a calibrated online test with latency-aware repair.

CheckVLA positions itself by addressing these specific gaps. It introduces a mechanism for execution-time verification that employs an action-conditioned world model to predict consequences, enabling the detection of deviations within an executing chunk. It further integrates a calibrated intervention trigger and a latency-aware suffix repair strategy, thereby restoring feedback to open-loop chunked execution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for CheckVLA stems from the limitations of open-loop action chunking in VLA policies for long-horizon mobile manipulation. While chunking offers benefits such as amortized inference and temporal consistency, it also creates a blind spot where unexpected physical deviations during chunk execution can go unnoticed and lead to compounding errors.

The key objectives of this research were:

1. **Develop an execution-time verification mechanism:** To enable continuous monitoring of whether the actual observed consequences of committed actions align with their expected outcomes, even within an open-loop action chunk. This aims to overcome the inability of commit-time policy confidence to react to post-dispatch deviations and the lack of action-conditioned context in observation-only monitors.
2. **Formulate action chunks as testable predictions:** To conceptualize a committed action chunk not just as a sequence of commands, but also as an implicit prediction of how observations should evolve over the short term. This allows the system to actively test these predictions against reality.
3. **Integrate an action-conditioned world model for consequence prediction:** To leverage a separately trained world model to predict future observations conditioned on the currently executing actions. This model serves as the reference for detecting discrepancies.
4. **Design a calibrated intervention trigger:** To establish a reliable and statistically bounded mechanism for deciding *when* to intervene. This involves aggregating prediction-observation discrepancies into a risk score and using a conformally calibrated threshold to control the episode-level probability of an unnecessary first intervention.
5. **Implement a latency-aware suffix rewriting and repair strategy:** To ensure that when an intervention is triggered, the subsequent repair actions are generated and deployed in a manner consistent with the system's inference latency. This includes incorporating a hard prefix to match already executing actions and using risk-adaptive guidance to determine the strength of revision for the remaining action suffix.
6. **Incorporate episodic context:** To enable the policy to retain memory of completed subgoals and prior progress across repairs, preventing catastrophic forgetting or regressions to earlier task states.
7. **Evaluate performance on complex, long-horizon tasks:** To demonstrate that the proposed CheckVLA framework can lead to tangible improvements in success rates on challenging mobile manipulation benchmarks under realistic constraints, such as matched policy invocation budgets.
8. **Conduct controlled studies:** To audit the individual contributions of different components (detection, repair, memory, latency, robustness, runtime) and to analyze failure modes, providing empirical evidence for the design choices.

In essence, the motivation is to restore a form of "feedback loop" during the execution of open-loop action chunks, allowing the robot to detect and recover from in-chunk failures before they propagate extensively, thereby increasing the robustness and success rate of long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CheckVLA is designed as a closed-loop execution framework structured around four interconnected components: action-conditioned rolling prediction, calibrated risk triggering, latency-aware suffix rewriting, and episodic context.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system begins at a chunk boundary `t` where a VLA policy `π_θ` generates an action chunk `A_t = (a_t,0, ..., a_t,H-1)` conditioned on the instruction `L`, multi-view observation `o_t`, and episodic context `ρ_t`. During chunk execution, the high-level policy does not receive new visual input. The system monitors the execution, and if a deviation occurs at `t*`, a repair process is initiated. The repair considers an inference latency `d_lat` (control steps for replanning) and an effective deployment `d_eff` to ensure only deployable actions (`h ≥ d_eff`) are replaced.

#### 3.2. Action-Conditioned Rolling Prediction

1. **Feature Extraction:** The latest observation `o_t'` is encoded into a feature vector `z_t'` using a separately pretrained, frozen monitoring encoder `ϕ`.
2. **World Model (Ψ):** A world model `Ψ` predicts the next `k` steps of observation features (`z_t'+i+1`). This prediction is conditioned on the latest observation feature `z_t'`, previous predicted features, the still-valid committed actions (`a_t':t'+i`), and a nominal proprioceptive rollout (`s_t':t'+i`).
3. **Re-anchoring:** Each new observation re-anchors the prediction rollout, limiting autoregressive drift. Predictions spanning a chunk boundary are generated only after the next chunk is committed. After a rewrite, predictions conditioned on superseded actions are invalidated.
4. **Training Ψ:** The world model `Ψ` is initially trained with teacher forcing (matching frozen encoder targets) and subsequently on its own rollouts to match the online autoregressive context. Training occurs in feature space, without pixel decoding. The frozen encoder ensures monitoring is isolated from policy optimization.

#### 3.3. Calibrated Risk Triggering

1. **Discrepancy Calculation:** When a new observation `z_t` arrives, the system retrieves the corresponding prediction `z_t|t-ℓ(t)` and calculates a distance `d_t` (e.g., cosine distance) between the predicted and observed features.
2. **Standardization:** The raw distance `d_t` is standardized (`d̃_t`) using mean `μ_d^(ℓ)` and standard deviation `σ_d^(ℓ)` statistics derived from held-out successful trajectories, specific to the prediction span `ℓ(t)`.
3. **Temporal Aggregation:** A causal risk head `R` aggregates `d̃_t` and contextual information (`x_τ = (d̃_τ, ℓ(τ)/k, h_τ/H, ∆_sw,τ)`) over a window `w`. The risk head also incorporates a compact summary `m(ρ_t)` from the episodic memory. This produces a risk score `r_t ∈`.
4. **Risk Head Training:** `R` is trained on nominally successful episodes, natural failures, and onset-annotated physical perturbations. Supervision focuses on pre-failure evidence.
5. **Conformal Calibration:** A functional conformal threshold `δ_t` is dynamically computed based on the time-varying mean `μ_r,t` and standard deviation `σ_r,t` of risk scores from nominal successes, and a finite-sample quantile `q̂_α` from calibration trajectories. This calibration provides an episode-level guarantee that the probability of an unnecessary first intervention is bounded by `α`.
6. **Intervention Trigger:** The first instance `t*` where `r_t > δ_t` triggers a repair.

#### 3.4. Suffix Rewriting

1. **Latency-Aware Prefix:** During the inference time `d_lat` (or `d_eff`) required for replanning, the old action chunk continues to execute. The new chunk's initial actions (`h < d_lat`) are hard-clamped to these executing actions, forming an irreversible prefix to reduce command discontinuity.
2. **Risk-Adaptive Guidance:** The remaining suffix of the new action chunk (`h ≥ d_lat`) is generated by the same VLA policy, but its generation is guided by the superseded chunk. The strength of this guidance is determined by the standardized exceedance `e_t*` of the risk score above the threshold. A base weight `w_0(e)` (decreasing with `e`) and channel-specific positional decay rates `λ_m` define the overall guidance weight `W_h,m`. This weight mixes the reference actions into the policy's flow velocity, allowing for stronger corrections with higher risk exceedance.
3. **Deployment:** The new, guided action suffix is deployed at `h = d_lat` (or `d_eff`).

#### 3.5. Episodic Context

1. **Keyframe Bank (B_t):** An event-driven bank stores pooled policy features `F_j` from real observations, along with timestamps. This bank persists across chunk boundaries and repairs.
2. **Write Criteria:** Writes to the bank are governed by non-learned criteria, including delay-confirmed local minima of joint-space displacement and diversity checks to avoid redundant frames.
3. **Context Fusion:** The policy uses gated cross-attention to fuse the full bank into its current context. The risk head receives only a compact summary `m(ρ_t)` (e.g., time since last frame, number of frames, mean last frame feature).

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

CheckVLA utilizes the `π_0.5` flow-matching backbone and decoupled mobility and manipulation experts that interact via joint self-attention over a shared VLM prefix. The monitoring encoder is a frozen V-JEPA 2-AC encoder.

#### 3.7. Evaluation Protocol

The system was evaluated on RoboCasa365, a simulation benchmark for mobile manipulation. Policy training was restricted to Human300 demonstrations. The verifier was trained on auxiliary nominal, natural-failure, and physics-perturbed rollouts from training-side tasks. Performance was measured on Atomic-Seen (A-S), Composite-Seen (C-S), and Composite-Unseen (C-U) tasks, using metrics such as average success rate, episode-level Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER), timely recall, rescue rate, harm rate, and runtime characteristics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the effectiveness and components of CheckVLA:

- **Overall Task Success:** CheckVLA achieved a 36.1% average success rate on RoboCasa365, establishing a new state of the art among the published Human300 policy-pretraining methods discussed in the paper. This represents an 8.5 percentage point improvement over invocation-matched periodic replanning (27.6%) under a common training recipe and paired evaluation seeds. CheckVLA ranked first on Composite-Seen tasks (30.9%) and second on Composite-Unseen tasks (10.2%), indicating its benefit for tasks involving chained subgoals.

- **Contribution of Verified Trigger and Suffix Rewriting:**
 - The "verified trigger" component, which replaces periodic replanning with a deviation-triggered replan under fixed suffix guidance, contributed a 3.9 percentage point increase in average success.
 - Further incorporating "risk-adaptive guidance" added 2.6 percentage points, and "episodic memory" added another 2.0 points, demonstrating the additive benefits of CheckVLA's components.

- **Importance of Action Conditioning:**
 - At a target episode-level Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) of 5%, the full action-conditioned verifier achieved a timely recall of 77.9%.
 - In contrast, an observation-only world predictor yielded 48.6% timely recall, and an action-shuffled world model (breaking action-consequence correspondence) resulted in 37.9% timely recall. This supports the hypothesis that action conditioning is critical for separating expected from unexpected observations.
 - Perturbed success rates also showed a similar pattern: 47.6% for the full verifier, 35.2% for observation-only, and 31.0% for action-shuffled.

- **Verifier Mechanism Ablations:**
 - Removing rolling re-anchoring from the verifier reduced timely recall by 15.1 percentage points (from 77.9% to 62.8%).
 - Replacing temporal aggregation with an instantaneous score reduced timely recall by 11.8 percentage points (from 77.9% to 66.1%).
 - Using a validation-tuned constant threshold instead of the time-varying conformal threshold increased the FWER from 4.8% to 7.1% while providing only a small gain in timely recall (71.5% vs. 77.9% for the full method). These results underscore the effectiveness of the composite design elements for timeliness and FWER control.

- **Policy-Side Alternatives:** Compared to four policy-side commit-time detectors (Monte Carlo action entropy, flow-path variance, sampling disagreement, and a frozen policy-feature probe), the full action-conditioned verifier exceeded the strongest by 9.3 points in timely recall and 3.9 points in perturbed success, without requiring additional VLA evaluations per step (which would add 382.2 VLA evaluations per episode if refreshed at the observation rate). This indicates that post-dispatch action-conditioned evidence provides unique information at monitor, rather than policy, cost.

- **"Confidently-Wrong" Quadrant Analysis:** An analysis of natural executions, segmented by policy uncertainty and world-model risk, revealed that 25.0% of episodes exhibited low policy uncertainty but high world-model risk. This "confidently-wrong" quadrant concentrated 48.4% of all failures, with 76.0% of these failures being timely recoverable. This finding suggests the complementarity of the two signals for robust execution.

- **Repair Rule Effectiveness:** Among various fixed-trigger repair rules evaluated, the proposed adaptive guidance strategy achieved 16.9% rescue at 2.8% harm (one-shot outcomes), outperforming fixed guidance (12.8% rescue, 3.7% harm) and RTC inpainting (13.7% rescue, 4.1% harm). This indicates that the adaptive retention mechanism, based on risk exceedance, contributes to improved recovery while minimizing negative impact on otherwise successful trajectories.

- **Episodic Memory Impact:** The event-driven keyframe bank significantly reduced completed-subgoal regressions, from 1.34 to 0.18 per episode, and contributed to overall success gains, particularly on composite tasks. This suggests its role in preserving task progress across interventions.

- **Deployment Cost:** CheckVLA added 88.4M monitor parameters and 16.9 GFLOPs per step, resulting in an average wall-clock cost of 1.18× that of the open-loop baseline. The monitor's p95 latency remained below one control period, ensuring real-time operation. On held-out natural executions, it improved success from 61.4% to 68.9% at a harm rate of 2.7%.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CheckVLA framework presents an approach to enhance the robustness of VLA policies in long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Restoration of In-Chunk Feedback:** A primary contribution is the demonstration that a committed action chunk can be treated as a testable prediction of future observations. This paradigm allows for the restoration of a feedback loop during open-loop execution, where previously deviations might have gone unnoticed and propagated. By actively comparing action-conditioned predictions with observed reality, CheckVLA enables timely detection of execution failures.

- **Enhanced Robustness and Reliability:** The ability to detect deviations and initiate latency-aware repairs within an action chunk can significantly improve the robustness and reliability of robotic systems, particularly in contact-rich and long-horizon tasks where compounding errors are a major challenge. The reported improvements in average task success rates in simulation support this potential.

- **Complementary to Policy Scaling:** The findings suggest that execution-time verification is not merely an alternative to developing more capable VLA policies, but rather a complementary mechanism. Even with highly capable open-loop policies, unexpected real-world dynamics can lead to failures. CheckVLA provides a means to mitigate such failures, offering improvements that a stronger open-loop policy alone might not provide. This implies that future advancements in embodied AI could combine sophisticated policy learning with robust execution monitoring.

- **Calibrated Intervention and Adaptive Repair:** The use of functional conformal calibration for triggering interventions offers a statistical guarantee on the episode-level false alarm rate, which is a desirable property for reliable robotic deployment. The risk-adaptive guidance mechanism for suffix rewriting allows the system to respond proportionally to the detected deviation, moving beyond fixed replanning strategies.

- **Insights into Failure Modes:** The "confidently-wrong" quadrant analysis provides valuable insights into scenarios where policy confidence is high but the world model indicates a high risk of failure. This highlights the value of complementary monitoring signals for diagnosing subtle failure conditions.

- **Foundation for Future Safety and Autonomy:** By providing a framework for in-situ verification and adaptive recovery, CheckVLA contributes to the development of more autonomous and safer robotic systems. While the current work is in simulation, it lays groundwork for addressing critical challenges in real-world deployment.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Simulation-bound Results:** Current evidence is confined to the RoboCasa365 simulation environment. Transferability to physical hardware requires further validation.
- **Bounded Guarantees:** The functional conformal calibration primarily controls the probability of an unnecessary *first* intervention on exchangeable nominal-success episodes. It does not guarantee failure recall, post-repair safety, or performance under significant distribution shifts.
- **Dependency on Policy and Latency:** The effectiveness of repair is constrained by the underlying VLA policy's capabilities and the system's inference latency. If too little deployable suffix remains or if no viable replacement action exists, intervention may not be sufficient for recovery.
- **Sensitivity to Environment Changes:** Changes in sensing modalities, hardware timing, or task distributions would necessitate recalibration and retesting of the system.

Future research could focus on dynamic latency-aware scheduling, hardware validation, and extending the theoretical guarantees to cover a broader range of failure scenarios and distribution shifts.
