# Efficient Sim-to-real Transfer of Contact-Rich Manipulation Skills with Online Admittance Residual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces a hybrid offline-online framework for efficiently transferring contact-rich manipulation skills from simulation to real robots. It achieves robust performance and generalization by learning motion and initial compliance parameters in simulation, then employing real-time online adaptation to refine only the critical compliance control parameters using force feedback, demonstrating high success rates across various assembly and pivoting tasks.

## Problem

- Learning contact-rich manipulation skills on real robots is data-inefficient, slow, and carries significant risk of hardware damage.
- The 'sim-to-real gap' critically hinders direct transfer of simulation-trained policies for contact-rich tasks, particularly affecting compliance control parameters.
- Many successful robotic contact tasks still rely on laborious and non-generalizable manual tuning of force control parameters by human experts.

## Method

- An offline phase leverages ==model-free reinforcement learning (SAC)== in simulation with ==domain randomization== to learn desired motion trajectories and initial ==admittance control== parameters.
- An online phase adapts these initial parameters on the real robot by formulating a ==local optimization problem==, computing optimal ==residual admittance parameters== based on real-time force sensor data.
- The online optimization minimizes a combined objective of trajectory smoothness and task completion, incorporating robot dynamics and ==stability constraints== for parameter adjustment.

## Results

- The framework achieved **100%** success for assembly (peg-in-hole) and **90%** for pivoting tasks on a real robot, substantially outperforming direct transfer baselines (near **0%** success) and matching or exceeding manually tuned methods in efficiency.
- It demonstrated strong generalization, achieving **100%** success on polygon-shaped peg-holes and **90%** on tight-fit connectors, and over **70%** success across various pivoting objects with diverse geometries and weights, where manual tuning and direct transfer largely failed.
- Online adaptation dynamically adjusted admittance parameters (e.g., initially decreasing stiffness and increasing damping) to ensure smooth contact, control interaction forces, and facilitate efficient task completion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - While desired motion trajectories can often transfer effectively from simulation, compliance control parameters are far more sensitive to the sim-to-real gap due to discrepancies in contact dynamics.
> - Targeted online adaptation of only the residual compliance parameters, rather than re-learning the entire policy or dynamics model, provides an efficient and effective strategy to bridge this gap.
> - Real-time force feedback can be robustly integrated into an online optimization loop to dynamically adjust robot compliance, enabling stable and robust interactions across varied real-world scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving physical contact, is a critical area of research with broad applications in manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics. Robots performing tasks like assembly, object pivoting, or screwing must interact with their environment in a controlled and compliant manner. This paper addresses a significant challenge in this domain: efficiently transferring manipulation skills learned in simulation to real-world robots.

Historically, learning contact-rich skills has been approached in two main ways:
1. **Learning manipulation trajectories while manually tuning force control parameters:** This approach has shown success, but relies heavily on human expertise, which is time-consuming, specific to each task variation, and not generalizable.
2. **Jointly learning robot motion and force control parameters:** This more advanced approach aims for fully autonomous skill acquisition. However, performing this learning directly on real robots is data-inefficient, slow, and risks damaging the robot or environment. Learning in simulation is safe and efficient, but faces the notorious "sim-to-real gap."

The sim-to-real gap is a major bottleneck in deploying simulation-trained policies to the real world. While kinematic properties (e.g., object shapes, desired trajectories) often transfer relatively well, dynamic properties, especially contact dynamics (stiffness, friction), are notoriously difficult to accurately simulate and are highly sensitive to real-world variations. This sensitivity disproportionately affects compliance control parameters (like those in admittance or impedance control), which are crucial for stable and safe contact.

This paper explicitly positions itself within the context of bridging this sim-to-real gap, specifically for the compliance control parameters. It builds upon various existing strategies for sim-to-real transfer, including system identification, transfer learning, domain randomization, and online adaptation. It also draws from research in learning variable impedance/admittance control, which aims to automate the tuning of these critical parameters.

The key insight that distinguishes this work is the recognition that while desired *motion trajectories* can often be transferred directly from simulation (especially with domain randomization), the *compliance control parameters* are far more susceptible to the sim-to-real gap due to discrepancies in contact dynamics. Therefore, the proposed framework focuses on an **offline learning of motion and initial compliance parameters in simulation**, followed by **online, real-time adaptation of *only the residual compliance control parameters*** using real-world force feedback. This targeted online adaptation directly addresses the most challenging aspect of sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich tasks, offering a practical and efficient solution that avoids full real-world retraining or extensive manual tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient framework for learning contact-rich manipulation skills that can effectively and safely transfer from simulation to the real world, addressing the inherent challenges of real-world data inefficiency and the sim-to-real gap.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations of existing approaches:
- **Manual tuning burden:** Many successful robotic contact tasks still rely on human experts to manually tune force control parameters (e.g., stiffness and damping in admittance/impedance control). This process is laborious, time-consuming, highly skill-dependent, and produces solutions that are rarely generalizable to new task settings or objects.
- **Real-world learning risks:** Directly learning complex contact-rich skills on real robots, even with advanced reinforcement learning (RL) techniques, is often impractical. It requires vast amounts of data, takes considerable time, and carries a significant risk of damage to the robot, the manipulated object, or the environment due to uncontrolled forces or unstable interactions.
- **Sim-to-real gap in dynamics:** While simulation offers a safe and scalable environment for learning, directly transferring policies learned in simulation to real robots often fails, especially for contact-rich tasks. This is primarily because precise simulation of contact dynamics (e.g., friction, stiffness, damping properties of materials) is exceedingly difficult and prone to discrepancies between simulation and reality. These discrepancies critically impact the learned compliance control parameters, making them non-transferable.
- **Need for robustness and generalization:** Robots need to perform tasks not just in controlled, identical environments, but also adapt to variations in object properties (size, shape, weight), clearances, and environmental conditions. Existing methods often struggle with this generalizability without significant re-tuning.

To address these motivations, the authors set out to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. To learn a robust manipulation policy (both motion and initial compliance parameters) in simulation, leveraging domain randomization to enhance transferability.
2. To design an online adaptation mechanism that refines only the critical compliance control parameters in real-time on the physical robot, effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap for contact dynamics.
3. To validate the framework's effectiveness and robustness through comparative experiments on diverse contact-rich tasks (assembly, pivoting, screwing) against direct transfer and manual tuning baselines.
4. To demonstrate the framework's ability to generalize to various task settings with different kinematic and dynamic properties without requiring extensive re-tuning.
5. To ensure safe and stable robot interaction by dynamically optimizing compliance parameters based on real-time force measurements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework is a **hybrid offline-online approach** designed to learn robust contact-rich manipulation skills and achieve efficient sim-to-real transfer. It comprises two distinct phases: skill learning in simulation and online admittance adaptation on the real robot.

#### 3.1. Offline Learning in Simulation

The first phase focuses on learning the foundational manipulation skills in a simulated environment using model-free reinforcement learning (RL).
- **Simulation Environment:** MuJoCo is used, featuring a FANUC LRMate 200iD robot with an F/T sensor.
- **Skill Definition:** The skill $\pi(x_d, P | s)$ generates both the robot's desired trajectory $x_d$ and an initial set of compliance control parameters $P$ (specifically, for an admittance controller) given the current state $s$.
- **Reinforcement Learning Setup:**
 - **Algorithm:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is employed for its effectiveness in continuous control tasks.
 - **Tasks:** Three canonical contact-rich tasks are used:
 - **Assembly (Peg-in-Hole):** Goal is to align and insert a peg into a hole. State includes peg pose/velocity relative to hole and external force. Action includes end-effector velocity and diagonal stiffness parameters of the admittance controller. Reward encourages successful insertion based on position error.
 - **Pivoting:** Goal is to push an object against a wall to a stand-up pose. State includes robot pose and external force. Action includes 2D velocity commands and stiffness parameters. Reward is based on rotational distance to goal orientation.
 - **Screwing:** A more complex task combining assembly and rotation.
 - **Admittance Control Parameters:** The admittance controller defines the robot's dynamic behavior as a mass-spring-damping system ($M(\ddot{x}_c - \ddot{x}_d) + D(\dot{x}_c - \dot{x}_d) + K(x_c - x_d) = F_{ext}$). For simplicity, $M, K, D$ are assumed diagonal. In this offline phase, the RL agent learns diagonal elements of the stiffness matrix $K$, while inertia $M$ is fixed and damping $D$ is critically damped based on $M$ and $K$.
 - **Domain Randomization:** To improve the robustness and transferability of learned skills, Gaussian noise is added to F/T sensor readings, and the robot's initial pose is randomized for both assembly and pivoting tasks. This helps the policy generalize to minor variations between simulation and reality.

#### 3.2. Online Optimization-Based Admittance Learning

The second phase adapts the simulation-learned skills to the real robot by online optimizing the admittance control parameters. The core idea is that the offline-learned *motion trajectory* is largely transferable, but the *compliance parameters* require fine-tuning in the real world due to the sim-to-real gap in contact dynamics.
- **Goal:** Adapt the admittance control parameters (specifically, their residuals) in real-time to maximize robot performance (smoothness, task completion) using real sensor measurements.
- **Optimization Formulation:** Online learning is formulated as a local optimization problem to find the optimal residual admittance parameters $\delta u$.
 - **Optimization Variable:** $u = [m^{-1}_1..., m^{-1}_6, k'_1..., k'_6, d'_1..., d'_6]^T$, representing the diagonal elements of $M^{-1}$, $M^{-1}K$, and $M^{-1}D$. The optimization is over $\delta u$, where $u = u_{init} + \delta u$ and $u_{init}$ comes from the offline learned policy.
 - **Robot Dynamics Constraint:** The admittance control equation (Eq. 1) is converted into a state-space form that relates the robot's error state ($e = x_c - x_d$, $\dot{e}$) to external force $F_{ext}$ and admittance parameters $u$.
 - **Contact Force Estimation:** Explicitly modeling contact force is challenging. The authors propose a "record & replay" strategy: force/torque measurements from the robot's sensor are recorded over a short time window and then replayed during the optimization horizon. This provides a direct, data-driven estimate of the actual forces experienced. (Other methods like hybrid impulse dynamics, analytical contact models, or online force fitting were considered but found less suitable, as detailed in the Appendix).
 - **Stability Constraint:** All admittance parameters (derived from $u$) must remain positive-definite to ensure controller stability.
 - **Objective Function:** A combined objective function $C(x)$ is minimized, comprising:
 - **Trajectory Smoothness (FITAVE-like):** $\int_0^T t|\dot{e}(t)|dt$, which penalizes velocity errors to promote stable and smooth contact.
 - **Task Completion (ITAE-like):** $\int_0^T t|e(t)|dt$, which minimizes position errors to ensure the robot tracks the desired trajectory and completes the task.
 - A weighting scalar $w \in$ balances these two criteria.
- **Online Learning Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
 1. Execute the offline-learned skill on the real robot.
 2. Continuously record force sensor data.
 3. Every $T$ seconds, the recorded force measurements, current robot state, and offline-learned admittance parameters ($u_{init}$) are used in the optimization problem (Eq. 4) to compute $\delta u^*$.
 4. The admittance parameters $M, K, D$ are updated to $u_{init} + \delta u^*$.
 5. This process runs in a closed-loop, iterative manner, allowing real-time adaptation and refinement of the robot's compliant behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments on three contact-rich manipulation tasks: peg-in-hole assembly, object pivoting, and a complex screwing task, evaluating the framework's robustness in sim-to-real transfer and its generalizability to diverse task settings. They compared their **Proposed Method** against two baselines: **Direct Transfer** (directly applying sim-learned motion and parameters) and **Manual Tune** (applying sim-learned motion with human-tuned compliance parameters). Evaluation metrics included success rate, completion time, and maximum contact force.

#### 4.1. Skill Learning in Simulation

- The model-free RL (SAC) successfully learned manipulation skills for assembly and pivoting in MuJoCo, achieving 100% success rates in simulation. This included learning variable admittance policies for searching and insertion in assembly, and for stable pushing and pivoting.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-Real Transfer Performance

- **Direct Transfer Baseline:** This baseline consistently failed to produce safe and stable interactions in the real world. For assembly, the peg bounced excessively, leading to high contact forces and task failure (3/10 success for assembly, 0/10 for pivoting). This starkly demonstrated the critical nature of the sim-to-real gap for compliance parameters.
- **Manual Tune Baseline:** This method achieved a 100% success rate for both assembly and pivoting tasks when human experts carefully tuned the admittance parameters. This finding strongly supports the authors' hypothesis that the *manipulation trajectory* learned in simulation can be directly transferable, provided the *compliance parameters* are correctly set for the real-world dynamics. However, it highlighted the labor-intensive nature of manual tuning.
- **Proposed Method:** The online admittance residual learning framework achieved excellent sim-to-real transfer:
 - **Assembly:** 10/10 (100%) success rate, with a shorter average completion time (19.0s vs 28.1s for Manual Tune) and controlled contact forces (23.6N vs 10.3N for Manual Tune, the proposed approach allows for higher forces at different stages to speed up the process).
 - **Pivoting:** 9/10 (90%) success rate, also with a shorter average completion time (25.6s vs 25.3s for Manual Tune).
- **Dynamic Parameter Adjustment:** Analysis of contact force and admittance parameter profiles (Fig. 2c, d) showed that the proposed method effectively adjusted parameters in real-time. Initially, it decreased stiffness (K) and increased damping (D) to achieve smooth contact and reduce initial large forces, then adjusted them to suitable values for efficient task completion.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Different Task Settings

- **Assembly Tasks (various shapes/clearances):** The proposed method exhibited strong generalization. It achieved 100% success on polygon-shaped peg-holes (triangles, pentagons) and a commendable 90% success on tighter-fitting real-world connectors (Ethernet, waterproof), significantly outperforming baselines. Manual Tune struggled with tight-fit connectors (1/10 and 0/10 success), while Direct Transfer almost universally failed (0/10 or 1/10 success).
- **Pivoting Tasks (various objects/weights):** The proposed approach demonstrated robust generalization across objects with diverse geometries and weights, achieving success rates exceeding 70% for all tested objects (adapter, eraser, Pocky short/long). Manual Tune was only successful for objects very similar to the trained one (eraser) and largely failed for others. Direct Transfer failed entirely.
- **Screwing Task:** The method successfully combined offline-learned assembly skills with a rotational primitive for a more complex screwing task, consistently aligning and securing an M8 bolt and nut (100% success in 5 trials) through continuous online admittance optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by effectively addressing the persistent challenge of sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Bridging a Critical Sim-to-Real Gap:** The paper identifies and successfully tackles the core issue that dynamic interaction parameters (compliance control gains) are most affected by the sim-to-real gap. By focusing online adaptation on these specific parameters, it provides a more targeted and efficient solution than attempting to re-learn entire policies or dynamics models in the real world.
- **Reducing Development Time and Cost:** The framework drastically reduces the need for laborious and often dangerous manual tuning of control parameters by human experts. This translates directly into faster development cycles and lower operational costs for deploying robotic systems in complex, contact-rich applications.
- **Enhancing Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** The ability of robots to adapt their compliant behavior in real-time, based on live force feedback, makes them more robust to uncertainties and variations in the real environment. This is crucial for reliable operation in unstructured or semi-structured settings.
- **Improved Generalizability:** The demonstrated capacity for the online adaptation to generalize to new task settings (different object geometries, clearances, weights) without re-training the base policy significantly extends the applicability of learned skills. This moves robots closer to performing a wider variety of tasks in dynamic environments.
- **Paving the Way for Safer Interactions:** By dynamically adjusting compliance to maintain stable contact and control interaction forces, the method inherently contributes to safer robot operation, reducing the risk of damage to the robot, objects, or nearby humans. This is particularly important for delicate assembly or human-robot collaboration scenarios.
- **Practical Implications for Industry:** The successful application to tasks like assembly and screwing, combined with the acknowledgment of industrial partners (FANUC), suggests strong potential for immediate impact in manufacturing and automation. It offers a pathway to deploy simulation-trained manipulation skills efficiently in real-world factory settings.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as noted by the authors):**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that provide avenues for future research:
1. **Fragile Object Handling:** The initial policy might be sub-optimal, which could be problematic for fragile objects. Future work could explore safer initial policies through safe reinforcement learning or more robust online adaptation from the very first contact.
2. **Pre-Grasped Objects:** The current setup assumes objects are pre-grasped. Integrating grasping into the learning pipeline would make the framework more holistic for real-world scenarios.
3. **Admittance Parameter Scope:** Only diagonal elements of admittance parameters are currently optimized. Expanding to non-diagonal elements could offer more nuanced and potentially better compliant behaviors.
4. **Contact Force Estimation:** The "record & replay" strategy, while effective, is a simplification. Exploring more sophisticated analytical contact models or hybrid dynamics models with online parameter fitting could further enhance performance and robustness, especially for highly dynamic or complex contact scenarios.
5. **Update Frequency:** Increasing the update frequency of online optimization could improve adaptation to rapid changes in contact dynamics.

In conclusion, this paper presents a compelling and practically relevant framework that addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics. By strategically combining offline simulation-based learning with targeted online adaptation of critical dynamic parameters, it offers an efficient, robust, and generalizable solution for deploying contact-rich manipulation skills in the real world, promising a significant positive impact on the development of intelligent robotic systems.
