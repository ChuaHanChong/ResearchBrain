# Self-Play Pretraining with Zero Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Play Pretraining with Zero Data trains two randomly initialized byte-level transformers in tandem: a generator proposes programs for a minimal universal Turing machine, and a learner autoregressively predicts the executed byte outputs. A reinforcement-learning reward based on preconditioned gradient alignment keeps proposed programs at the learner's frontier, giving an adaptive curriculum. Zero-shot loss on natural text, images, audio, speech, music, DNA and code improves as a power law in compute.

## Problem

- Pretraining data is still curated on the model's behalf through corpus construction, mixture design and hand-built synthetic generators, capping progress at human knowledge rather than compute.
- Prior universal-prediction work samples programs from a fixed Solomonoff-style prior, wasting compute because only a tiny subset of programs is useful at any given learner stage.
- Hand-designed synthetic distributions such as probabilistic context-free grammars embed a narrow inductive bias that transfers to language-like data but not reliably to images, audio or music.

## Method

- Generator proposes programs in a ==Brainf*ck-like Turing-complete language== executed with a random input tape; the learner trains by ==next-token prediction== on the resulting raw bytes.
- ==Learning-progress reward==: absolute inner product between the learner's gradient on a program's output and its parameter movement over a growing lookback window, under a ==diagonal AdamW preconditioner==.
- Generator optimized by a ==GRPO==-style policy gradient with KL regularization toward a uniform description-length prior, plus reward-weighted ==expert-iteration== fine-tuning over fresh, mutated and replayed programs.

## Results

- Compute-optimal frontiers fit an asymptotic power law across nine datasets, with exponents of **0.123** on text, **0.145** on CIFAR-10 bytes and **0.260** on 8-bit audio, comparable to natural-data pretraining.
- Sampling the fixed universal prior scales far slower; across **1.64e8** prior programs only arithmetic sequences appear, while self-play discovers Fibonacci, geometric, quadratic and cubic families by round **512**.
- The learner reaches near-**100%** in-context accuracy on reverse-string, stack and associative-recall tasks, and a self-play warm start cuts ESC-50 pretraining from **496M** to **320M** tokens.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adaptive curriculum matters more than hypothesis-space expressiveness: the same universal program space scales slowly when sampled from a fixed prior instead of a learner-tracking generator.
> - Rewarding difficulty alone degenerates into injected noise; reward alignment with what the learner has already been learning to keep proposals on the useful frontier.
> - Natural-data scaling likely conflates universal predictive structure with world-specific contingent facts; compute can supply the former, but contingent knowledge still requires real data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Modern language and multimodal models have generally improved through larger neural networks, greater computational budgets, and exposure to increasingly large collections of human- or machine-curated data. Recent research has also examined synthetic data, including formal languages, generated reasoning traces, procedural images, code, and synthetic transformations of existing corpora. These methods demonstrate that training data need not exactly match the target domain to transfer useful capabilities. However, most synthetic-data approaches specify the generation process in advance or derive synthetic examples from an existing natural-data source.

This paper investigates a more general alternative: allowing the training system to search for its own data-generating processes without using natural data during pretraining. Its conceptual basis combines three research traditions. First, it draws on Solomonoff induction and universal prediction, which consider prediction over the space of all computable processes. Second, it draws on intrinsic motivation and automatic curriculum learning, where an agent is rewarded for discovering tasks that generate learning progress. Third, it relates to recent self-play methods in mathematics, reasoning, coding, and tool use, although those systems often begin with pretrained models or use externally defined task environments.

The paper’s central distinction is between **contingent information** and **universal predictive structure**. Contingent information consists of facts specific to a dataset or world, such as the content of web documents or particular biological sequences. Universal structure includes reusable regularities such as copying, recursion, hierarchical composition, symbolic manipulation, and temporal dependence. The authors hypothesize that self-play over a sufficiently expressive program space can generate the latter, even without access to the former. The resulting model would not know factual information about the world, but it might acquire computational and statistical abilities that support prediction across many domains.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to test whether a learner can improve its zero-shot prediction of natural data when all of its training examples are generated synthetically from programs, with no natural data used for gradient updates. The authors describe this as a tabula rasa experiment intended to isolate the contribution of the self-play procedure.

A second objective is to determine whether an adaptive generator is necessary. A universal programming language provides broad representational capacity, but most programs in such a space are uninformative, repetitive, or computationally difficult without yielding reusable structure. The generator must therefore learn where to allocate search effort. The paper tests whether rewarding programs according to the learning progress they induce produces a more useful curriculum than sampling programs from a fixed universal prior.

A third objective is to examine scaling behavior. Conventional pretraining exhibits approximate power-law relationships between compute, model size, data, and loss. The authors ask whether comparable scaling can arise when additional compute is spent searching for and learning from synthetic programs rather than processing more natural examples.

Finally, the paper evaluates whether self-play produces capabilities beyond lower held-out loss. It tests in-context learning on tasks that are not explicitly provided during training and examines whether the generator discovers recognizable mathematical sequences, such as Fibonacci, geometric, quadratic, and cubic sequences.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Learner–Generator System

The method uses two independently parameterized decoder-only Llama-style transformers with identical architectures. Both models begin from random initialization. The **generator** produces programs, while the **learner** predicts the byte sequences emitted by those programs.

Programs are written in a Brainf*ck-like, Turing-complete language. The instruction set includes operations for moving across a tape, incrementing and decrementing byte-valued cells, looping, reading random input bytes, and emitting output bytes. The authors add ten single-character macro-instructions for common operations. A terminating token marks the end of a program.

Each program is executed on a fixed universal Turing machine. Random input tapes allow one program to define a distribution of possible outputs rather than only a single deterministic sequence. Execution is bounded by finite memory, runtime, and output-length limits, and all generated programs are assigned defined execution behavior, including those with unmatched brackets. The learner receives only the emitted byte sequences, not the programs that generated them.

Both models use byte-level tokenization with a 256-symbol vocabulary. This makes the same learner interface applicable to text, images, audio, speech, DNA, code, melodies, and mathematical data. The experiments use context lengths of up to 4,096 tokens and models below 25 million parameters.

#### 3.2. Self-Play Rounds and Program Pools

At each self-play round, the generator produces a batch of programs. The program pool combines three sources: fresh samples from the current generator, local mutations of promising earlier programs, and replayed programs from previous rounds. Fresh sampling supports broad exploration, mutation refines promising regions, and replay reduces catastrophic forgetting.

Mutation uses a MAP-Elites-style archive organized by program length and dynamic loop depth. This prevents the search from concentrating only on the highest-reward programs and preserves structural diversity. Archived rewards decay over time because a program that was useful earlier may become mastered and cease to provide learning progress.

The learner takes a gradient step using ordinary next-token cross-entropy on the outputs of all programs in the pool. Thus, the learner’s objective is standard autoregressive prediction, while the novelty lies in how the training distribution is generated.

#### 3.3. Learning-Progress Reward

The generator is trained with reinforcement learning. The reward is designed to identify examples that are neither already mastered nor completely unrelated to the learner’s current abilities.

For a program output \(y_i\), the reward is the absolute value of the inner product between the learner’s gradient on that output and the learner’s recent parameter movement. The latter is measured over a lookback window extending from approximately half the current training horizon to the current checkpoint. The inner product is preconditioned using the diagonal AdamW optimizer operator.

This reward favors outputs that induce substantial gradients in directions aligned with the learner’s recent progress. Outputs that are already mastered produce small gradients, while random or unlearnable outputs are less likely to align with the learner’s trajectory. The authors use forward-mode automatic differentiation to compute the alignment without materializing full gradients.

The generator’s reinforcement-learning objective includes a KL penalty toward a fixed uniform program prior. This prior assigns higher probability to shorter programs, analogous to a Solomonoff-style description-length prior. The generator also receives reward-weighted supervised fine-tuning on the program pool. This expert-iteration component helps retain high-value programs and mitigates forgetting. Replay samples use sequence-level importance corrections because they were generated by earlier versions of the policy.

#### 3.4. Evaluation and Scaling Analysis

The authors construct compute-optimal frontiers over model size, training checkpoints, and ensemble size. Effective compute is defined as the product of the number of ensemble members, model parameters, and training tokens. For each dataset, they fit a power law of the form \(L(C)=E+AC^{-\alpha}\), where \(L\) is validation loss in bits per byte.

Evaluation uses held-out datasets across several modalities: DCLM web text, GitHub Python, C source code, Metamath proofs, CIFAR-10 image bytes, raw speech and audio, symbolic melodies from Mutopia, DNA sequences, and mathematical data. Hyperparameters are selected using validation loss on DCLM and DNA, which introduces limited validation-data leakage for model selection, although these datasets are not used for training updates.

The authors compare self-play against two fixed synthetic distributions: programs sampled from a universal prior and random probabilistic context-free grammars. They also conduct reward ablations and evaluate in-context learning on reverse-string, stack, associative-recall, sum, maximum, and minimum tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Zero-Shot Transfer and Scaling

Self-play learners show predictable reductions in zero-shot loss as effective compute increases across text, images, speech, audio, music, code, formal mathematics, and DNA. The fitted compute exponents are broadly comparable to published exponents for direct pretraining on corresponding natural modalities. Reported exponents include approximately 0.123 for DCLM text, 0.066 for CIFAR-10 images, 0.141 for 8-bit audio, 0.249 for symbolic melody, 0.129 for Metamath, 0.116 for C source, and 0.113 for Python. DNA is an exception, with a substantially smaller exponent of approximately 0.01–0.06 in the cited comparison.

These results indicate that self-play compute can produce transferable predictive structure even when the learner never trains on the evaluation distributions. However, the absolute losses remain higher than those obtained through conventional pretraining because self-play does not supply contingent information about the target datasets.

#### 4.2. Importance of Adaptive Curriculum

Sampling from the same universal program space using a fixed Solomonoff-style prior scales substantially more slowly than adaptive self-play. This comparison isolates the contribution of the learned curriculum: access to a universal language is not sufficient, because most of the program space does not efficiently yield useful training examples.

Random PCFG pretraining performs strongly on language-like data, including text and code, but transfers less consistently to images, music, speech, and audio. Self-play is less specialized and performs more broadly across modalities. Reward ablations support the chosen learning-progress signal. Shuffling rewards across programs, using only a one-step parameter change, or replacing the alignment reward with direct loss differences generally worsens performance. A negated reward produces results worse than the standard setup, suggesting that the reward ranking is meaningful.

#### 4.3. Discovery of Mathematical Structure

The generator discovers programs that emit recognizable mathematical sequences. Arithmetic sequences appear early, while Fibonacci, geometric, quadratic, and cubic families are found by approximately round 512 in the reported experiments. In contrast, a uniform-program baseline generated 164 million programs and found arithmetic sequences but no examples from the other four families. Under the baseline’s sampling rate, the expected discovery time for those families exceeded 53,000 rounds.

This finding provides a qualitative indication that the generator is not merely producing difficult or high-entropy outputs. It is locating compact, reusable computational structures that can support learner progress.

#### 4.4. In-Context Learning

Self-play learners develop in-context learning on all six tested tasks. With sufficient demonstrations, performance approaches nearly perfect accuracy on reverse-string, stack, and associative-recall tasks, while mathematical tasks such as sum, maximum, and minimum also improve with examples.

The authors observe interpretable strategy changes during the sum task. The model initially predicts frequent or copied bytes, becomes less confident after unsuccessful strategies, and then begins to infer modular addition, first for lower-order bits and later for higher-order bits. Models trained from the fixed universal prior show little evidence of broad in-context learning, while PCFG models perform strongly mainly on associative recall. This suggests that self-play can induce general procedures rather than only local sequence statistics.

#### 4.5. Pre-Pretraining

An additional experiment uses a self-play checkpoint to initialize ordinary training on natural text, images, or audio. Across all three modalities, the self-play initialization starts with lower loss and reaches comparable validation levels with fewer natural-data tokens. For example, the reported convergence reductions are from 496 million to 320 million tokens for ESC-50 audio and from 588 million to 421 million tokens for CIFAR-10. The authors interpret this as evidence that self-play can function as a form of modality-independent pre-pretraining.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides an experimental framework for studying how much of model capability can arise from exposure to universal, reusable structure rather than domain-specific facts. Its main contribution is not the claim that natural data are unnecessary in practical systems; rather, it separates transferable computational regularities from contingent knowledge and demonstrates that the former can be generated through adaptive search.

If the results extend to larger models, self-play pretraining could become a complement to standard data-based training. It might provide reusable initialization, improve data efficiency, or continue generating useful curricula when additional natural data are costly, redundant, restricted, or unavailable. The method also offers a possible route for studying the origins of in-context learning and algorithmic generalization.

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. The models are small, the context length is fixed at 4,096 tokens, and the program language includes hand-designed macro-instructions. The reward and hyperparameters were selected through preliminary experimentation, and natural validation data influenced model selection. The experiments also do not establish that discovered mathematical sequences causally explain transfer. Most importantly, scaling beyond the reported model sizes and compute ranges remains untested. The paper therefore supports the feasibility of adaptive zero-data pretraining as a research direction, while leaving open whether it can compete with or materially extend natural-data pretraining at contemporary foundation-model scales.
