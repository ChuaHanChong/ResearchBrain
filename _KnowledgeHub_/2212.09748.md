# Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), a new class of diffusion models that replace the conventional U-Net backbone with a transformer architecture. By leveraging the scalability of transformers, DiTs achieve new state-of-the-art Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) scores on class-conditional ImageNet at 256x256 (2.27 FID) and 512x512 (3.04 FID) resolutions while being more compute-efficient.

## Problem

- Most successful image diffusion models rely on the U-Net architecture, which lacks the generalizability and established scaling properties of transformer architectures.
- A lack of clear understanding or empirical evidence for architectural scaling laws in diffusion models concerning computational complexity and sample quality.
- The U-Net backbone used by nearly all diffusion models was originally designed for biomedical image segmentation, an inherited rather than purpose-built architectural choice.

## Method

- Proposing **Diffusion Transformers (DiTs)** by replacing the ==U-Net backbone== with a ==transformer architecture== within a ==latent diffusion model== framework.
- Integrating noise timestep and class label conditioning into the transformer blocks via ==patchify== tokenization, with the ==adaLN-Zero== mechanism identified as the most effective strategy.
- Systematically analyzing scalability by measuring performance improvements with increasing computational budget (==Gflops==) across various model sizes and patch sizes, using ==classifier-free guidance== during sampling.

## Results

- Achieved a state-of-the-art **FID of 2.27** on class-conditional ImageNet 256x256 and **3.04** on 512x512, surpassing prior bests from LDM-4, StyleGAN-XL, and ADM.
- Demonstrated that **DiT-XL/2** achieves higher quality with significantly fewer Gflops during inference compared to pixel-space U-Net models (e.g., **118.6 Gflops vs. 1120 Gflops** for ADM at 256x256).
- Observed consistent training stability across all DiT configurations without the need for learning rate warmup or strong regularization, with a strong **-0.93** negative correlation between Gflops and FID.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The U-Net's inductive bias is not essential for high-performance diffusion models; a general-purpose transformer architecture can achieve superior results.
> - A strong negative correlation (-0.93) exists between a DiT model's forward pass Gflops and its FID score, demonstrating clear scaling laws.
> - Larger DiT models are more compute-efficient than smaller ones, yielding better returns on computational investment in the long run.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is strategically positioned at the confluence of two of the most impactful recent trends in deep learning: the rise of **diffusion models** for high-fidelity image generation and the pervasive success of **transformer architectures** across diverse domains.

**Diffusion Models:** Over the past few years, denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) have emerged as state-of-the-art generative models for image synthesis, often surpassing Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) in sample quality and diversity. Pioneering works like Ho et al. (2020) and Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) demonstrated their capabilities. A striking consistency across almost all successful image-level diffusion models, however, has been their reliance on the **U-Net** architecture as the de-facto backbone. The U-Net, originally designed for biomedical image segmentation, was adapted from prior autoregressive models and conditional GANs, featuring convolutional layers and skip connections.

**Transformer Architectures:** Concurrently, transformers, first introduced in "Attention is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) for natural language processing, have revolutionized nearly every subfield of AI. In computer vision, Vision Transformers (ViTs) (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) demonstrated that transformers could effectively process images by treating them as sequences of patches, exhibiting remarkable scalability properties. Transformers have shown that by simply increasing model size, training compute, and data, performance consistently improves, often surpassing traditional convolutional networks.

**The Gap and Unification:** The central observation of this paper is the architectural divergence: diffusion models, despite their success, clung to U-Nets, while other domains embraced transformers. This work explicitly aims to bridge this gap. The authors hypothesize that the U-Net's inductive bias might *not* be indispensable for diffusion models and that replacing it with a transformer backbone could unlock the scalability and efficiency benefits observed elsewhere. By doing so, the paper contributes to a broader trend of **architecture unification** in deep learning, suggesting that general-purpose, scalable architectures like transformers could serve as universal building blocks across diverse tasks and modalities. The paper specifically leverages the **Latent Diffusion Model (LDM)** framework (Rombach et al., 2022), which trains diffusion models in a compressed latent space rather than directly in pixel space, making them more computationally efficient and thus an ideal playground for architectural exploration. This allows the authors to focus on the diffusion backbone itself, rather than the high computational cost of pixel-space operations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations guiding this research can be summarized as follows:

- **Challenge U-Net Dogma:** To empirically demonstrate that the U-Net architecture's specific inductive bias is not fundamental to the high performance of diffusion models, and that alternative, more general architectures can achieve, or even surpass, current state-of-the-art results.
- **Introduce Diffusion Transformers (DiTs):** To propose and thoroughly investigate a new class of diffusion models, dubbed Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), which replace the conventional U-Net backbone with a transformer architecture, adhering to best practices from Vision Transformers (ViTs).
- **Analyze Scalability through Gflops:** To systematically study the scalability properties of these Diffusion Transformers. A core motivation is to move beyond mere parameter counts and analyze scalability through the lens of forward pass computational complexity, measured in Gflops. The goal is to establish a strong correlation between increased computational budget (Gflops) and improved sample quality (lower FID).
- **Achieve State-of-the-Art Performance:** To push the boundaries of image generation quality by leveraging the scalability of transformers, aiming to achieve new state-of-the-art results on highly competitive benchmarks like class-conditional ImageNet at 256x256 and 512x512 resolutions.
- **Leverage Transformer Benefits for Generative Models:** To enable diffusion models to benefit from the well-established advantages of transformers, such as their inherent scalability, robustness, and potential for efficiency, which have been widely observed in other domains.
- **Promote Architecture Unification:** To contribute to the broader trend of architectural unification in machine learning. By showing that a standardized transformer design can excel in diffusion models, the authors aim to facilitate knowledge transfer, best practices, and cross-domain research, potentially simplifying the deep learning architecture landscape.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) by integrating a transformer architecture as the backbone of a latent diffusion model.

**Framework:**
The core approach is built upon the **Latent Diffusion Model (LDM)** framework. This involves a two-stage process:
1. **Learned Autoencoder:** A pre-trained, off-the-shelf variational autoencoder (VAE) compresses high-resolution images into smaller, more manageable latent representations (e.g., a 256x256x3 image is encoded into a 32x32x4 latent space). This VAE's encoder (E) and decoder (D) are frozen during diffusion model training.
2. **Diffusion Model in Latent Space:** Instead of operating directly on pixels, the DiT model learns the denoising process within this compressed latent space. Once a latent representation is sampled by the DiT, the VAE decoder converts it back into an image. This significantly reduces computational cost compared to pixel-space diffusion models.

**Diffusion Transformer (DiT) Architecture:**
The DiT architecture is designed to be as faithful as possible to standard Vision Transformers (ViTs) to inherit their scaling properties.

- **Input Processing (Patchify):** The input to DiT is the noised spatial latent representation from the VAE (e.g., 32x32x4). A "patchify" layer converts this spatial input into a sequence of non-overlapping tokens. Each token represents a patch from the latent space, which is then linearly embedded into a hidden dimension `d`. Standard frequency-based positional embeddings are added to these tokens. A key hyperparameter explored is the **patch size (p)** (2, 4, 8), where a smaller `p` leads to a longer sequence of tokens and thus higher Gflops.
- **DiT Blocks (Transformer Blocks):** The sequence of tokens is then processed by a stack of `N` transformer blocks. The paper explores four variants for incorporating conditional information (noise timestep `t` and class label `c`):
 - **In-context conditioning:** `t` and `c` are embedded as additional tokens appended to the image token sequence, treated identically to image patches. Simple but less performant.
 - **Cross-attention block:** `t` and `c` form a separate sequence that the image tokens cross-attend to, similar to the original transformer design. Adds more Gflops.
 - **Adaptive Layer Norm (adaLN) block:** This method replaces standard Layer Normalization within the transformer block. Scale (`γ`) and shift (`β`) parameters for normalization are regressed from the sum of `t` and `c` embeddings. This applies the same conditioning function across all tokens and is compute-efficient.
 - **adaLN-Zero block:** An extension of adaLN that also regresses dimension-wise scaling parameters (`α`) applied immediately before residual connections. Crucially, these `α` parameters are zero-initialized, effectively initializing each DiT block as an identity function. This strategy has proven beneficial in ResNets and is adopted here for stability and performance. **This variant was found to be the most effective.**
- **Model Sizes:** The authors explore a range of model sizes (DiT-S, DiT-B, DiT-L, DiT-XL) based on ViT configurations, scaling the number of layers (`N`), hidden dimension (`d`), and attention heads.
- **Decoder:** After the final DiT block, a linear decoder transforms the sequence of processed tokens back into the predicted noise and diagonal covariance, matching the shape of the original spatial latent input.

**Training and Evaluation:**

- **Dataset:** Training is performed on the challenging class-conditional ImageNet dataset at 256x256 and 512x512 resolutions.
- **Optimization:** AdamW optimizer with a constant learning rate of 1e-4, no weight decay, and a batch size of 256. Notably, they did not find learning rate warmup or regularization (common in ViT training) necessary, reporting high training stability.
- **Diffusion Hyperparameters:** Most diffusion hyperparameters (e.g., linear variance schedule, noise prediction target, covariance parameterization) are inherited from ADM (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021).
- **Guidance:** Classifier-free guidance is used during sampling to improve sample quality, where a model trained to predict both conditional and unconditional noise is used to extrapolate the conditional score.
- **Metrics:** Fréchet Inception Distance (FID-50K) is the primary metric for sample quality, along with Inception Score (IS), sFID, Precision, and Recall. FID evaluation follows standard practices, using the ADM TensorFlow suite for consistent comparisons.
- **Compute:** Models are implemented in JAX and trained on TPU-v3 pods, emphasizing the need for significant computational resources for large-scale model training.

The methodological strength lies in its systematic exploration of the DiT design space, rigorous benchmarking, and clear focus on Gflops as a measure of complexity, allowing for direct comparisons of architectural efficiency and scaling behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several compelling findings that strongly support the efficacy and scalability of Diffusion Transformers:

- **Superior Conditioning Mechanism (adaLN-Zero):**
 - Among the four conditioning strategies explored, the **adaLN-Zero block** consistently yielded the best performance (lowest FID).
 - It outperformed both in-context conditioning and cross-attention, despite being more compute-efficient than cross-attention and comparably efficient to vanilla adaLN.
 - The zero-initialization of residual connections within the adaLN-Zero block, which initializes each DiT block as an identity function, was crucial for its superior performance over vanilla adaLN. This highlights the importance of proper initialization for large-scale transformer training.

- **Strong Correlation between Gflops and Sample Quality:**
 - The most significant finding is the **strong negative correlation (-0.93)** between a DiT model's forward pass Gflops (computational complexity) and its FID score.
 - This indicates that increasing the computational budget of the DiT backbone, whether by increasing the transformer's depth/width (model size like S, B, L, XL) or by decreasing the patch size (leading to more input tokens), consistently leads to better sample quality (lower FID).
 - Models with similar Gflops, even if achieved through different combinations of model size and patch size (e.g., DiT-S/2 and DiT-B/4), exhibit similar FID values, emphasizing Gflops as the critical scaling factor.

- **Larger DiT Models are More Compute-Efficient:**
 - When plotting FID against total training compute (Gflops * batch size * training steps), the authors found that **larger DiT models are more compute-efficient**.
 - They achieve a given FID with less total training compute compared to smaller models. This means investing in larger models yields better returns on computational investment in the long run.
 - Furthermore, scaling up sampling compute (e.g., using more DDPM steps) for smaller models *cannot compensate* for a lack of model compute. Larger DiT models with fewer sampling steps still outperform smaller models using significantly more sampling steps.

- **State-of-the-Art Performance on ImageNet:**
 - **ImageNet 256x256:** The largest model, **DiT-XL/2**, trained for 7M steps, achieved a new state-of-the-art FID of **2.27** on the class-conditional ImageNet 256x256 benchmark (with classifier-free guidance at scale 1.5). This surpasses previous bests from LDM-4 (3.60 FID) and even StyleGAN-XL (2.30 FID), marking a significant improvement.
 - **ImageNet 512x512:** DiT-XL/2 also set a new state-of-the-art FID of **3.04** on the class-conditional ImageNet 512x512 benchmark (with classifier-free guidance at scale 1.5), outperforming ADM (3.85 FID).
 - **Compute Efficiency in Comparison:** Despite achieving SOTA results, DiT-XL/2 is remarkably compute-efficient. For example, the 256x256 DiT-XL/2 uses 118.6 Gflops, significantly less than pixel-space U-Nets like ADM (1120 Gflops) or ADM-U (742 Gflops), and comparable to LDM-4 (103.6 Gflops) while achieving better quality. The 512x512 DiT-XL/2 uses 524.6 Gflops, far less than ADM (1983 Gflops) or ADM-U (2813 Gflops) at that resolution.

- **Training Stability:** Contrary to some common practices for training large ViTs, the DiT models trained stably across all configurations without the need for learning rate warmup or strong regularization, indicating the robustness of the architecture in this context.

- **Consistent Scaling Across Metrics:** The benefits of scaling (increasing Gflops) were observed across multiple generative evaluation metrics, including Inception Score, sFID, Precision, and Recall, not just FID.

In essence, the findings validate that transformers are not only viable but superior backbones for diffusion models, demonstrating clear scaling laws where increased computational capacity directly translates to higher generation quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) has profound significance and substantial potential impact on the field of generative AI:

- **Architectural Paradigm Shift for Diffusion Models:** The most immediate impact is challenging the entrenched role of U-Nets as the de-facto backbone for diffusion models. This paper conclusively demonstrates that a more generic, transformer-based architecture can not only match but significantly **outperform** specialized U-Nets on critical benchmarks. This opens up a new design space for diffusion models and encourages researchers to explore beyond convolutional inductive biases.

- **Reinforcement of Architecture Unification:** This work strongly reinforces the broader trend of unifying neural network architectures around the transformer paradigm. By proving that transformers can excel in yet another complex domain (non-autoregressive image generation), it suggests that many domain-specific architectural choices might be less critical than previously thought, and that a single, scalable architecture could be universally applicable. This could lead to shared best practices, tools, and training recipes across different AI research areas, streamlining development and accelerating progress.

- **Clear Scaling Laws for Generative Models:** The systematic analysis of Gflops as a predictor of sample quality provides clear scaling laws for diffusion models. This understanding is invaluable for guiding future research and development, allowing researchers to predict performance gains from increased computational resources and design more efficient experiments. The finding that larger models are more compute-efficient in the long run justifies investing in and training larger DiT models.

- **Setting New State-of-the-Art:** Achieving new state-of-the-art FID scores on highly competitive ImageNet benchmarks (both 256x256 and 512x512) solidifies DiTs as leading generative models. This quantitative superiority will likely drive adoption and further development in the research community.

- **Catalyst for Text-to-Image Models:** The most immediate and practical impact lies in its implications for large-scale text-to-image models. The paper explicitly mentions that "DiT could also be explored as a drop-in backbone for text-to-image models like DALL·E 2 and Stable Diffusion." Given that Stable Diffusion, a widely used and highly influential model, is built upon a Latent Diffusion Model with a U-Net backbone, replacing this backbone with a DiT could lead to substantial improvements in image quality, fidelity, and potentially even training or inference efficiency. (Indeed, subsequent models like Stable Diffusion 3 have integrated transformer-based architectures, vindicating this prediction.) This could democratize access to even higher-quality generative capabilities.

- **Future Research Directions:** The paper lays robust groundwork for future research. It encourages continued scaling of DiTs to even larger models and token counts, exploration of DiT for other modalities or more complex generative tasks (e.g., video generation), and deeper investigation into the theoretical underpinnings of transformer-based diffusion. The intriguing finding about classifier-free guidance on a subset of channels also opens up specific avenues for further study.

In conclusion, "Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers" is a landmark paper that challenges conventional architectural choices in generative AI, demonstrating the power of transformers to unlock unprecedented scalability and performance in diffusion models. Its findings not only advance the state of the art in image generation but also contribute significantly to the broader understanding of scalable architectures in deep learning.
