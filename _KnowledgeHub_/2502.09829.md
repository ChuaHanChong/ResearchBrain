# Efficient Evaluation of Multi-Task Robot Policies With Active Experiment Selection

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a cost-aware active testing framework to efficiently evaluate multi-task robot policies by strategically selecting informative experiments. The system leverages natural language task descriptions to model performance distributions for various policy-task pairs, reducing evaluation costs while accurately estimating mean policy performance.

## Problem

- Evaluating multi-task robot policies is prohibitively expensive due to high costs of physical trials, manual environment resets, and task-switching overhead.
- The evaluation space for numerous policies and diverse tasks grows combinatorially, making exhaustive testing impractical.
- Naive or random sampling for evaluation yields unreliable and incomplete performance assessments, hindering informed decision-making in robotics development.

## Method

- The framework formalizes evaluation as a ==population parameter estimation== problem, modeling robot performance as probability distributions (==Bernoulli== or ==Mixture of Gaussians==).
- A ==surrogate model==, using policy and natural language task embeddings, predicts performance for all policy-task pairs, emphasizing verbs in task descriptions for better similarity capture.
- An iterative, **cost-aware active experiment selection** procedure, based on ==Expected Information Gain (EIG)== and incorporating task-switching costs, efficiently queries the most informative experiments.

## Results

- Language-based task embeddings consistently outperformed random embeddings for performance estimation, confirming their utility in capturing task relationships.
- EIG-based active experiment selection strategies achieved significantly lower **L1 error** in estimating mean policy performance compared to random sampling baselines, demonstrating greater efficiency.
- The framework demonstrated the ability to progressively refine predicted mean performance distributions, moving from initial estimates to a qualitative resemblance of true performance with fewer trials.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Natural language descriptions, particularly verb-centric embeddings, provide a valuable prior for modeling and predicting robot policy performance across different tasks.
> - While learning the full performance distribution (e.g., multimodality, variance) remains challenging with limited data, EIG-based active testing significantly improves the accuracy of mean performance estimation with fewer experiments.
> - Incorporating task-switching costs into the acquisition function guides experiment selection towards more cost-efficient evaluation sequences, especially for datasets with higher setup costs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in robot learning, particularly with large-scale datasets and pre-trained policies, have enabled robots to tackle a diverse array of tasks. However, this increased capability has simultaneously introduced a significant challenge: **evaluation**. Unlike fields such as computer vision (CV) or natural language processing (NLP), where evaluation datasets are static, readily available, and inexpensive to run (e.g., ImageNet, GLUE benchmarks), physical robotics experiments are inherently costly. Each trial is sequential, demands experimenter effort for manual environment resets, and changing tasks often involves re-arranging physical objects or even swapping robots. This makes exhaustive evaluation impractical and inefficient, leading to the core problem this paper addresses.

This work sits at the intersection of **robot learning, active learning/active testing, and efficient experimental design.**
In **robot learning**, the need for robust real-world evaluation is critical due to the persistent "sim-to-real gap," where performance in simulation often does not directly translate to the real world without further adaptation or verification. Existing work in robot evaluation has explored areas like selecting initial conditions, assessing black-box planners, or bounding policy performance. However, these often don't address the combinatorial explosion of evaluating multiple policies across many diverse tasks while minimizing cost.

From an **active learning and active testing** perspective, the paper leverages established principles of querying informative data points to efficiently learn about a system. While active learning typically focuses on selecting training data, active testing extends this to selecting *test* instances to better predict model performance. The novel contribution here is applying these principles to the unique constraints of robotics, specifically incorporating explicit costs associated with different types of experiments (e.g., switching tasks). Most prior active testing work has focused on classification or regression, but this paper explicitly models robot performance as a *distribution* (e.g., Bernoulli for success, mixture of Gaussians for continuous outcomes), acknowledging the high variance inherent in physical robot trials.

By modeling evaluation as a population parameter estimation problem and integrating cost-aware active testing, the authors aim to bridge the gap between the scalability of modern robot policies and the bottleneck of their real-world validation. This positions the work as a crucial step towards developing more systematic, scalable, and practical methodologies for benchmarking and improving robot intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the escalating impracticality of evaluating learned robot control policies. As the number of sophisticated robot policies and the diversity of tasks they can perform grow, the traditional approach of exhaustively testing every policy on every task multiple times becomes prohibitively expensive in terms of time, effort, and manual intervention. Specific pain points include:
- **Sequential and Costly Physical Trials:** Each policy rollout consumes experimenter time and requires manual environment resets.
- **High Task-Switching Costs:** Changing tasks often necessitates re-arranging physical objects, which can be time-consuming and labor-intensive, or even changing the robot setup.
- **Combinatorial Explosion:** The evaluation space grows exponentially with the number of policies and tasks, making exhaustive testing unfeasible for multi-task robot systems.
- **Incomplete and Unreliable Naive Sampling:** Randomly selecting a subset of tasks and policies for evaluation yields unreliable and incomplete results, hindering informed decision-making (e.g., selecting the best policy, tuning hyperparameters).

Given these challenges, the **key objectives** of this work are:

1. **Formalize Multi-Task Robot Policy Evaluation:** To reframe the problem of evaluating diverse robot policies across numerous tasks as a population parameter estimation problem, where the goal is to estimate the underlying performance distribution for every policy-task pair.
2. **Leverage Task Relationships for Performance Prediction:** To identify and exploit latent relationships and similarities between different tasks (e.g., "pick up an apple" being similar to "pick up an orange") to better predict policy performance on unseen or sparsely evaluated tasks. Natural language descriptions are explored as a useful prior for modeling these relationships.
3. **Develop an Active Testing Protocol:** To create an iterative strategy that sequentially selects the most informative experiments to run, thereby maximizing the knowledge gained about the performance landscape while minimizing the number of physical trials.
4. **Incorporate Cost-Aware Sampling Strategies:** To integrate the varying costs associated with different experiments (e.g., execution cost, task-switching cost) into the experiment selection process, ensuring that the chosen trials are not only informative but also cost-efficient.
5. **Accommodate Diverse Performance Outcomes:** To design a framework capable of handling both continuous performance metrics (e.g., reward, task progress) and discrete (binary success/failure) outcomes, by modeling them as appropriate probability distributions.

Ultimately, the overarching motivation is to enable **efficient, systematic, and scalable real-world evaluation** of multi-task robot policies, thereby reducing the burden on experimenters and accelerating the development and deployment of more capable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework tackles the challenge of efficient robot policy evaluation through a two-pronged approach: (1) learning a **surrogate model** to estimate the full distribution of robot performance across all policy-task pairs, and (2) designing **cost-aware active experiment selection strategies** to efficiently sample informative trials.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is formalized as **population parameter estimation** for a grid of $M$ robot policies and $N$ tasks. Each policy $\pi_i$ on task $T_j$ has an unknown "true" performance distribution $P_{ij}$. The goal is to estimate the parameters $\theta_{ij}$ of a model distribution $Q_{ij}$ that approximates $P_{ij}$, given a limited and sequentially growing dataset $D$ of observed outcomes. Performance outcomes $X_{ij}$ can be continuous (e.g., task progress, reward) or binary (success/failure), leading to $Q_{ij}$ being a Mixture of Gaussians or a Bernoulli distribution, respectively.

The framework adopts an **active testing** paradigm. Instead of exhaustively evaluating all $M \times N$ policy-task pairs, experiments are selected iteratively using an acquisition function $a(\pi_i, T_j)$. A critical aspect is **evaluation cost**. Each evaluation incurs a fixed cost $c_{eval}(T_j)$, and switching from task $T_{current}$ to a new task $T_j$ incurs an additional, often higher, switching cost $c_{switch}(T_{current}, T_j)$. The objective is to minimize the total cost while accurately estimating all $\theta_{ij}$.

#### 3.2. Surrogate Model for Performance Estimation

The core of the methodology is a **surrogate model** that predicts the population parameters $\hat{\theta}_{ij}$ for any given policy-task pair. This model is an MLP that takes as input concatenated embeddings of the policy and the task: $\hat{\theta}_{ij} = \text{MLP}(e_{\pi_i}, e_{T_j})$.

- **Policy Representation ($e_{\pi_i}$):** Since the framework is policy-agnostic and does not assume access to policy model weights, policy embeddings are represented as fixed, randomly initialized vectors that serve as unique identifiers for each policy. This assumes no inherent, predefined similarity structure between policies.
- **Task Representation ($e_{T_j}$):** This is a key area of innovation. Natural language descriptions of tasks are leveraged using pre-trained language embeddings (MiniLMv2). To improve task similarity capture, the authors found that simply using the full task description embedding was insufficient, as it often over-focused on nouns. Their solution involves:
 1. Extracting verbs and verb phrases from the natural language task description.
 2. Computing separate language embeddings for the verbs ($e_{verb}^{T_j}$) and the full task description ($e_{task}^{T_j}$).
 3. Combining these with a weighted sum, along with a small noise term to help separate very close embeddings: $e_{T_j} = 0.8 \cdot e_{verb}^{T_j} + 0.2 \cdot e_{task}^{T_j} + 0.1 \cdot N(0,1)$. This emphasizes the action (verb) while retaining overall task context.
- **Population Parameter Estimation:**
 - For **continuous outcomes**, $Q_{ij}$ is modeled as a **Mixture of Gaussians** ($K=2$), capturing common bimodal robot performance (catastrophic failure vs. non-zero performance). The MLP predicts mixing coefficients, means, and standard deviations, trained with mixture density loss.
 - For **binary outcomes** (success/failure), $Q_{ij}$ is a **Bernoulli distribution**. The MLP predicts the success probability $p \in$, trained using cross-entropy loss.
As more experiments are conducted and outcomes are added to $D$, the surrogate model is continuously retrained to refine its predictions.

#### 3.3. Cost-Aware Active Experiment Selection

To select experiments efficiently, the framework uses **Expected Information Gain (EIG)** as the measure of informativeness. EIG quantifies how much an experiment is expected to reduce the predictive uncertainty of the performance distribution for a given policy-task pair. The BALD (Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement) formulation is used, comparing marginal entropy with expected conditional entropy. To estimate EIG without a closed-form solution for mixture models, Monte Carlo dropout (applying dropout at test time) is used to generate multiple samples of predicted parameter distributions.

To integrate cost, a **Cost-aware EIG** acquisition function is defined:
$a_{cost-aware}(\pi_i, T_j, T_{current}) = \frac{I(\pi_i, T_j)}{(\lambda \cdot c_{switch}(T_{current}, T_j)) + 1}$
where $I(\pi_i, T_j)$ is the EIG, $c_{switch}$ is the cost of switching from the current task, and $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter balancing informativeness and cost.

The **Active Experiment Selection Procedure** (Algorithm 1) proceeds as follows:
1. **Initialization:** A cold-start problem is mitigated by performing 3 evaluations for all policies on a single randomly selected task.
2. **Iterative Selection:** In each step, the surrogate model (trained on all current data) is used with MC dropout to compute cost-aware EIG for all untried policy-task pairs.
3. **Exploration-Exploitation:** An epsilon-greedy strategy ($\epsilon=0.1$) is used to either randomly select an experiment (exploration) or choose the one with the maximum cost-aware EIG (exploitation).
4. **Execution and Update:** The selected experiment is executed 3 times, outcomes are added to the dataset, and the surrogate model is retrained. Total cost is updated, including task-switching costs if applicable.

This iterative process ensures that the evaluation effort is directed towards experiments that are most likely to provide significant new information, relative to their cost, thereby building a comprehensive understanding of the robot's capabilities with minimal expense.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate their framework on four offline datasets derived from existing robot learning benchmarks and simulated environments: HAMSTER, OpenVLA, MetaWorld Policies, and MetaWorld Checkpoints. These datasets offer diverse task types, policy variations, and performance metrics (continuous task progress/reward, binary success rates), allowing for a robust assessment of the proposed methods.

#### 4.1. Task and Policy Representation Effectiveness

The study investigated the impact of different task and policy embedding strategies on the surrogate model's ability to estimate performance parameters, measured by the average log likelihood of all outcomes under the predicted distribution.

- **Task Representations:**
 - **Language-based embeddings (Verb and Language) consistently outperformed Random embeddings.** This confirms that natural language descriptions provide a valuable prior for understanding relationships between tasks and predicting policy performance. The Verb-centric weighting often showed a slight improvement over pure Language embeddings, particularly for datasets with more diverse actions (e.g., MetaWorld Checkpoints).
 - **Optimal embeddings** (learned from the full dataset a priori) consistently yielded the best performance, establishing an upper bound. The persistent gap between language-based and optimal embeddings suggests that while language is informative, it doesn't capture all the shared information about task relationships (e.g., visual context, physical properties not explicitly in text).
 - The utility of language-based representations varied with dataset characteristics. HAMSTER, with many object changes for similar actions ("pick up milk," "pick up shrimp"), benefited more distinctly from language embeddings, as these tasks naturally clustered semantically. OpenVLA, with less separable tasks, showed smaller gains.

- **Policy Representations:**
 - Surprisingly, **optimal policy embeddings did not provide significant gains compared to random policy embeddings.** The authors hypothesize this might be due to the relatively small number of policies evaluated (4-11), which limits the ability to learn meaningful relationships, or that the learning process for optimal embeddings heavily relies on task embeddings, overshadowing policy-specific similarities. This indicates that shared information *between policies* is harder to capture or less pronounced than shared information *between tasks* in these settings.

#### 4.2. Cost-Aware Experiment Selection Performance

The active experiment selection strategies were evaluated on two metrics: average log likelihood (assessing how well the model represents the full performance distribution) and L1 error of the mean (assessing the accuracy of mean performance estimates), both plotted against accumulated evaluation cost.

- **Log Likelihood vs. Mean Estimation:**
 - **EIG-based approaches (both policy-task and task-based) showed only marginal or inconsistent improvements in overall log likelihood compared to random sampling baselines.** This suggests that learning the *full underlying probability distribution* (including variance, multimodality) remains challenging, especially in the early, data-sparse stages of active learning.
 - However, when considering the **L1 error of the mean, EIG-based approaches clearly dominated.** They were able to estimate the mean performance of policy-task pairs significantly more accurately and at a lower accumulated cost than random sampling strategies. This is a crucial finding, as estimating the mean success rate or reward is often the primary goal for experimenters.

- **Policy-Task vs. Task-Based Sampling:**
 - **Task-based sampling strategies** (selecting a task and evaluating all policies on it) were generally more cost-efficient for datasets like OpenVLA and HAMSTER, which have fewer policies. This aligns with real-world practices where experimenters often set up a task and then run various policies.
 - **Cost-aware EIG (policy-task sampling)** tended to perform better for MetaWorld datasets, especially when there was a high cost to switch tasks. This is because evaluating 10 policies on a single task (MetaWorld Policies) might not be as informative per unit cost compared to carefully selecting a single policy-task pair that yields high information gain, particularly when there are many policies and the cost of evaluating all of them on one task is high. Policy-task sampling also generally switched tasks more frequently, leading to faster cost accumulation.

- **Learning the Performance Landscape:** Visualizations demonstrated that as more experiments were sampled, the predicted mean performance distributions progressively refined, moving from initial rough estimates to a qualitative resemblance of the true performance landscape, reinforcing the learning capability of the surrogate model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in addressing a critical bottleneck in robot learning: the prohibitively high cost and effort associated with evaluating multi-task robot policies in the real world. By intelligently shifting from exhaustive or naive evaluation to **cost-aware active testing**, the framework offers a path towards more efficient, systematic, and scalable robot policy development.

The key significance and potential impact are:

1. **Reduced Evaluation Costs and Accelerated Development:** The primary impact is the potential to drastically reduce the experimenter time, manual effort, and physical resources required for robot evaluation. This allows researchers and engineers to iterate on robot policies faster, conduct more thorough comparisons between different architectures, and perform more efficient hyperparameter tuning, thereby accelerating the pace of innovation in robotics.
2. **Enabling Systematic Benchmarking:** By providing a structured, cost-efficient methodology, this work moves robotics closer to the standardized, scalable benchmarking practices prevalent in fields like computer vision and natural language processing. This is crucial for fairly comparing different robot learning approaches and tracking progress in the field.
3. **Improved Decision-Making:** By better estimating the mean performance of policies across tasks with fewer experiments, the framework enables experimenters to make more informed decisions about which policies to deploy, which checkpoints are best, or where further development effort is most needed.
4. **Leveraging Natural Language for Robot Intelligence:** The successful integration of natural language embeddings to capture task relationships highlights the growing importance of language as a powerful prior for generalizable robot intelligence. This approach can make robot evaluation more intuitive, as tasks described semantically can directly inform the evaluation strategy.
5. **Handling Real-World Variance:** The explicit modeling of robot performance as probability distributions (Bernoulli, Mixture of Gaussians) acknowledges and embraces the inherent variance and stochasticity of real-world robot operations, moving beyond simplistic point estimates.
6. **Foundation for Future Work:** The paper lays robust groundwork for several exciting future directions, such as developing multi-step look-ahead planning for active experiment selection, exploring more sophisticated policy embeddings (e.g., from policy training data), and integrating hierarchical task relationships. These extensions could further enhance the efficiency and intelligence of robot evaluation.

While the current limitations (e.g., cold-start robustness, naive cost models, basic MLP surrogate) point to areas for further refinement, the proposed framework represents a practical and impactful step towards making robot policy evaluation a less burdensome and more data-efficient process, ultimately fostering the development of more capable and reliable robotic systems.
