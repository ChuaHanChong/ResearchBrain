# Dream.exe: Can Video Generation Models Dream Executable Robot Manipulation?

> [!summary] Summary
> Show Lab researchers at the National University of Singapore developed Dream.exe, a novel benchmark evaluating video generation models based on the physical executability of their generated robot manipulation trajectories in a physics simulator. The framework uncovered a negligible correlation between a video's visual quality and its physical task success, with general-purpose models achieving up to 21.4% binary success on simpler tasks but struggling significantly with complex, multi-stage manipulation.

## Problem

- Existing video generation benchmarks focus primarily on visual quality, temporal consistency, and text-video alignment, failing to assess if generated motions are physically executable by a robot.
- The 'world model hypothesis'—that video generation models implicitly learn physical causality—lacked direct, measurable validation through physical interaction and task success.
- Generated videos, often proposed as scalable behavioral priors for robot learning, lacked a robust evaluation framework to guarantee their physical executability and real-world task success.

## Method

- **Dream.exe** introduces a ==closed-loop evaluation pipeline== that converts generated robot manipulation videos into ==executable robot trajectories== and assesses their success in a ==MuJoCo== physics simulator.
- The benchmark utilizes a task suite of 101 manually curated episodes from ==RoboCasa365==, categorized into three levels of increasing physical and temporal complexity (atomic, multi-object, multi-stage).
- A five-step ==video-to-trajectory pipeline== extracts 3D end-effector and object trajectories from 2D video frames, infers gripper actions, and assembles them into an action stream for robot execution.

## Results

- General-purpose video generators achieved task-level binary success rates of up to **21.4%** on Level 2 multi-object manipulation tasks, demonstrating some physical knowledge acquisition from internet-scale data.
- All evaluated models showed significant performance degradation on Level 3 multi-stage tasks, with most general-purpose models achieving **zero** binary success rate, indicating limitations in long-horizon physical reasoning.
- Robot-specific models (e.g., CosmosPolicy) achieved higher trajectory executability (**E-SR up to 0.75**) but did not consistently outperform general generators in overall task-level success, suggesting potential generalization challenges for domain-specific models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - There is a low correlation (Pearson r ≈ -0.03) between the visual quality and physical plausibility of generated videos and their actual task-level execution success.
> - Current video generation models exhibit significant limitations in maintaining physical coherence over long task horizons, particularly on multi-stage tasks, often failing due to 'phantom grasps' or 'kinematic breakdowns'.
> - Depth estimation within the video-to-trajectory pipeline acts as a critical bottleneck, with task success rates notably improving when using ground-truth depth compared to learned depth.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of video generation models and robot learning, addressing a fundamental question regarding the physical grounding of synthetic video content.

Recent advancements in video generation models, exemplified by systems such as Wan, Kling, Imagen Video, Veo, and Sora, have enabled the synthesis of photorealistic and temporally consistent videos depicting various dynamic scenes, including fluid dynamics, human motion, and object interactions. This visual fluency has prompted a hypothesis within the research community that these large-scale video generation models may be learning implicit "world models," acquiring internal representations of physical causality from the statistical regularities present in internet-scale video data.

This "world model hypothesis" has garnered interest in the field of robot learning. Researchers have proposed leveraging generated videos as a source of scalable behavioral priors, aiming to reduce the reliance on costly physical demonstrations for training robots. Existing approaches in this area include framing planning as video generation (UniPi), synthesizing visual subgoals (SuSIE), distilling visuomotor policies from generated demonstrations (Dreamitate), and fine-tuning video foundation models for visuomotor control (Cosmos Policy, DreamGen, DreamZero, VideoVLA).

However, a gap exists in the evaluation of these video generation models. Standard benchmarks for video generation, such as Eval-Crafter, VBench, and T2V-CompBench, primarily assess models based on visual quality, temporal consistency, and text-video alignment. While these metrics evaluate how natural or aesthetically pleasing a video appears, they do not directly probe whether the implied motions are physically executable or capable of achieving a depicted task in the physical world. For instance, a video showing a robot arm passing through a table would be visually plausible under these metrics, despite being physically impossible.

Some recent work has begun to address this gap by evaluating physical plausibility through visual language models (VLMs) or human raters (VideoPhy, PhyGen-Bench, WorldSimBench, MIND, Kang et al. 2024). These efforts examine whether generated videos adhere to common physical laws or depict plausible phenomena. Despite this progress, none of these existing benchmarks establish a closed-loop connection with a robot controller, meaning they do not test whether a generated trajectory can actually succeed when executed in a physics simulator.

Dream.exe directly addresses this limitation by introducing a framework that evaluates video generation models based on their physical executability. By treating task success in a simulation environment as the primary grounding signal, this work aims to provide a concrete and measurable test for the world model hypothesis, bridging the divide between visual fidelity and physical action.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to directly investigate how effectively video generation models reflect the physical world. Specifically, the authors pose the question: "Can Video Generation Models Dream Executable Robot Manipulation?" This encompasses whether the motions depicted in generated videos, when translated into real-world robot actions, can accomplish the intended manipulation tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from two main areas:
1. **Testing the "World Model" Hypothesis:** A prevalent interpretation within the video generation community is that models trained on internet-scale video data implicitly learn "world models" that encode physical causality. The authors argue that this hypothesis has not been directly tested. Current evaluation metrics for video generation focus on perceptual quality, which can be achieved even with physically impossible motions. Dream.exe is motivated by the need for a principled method to ascertain whether these models' physical knowledge is keeping pace with their visual realism.
2. **Advancing Robot Learning with Generative Priors:** Generated videos are increasingly proposed as scalable behavioral priors that could alleviate the dependence on expensive and time-consuming physical robot demonstrations. For these generative priors to be truly useful in robotics, their outputs must be physically executable and lead to task success. The paper highlights that without a robust evaluation framework for physical executability, the utility of video generation models for robot learning remains uncertain.

The core objective is to establish a clear and unambiguous criterion for evaluating the physical understanding embedded in video generation models: if a model has genuinely internalized the physical laws governing a manipulation task, the trajectory implied by its generated video should result in task success when executed by a robot. Dream.exe is designed to operationalize this criterion at scale, providing a benchmark that offers a diagnostic window into the physical capabilities of video generation models beyond purely visual assessments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Dream.exe framework evaluates video generation models through a multi-stage, closed-loop pipeline that translates generated videos into executable robot trajectories and assesses their success in a physics simulator.

#### 3.1. Task Suite Design

The benchmark utilizes a task suite built upon 101 manually curated episodes from RoboCasa365, a large-scale simulation framework for everyday manipulation tasks. This selection process involved rigorous filtering to ensure:
- **Reproducibility:** Each scenario allows for strict initial state recovery for consistent comparison.
- **Suitability:** Episodes were reviewed for optimal camera viewpoints, clear object visibility, unambiguous object identities, and end-effector motion clarity. Tasks requiring base navigation were excluded.
The tasks are stratified into three levels of increasing physical and temporal complexity:
- **Level 1 (Atomic single-object manipulation):** Involves a single object and a continuous interaction primitive (e.g., pick-and-place, knob rotation). These tasks primarily test geometric consistency and grasp/release timing.
- **Level 2 (Multi-object interaction):** Involves two or more objects with coupled states (e.g., placing an object into a container, stacking). Success requires reasoning about spatial relationships and sequential dependencies.
- **Level 3 (Multi-stage composite tasks):** Decomposes into two or more distinct semantic stages (e.g., opening a drawer then retrieving an object). These tasks assess long-horizon physical coherence and sub-goal sequencing.

#### 3.2. Models Evaluated

The study evaluates eight distinct models across three categories to provide a representative overview of the current video generation landscape:
- **Frontier Closed-Source Generators:** Five commercial image-to-video models—Hailuo 2.3 (MiniMax), Kling 3.0 (Kuaishou), Wan 2.7 (Alibaba), SeedDance 2.0 (ByteDance), and Veo 3.1 (Google DeepMind)—are included as they represent the leading edge in general-purpose video generation quality.
- **Open-Source Generators:** Two open-weight models, Wan 2.2 and LTX 2.3, are evaluated to establish a reproducible baseline. Additionally, two variants of Wan 2.2 (Wan 2.2-LoRA
 2K
 and Wan 2.2-LoRA
 7K
 ) are fine-tuned on RoboCasa365 episodes (outside the test set) to explore the impact of in-domain data.
- **Robot-Specific Policy Model:** Cosmos Policy (NVIDIA), a video generation model specifically trained on robot manipulation data, is included. It is evaluated in two configurations: CosmosPolicy-DefaultCam (using three default camera views) and CosmosPolicy-BenchCam (replacing the primary view with a curated Dream.exe scene camera).

#### 3.3. Evaluation Pipeline

The Dream.exe evaluation proceeds through four main stages:

- **Stage 1: Video Generation:** Given an initial scene image and a natural-language task prompt, each model generates a manipulation video. Short clips are generated for Level 1 and 2 tasks, while longer videos are produced for Level 3 multi-stage tasks. Two instruction variants are used: standard (verbatim from dataset) and enhanced (rephrased by Gemini 3 Pro), with results averaged across both.

- **Stage 2: Visual Quality Evaluation:** Generated videos are assessed for visual stability, physical plausibility, and task adherence using two VLM judges (Gemini 3 Pro and Qwen3-VL-Plus) and human annotators. This track provides a perceptual baseline, comparing how natural and task-aligned the videos appear.

- **Stage 3: Video-to-Trajectory Extraction and Evaluation:** This is a critical five-step pipeline for converting generated videos into executable robot action streams:
 1. **Region Mask Initialization:** Identifies the spatial regions of the end-effector and manipulated object in the first frame using simulator segmentation masks or open-vocabulary detection (Grounding DINO + SAM2).
 2. **2D Point Tracking:** Samples query points within identified regions and tracks them across all video frames using CoTracker, yielding per-frame pixel coordinates and visibility.
 3. **Depth Estimation and 3D Lifting:** A robot-adapted DVD (LoRA-fine-tuned on robot rollout videos) model estimates affine depth, which is then calibrated to metric scale using the initial scene's depth. Valid tracked 2D pixels are lifted to 3D points in the world frame using camera parameters. End-effector pixels are weighted more heavily during depth estimation fine-tuning.
 4. **End-Effector Trajectory Extraction:** A visual center is estimated from the lifted 3D points. This visual center trajectory is then calibrated to the robot's tool-center point (TCP) using an initial state offset. End-effector orientation is estimated via Kabsch alignment of lifted points across frames.
 5. **Gripper-Aware Action Assembly:** The gripper open/close schedule is inferred from the relative motion between the end-effector and the manipulated object. Task-specific priors constrain expected gripper events for each interaction type, and these stage-local schedules are merged into a unified action stream.
 Extracted 3D trajectories are evaluated against ground-truth rollout trajectories using metrics like symmetric Hausdorff Distance (HSD) for shape, Wasserstein-1 distance (DYN) for speed distribution, and Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (NDTW) for temporal alignment.

- **Stage 4: Robot Execution and Evaluation:** The assembled action stream is executed in the MuJoCo physics simulator using the robosuite framework on a Franka Panda robot. The scene is reset to its initial state for each trial. Execution is closed-loop; at defined checkpoints, the robot's current pose is compared to the target pose, and corrective actions are applied if deviations exceed thresholds (5mm position, 0.03 rad rotation), preventing open-loop error accumulation.
 Two levels of execution metrics are reported:
 - **Trajectory Executability:** Measures how well the robot controller can follow the extracted trajectory, including E-SR (checkpoint reachability), nDTW (commanded-vs-executed TCP disagreement), Pos95/Rot95 (95th-percentile position/rotation errors), and Smth (path-normalized executed smoothness).
 - **Task-Level Execution:** Measures whether the robot successfully completes the manipulation task, including SR-B (binary success rate), SR-P (continuous partial-completion score), and sub-goal completion scores (Rel for release quality, Place for target proximity, Art for articulation progress, Core for core sub-goal fraction).
 Reference bounds are established by executing ground-truth simulator rollout videos through the pipeline, both with the learned depth estimator ("Rollout Video") and with ground-truth simulator depth ("Rollout Video w/ GT Depth"), to isolate the limitations of the video-to-execution pipeline itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation across eight models and 101 manipulation tasks yielded several findings regarding the physical executability of video generation models:

#### 4.1. Visual Quality Evaluation

- **VLM Scores (Table 1):** CosmosPolicy-BenchCam generally scored highest on robot-subject stability, which is attributed to its domain-specific training. Veo 3.1 achieved the highest scores for task adherence, while LTX 2.3 led in physical plausibility.
- **Human Evaluation (Table 5):** Among general-purpose generators, Wan 2.7 received the highest stability rating, SeedDance 2.0 the highest physical plausibility, and Kling 3.0 led in task adherence and expected execution result. CosmosPolicy variants scored high on stability and physical plausibility but lower on task adherence, consistent with their tendency to produce robotic motion that does not necessarily complete the specified task.

#### 4.2. Video-to-Trajectory Evaluation

- **Trajectory Similarity (Table 2):** Wan 2.7 demonstrated strong performance, leading or being competitive in end-effector trajectory similarity metrics (HSD, DYN, NDTW). CosmosPolicy-BenchCam, specialized in robot data, led in object trajectory similarity. Notably, general-purpose models like Wan 2.7 and Kling 3.0 showed end-effector trajectory performance comparable to or exceeding CosmosPolicy on several metrics, suggesting that large-scale pretraining on general video data can generate suitable robot trajectories.

#### 4.3. Robot Execution Evaluation

- **Trajectory Executability (Table 3):** CosmosPolicy variants, designed for robot actions, achieved the highest checkpoint executability (E-SR) across difficulty levels, indicating better adherence to commanded trajectories during execution. Overall E-SR for general generators ranged from 0.40 to 0.75, with CosmosPolicy reaching 0.75.
- **Task-Level Execution (Table 4):**
 - **Difficulty Gradient:** A clear difficulty gradient was observed. Level 1 tasks saw CosmosPolicy-BenchCam achieve the highest binary success rate (SR-B) at 20.8%. At Level 2, SeedDance 2.0 and Wan 2.7 led with 21.4% SR-B. Level 3 (multi-stage tasks) proved challenging, with most generation models achieving zero SR-B, and only Kling 3.0 achieving a non-zero 6.2% SR-B. However, non-zero sub-goal scores indicated partial progress for many models.
 - **General vs. Robot-Specific Models:** General-purpose video generators, despite extracting actions via an indirect video-to-trajectory pipeline, achieved task-level SR-B comparable to or even surpassing CosmosPolicy on certain tasks. This outcome suggests stronger generalization across diverse tasks and camera viewpoints for general generators.
 - **Depth Estimation Bottleneck:** The reference rows showed that using ground-truth depth from the simulator ("Rollout Video w/ GT Depth") significantly improved task success compared to using learned depth ("Rollout Video"). This indicates that depth estimation remains a bottleneck in the video-to-execution pipeline, uniformly affecting all general-purpose generators.

#### 4.4. Additional Findings

- **Visual Quality vs. Executability Discrepancy:** The study found a low correlation between visual quality and physical executability. Specifically, the Pearson correlation between VLM-rated physical plausibility (Table 1) and task binary success rate (SR-B) (Table 4) was approximately r = -0.03, indicating a negligible relationship. Models with high visual quality (e.g., LTX 2.3 on physical plausibility, Veo 3.1 on task adherence) often exhibited low execution success, while visually less refined models (e.g., SeedDance 2.0, Kling 3.0) sometimes yielded stronger task-level outcomes. Human evaluation corroborated this lack of correlation.
- **Generative Priors and Long Horizons:** General-purpose models demonstrated non-trivial task success on simpler tasks (e.g., SeedDance 2.0 with 15.1% SR-B at Level 1, and SeedDance 2.0/Wan 2.7 with 21.4% SR-B at Level 2), suggesting that internet-scale data does encode meaningful physical knowledge. However, their performance significantly degraded on Level 3 multi-stage tasks, indicating limitations in maintaining physical coherence over long task horizons.
- **Robot-Specific Training vs. Generalization:** CosmosPolicy, despite its robot-specific training, did not consistently outperform general generators in task success rate, particularly at Level 2. While it achieved higher trajectory executability (E-SR), its sensitivity to camera viewpoint and task domain appeared to limit its generalization compared to models trained on broader internet video.
- **In-Domain Fine-Tuning Limitations:** Fine-tuning Wan 2.2 on in-domain RoboCasa episodes improved the visual appearance of robotic motion and enhanced trajectory similarity metrics but did not lead to significant improvements in task success rates. This suggests that merely learning the visual style of robot manipulation without acquiring underlying physical constraints is insufficient for task success.
- **Failure Modes:** Three recurring failure categories were identified (Figure 3): object levitation (objects floating without physical support), phantom grasp (objects moving with the gripper without clear contact), and kinematic breakdown (robot or object exhibiting physically impossible motions). Phantom grasps and kinematic breakdowns accounted for the majority of execution failures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Dream.exe research introduces a novel framework for evaluating video generation models, significantly impacting the fields of robotics and generative AI.

**Pioneering Evaluation Methodology:** Dream.exe is presented as the first benchmark to directly evaluate video generation models on physical executability, shifting the evaluation paradigm from purely perceptual quality to tangible task success in a simulated physical environment. This closed-loop approach—from video generation to trajectory extraction and robot execution—provides a direct, measurable grounding signal that was previously unavailable.

**Direct Test of the World Model Hypothesis:** The framework offers a concrete and scalable method to test the hypothesis that large-scale video generation models learn implicit world models encoding physical causality. By demonstrating that some generated motions can, to a measurable degree, translate into successful robot behaviors, the work provides empirical support for the existence of such physical knowledge within these models.

**Revealing Discrepancies and Limitations:** A key finding is the low correlation between visual quality metrics and physical executability. This highlights a critical limitation of current visual evaluation benchmarks for applications requiring physical interaction. Dream.exe exposes a dimension of model capability that standard visual evaluations do not capture, providing valuable diagnostic insights into where models succeed and fail in understanding physical laws.

#### 5.1. Implications for Robot Learning

- **Generative Priors Utility:** The measurable execution success of several general-purpose models, even without robot-specific supervision, suggests that internet-scale data indeed encodes meaningful physical priors that can be leveraged for robot learning. This opens avenues for utilizing readily available video data to bootstrap robot skills.
- **Challenges and Future Directions:** The consistent failures on long-horizon, multi-stage tasks and the identified failure modes (e.g., phantom grasps, kinematic breakdowns) pinpoint specific areas where current models lack robust physical understanding. The bottleneck of depth estimation in the video-to-execution pipeline also highlights a crucial area for improvement in lifting 2D video information to 3D executable trajectories. This motivates future research to enhance the physical grounding of generated content and the robustness of video-to-action pipelines.
- **Robot-Specific Training vs. Generalization:** The finding that general generators can sometimes outperform robot-specific models in task success rate (due to better generalization across tasks and camera viewpoints) suggests a potential trade-off between domain-specific precision and broad applicability.

**Open-Sourcing for Community Advancement:** By open-sourcing the Dream.exe benchmark, the authors aim to provide the research community with essential diagnostic tools and a common evaluation platform. This will facilitate systematic investigation into the physical capabilities of future video generation models and accelerate progress at the intersection of generative AI and robot learning. The benchmark is expected to motivate the development of models that prioritize physical executability alongside visual fidelity.
