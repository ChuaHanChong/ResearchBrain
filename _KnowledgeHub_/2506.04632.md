# Risk-Sensitive Agent Compositions

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework is introduced for composing AI agents into an agent graph, specifically optimizing for risk measures like Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) concerning safety, fairness, or privacy violations. The `BucketedVaR` algorithm, a dynamic programming approach, efficiently identifies optimal agent compositions, providing tight VaR and CVaR approximations and demonstrating scalability in compositional reinforcement learning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Complex AI systems composed of multiple specialized agents face challenges in real-world deployment due to "tail behaviors" (low-probability, high-consequence events) that violate non-functional requirements like safety, fairness, and privacy.
- Existing risk-sensitive planning often focuses on cumulative loss over sequences, which is not suitable for violations where the maximum incurred loss along a path is the critical metric.
- The exponential number of possible agent compositions within complex agent graphs makes brute-force risk analysis computationally infeasible.

## Method

- Formalizes agentic workflows as "==agent graphs==" where vertices are states and edges are probabilistic agents, each with a loss function quantifying violations (e.g., safety).
- Defines the composed loss for a path as the maximum of individual agent losses, aligning with critical non-functional requirements, and optimizes for Value-at-Risk (VaR) or Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR).
- Proposes `BucketedVaR`, an efficient dynamic programming algorithm that uses a union bound approximation for VaR and discretizes the total risk budget to identify near-optimal agent compositions.

## Results

- `BucketedVaR` consistently identified the same optimal paths as a computationally expensive baseline algorithm across various compositional reinforcement learning benchmarks.
- The algorithm produced tight approximations for both VaR
	 and CVaR
	, with empirical quantile estimates stabilizing around desired values as sample size (10^4) and number of buckets (100) increased.
- Empirically maintained tight VaR approximations even with up to 40 agents in a path and showed robustness to moderate correlations in agent losses (up to 
	=**0.5**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The problem of minimizing maximal loss for non-functional requirements in agent compositions can be efficiently addressed by approximating VaR using a dynamic programming approach and discretizing the risk budget.
> - A union bound approximation of path-level VaR, derived from individual agent VaR estimations, enables breaking down the problem into subproblems solvable with dynamic programming on the agent graph.
> - Even under assumptions like independence of agent losses, theoretical guarantees for correctness, efficiency, and near-optimality can be established, making the approach scalable and reliable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on complex agentic systems and the critical need for robust risk management in their deployment. Modern AI applications frequently decompose intricate objectives into a series of subtasks, which are then addressed by compositions of specialized AI agents. These agents can range from generalist models like Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to reinforcement learning (RL) or imitation learning policies for control tasks. Such compositional systems have demonstrated utility across domains including software development, information retrieval, scientific discovery, and robot control.

While these systems excel at maximizing task success, their real-world application necessitates a rigorous analysis of risks. The inherent stochasticity of both agents and their environments means that low-probability, high-consequence "tail behaviors" can emerge from agent interactions. This concern aligns with a substantial body of literature on risk-sensitive planning and control, dating back to the 1970s, which advocates for optimizing risk measures of loss or reward distributions rather than solely focusing on expected values. Common risk measures include Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), which account for attributes like variance and tail behavior.

A key distinction of the present work from much of the existing risk-sensitive planning literature (e.g., Ahmadi et al., 2021; Kretínský and Meggendorfer, 2018; Bastani et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023) is its focus. Prior research often considers the *cumulative loss* over a sequence of actions or states, which is appropriate for costs. In contrast, this paper addresses scenarios where the loss quantifies violations of requirements such as safety, fairness, and privacy, for which the *maximum incurred loss* across a sequence of agents is often the more relevant metric.

Furthermore, this research intersects with fields like hierarchical reinforcement learning (Jothimurugan et al., 2021; Dalal et al., 2024), task and motion planning (Kaelbling and Lozano-Pérez, 2011), and automated generation of agentic workflows (Niu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). However, these related areas typically prioritize the overall task success probability and often do not explicitly integrate risk minimization with respect to specific behavioral desiderata (like safety, fairness, or privacy) into their core objectives. This paper aims to bridge this gap by introducing a framework for risk minimization specifically for agent compositions, focusing on tail risks associated with violations rather than cumulative costs or simple success rates.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Motivation

The increasing complexity and autonomy of AI systems, particularly those that orchestrate multiple specialized agents to achieve complex goals, highlight a critical need to move beyond mere task success optimization. In real-world deployment scenarios, it is imperative to ensure that these agentic systems adhere to various non-functional requirements such as safety, fairness, and privacy. Violations of these requirements, even if infrequent, can lead to severe consequences. The inherent stochasticity in both the environment and the behavior of individual AI agents means that analyzing and mitigating "tail behaviors"—low-probability but high-impact events—is essential. This work is motivated by the absence of a comprehensive framework to rigorously analyze and minimize such risks across compositions of AI agents. Specifically, for properties like safety or privacy violations, the critical metric is often the worst-case event within a sequence, rather than an accumulated sum, necessitating a distinct approach to risk quantification.

#### 2.2. Problem Formalization

The paper formalizes agentic workflows using a structure called an "agent graph."
- An **agent graph** is defined as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where:
 - **Vertices** represent intermediate states or stages of a complex task, each associated with an input domain.
 - **Edges** represent individual AI agents, denoted $f_e$. Each agent takes an input from the source vertex's domain and produces a probability distribution over computational traces ($T_e$) and outputs to the target vertex's domain ($X_v$). This allows for modeling stochastic agent behaviors and environmental interactions.
 - **Paths** from a designated source vertex ($s$) to a terminal vertex ($t$) correspond to feasible compositions of agents that collectively achieve the overall long-horizon objective.
- To quantify risk, a **loss function** $L_e : T_e \rightarrow R$ is associated with each agent (edge). This function evaluates the computational trace $t_e$ of an agent $f_e$ and returns a real-valued loss. Crucially, this loss function is designed to quantify violations of requirements like safety, fairness, or privacy, decoupling it from traditional task accuracy or performance metrics.
- For a **composition of agents** along a path $p = e_1 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow e_m$, the **composed loss function** $L_p(t_1, \dots, t_m)$ is defined as the *maximum* of the individual agent losses: $\max_i \{L_{e_i}(t_i)\}$. This choice reflects the idea that a single safety violation or privacy breach, regardless of when it occurs in a sequence, constitutes a failure to meet requirements.
- The work focuses on two established **risk measures**:
 - **Value-at-Risk (VaR$\alpha$)**: Defined as the (1-$\alpha$)-quantile of the loss distribution. Minimizing VaR$\alpha$ means finding an agent composition where, with a probability of at least (1-$\alpha$), the maximum loss incurred along the path does not exceed a certain threshold.
 - **Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR$\alpha$)**: Also known as expected shortfall, it is the expected loss in the worst $\alpha$ fraction of the loss distribution, offering a more sensitive measure of tail risk than VaR.

#### 2.3. Objective

Given an agent graph and a user-defined risk budget $\alpha$, the primary objective is to solve the **Risk Minimization over Agent Graphs (RMAG)** problem. This involves identifying the specific path (i.e., the optimal composition of agents) from the source to the terminal vertex that minimizes the chosen risk measure (VaR$\alpha$ or CVaR$\alpha$) of the composed loss distribution. A significant challenge in achieving this objective is the potentially exponential number of feasible agent compositions within a complex agent graph, rendering a brute-force approach computationally infeasible. The work aims to develop an efficient algorithm that can approximate this optimization problem with theoretical guarantees, assuming only black-box access to the individual agents for sampling their trace-output distributions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Formalization of Agentic Workflows and Risk

The paper formalizes an agent as a function $f: X \rightarrow D(T \times Y)$, mapping an input from domain $X$ to a probability distribution over computational traces $T$ and outputs $Y$. This probabilistic formulation accounts for stochastic agent behaviors and environmental interactions. A loss function $L: T \rightarrow R$ is then applied to the agent's trace to quantify undesirable behaviors (e.g., safety violations, fairness issues), allowing for a separation of risk from task performance.

The **agent graph** $G = (V, E, X, T, F, L, s, t, D_s)$ structure encapsulates the compositional workflow:
- $V$: set of vertices.
- $E$: set of edges forming a directed acyclic graph.
- $X$: maps each vertex to an input domain.
- $T$: maps each edge to a trace set.
- $F$: maps each edge $e=(u,v)$ to an agent $f_e: X_u \rightarrow D(T_e \times X_v)$.
- $L$: maps each edge $e$ to a loss function $L_e: T_e \rightarrow R$.
- $s$: source vertex with initial input distribution $D_s$.
- $t$: terminal vertex representing the overall objective's output domain.

For a path $p = e_1 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow e_m$ from $s$ to $t$, the sequence of agents $(f_{e_1}, \dots, f_{e_m})$ induces a joint distribution $D_p$ over sequences of traces $(t_1, \dots, t_m)$. The **composed loss function** for a path $p$ is defined as $L_p(t_1, \dots, t_m) := \max_i \{L_{e_i}(t_i)\}$. The RMAG objective is then defined as $\arg \min_{p \in P} \rho[L_p(Z_p)]$, where $P$ is the set of all paths from $s$ to $t$, and $\rho$ is either VaR$\alpha$ or CVaR$\alpha$.

#### 3.2. The BucketedVaR Algorithm

To address the exponential complexity of enumerating paths, the paper introduces `BucketedVaR`, an efficient dynamic programming algorithm designed to find a near-optimal path minimizing VaR$\alpha$.

The core idea for estimating VaR$\alpha$ for a path $p$ with individual agent losses $R_i = L_{e_i}(Z_i)$ (where $L_p(Z_p) = \max(R_1, \dots, R_m)$) relies on a union bound approximation:
$\text{VaR}_\alpha[L_p(Z_p)] \le \max_i \{\text{VaR}_{\alpha_i}[R_i]\}$
This approximation allows for breaking down the path-level VaR estimation into individual agent VaR estimations, given an allocation of the total risk budget $\alpha$ among the agents such that $\sum \alpha_i = \alpha$.

The `BucketedVaR` algorithm operates as follows:
1. **Discretized Risk Budgets**: The total risk budget $\alpha$ is divided into a set of discrete buckets $B = \{0, \frac{\alpha}{d}, \dots, \alpha\}$, where $d$ is the number of buckets.
2. **Dynamic Programming Structure**: The algorithm traverses the agent graph in topological order. For each vertex $v$ and each discrete risk budget $\overline{\alpha} \in B$, it computes:
 - `VaR[v, α_bar]`: The minimum estimated VaR for a partial path from $s$ to $v$ that has accumulated a total risk budget of $\overline{\alpha}$.
 - `best_paths[v, α_bar]`: The sequence of vertices constituting this optimal partial path.
 - `best_samples[v, α_bar]`: The samples (outputs) propagated along this optimal partial path, which serve as inputs for subsequent agents.
3. **Inductive Step**: For a given vertex $v$ and accumulated budget $\overline{\alpha}$, the algorithm considers all predecessors $v'$ of $v$ and all possible budget allocations $\alpha' \le \overline{\alpha}$ that lead to $v'$. The remaining budget $(\overline{\alpha} - \alpha')$ is then allocated to the edge $(v', v)$.
 - It samples `n` trace-output pairs from the agent $f_{(v',v)}$ using the output samples from `best_samples[v', α']`.
 - It computes the losses $\{L_{(v',v)}(t_j)\}$ for these samples and estimates the empirical $(1 - (\overline{\alpha} - \alpha'))$-quantile, denoted `edgeVaR`.
 - The path VaR for extending the path to $v$ is calculated as $\max(\text{VaR}[v', \alpha'], \text{edgeVaR})$.
 - The algorithm updates `VaR[v, α_bar]` and `best_paths[v, α_bar]` with the minimum such path VaR found.
4. **Final Output**: After processing all vertices, the algorithm returns `VaR[t, α]` and `best_paths[t, α]`, representing the minimum VaR estimate and the corresponding optimal path from $s$ to $t$.

#### 3.3. CVaR Approximation

The algorithm can also approximate CVaR$\alpha$ as a byproduct. Recognizing that CVaR$\alpha$ is defined as an integral over VaR quantiles (Equation 2), it can be approximated as an average of VaR values:
$\text{CVaR}_\alpha[L_p(Z)] \approx \frac{1}{d} \sum_{k=1}^d \text{VaR}_{\frac{k\alpha}{d}}[L_p(Z)]$
Since `BucketedVaR` already computes VaR estimates for various risk levels (buckets) along paths, these quantities can be averaged to obtain a CVaR estimate. Increasing the sample size $n$ and the number of buckets $d$ improves the accuracy of this CVaR approximation.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Guarantees

The paper provides theoretical results concerning the correctness, efficiency, and near-optimality of `BucketedVaR`:
- **Theorem 1 (Correctness and Efficiency)**:
 - With high probability ($1-\delta$), the estimated VaR $q$ for the returned path $p$ is guaranteed to be greater than or equal to the true $(1 - \alpha - \gamma)$-quantile of the path's loss distribution, where $\gamma$ is an error term that decreases with sample size $n$ and depends on the number of buckets $d$ and vertices $|V|$. This bound is derived using a union bound and the Dvoretzky-Kiefer-Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality for empirical CDF convergence.
 - The time complexity is $O(n(d+1)^2|V|^2)$, showing polynomial scaling with respect to sample size, number of buckets, and number of vertices.
- **Theorem 2 (Near-Optimality under Independence)**:
 - Under the assumption that agent losses along each edge are *independent random variables*, the estimated VaR $q$ (as $n, d \rightarrow \infty$) is bounded: $q \le \text{quantile}(L_{p^*}(Z_{p^*}), 1 - \alpha + \alpha^2/2)$, where $p^*$ is the path optimizing the true RMAG objective. This implies that the path found by `BucketedVaR` is asymptotically suboptimal by an additive factor of $\alpha^2/2$ regarding the quantiles of the optimal path loss. The independence assumption is argued to hold when losses quantify behavioral properties (safety, fairness, privacy) rather than direct task success metrics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Empirical Evaluation Setup

The `BucketedVaR` algorithm was empirically evaluated across a suite of compositional reinforcement learning (RL) benchmarks. These environments feature long-horizon tasks requiring the sequential composition of multiple RL-trained agents. The agent graphs in these benchmarks define vertices as state spaces and edges as control policies that produce distributions over trajectories and final states. Loss functions were specifically designed for each task to capture risk requirements.

- **Safety in Navigation and Manipulation**:
 - **16-Rooms**: Adapted from Jothimurugan et al. (2021), this environment involves a point mass navigating between rooms while avoiding obstacles. Loss functions were defined as the negative of cumulative reward, where reward quantifies successful reach-avoid subtasks (e.g., reaching a target region while maintaining distance from dangerous states).
 - **Fetch**: From Gymnasium-Robotics, this benchmark involves controlling a 7-DoF robotic arm for pick-and-place tasks. Loss functions were similarly derived from reach-avoid specifications.
 - **DroneNav**: An adaptation of the 16-Rooms environment (Example 1 in paper) with varying obstacle sizes, designed to highlight risk differences where success probability might be identical.
- **Fair Resource Consumption**:
 - **BoxRelay**: Implemented using the Miniworld framework, this task requires a player to pick up and drop boxes at different locations while managing limited battery power. Loss functions quantify the number of time steps a box is carried, aiming to minimize resource consumption.

In all experiments, agents were treated as black boxes. VaR$\alpha$ and CVaR$\alpha$ approximations were assessed by comparing the algorithm's output to empirical quantiles/coverages computed on a large number ($10^4$) of fresh samples from the chosen optimal path. An approximation was considered "tight" if its empirical quantile was close to $1-\alpha$.

#### 4.2. Experimental Results

**Experiment 1: Comparison with Optimal Baseline Algorithm**
- `BucketedVaR` consistently identified the *same optimal path* as a computationally expensive baseline algorithm (which explicitly estimates VaR$\alpha$ and CVaR$\alpha$ for every feasible path).
- The algorithm produced *tight approximations* for both VaR$\alpha$ and CVaR$\alpha$ across all benchmarks and risk levels. The absolute percentage difference in empirical quantiles between `BucketedVaR` and the baseline was generally within a few percentage points (Figure 2, Tables 1 & 2).
- It demonstrated the ability to find non-trivial allocations of the risk budget among agents along the chosen path.

**Experiment 2: Increasing Sample Size and Number of Buckets**
- **Varying Sample Size**: With the number of buckets fixed (100) and $\alpha=0.1$, increasing the sample size `n` from 500 to $10^4$ resulted in the empirical quantile estimates quickly stabilizing around the desired $0.90$ (i.e., $1-\alpha$) across benchmarks (Figure 3a). This indicates improved accuracy with more samples.
- **Varying Number of Buckets**: With the sample size fixed ($10^4$) and $\alpha=0.1$, increasing the number of buckets `d` from 5 to 100 also showed a stabilization of empirical quantile estimates. The 16-Rooms benchmark, having longer paths, required a higher number of buckets to yield good VaR estimates (Figure 3b).

**Experiment 3: Scaling Number of Agents**
- To test scalability, artificial long paths (up to 40 agents) were simulated using repeated sequences of agents from the 16-Rooms benchmark, ensuring independent loss assumptions.
- The `BucketedVaR` algorithm maintained tight approximations of VaR$\alpha$ even with a significantly increasing number of agents along the path (Figure 3c), empirically supporting the scalability indicated by the theoretical results under independence.

**Experiment 4: Robustness to Correlated Losses**
- An artificial agent graph with losses sampled from correlated Gaussian distributions was used to investigate the algorithm's performance when the independence assumption is violated.
- While the approximation degraded under complete correlation ($\rho=1$), the algorithm was observed to produce "reasonable approximations" for correlation levels up to $\rho=0.5$ (Figure 4). This suggests a degree of practical robustness to moderate correlations, extending its applicability beyond strictly independent loss scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Contributions and Impact

This research introduces a framework and an efficient algorithm for risk-sensitive agent compositions, addressing a crucial gap in the development of reliable and deployable AI systems. Its primary contributions include:
- **Novel Problem Formalization**: By framing agentic workflows as agent graphs and defining the composed loss as the *maximum* of individual agent losses, the work offers a more appropriate model for quantifying risks related to safety, fairness, and privacy violations, diverging from traditional cumulative loss approaches.
- **Efficient Algorithm for Risk Minimization**: The `BucketedVaR` algorithm provides a polynomial-time solution to the otherwise exponentially complex problem of finding optimal agent compositions that minimize Value-at-Risk. This efficiency is achieved through dynamic programming and a union-bound-based approximation of VaR.
- **Theoretical Guarantees**: The paper provides mathematical proofs for the correctness, efficiency, and asymptotic near-optimality of `BucketedVaR` under independence assumptions. These guarantees strengthen the credibility and applicability of the proposed method.
- **Empirical Validation on Realistic Benchmarks**: Extensive experimental evaluation on compositional reinforcement learning tasks (e.g., safety-critical navigation, fair resource consumption) demonstrates the algorithm's practical effectiveness. It consistently identifies optimal agent compositions and provides tight approximations of VaR and CVaR, confirming its utility in diverse application contexts.
- **Robustness to Correlation**: Empirical findings suggest that the algorithm maintains reasonable performance even with moderate correlation between agent losses, indicating broader applicability in real-world scenarios where perfect independence might not hold.

The potential impact of this work is significant for the safe and responsible deployment of complex AI systems. By enabling the selection of agent compositions that are not only effective but also demonstrably risk-aware concerning critical non-functional properties, it contributes directly to enhancing the trustworthiness and societal acceptance of advanced AI applications in sensitive domains like robotics, autonomous systems, and content generation.

#### 5.2. Limitations

The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Independence Assumption**: The theoretical guarantees for the tightness of VaR estimates rely on the assumption of independent agent losses. Although empirical results suggest robustness to moderate correlations, strict independence may not always hold in practice, potentially affecting the accuracy of the theoretical bounds. The authors argue that this assumption holds when losses are behavioral metrics, orthogonal to task success.
- **Loss Function Definition**: The need for well-defined loss functions can be a practical challenge, especially for complex agents like LLMs, where quantifying violations of fairness or privacy might require expensive human annotations or potentially noisy LLM-as-a-judge methods.
- **Sample Complexity**: Obtaining accurate risk measure estimates can require a high number of samples, which might be computationally intensive or time-consuming in certain environments.

#### 5.3. Future Directions

The authors outline several promising avenues for future research:
- **Integration with Existing Frameworks**: Incorporating the `BucketedVaR` algorithm into current agentic system development frameworks to facilitate its practical adoption.
- **Computational Optimization**: Exploring GPU implementations to parallelize the computation of intermediate VaR estimates, thereby speeding up the algorithm.
- **Extension to Other Risk Measures**: Adapting the framework to approximate other coherent risk measures (e.g., entropic value-at-risk) beyond VaR and CVaR.
- **Online Risk Minimization**: Developing online optimization strategies, such as multi-armed bandit-style algorithms, to minimize long-term regret with respect to the optimal composition. This could potentially reduce sample complexity by enabling the system to converge to the optimal composition more directly, without exhaustively computing full risk measures for all paths.
