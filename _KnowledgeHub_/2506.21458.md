# Spatial Mental Modeling from Limited Views

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce MINDCUBE, a benchmark for evaluating Vision-Language Models' spatial mental modeling from limited views, revealing that current models struggle with inferring hidden information. They demonstrate that supervised fine-tuning combined with reinforcement learning, teaching models to internally generate cognitive maps and reasoning chains, significantly improves spatial reasoning, boosting performance from 47.62% to 70.67% accuracy.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) struggle with spatial reasoning from limited views, exhibiting near-random performance on tasks requiring inferring hidden information or maintaining cross-view consistency.
- Current VLM benchmarks primarily focus on passive perception, failing to adequately evaluate models' ability to build and leverage robust internal "spatial mental models" for dynamic, partially observable environments.
- VLMs lack the ability to effectively synthesize spatial information across sparse viewpoints, reason about objects not directly visible, or perform "what-if" simulations of viewpoint changes, critical for embodied AI.
- There is a need to understand whether cognitive-inspired scaffolding and advanced training paradigms can bridge the gap between current VLM capabilities and human-like spatial understanding.

## Method

- The paper introduces ==MINDCUBE==, a new benchmark with 21,154 questions across 3,268 images, designed to specifically evaluate VLMs' spatial mental modeling from limited views, including reasoning about unseen elements and dynamic viewpoint changes.
- It proposes training VLMs to explicitly generate internal spatial representations, specifically ==2D cognitive maps== and ==free-form reasoning chains==, as a means to foster robust spatial understanding.
- The research employs a ==multi-stage training paradigm==: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to teach VLMs to internalize and generate these spatial representations, followed by reinforcement learning (RL) to refine their spatial reasoning for higher accuracy.
- Different cognitive scaffolding approaches are explored, including view interpolation, direct input of cognitive maps, and Chain-of-Thought prompting, to understand their effects on VLM spatial reasoning.

## Results

- State-of-the-art VLMs performed poorly on the MINDCUBE benchmark, with the best model achieving only **47.62%** accuracy against a ~**32%** chance baseline, confirming a significant spatial reasoning gap.
- Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on joint cognitive map generation and free-form reasoning chains boosted VLM accuracy to **60.76%** (+**22.95%** over baseline), with generated maps achieving **88.79%** overall similarity to ground truth.
- Reinforcement learning (RL) warm-started from the best SFT checkpoint further improved accuracy to **70.67%** (+**9.91%** over best SFT, +**32.86%** over baseline), producing high-quality plain cognitive maps.
- A bottleneck analysis revealed that fine-tuning only the LLM component of the VLM captured nearly all performance gains (**51.43%** vs. **52.28%** for full fine-tuning), whereas fine-tuning only the vision encoder yielded no improvement.
- Providing pre-computed augmented cognitive maps as direct input to frozen VLMs degraded performance, while prompting for free-form reasoning or map generation offered modest gains (e.g., +**3.62%** for Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out prompting).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Large Language Model (LLM) component, not the vision encoder, is the primary bottleneck for spatial reasoning in VLMs, indicating reasoning capabilities are key.
> - Teaching VLMs to explicitly generate and reason over internal cognitive maps, inspired by human spatial models, significantly improves their spatial understanding.
> - Combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for initial learning with reinforcement learning (RL) for refinement proves highly effective, achieving a "1+1 > 2" synergy in developing robust spatial intelligence.
> - Models trained to jointly generate cognitive maps and reasoning chains achieve superior performance in spatial reasoning tasks compared to models trained on raw QA pairs or map generation alone.
> - Simpler, object-only cognitive maps (Plain-CGMap) are more effective for SFT than augmented maps that include camera views, likely due to reduced complexity in learning internal representations.
> - RL significantly refines spatial reasoning when warm-started from a strong SFT checkpoint, boosting accuracy by nearly 10% over the best SFT results, indicating its role in polishing learned behaviors rather than building from scratch.
> - The act of prompting frozen VLMs to generate free-form reasoning or cognitive maps, even imperfectly, improves their QA accuracy, suggesting that explicit internal thought processes aid spatial understanding.
> - The study confirms that state-of-the-art VLMs perform near-randomly on complex spatial tasks requiring inference about unseen elements and cross-view consistency, highlighting a critical gap in their capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in spatial reasoning. Recent advancements in VLMs have enabled significant progress in tasks involving passive perception, such as image captioning and visual question answering (VQA). However, the broader research landscape highlights a recognized gap between current VLM capabilities and human-like spatial cognition, particularly when interacting with partially observable or dynamic environments.

Human cognition naturally involves forming "spatial mental models," which are internal, dynamic representations of unseen space. These models enable individuals to reason about layouts, perspective, motion, and occluded objects from limited observations. Existing literature in spatial cognition (e.g., Johnson-Laird, Tversky) emphasizes the schematic, manipulable, and inferential nature of these internal representations, distinguishing them from metric-precise replicas of the environment.

Current VLMs, despite their ability to process multi-modal information, demonstrate limitations in synthesizing spatial information from fragmented views, maintaining spatial consistency across shifting viewpoints, and performing mental simulations to infer hidden relationships. Prior efforts to enhance VLM spatial understanding have included coordinate-aware prompting, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, explicit spatial representation alignment, and reinforcement learning approaches. However, many existing benchmarks and methods are noted to neglect the "mental-level" spatial reasoning that underpins human cognitive abilities, focusing instead on observable spatial relations. This research addresses this disparity by introducing a benchmark and exploring methods specifically designed to approximate and facilitate the emergence of such internal spatial mental models in VLMs, moving beyond static perception towards dynamic, inferential spatial understanding. The work positions itself as a step towards bridging the gap between machine and human spatial reasoning by emphasizing the construction and utilization of internal structured spatial representations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are as follows:

1. **Expose the Spatial Mental Modeling Gap in VLMs:** The foundational motivation is to systematically identify and quantify the deficiency of current state-of-the-art VLMs in forming coherent spatial mental models. The authors observe that while humans effortlessly infer unseen layouts and objects from limited views, VLMs struggle with synthesizing partial observations and maintaining cross-view consistency.
2. **Develop a Comprehensive Benchmark for Spatial Mental Modeling:** To address the lack of specialized evaluation settings, a key objective is to create a robust benchmark, MI N D CU B E. This benchmark is designed to specifically test critical aspects of spatial mental modeling, including:
 - Reasoning with partial observations where objects are occluded or out of view.
 - Maintaining cross-view consistency across shifting viewpoints.
 - Performing mental simulation to infer hidden spatial relationships and "what-if" movements.
 The benchmark is motivated by the need for fine-grained analysis of VLM performance in dynamic and partially observable spatial scenarios.
3. **Investigate Approaches to Facilitate Spatial Mental Models in VLMs:** Given the identified performance gap, a central objective is to explore and evaluate different cognitive scaffolding approaches that can help VLMs approximate human-like spatial mental models. This includes investigating:
 - **External Data Structures:** Such as providing additional intermediate views (View Interpolation) or explicit structured spatial representations (Cognitive Maps).
 - **Internal Reasoning Mechanisms:** Such as guiding the model to verbalize its thought process through natural language reasoning chains (Free-Form Reasoning) or to generate structured internal representations.
4. **Identify Effective Training Strategies for Spatial Mental Model Emergence:** Beyond simply providing scaffolds, the research aims to determine whether supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) can teach VLMs to autonomously construct and leverage these spatial mental models. This is motivated by the observation that passive input of spatial structures alone may not be sufficient, necessitating a learning process to integrate and utilize such information effectively.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to enable VLMs to move beyond passive perception towards more active and robust spatial reasoning in complex, partially observable environments, mirroring a fundamental human cognitive ability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research comprises three main phases: benchmark creation and initial evaluation, investigation of cognitive scaffolds in frozen VLMs, and training strategies for spatial mental model emergence via SFT and RL.

### 4. MI N D CU B E Benchmark and Evaluation

**Benchmark Design:** The MI N D CU B E benchmark was developed to evaluate VLMs' spatial reasoning under partial observations and dynamic viewpoints. It includes 21,154 questions across 3,268 images, organized into 976 multi-view groups. The images are sourced from Arkitscenes, WildRGB-D, DL3DV-10K, and self-collected data.

**Settings:** The benchmark features three distinct camera movement patterns:
- **Rotation:** The camera remains stationary but rotates, capturing 2 to 4 orthogonal views around a central object. This tests understanding from incremental viewpoint shifts.
- **Around:** The camera rotates around a central object, positioned between it and several surrounding objects. This setting leverages occlusion to assess object permanence and conversion of lateral to depth relationships.
- **Among:** The camera moves around a central object, with each of four orthogonal views showing the central object in front of a different surrounding object. This requires maintaining spatial consistency across views with varying occlusions.

**Taxonomy:** Questions are categorized along four dimensions:
- **Visual Patterns:** Linear or non-linear object arrangements.
- **"What if" Dynamics:** Pure translation, pure rotation, simultaneous translation-rotation, or sequential translation-rotation (text-described).
- **Relation Query:** Agent-Agent (self-positioning), Agent-Object (object orientation relative to agent), or Object-Object (relation between two objects).
- **Perspective Taking:** Self-perspective, Other's Perspective Taking-1 (visibility from another agent's view), or Other's Perspective Taking-2 (spatial relations from another agent's view).

**Dataset Curation:** A pipeline was implemented involving: (1) selecting multi-view image groups matching the taxonomy's movement patterns and spatial criteria, (2) annotating key spatial information (spatial relationships, object grouping, semantic orientation, occlusion levels), and (3) algorithmically generating taxonomy-aligned questions with targeted distractors.

**Initial VLM Evaluation:** A diverse set of 17 state-of-the-art VLMs (open-weight multi-image models, proprietary models, and spatial models) were evaluated on MI N D CU B E. The evaluation revealed that most models performed marginally better than random guessing. For instance, DeepSeek-VL2-Small achieved 47.62% overall, while random chance was 32.35-33.02%. Some models showed category-specific strengths (e.g., GPT-5 on Rotation, Mantis-8B on Around), but no single model excelled universally. This poor performance motivated further investigation into scaffolding strategies.

### 5. Cognitive Scaffolding in Frozen VLMs

To address the VLM limitations, three data structures, inspired by human cognitive science, were investigated as "cognitive scaffolds" for spatial mental models:

1. **View Interpolation:** This technique generates intermediate frames between sparse input views to provide perceptual continuity, approximating mental animation. Configurations included inserting 1 or 2 interpolated frames.
2. **Augmented Cognitive Map:** This is a 2D schematic JSON-like representation of object layouts on a 10x10 grid, encoding both object positions/orientations and camera view positions/orientations. A "Plain Cognitive Map" variant focusing only on object positions was also investigated.
3. **Free-Form Reasoning (FFR):** This involves prompting the VLM to generate a step-by-step natural language Chain-of-Thought (CoT) before providing the answer, verbalizing the mental simulation and inference process.

**Experiment Setup:** Experiments were conducted on Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct using MINDCUBE-TINY (a diagnostic subset of 1,050 questions), without any model retraining. Configurations varied by input structure (e.g., raw views, views + interpolated frames, cognitive map as input) and output format (direct answer, FFR, or cognitive map generation + answer/FFR). Evaluation metrics included QA accuracy and graph metrics for generated cognitive maps (Overall Similarity, Isomorphic Rate, Directional Similarity, Facing Similarity).

### 6. Training for Spatial Mental Model Emergence (SFT and RL)

To teach VLMs to build and leverage spatial mental models, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) were applied.

**SFT Data Curation:**
- **Cognitive Map Generation:** Ground truth cognitive maps (both Augmented and Plain) were programmatically generated from the MI N D CU B E annotations. A script used rules and heuristics based on spatial arrangement settings (rotation, among, around, translation) to assign 2D coordinates and facing directions to objects and camera views within a 10x10 grid, outputting a structured JSON.
- **Free-Form Reasoning Chain Generation:** Grounded reasoning chains were manually constructed for each QA pair, following a three-step principle: (1) initial scene understanding from individual views, (2) cross-view consistency and environment integration (identifying anchor objects), and (3) question-driven spatial inference (mental simulation, perspective-taking, logical deduction). These chains served as supervisory signals for FFR training.

**SFT Task Configurations:** SFT experiments were performed using Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct on 10,000 QA pairs. Configurations included:
- **Raw-QA:** Fine-tuning on raw inputs to produce direct answers.
- **FFR:** Fine-tuning to generate reasoning chains then answers.
- **Aug-CGMap-Out / Plain-CGMap-Out:** Fine-tuning to generate a cognitive map then a direct answer.
- **Aug-CGMap-FFR-Out / Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out:** Fine-tuning to jointly generate a cognitive map and free-form reasoning, followed by an answer ("map-then-reason").
Training involved full-parameter fine-tuning using DeepSpeed with ZeRO Stage 3 optimization, a learning rate of 1e-5, and an effective batch size of 256.

**RL Task Configurations and Reward Design:** The VAGEN framework with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was used for RL. Configurations included training from scratch or warm-starting from the best SFT checkpoint, focusing on FFR-only or CGMap-FFR outputs.
- **Reward Function:** A sparse reward system was used: +1 for structurally valid output (e.g., correct JSON format for map), and +5 for a correct final answer. This encouraged both structural correctness and task accuracy.
- **Initialization:** RL variants were initialized either from scratch (pre-trained VLM) or from the SFT checkpoint that achieved the best performance (Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out SFT model).

**Object Presentation Order Ablation:** The impact of object listing order in cognitive map supervision was investigated (Fixed Spatial Order vs. Randomized Order) for both SFT and RL, to assess reliance on sequential patterns versus genuine spatial understanding.

### 7. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding VLM spatial mental modeling:

#### 7.1. Initial VLM Performance on MI N D CU B E

- **Significant Performance Gap:** State-of-the-art VLMs, both open-weight and proprietary, exhibited limited capabilities in spatial reasoning from limited views. The best model, DeepSeek-VL2-Small, achieved only 47.62% accuracy, performing marginally better than random guessing (32.35-33.02%).
- **Category-Specific Strengths:** No single model consistently excelled across all spatial categories. GPT-5 showed strength in the `Rotation` setting (93.33%), while Mantis-8B (SigLip) performed best in `Around` (50.99%). This indicated a lack of generalized spatial reasoning.
- **Human Performance Benchmark:** Human annotators achieved an average accuracy of 94.55% on the MI N D CU B E-TI N Y subset, highlighting the substantial gap between human and VLM performance.
- **Vision-Centricity:** Text-only evaluations (using brief or dense captions) showed significant performance drops (e.g., RoboBrain-8B dropped 7.83% with brief captions), confirming the benchmark's reliance on visual input.

#### 7.2. Scaffolding Spatial Mental Models in Frozen VLMs

- **Limited Benefit from Perceptual Continuity:** Adding interpolated views (VI-1, VI-2) provided no meaningful performance gain (e.g., Raw-QA: 37.81%, VI-1: 37.90%). This suggested that merely increasing visual density did not inherently improve spatial mental modeling.
- **Ineffectiveness of Passive Map Input:** Providing a pre-computed augmented cognitive map directly as input (Aug-CGMap-In) severely degraded performance (32.00%). VLMs struggled to leverage external structured spatial information without active reasoning engagement.
- **Crucial Role of Explicit Reasoning:** Enabling free-form reasoning (FFR) alone resulted in a substantial boost (40.48% vs. 37.81% for Raw-QA), indicating that the verbalization of thought processes aids spatial inference.
- **Initial Benefit of Map Generation + Reasoning:** Prompting the model to generate a cognitive map before reasoning (Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out) yielded further improvements (41.33%). This suggested that the act of internalizing and structuring the spatial layout, even imperfectly, supported better subsequent reasoning.
- **Low Map Generation Quality:** Despite the syntactic validity of generated maps, their quality in frozen VLMs was low, with very low isomorphism rates (Aug-CGMap-FFR-Out: 0.10%, Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out: 7.43%) and modest overall similarity scores (<50%), indicating limited inherent mapping ability.

#### 7.3. Training for the Emergence of Spatial Mental Models (SFT)

- **SFT Effectiveness:** Supervised fine-tuning significantly improved VLM performance on spatial reasoning. Fine-tuning on Raw-QA alone raised accuracy from 37.81% (frozen) to 52.67%.
- **"Map-Then-Reason" Synergy:** The configuration that jointly trained models to generate a plain cognitive map and then perform free-form reasoning (Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out) achieved the highest SFT performance, reaching 57.81% accuracy (+5.14% over Raw-QA SFT). This demonstrated a synergistic effect between structured spatial representation and natural language inference.
- **Trade-off in Learning Dynamics:** Models trained solely on map generation (e.g., Plain-CGMap-Out) learned the map structure rapidly and accurately but plateaued in QA accuracy. In contrast, the joint "map-then-reason" approach learned map structure more slowly and less perfectly, yet its QA accuracy continued to improve and surpassed all other configurations, suggesting it fostered a functionally effective spatial representation rather than mere structural replication.
- **LLM as Bottleneck:** A bottleneck analysis revealed that fine-tuning the Large Language Model (LLM) component was almost exclusively responsible for performance gains, while fine-tuning only the vision encoder showed no improvement. This indicated that the core challenge lay in the reasoning and integration of spatial information rather than raw visual perception.

#### 7.4. Reinforcement Learning Refinement

- **RL from Scratch Limitations:** Training RL from scratch with sparse rewards provided insufficient guidance, yielding only marginal gains (RL-FFR: 49.52%, RL-Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out: 50.86%). The models struggled to learn meaningful geometry in generated maps from scratch, with low isomorphism rates (e.g., 6.67%).
- **RL Builds on SFT Foundation:** RL proved most effective when warm-started from an optimal SFT checkpoint. The RL-Plain-CGMap-FFR-Out (from SFT) configuration achieved the highest overall accuracy of 61.33% (+3.52% over the best SFT model, +23.5% over the frozen baseline). This indicated that RL primarily refines and optimizes strong priors learned during SFT.
- **Consistent Advantage of Structured Output:** Across RL settings, configurations incorporating cognitive map generation alongside reasoning consistently outperformed FFR-only approaches, reinforcing the benefit of structured spatial scaffolding.

#### 7.5. Effect of Object Presentation Order

- **Consistency Across Orders:** The relative performance ranking of configurations remained consistent whether objects were listed in fixed spatial order or randomized order.
- **Randomized Order for Genuine Understanding:** Randomized order in map supervision led to lower map reconstruction quality (e.g., 45.52% vs. 89.05% isomorphism for Plain-CGMap-Out) but was adopted as the primary setting. This was because it forced the model to construct spatial understanding from scratch for each example, without relying on ordering shortcuts, thus better reflecting genuine internal representation abilities.

### 8. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research carry several significances and potential impacts for the development of Vision-Language Models and spatial AI:

1. **Advancing Spatial AI Capabilities:** The introduction of the MI N D CU B E benchmark addresses a critical need for rigorous evaluation of spatial mental modeling in VLMs. It highlights that current models, despite their progress in other domains, still lack fundamental human-like abilities to reason about unseen spaces and dynamic viewpoints. The benchmark provides a standardized tool for future research to measure progress in this area.
2. **Validation of Internal Representations:** The research empirically demonstrates that explicitly scaffolding internal spatial representations, specifically through the "map-then-reason" approach, significantly improves VLM performance on complex spatial reasoning tasks. This suggests that for VLMs to achieve more robust spatial intelligence, they may need to actively construct and utilize internal structured spatial models, rather than relying solely on direct perceptual processing or linguistic inference.
3. **Guidance for VLM Architecture and Training:** The results indicate that the language model component of VLMs is a primary bottleneck for spatial understanding, rather than the vision encoder. This insight can guide future architectural designs and fine-tuning strategies, suggesting a focus on enhancing the LLM's ability to integrate, transform, and reason over spatial information. The success of the two-stage SFT and RL approach further points to effective training paradigms that build foundational task understanding before refining spatial reasoning through outcome-driven feedback.
4. **Inspiration from Cognitive Science:** By drawing inspiration from human spatial cognition theories (e.g., mental models, cognitive maps), the research offers a pathway to imbue AI systems with more human-like reasoning capabilities. The "map-then-reason" paradigm is an approximation of how humans might mentally represent and manipulate spatial information, offering a biologically plausible direction for AI development.
5. **Potential for Embodied AI and Robotics:** Improved spatial mental modeling in VLMs has direct implications for embodied AI agents and robotics. Agents operating in partially observable real-world environments (e.g., navigation, manipulation, human-robot interaction) require robust internal spatial representations to plan, predict, and adapt. This work contributes towards developing AI systems that can form a consistent understanding of their surroundings, even when objects are occluded or viewpoints change, thereby enhancing their autonomy and effectiveness in dynamic settings.
6. **Development of Interpretable AI:** The use of explicit cognitive maps and free-form reasoning chains as intermediate outputs can contribute to more interpretable AI systems. By externalizing the model's "spatial thoughts," researchers can better understand how VLMs arrive at their conclusions, facilitating debugging and trust in autonomous systems.

In summary, this research provides a benchmark that reveals a core limitation of VLMs in spatial mental modeling and proposes a "map-then-reason" scaffolding and training methodology that significantly improves their ability to reason about unobservable space, offering foundational steps towards more intelligent and human-aligned spatial AI.
