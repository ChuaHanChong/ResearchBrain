# Robot Parkour Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford, CMU, Tsinghua, and Shanghai Qi Zhi developed a system that enables low-cost quadrupedal robots to autonomously perform diverse parkour maneuvers like climbing and leaping using a single end-to-end vision-based policy. The approach leverages a two-stage reinforcement learning method with soft dynamics constraints for efficient skill acquisition and a distillation process for unifying behaviors, achieving high success rates on real-world Unitree A1 and Go1 robots.

## Problem

- Achieving diverse, vision-based, and generalizable parkour skills on low-cost legged robots using learning-based methods remained a significant challenge.
- Existing learning-based methods were often either diverse but blind to the environment, or vision-based but specialized for a single task, lacking the ability to perform varied, perception-driven parkour maneuvers.
- Traditional reinforcement learning struggled with the exploration challenges of complex parkour environments, and crafting intricate reward functions for diverse behaviors was labor-intensive.

## Method

- A ==two-stage reinforcement learning== approach trains individual specialized parkour skills (climb, leap, crawl, tilt, run) using a simple reward function. The first stage uses ==soft dynamics constraints== with penetrable obstacles and a penetration penalty to aid exploration, while the second stage fine-tunes with ==hard dynamics constraints== for realistic physics.
- A single, unified 'parkour policy' is learned through ==distillation==, where a ==vision-based student policy== imitates the actions of privileged-information 'teacher' policies (the specialized skills) in a diverse simulation environment.
- Robust ==sim-to-real transfer== is achieved through ==visual domain adaptation== techniques for depth images, handling latency between perception and action, and implementing motor safety measures to protect low-cost hardware.

## Results

- The system enabled Unitree A1 robots to climb obstacles up to **0.40m** (**1.53x** robot height) with **80%** success and leap over gaps as large as **0.60m** (**1.5x** robot length) with **80%** success in indoor experiments.
- Qualitative findings included the emergence of re-trying behaviors, where the robot autonomously repositioned itself for another attempt if it failed to clear an obstacle.
- The learned policy demonstrated robust sim-to-real transfer and generalization, successfully performing parkour maneuvers on two different low-cost robots (Unitree A1 and Go1) in diverse outdoor environments, including navigating stairs, gaps, and slippery terrain.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision is crucial for agile parkour skills, as policies without visual input failed to perform complex maneuvers like climbing and leaping.
> - A two-stage reinforcement learning approach with initially 'porous' obstacles and a soft penetration penalty significantly accelerates learning and enables robots to master challenging skills that are otherwise difficult to explore.
> - A single, end-to-end vision-based policy can be distilled from multiple specialized skill policies, allowing robots to autonomously select and execute diverse parkour behaviors based solely on raw visual input.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot parkour, the ability of robots to swiftly and autonomously overcome diverse obstacles in complex environments, represents a grand challenge in legged locomotion. This paper positions itself at the forefront of this challenge, specifically addressing the gap in achieving **diverse, vision-based, and generalizable parkour skills on low-cost legged robots using learning-based methods.**

Historically, agile locomotion in robots, exemplified by Boston Dynamics' Atlas, has relied on massive engineering efforts, precise modeling, and high-cost hardware. While these model-based control approaches have achieved impressive feats, their high complexity and prohibitive cost limit reproducibility and widespread adoption.

The emergence of learning-based methods, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) combined with sim-to-real transfer, has revolutionized robot locomotion by demonstrating robust performance on various tasks like walking, climbing stairs, and mimicking animals. However, the authors identify several limitations in the existing learning-based landscape concerning parkour:

- **Diversity vs. Vision:** Previous RL methods tend to be either "diverse but blind" (e.g., learning various gaits without perceiving the environment) or "vision-based but specialized" (e.g., a policy specifically for stair climbing, but not generalizable to other obstacles). Autonomous parkour demands policies that are both vision-based to perceive varied scenarios and diverse to execute different maneuvers.
- **Complex Reward Functions:** Learning diverse behaviors often requires meticulously crafted and complex reward functions, where each new behavior might necessitate manual tuning of hyperparameters, hindering scalability. Motion imitation from animal data can circumvent this, but often lacks egocentric vision data and sufficient diversity for complex parkour.
- **Challenging Obstacles for Low-Cost Robots:** Small, low-cost robots face unique challenges in overcoming obstacles that are proportionally much larger relative to their size, pushing their physical capabilities to the limit.
- **Perception-Action Coupling at High Speed:** High-speed locomotion introduces significant visual perception challenges, including dynamic blur and communication delays, which can lead to large perception discrepancies and hinder effective agile movement.
- **Hardware Safety:** Agile parkour inherently stresses robot hardware, requiring proactive measures to prevent motor damage, a factor often overlooked in purely simulation-based research.

This paper directly tackles these challenges by proposing a system that learns a *single, end-to-end, vision-based parkour policy* capable of diverse skills using a *simple reward function*, without relying on reference motion data. By demonstrating this on *low-cost quadrupedal robots* (Unitree A1 and Go1), the authors aim to democratize advanced robotic agility, shifting the paradigm from expensive, custom-engineered solutions to accessible, learning-driven ones. This work contributes to the broader fields of visuomotor control, sim-to-real transfer, and agile robot locomotion, pushing the boundaries of what low-cost platforms can achieve autonomously in complex, unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a robust and generalizable system that enables low-cost quadrupedal robots to perform diverse parkour skills autonomously in complex real-world environments. This is broken down into several key aims:

- **Learning Diverse Parkour Skills:** The primary objective is to enable robots to master a wide repertoire of parkour maneuvers, specifically running, climbing over high obstacles, leaping over large gaps, crawling beneath low barriers, and squeezing through thin slits.
- **End-to-End Vision-Based Policy:** The goal is to train a single, unified policy that directly maps raw onboard depth sensing and proprioception to joint commands, allowing the robot to perceive its environment and select/execute the appropriate skill in real-time, without relying on privileged information or external state estimation.
- **Simplified Reward Structure:** To overcome the limitations of complex, manually tuned reward functions, the authors aim to achieve diverse skills using a simple, unified reward function that primarily encourages forward movement and energy conservation.
- **Overcoming Exploration Challenges in RL:** Parkour environments present significant exploration difficulties for standard RL algorithms. A key objective is to develop a method that facilitates efficient learning even for highly challenging maneuvers and obstacle dimensions.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Given that training occurs in simulation, a crucial objective is to ensure that the learned policies transfer seamlessly and robustly to real-world robots, addressing discrepancies in physics, perception, and hardware.
- **Deployment on Low-Cost Hardware:** A strong motivation is to demonstrate these advanced capabilities on readily available, cost-effective quadrupedal robots (Unitree A1/Go1), making sophisticated robot agility more accessible.

The motivation stems from the "grand challenge" of robot parkour, mirroring the athletic intelligence of humans and animals. While high-end robots like Atlas have shown impressive parkour, their cost and engineering complexity are prohibitive. The authors are driven to achieve similar levels of agility on low-cost platforms through learning, democratizing access to such capabilities and pushing the boundaries of autonomous robot performance in unstructured, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed "Robot Parkour Learning System" employs a multi-stage reinforcement learning and distillation approach to achieve diverse, vision-based parkour skills.

#### 3.1. Parkour Skills Learning via Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning (RL)

The core innovation for learning individual skills is a two-stage RL method inspired by direct collocation, designed to overcome difficult exploration problems. Each specialized skill (climb, leap, crawl, tilt, run) is trained separately.

- **Policy Architecture:** Each specialized skill policy is parameterized as a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) neural network. Inputs include proprioception (robot's state), last action, and crucial *privileged information* about the environment: distance to obstacle, obstacle height/width, and a one-hot category of the obstacle type. Physics properties (terrain friction, CoM, motor strength) are randomized to aid domain adaptation. The policy outputs target joint positions.
- **Simple Reward Function:** A single, general skill reward `r_skill` is used across all specialized skills, designed to encourage natural, energy-efficient movement:
 - `r_forward`: Penalizes deviation from target forward velocity and lateral velocity, and encourages straight movement (low yaw angular velocity).
 - `r_energy`: Penalizes motor power consumption (`|τ_j * ˙q_j|^2`), encouraging energy-efficient gaits.
 - `r_alive`: A constant bonus for staying upright.
 - This simple structure avoids tedious manual tuning for each behavior.

- **RL Pre-training with Soft Dynamics Constraints:** This is the first stage, addressing the challenge of exploring difficult environments.
 - **Porous Obstacles:** Obstacles are initially set to be "penetrable," allowing the robot to pass through them. This prevents the robot from getting stuck in local minima early in training by repeatedly colliding with impenetrable surfaces.
 - **Penetration Reward (`r_penetrate`):** A penalty term `r_penetrate` is introduced, which is inversely proportional to the volume and depth of penetration into obstacles. This gradually discourages penetration, effectively acting as a soft constraint that becomes harder as training progresses. More collision points are sampled around critical motor-containing body parts (hips, shoulders) to prioritize their safety.
 - **Automatic Curriculum:** An adaptive curriculum adjusts obstacle difficulty based on the robot's performance (specifically, its penetration reward). If the robot performs well (low penetration), obstacle difficulty (e.g., climb height, gap length) increases; if it struggles, difficulty decreases. This allows for progressive learning from easier to harder scenarios.
 - **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to train with the combined reward `r_skill + r_penetrate`.

- **RL Fine-tuning with Hard Dynamics Constraints:** Once pre-training converges, the second stage begins.
 - **Realistic Physics:** All dynamics constraints become "hard," meaning obstacles are no longer penetrable, reflecting real-world physics.
 - **Refined Behavior:** The pre-trained policies are fine-tuned using only the `r_skill` reward, allowing them to optimize for agile movement under realistic physical constraints.
 - *Note:* The running skill, trained without obstacles, directly uses hard dynamics constraints from the start.

#### 3.2. Learning a Single Parkour Policy by Distillation

The specialized policies use privileged information unavailable in the real world. To create a deployable, unified policy, distillation is used:

- **Teacher-Student Setup (DAgger):** The five specialized skill policies (`π_climb`, `π_leap`, `π_crawl`, `π_tilt`, `π_run`) act as "teachers." A single "student" policy, `π_parkour`, is trained to imitate their actions.
- **Vision-Based Student Policy:** `π_parkour` is a GRU-based network whose inputs are only onboard sensing (proprioception, last action, and a latent embedding of the *raw egocentric depth image* processed by a small CNN). It does not receive privileged information.
- **Distillation Process:** In a simulation environment featuring diverse obstacles, the `π_parkour` explores. At each state, based on the known obstacle type (ground truth in sim), the corresponding specialized "teacher" policy provides the optimal action. The `π_parkour` is then trained to minimize the divergence (binary cross-entropy loss) between its predicted action and the teacher's action. This trains the student to autonomously select and execute skills based solely on visual perception.

#### 3.3. Sim-to-Real and Deployment

Robust transfer to real robots is critical:

- **Visual Domain Adaptation:** To bridge the visual gap between simulated and real depth images (48x64 resolution, 10Hz refresh rate), pre-processing techniques are applied:
 - **Simulation:** Depth clipping, Gaussian noise, and random artifacts are added to rendered depth images.
 - **Real World:** Depth clipping, hole-filling, spatial smoothing, and temporal smoothing are applied to raw RealSense D435 depth images.
- **Latency Handling:** The robot operates at 50Hz, but depth images refresh at 10Hz. The policy asynchronously fetches the latest visual embedding. Proprioception and visual delays are randomized during simulation training.
- **Motor Safety:** To prevent damage to the low-cost robot's motors, a torque limit (25Nm) is enforced by clipping target joint positions from the PD controller output.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates compelling results in both simulation and real-world experiments, showcasing the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed parkour learning system.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Vision is Crucial:** Comparisons with a "Blind" policy (zeroed depth images) showed its inability to perform climbing, leaping, or tilting, and only 13% success for crawling. This unequivocally confirms that vision is essential for agile parkour skills that require perceiving obstacle properties.
- **RL Pre-training with Soft Dynamics Constraints is Effective:**
 - The "Oracles w/ Soft Dyn" (our two-stage method for specialized skills) achieved significantly faster learning progress and higher final success rates (around 95%) compared to "Oracles w/o Soft Dyn" (direct hard constraints) and "RND" (exploration baseline).
 - Crucially, "Oracles w/o Soft Dyn" and "RND" failed to make any learning progress on the most difficult skills (climbing and leaping), whereas "Oracles w/ Soft Dyn" successfully mastered them. This validates the importance of the soft dynamics constraints and automatic curriculum for efficient exploration in challenging environments.
- **Recurrent Networks Enable Memory:** The GRU-based "Ours (parkour policy)" significantly outperformed the "MLP" baseline, particularly in climbing and leaping. This highlights that memory (provided by the GRU) is vital for skills where visual information needs to be maintained and integrated over time, such as controlling rear legs during climbing when the obstacle is no longer in direct view of the front camera.
- **Distillation is Effective for Sim-to-Real (and Generalization):**
 - The final "Ours (parkour policy)" achieved high success rates across all diverse skills in simulation (e.g., 86% climb, 80% leap, 100% crawl, 73% tilt, 100% run) using only onboard sensing, demonstrating successful distillation from privileged-information teachers.
 - The "No Distill" ablation, which attempts to train a vision-based policy directly without privileged information or distillation, failed to learn climbing, leaping, and tilting, underscoring the complexity of training directly from raw visual observations.
 - The "RMA" baseline, while performing well on a single trained skill (tilting), could not distill multiple specialized policies into one general parkour policy, further highlighting the advantage of the DAgger-based distillation approach for skill integration.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

- **Emergent Re-trying Behaviors:** A notable qualitative finding was the emergence of re-trying behaviors. If the robot failed to clear a high obstacle on the first attempt, it would autonomously push itself back to gain adequate run-up space for subsequent attempts, demonstrating adaptive and intelligent behavior not explicitly programmed.
- **Quantitative Indoor Performance:** On the Unitree A1 robot, the parkour policy achieved impressive success rates under varying difficulties, outperforming blind policies and even teleoperated MPC controllers (A1 special mode).
 - Climbing obstacles as high as 0.40m (1.53x robot height) with 80% success.
 - Leaping over gaps as large as 0.60m (1.5x robot length) with 80% success.
 - Crawling beneath barriers as low as 0.2m (0.76x robot height) with 90% success.
 - Squeezing through thin slits of 0.28m (less than robot width) by tilting.
- **Outdoor Generalization:** The system successfully deployed on two different low-cost robots (A1 and Go1) and demonstrated robust performance in challenging outdoor environments. This included:
 - Leaping over disconnected stone stools with 0.6m gaps.
 - Continuously climbing multiple 0.42m high stairs.
 - Crawling beneath a camping cart.
 - Handling slippery grass terrain.
 This demonstrates the strong sim-to-real transfer and the generalization capabilities of the learned policy to varied unstructured terrains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the field of agile robot locomotion and reinforcement learning, with several key contributions and potential impacts:

- **Democratization of Advanced Robot Agility:** By enabling low-cost quadrupedal robots to perform complex parkour maneuvers that were previously the domain of highly expensive, custom-engineered platforms, this work significantly lowers the barrier to entry for advanced robotic capabilities. This could accelerate research and development, allowing more institutions and individuals to experiment with agile robots.
- **Novel Two-Stage RL for Challenging Exploration:** The proposed RL pre-training with soft dynamics constraints and an automatic curriculum is a powerful methodological contribution. It offers a generalizable strategy for efficiently learning highly dynamic and difficult skills in simulation, particularly where direct exploration with hard constraints would lead to persistent local minima or sparse rewards. This approach has implications for various robotic learning tasks beyond locomotion.
- **Unified Vision-Based Skill Learning:** Developing a *single*, end-to-end, vision-based policy capable of autonomously selecting and executing a diverse range of parkour skills from raw depth images is a major achievement. This moves beyond specialized controllers for individual skills, enabling more intelligent and adaptive robot behavior in unstructured environments.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The meticulous attention to sim-to-real gaps, including visual domain adaptation, latency calibration, and motor safety measures, is crucial for practical robot deployment. The successful real-world demonstrations on two different low-cost platforms validate the robustness of the entire system.
- **Emergent Intelligent Behaviors:** The observation of emergent re-trying behaviors during climbing highlights the power of reinforcement learning to derive sophisticated strategies from simple rewards, adding a layer of adaptive intelligence to robot autonomy.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The stated commitment to providing an "open-source system for robot parkour learning" will be invaluable to the research community, offering a platform for further development, benchmarking, and replication of agile locomotion policies.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**

The authors acknowledge a key limitation: the reliance on *manually constructed simulation environments*. This means that the system's ability to learn new skills is constrained by the need to manually design and integrate new obstacle types and appearances into the simulation. This reduces the autonomy of skill acquisition.

Future work aims to address this by:
- **Automatic Environment Construction:** Leveraging recent advances in 3D vision and graphics to automatically generate diverse simulation environments from large-scale real-world data. This could unlock truly autonomous skill learning without manual human intervention for new scenarios.
- **RGB-based Learning:** Investigating the use of RGB images (potentially combined with semantic information) instead of solely depth images. RGB provides richer semantic context which could enable more sophisticated perception and decision-making for complex environments and objects.

Overall, this research pushes the boundaries of autonomous robot agility, making advanced parkour capabilities more accessible and showcasing innovative learning methodologies that pave the way for more general-purpose, intelligent robots capable of navigating and interacting with the complex real world.
