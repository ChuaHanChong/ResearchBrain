# In-Context Iterative Policy Improvement for Dynamic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> In-Context Policy Improvement (ICPI) leverages large language models' in-context learning to iteratively refine robotic policies for dynamic manipulation tasks, demonstrating robust performance across diverse simulated and real-world scenarios. The method achieves lower final policy costs and faster convergence in low-data regimes compared to traditional baselines and in-weights reasoning approaches, particularly on a real-world rolling task.

## Problem

- Dynamic manipulation tasks, such as throwing or swinging, are challenging due to high-dimensional state/action spaces, complex non-linear dynamics, and partial observability of physical properties.
- Traditional methods for dynamic manipulation often require extensive data collection, complex system identification, or task-specific learning algorithms, leading to data inefficiency and limited generalization.
- Existing applications of Large Language Models (LLMs) in robotics primarily rely on 'in-weights' reasoning for high-level planning, which is often insufficient for capturing low-level physical dynamics.

## Method

- The **In-Context Policy Improvement (ICPI)** method frames policy adjustment as a sequence-to-sequence completion task, allowing pre-trained LLMs to act as a ==policy improvement operator==.
- Robotic policy parameters and task outcomes (represented as relative errors) are tokenized and fed into the LLM's prompt alongside a small set of ==K-Nearest Neighbor== examples from a pre-curated dataset.
- The LLM predicts the required change in policy parameters (∆θ) for the next iteration, enabling an ==iterative refinement== process without weight updates or extensive data collection for new tasks.

## Results

- ICPI consistently achieved lower final policy costs and faster convergence across **five** diverse dynamic manipulation tasks (e.g., sliding, rope swinging), both in simulation and on a physical robot.
- On a real-world roll-to-goal task, ICPI yielded a mean cost of **17.107**, outperforming Linear KNN-20 (**33.824**), demonstrating its effectiveness in a low-data regime (approximately **300** examples).
- Performance scales with LLM size, with newer and larger models (gpt-4o) showing superior results compared to older or smaller models (gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4o-mini).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs' in-context learning capabilities can be effectively applied to complex dynamic manipulation, demonstrating their ability to act as "general pattern machines" beyond their original training domains.
> - Using a relative error representation for task outcomes significantly improves LLM performance by highlighting the task-relevant error signal, rather than requiring the LLM to infer it from raw state information.
> - ICPI, by leveraging in-context learning, substantially outperforms 'in-weights' reasoning for dynamic tasks, indicating that explicit pre-trained physics knowledge in LLMs is less critical than their ability to learn from provided examples.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the exciting and rapidly evolving intersection of Large Language Models (LLMs) and robotics, specifically addressing the challenging domain of dynamic manipulation.

The broader research landscape for LLMs in robotics has seen a surge of activity following the remarkable advancements in transformer-based architectures and their emergent abilities. Many recent works, as referenced in the paper, primarily leverage the "in-weights" reasoning capabilities of LLMs. These methods exploit the vast amount of knowledge and reasoning ability encoded within the LLM's parameters (learned during pre-training on internet-scale data) to perform high-level planning, generate robot control code, or derive reward functions for tasks like pick-and-place or object retrieval. These applications often involve processing textual or visual inputs and mapping them to high-level symbolic actions or instructions.

However, this paper distinguishes itself by focusing on a less explored, but equally powerful, emergent ability of LLMs: **in-context learning (ICL)**. ICL allows LLMs to perform few-shot learning by inferring patterns from input-output examples provided directly within the prompt, without requiring any updates to the model's weights. The paper highlights that this ability has been shown to generalize to patterns outside the LLM's original training domain, suggesting LLMs can act as "general pattern machines." This opens up opportunities for LLMs to assist in tasks where their pre-trained "in-weights" knowledge might be insufficient or irrelevant.

The specific application domain chosen—**dynamic manipulation**—is another critical aspect that positions this work. Unlike quasi-static tasks (where objects move slowly and inertia is negligible), dynamic manipulation involves complex, often non-linear, interactions with objects, exploiting their momentum and elasticity. Examples include throwing, swinging, or rolling objects. These tasks introduce significant challenges:
1. **Increased Dimensionality:** Deformable objects or complex multi-joint movements lead to high-dimensional state and action spaces.
2. **Complex Dynamics:** Frictional contacts, impacts, and unknown physical properties (e.g., mass, friction, stiffness) make accurate modeling difficult.
3. **Partial Observability:** Key physical parameters are often not directly observable through standard sensors like cameras.

Traditionally, addressing dynamic manipulation requires extensive data collection, complex system identification, and task-specific learning algorithms (e.g., Reinforcement Learning, data-driven trajectory optimization), often leading to single-task solutions that are not easily generalized. This research seeks to overcome these data-efficiency and generalization challenges by leveraging the pattern-matching capabilities of LLMs through in-context learning, offering a novel paradigm for iterative policy improvement. It directly contrasts with previous data-heavy approaches and the limitations of LLMs' in-weights reasoning for low-level physical dynamics. The approach can also be seen as a form of "algorithm distillation," where the LLM learns to mimic the improvement steps of a more laborious, explicit optimization process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **investigate and demonstrate the utility of in-context learning (ICL) with pre-trained Large Language Models (LLMs) for iterative policy improvement in dynamic manipulation tasks.** The authors aim to enable robots to iteratively refine their actions in dynamic environments in a data-efficient manner, akin to how humans learn from trial and error.

This core objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Exploiting LLMs' General Pattern Recognition:** The emergent ability of LLMs to perform few-shot in-context learning for general patterns, even outside their original training domain, provides an intriguing avenue to apply them to novel and challenging domains like robotics. The researchers are motivated by the proposition that large-scale language pre-training can enable few-shot learning in these new domains.

2. **Addressing Limitations of In-Weights Reasoning for Dynamics:** While LLMs excel at high-level reasoning based on knowledge embedded in their weights (in-weights learning), the paper posits that the low-level physical dynamics inherent in dynamic manipulation are unlikely to be well-reflected or explicitly present in the textual and visual data used for LLM pre-training. This motivates exploring alternative LLM mechanisms that do not rely on pre-trained physics knowledge.

3. **Tackling Challenges of Dynamic Manipulation:** Dynamic manipulation tasks inherently pose significant challenges such as increased dimensionality, complex and often unobservable system dynamics (e.g., unknown object mass, friction coefficients), and the difficulty of acquiring large datasets for learning. Traditional methods often require extensive data or complex model-based approaches. The authors are motivated to find a more sample-efficient and adaptable solution.

4. **Enabling Iterative Learning for Robots:** Drawing inspiration from human learning, where iterative refinement is common for mastering dynamic skills, the research aims to instill a similar ability in robots. By formulating policy improvement as an iterative process, the robot can observe the outcome of an action and adjust its approach for subsequent attempts, leading to task success.

5. **Achieving Data Efficiency:** Current state-of-the-art methods for dynamic manipulation often require massive datasets (e.g., millions of examples). A key motivation is to develop a method that performs well in the "low data regime" by leveraging the few-shot learning capabilities of LLMs, thereby reducing the burden of data collection in real-world robotic applications.

In essence, the research seeks to bridge the gap between powerful language models and complex physical robotics tasks by re-framing policy optimization as an in-context pattern completion problem, thereby offering a novel, data-efficient, and generalizable approach to dynamic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **In-Context Policy Improvement (ICPI)**, formulates the problem of learning to adjust parametric policies for dynamic manipulation tasks as a sequence-to-sequence completion task leveraging the in-context learning capabilities of pre-trained LLMs.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The overarching goal is to find the optimal policy parameters θ* that minimize a task-specific cost function C_τ for a given task instance τ, where the task execution is governed by a parametric policy π_θ. The authors address this by iteratively improving policy parameters.

#### 3.2. Policy Improvement Operator

The core of ICPI is the concept of a policy improvement operator, f. This operator takes the current best policy parameterization θ_i and the resulting state trajectory s_i_1:T (or a representation thereof) and outputs a change to the policy parameters, ∆θ_i. The improved policy for the next iteration is then θ_i+1 = θ_i + ∆θ_i.

Instead of training a dedicated neural network for this operator, the authors propose to use a pre-trained LLM. The LLM is provided with a small dataset D of policy improvement examples, d_j = (θ_j, s_j_1:T, ∆θ_j), directly in its prompt. For a new query (θ_i, s_i_1:T), the LLM is then asked to predict the corresponding ∆θ_i, effectively "fitting" the operator in-context during a single forward pass.

#### 3.3. Tokenization

To interface with the LLM, robotic information must be converted into text tokens:
- **Policy Parameters (θ and ∆θ):** These numeric values are directly tokenized as characters.
- **State Trajectory (s_1:T):** Instead of feeding the raw trajectory, a task-specific encoding of *relative error* (e_i) is used. For goal-reaching tasks (slide, roll), e_i is the difference between the final state and the goal (s_T - τ_g). For tasks like rope swing (where the goal is to pass through a location), it's the minimum distance to the goal during the trajectory. This "relative error" representation is chosen because it abstracts away task-specific physical properties (like object mass) and consistently indicates the direction of necessary correction (e.g., "undershooting the goal" always means "increase force"). This error value is then tokenized as characters.

#### 3.4. Dataset Example Selection

To manage the LLM's context window and focus on relevant examples, a subset of the dataset D is dynamically selected for each query. A K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) approach is used:
1. A k-D Tree is built from the dataset D, using a concatenated vector [θ_D_j, e_D_j] (normalized for comparable scales) for each example.
2. For a given query [θ_i, e_i], the k (set to 20 in experiments) most similar examples from D are retrieved using the KNN lookup.
3. These k examples are then provided in the LLM prompt, ordered by decreasing distance to the query, preceding the current query.

#### 3.5. Dataset Construction

The dataset D of policy improvement labels is crucial for the in-context learning. For each task instance τ:
- An "optimal" policy parameter θ* is first determined. For simulated tasks, this is achieved via a brute-force search. For the real-world goal-conditioned task, a "hindsight relabeling" technique is used, where goal labels are assigned based on the final state of a guiding execution.
- Then, various alternative θ parameterizations are sampled, and the corresponding improvement label ∆θ = θ* - θ is computed. This process is viewed as a form of "algorithm distillation," where the LLM learns to reproduce the iterative improvements that would otherwise require computationally intensive methods.

#### 3.6. Task Setups

The method is evaluated on five dynamic manipulation tasks, encompassing both simulation and a physical robot, using parametric policies:
1. **Slide Task (simulated):** A robot strikes a puck to a goal, varying puck radius and surface friction. Policy parameters control strike angle, distance, and time.
2. **Slide Goal-Conditioned (simulated):** Similar to slide, but goal location varies while puck properties are fixed.
3. **Rope Swing Task (simulated):** A robot swings a rope attached to a rod to pass through a goal, varying rod and rope lengths. Policy parameters control angular velocity and joint motions.
4. **Rope Swing Goal-Conditioned (simulated):** Similar to rope swing, but goal location varies while rod/rope lengths are fixed.
5. **Real Roll Goal-Conditioned (physical robot):** A MELFA-Assista robot strikes a billiard cue ball to a sampled goal pixel location on a table. Uses similar policy parameterization to slide-gc.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that In-Context Policy Improvement (ICPI) is a robust and data-efficient method for dynamic manipulation tasks, particularly in scenarios with limited training data.

#### 4.1. Iterative Policy Improvement Performance

ICPI was evaluated against several baselines, focusing on the low-data regime (dataset D of approximately 300 examples), a key strength of the in-context learning approach. The baselines included:
- **Random Shooting:** Randomly sampling policy adjustments.
- **Bayesian Optimization:** Directly optimizing the policy parameters.
- **KNN-k:** Averaging policy adjustments from k nearest neighbors in the dataset.
- **Linear KNN-k:** Fitting a linear model to k nearest neighbors to predict adjustments.
- **In-Context Sequence-Improvement (ICSI):** An alternative LLM-based in-context method using cost conditioning.
- **In-Weights Reasoning (IW):** Prompting the LLM to reason about the task and generate a policy based on its pre-trained knowledge.

**Key Findings:**
- **Superior Performance in Low-Data Regime:** ICPI consistently achieved lower final policy costs across most tasks compared to all baselines (Table 1), indicating its effectiveness in learning from a small number of examples. This was particularly evident on the real-robot task (`roll-gc-real`), where ICPI achieved a mean cost of 17.107, significantly outperforming Linear KNN-20 (33.824).
- **Consistent Robustness:** ICPI was the *only* method that performed well across all five diverse tasks, both in simulation and on the physical robot, highlighting its generalizability and robustness.
- **Faster Convergence:** Convergence plots (Fig. 3) showed that ICPI typically converged to a better policy faster than random shooting and the piece-wise linear approach (Lin. KNN-20), even on the real robot.
- **In-Context vs. In-Weights Efficacy:** ICPI (utilizing in-context learning) significantly outperformed the In-Weights Reasoning baseline. This is a crucial finding, suggesting that while LLMs may lack explicit physics knowledge in their weights for dynamic tasks, their ability to learn patterns from in-context examples is highly valuable.
- **Improved over General ICL:** ICPI also outperformed ICSI, which uses a more generic in-context sequence improvement technique, affirming the effectiveness of the proposed policy improvement formulation.

#### 4.2. Design Ablations

The study also investigated the impact of design choices on ICPI's performance:
- **Tokenization Strategy:** Providing the LLM with the *relative error* (e_i = s_i_t - τ_g) directly as input outperformed providing the raw state (s_i_t) and goal (τ_g) separately, even though both contain the same information. This suggests that the LLM benefits from feature engineering that highlights the task-relevant error signal, rather than needing to infer it from raw inputs.
- **Choice of LLM:** The performance of ICPI varied significantly with the choice of LLM. Newer and larger models (gpt-4o) consistently outperformed older (gpt-3.5-turbo) and smaller (gpt-4o-mini) models. This indicates that the advanced reasoning and pattern recognition capabilities of state-of-the-art LLMs are crucial for the success of ICPI.

Qualitative results (Figs. 1 and 4) visually illustrate the iterative improvement process, showing how policies become more accurate over iterations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant contribution to the fields of robotics and artificial intelligence, particularly in bridging the gap between large language models and complex physical interaction.

1. **Pioneering In-Context Learning for Dynamic Manipulation:** The most profound impact is demonstrating that in-context learning (ICL) from pre-trained LLMs can be effectively applied to challenging dynamic manipulation tasks. This is a novel and unexpected application, as most LLM-robotics work has focused on high-level, quasi-static tasks where in-weights reasoning is more directly applicable. This work expands the perceived utility of LLMs in robotics beyond symbolic planning to real-time, physical control adjustments.

2. **Unlocking Data Efficiency in Robotics:** Dynamic manipulation is notoriously data-hungry. By showing that ICPI can achieve strong performance with a remarkably small dataset (around 300 examples), this research offers a paradigm shift towards data-efficient robotic learning. This is critical for real-world deployment, where collecting vast amounts of interaction data can be prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.

3. **Novel Approach to Policy Optimization:** ICPI provides a new, model-agnostic approach to iterative policy improvement. Instead of requiring the design and training of task-specific neural networks or complex optimization algorithms, it leverages the inherent pattern-matching abilities of a general-purpose LLM through prompt engineering and example selection. This streamlines the development process for new dynamic tasks.

4. **Implications for LLM Capabilities:** The finding that LLMs can act as "general pattern machines" for domains vastly different from their training data (text and images) further deepens our understanding of their emergent abilities. It suggests that LLMs possess a fundamental capability for analogical reasoning and pattern generalization that extends beyond semantic understanding.

5. **Addressing Limitations of In-Weights Reasoning:** By demonstrating that ICL outperforms in-weights reasoning for dynamic tasks, the paper provides crucial guidance for future LLM-robotics research. It suggests that for problems requiring fine-grained physical interaction, focusing on how LLMs can *learn* from immediate environmental feedback (in-context) rather than relying solely on their pre-trained, abstract knowledge (in-weights) might be more fruitful.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper also lays the groundwork for several exciting avenues for future work. These include:
 - **Automating Data Collection:** Exploring methods like self-play or demonstrations to ease the burden of creating the initial policy improvement dataset.
 - **Advanced Feature Learning:** Investigating if future LLMs can reduce the need for manual feature engineering (like computing relative error) by learning to extract relevant features directly from raw state and goal information.
 - **Higher-Dimensional Action Spaces:** Extending ICPI to more dexterous and complex manipulation tasks involving higher-dimensional action spaces.
 - **Alternative Transformer Models:** Applying ICPI with robotics-specific transformer models that might be better suited for sequential robot data.

Despite acknowledging limitations such as the computational/financial overhead of LLMs and the current necessity for feature selection, this work provides a compelling demonstration of how LLMs can be creatively utilized to address long-standing challenges in robotics. It opens new pathways for developing intelligent robots capable of learning and adapting to dynamic environments with unprecedented efficiency.
