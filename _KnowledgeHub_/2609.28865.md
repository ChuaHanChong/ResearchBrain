# Direction-Scale Decomposition in Action Representation: Rethinking What to Tokenize for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Direction-Scale Decomposition (DSD) is an action representation for discrete-token VLA models that factorizes translation increments into a unit direction plus magnitude and rotation increments into an axis plus angle before normalization and tokenization. Directional components use fixed dataset-independent bounds while magnitudes stay in separate scale channels. Paired with uniform binning or BEAST, it raises LIBERO average success and gains 10.3 points on SimplerEnv under mixed-dataset training.

## Problem

- Raw delta end-effector representations tie tokens to execution speed, so spatially identical paths demonstrated at different velocities map to widely separated token sequences, hiding shared geometry.
- Dataset-specific percentile normalization assigns the same physical action different bins across datasets, forcing models to relearn identical motions and hindering heterogeneous co-training.
- Coordinate-wise denormalization on a target robot can rescale axes unequally or flip signs, distorting motion direction even when the predicted token pattern is correct.

## Method

- **Direction-Scale Decomposition** converts each 7D raw action into 9D: unit translation direction with Euclidean magnitude, unit rotation axis with angle via a numerically stable ==matrix-to-axis-angle== conversion, plus the unchanged gripper command.
- Only the two scale channels receive dataset-statistics normalization; directional components already lie in [-1,1], giving one shared convention, with shared parameters during co-training and target-specific parameters at fine-tuning.
- A small-scale masking rule down-weights the direction-token loss to a floor weight of 0.1 below a scale threshold, suppressing noisy supervision from near-zero motions; the representation composes with ==BIN== and ==BEAST==.

## Results

- Light-marker path tracking over 10 rollouts: DSD-BIN cut mean DTW distance **20.5%** versus BIN and DSD-BEAST cut it **10.1%** versus BEAST, raising calibrated scores **9.23** and **11.23 points**.
- LIBERO averages reached **92.3%** for DSD-BIN versus **88.5%** for BIN and **91.9%** for DSD-BEAST versus **88.0%**, matching a pretrained flow-matching policy and beating a pretrained compression-tokenizer baseline at **85.6%**.
- SimplerEnv five-dataset co-training lifted DSD-BIN from **40.7%** to **43.3%** while BIN dropped **40.0%** to **33.0%**; real-robot averages improved **13.3 points** without pretraining, **11.7 points** with.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Normalization is not neutral preprocessing: it defines the token targets, so the representation handed to a tokenizer matters as much as the tokenizer itself.
> - Routing task-irrelevant variation such as execution speed into dedicated scalar channels lets discrete policies reuse geometric structure instead of memorizing speed-specific token variants.
> - Adding heterogeneous datasets can degrade a policy when normalization conventions clash; dataset-independent directional bounds turn that extra data back into a gain.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Discrete-token vision-language-action models learn robot motion by turning pose changes into discrete action tokens. Robot demonstrations are relatively scarce. They also differ in how quickly actions are performed and in the numerical conventions used by datasets. The authors argue that raw pose increments can therefore make similar motions look different to a learner.

Their proposal is to record which way motion points separately from how far it moves. In a controlled test, a fixed camera tracks a light marker on the robot's end effector as it follows a reference loop. On that test, the version using the split representation (DSD-BIN) follows the normalized route more closely than the standard baseline (BIN).

Left to right: the robot and light-marker setup, demonstrations collected at five speeds, and 10 rollouts per method for BIN, DSD-BIN, BEAST, and DSD-BEAST. Gold dashed curves mark the reference path, colored lines show rollouts, and cross markers indicate safety-guard terminations. DSD-BIN rollouts lie closer to the reference than BIN rollouts. BEAST and DSD-BEAST each have two guard-terminated rollouts.

### 2. Split direction from distance

A VLA is a robot policy that uses vision and language to predict actions. In discrete-token VLAs, continuous commands are represented as tokens. A common raw per-arm command has three translation increments, three rotation increments, and one gripper command.

Direction–Scale Decomposition (DSD) makes three changes to this command:

- It converts translation into its Euclidean magnitude and a unit direction.
- It converts relative rotation into an angle and a unit axis.
- It retains the gripper command unchanged.

The resulting representation has nine scalar coordinates rather than seven. It retains the same six motion degrees of freedom and the gripper command. Direction and axis components use fixed bounds, while magnitude and angle are normalized in separate scale channels. The representation can feed existing tokenizers. DSD-BIN bins these components, and DSD-BEAST supplies the representation to BEAST's B-spline tokenizer.

This is a representation change. It does not reduce generated tokens, attempts, or elapsed time. Two conditions also apply:

- **Scale tokens still differ across datasets.** The common directional convention does not make scale tokens identical across datasets. The authors' cross-dataset example assumes compatible coordinate conventions and physical units.
- **Direction is unstable near zero.** At zero or near-zero scale, direction is unstable. The training procedure therefore down-weights direction loss below a threshold and retains a minimum weight of 0.1.

The figure makes three comparisons. (1) Slow and fast versions of a translation have different raw motion-token patterns, while DSD keeps direction fixed and puts the change in scale. (2) A translation $p=(4,4,3)$ mm receives different raw normalized coordinates under Bridge and VIOLA statistics, while DSD displays a common direction; scale tokens may differ. (3) In a Bridge-to-Berkeley-UR5 example, raw decoded motion changes direction by 69.84° under a normalization mismatch, while DSD preserves direction. These are explanatory examples, not measured robot-transfer results.

### 3. Two matched comparisons

**Path tracking under varied speed.** The authors collected 50 scripted demonstrations: five velocities, 10 repetitions each. They evaluated each method with 10 rollouts.

To score a rollout, each executed path and the reference were prepared in three steps:

1. Each path was independently centered.
1. Each was uniformly scaled by its maximum bounding-box span.
1. Each was resampled to 256 equally spaced arc-length points.

Endpoint-constrained monotone dynamic time warping (DTW) then measures mean Euclidean alignment distance, so lower is better. This measures agreement in normalized path shape and traversal order. It does not measure elapsed time or physical scale.

DSD-BIN's mean distance is 0.01588, versus 0.01997 for BIN, a reported 20.5% reduction. Two rollouts each for BEAST and DSD-BEAST were stopped by the maximum-rotation safety guard. The reported averages include failed rollouts.

**Mixed-dataset co-training.** SimplerEnv's overall result is success across four Bridge tasks: Carrot, Spoon, Stack, and Eggplant. Each task has 75 matched trials, for 300 in total. The five-dataset mixture adds Berkeley-autolab-UR5, stanford-hydra, taco-play, and viola to bridgeV2, spanning WidowX, UR5, and Franka robots.

Adding the mixture had opposite effects on the two methods:

- DSD-BIN rises from 40.7% on Bridge-only training to 43.3% with the mixture.
- BIN falls from 40.0% to 33.0%.

Under co-training, DSD-BIN's overall success rate is 43.3% versus 33.0% for BIN, a 10.3-percentage-point difference. Outcomes vary by task. Co-training improves DSD-BIN on Carrot and Eggplant but reduces its Spoon and Stack scores.

### 4. What the results establish

DSD represents a route with a stable direction, while a separate scale channel carries how far each step moves. In the controlled path test and the mixed-data comparison, the measured gains are consistent with the authors' account. By themselves, they do not prove that normalization mismatch or speed variation is the only cause of improved performance.

The main boundary is that the co-training evidence comes from one tested five-dataset mixture, not the full OXE collection.
