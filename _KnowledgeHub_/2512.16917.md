# Generative Adversarial Reasoner: Enhancing LLM Reasoning with Adversarial Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Johns Hopkins University introduce the Generative Adversarial Reasoner (GAR), a self-supervised framework that co-trains an LLM reasoner and a discriminator using adversarial reinforcement learning. This approach provides dense, slice-level feedback, leading to improved mathematical reasoning performance and code generation capabilities by effectively addressing process errors, with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B showing a +7.3 accuracy gain on AIME24.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently make process errors in complex reasoning tasks, such as incorrect calculations or brittle logic, despite strong final answer performance.
- Existing process-level supervision methods (e.g., human-annotated Process Reward Models or LLM-as-judge critics) are either too costly and subjective or noisy, inconsistent, and lack discriminative power.
- Current reinforcement learning post-training often relies on static reward models, which fail to adapt to the reasoner's evolving capabilities, leading to miscalibrated feedback and limiting further improvement.

## Method

- GAR introduces an on-policy joint training framework where an LLM Reasoner and an LLM ==Discriminator== co-evolve through adversarial reinforcement learning.
- Reasoning trajectories are partitioned into shorter, logically coherent 'slices' for efficient discriminator evaluation, providing dense, reliable step-level feedback.
- A dual reward system is employed: the Reasoner receives exact-match and discriminator-provided slice rewards, while the Discriminator maximizes a GAN-style term for distinguishing real/generated slices and an alignment term with overall task success.

## Results

- GAR consistently improved Pass@1 accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-**7B** improving by +7.3 on AIME24 (54.0 to 61.3) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-**8B** by +10.0 on AIME24 (43.7 to 53.7).
- The compute-efficient discriminator design, which constrains rationale generation to 128 tokens, reduced training time by over **50%** (e.g., from 43 to 19 hours for AIME24) while maintaining accuracy.
- GAR exhibited 'selective entropy' behavior, reducing uncertainty on incorrect solutions while preserving exploration for critical decision points in correct ones, and extended its applicability to tasks without verifiable final answers and for distilling reasoning patterns.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly training an adaptive discriminator with a reasoner effectively generates well-calibrated, on-policy step-level rewards, reducing reliance on expensive human annotations and improving robustness to label noise.
> - Segmenting long reasoning chains into logically sound 'slices' simplifies the discriminator's task, enabling efficient and accurate fine-grained error detection and credit assignment.
> - The combination of adversarial and alignment rewards for the discriminator ensures balanced and meaningful feedback, guiding the reasoner to improve while preventing reward hacking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved notable capabilities in mathematical reasoning, often reaching expert-level performance on various benchmarks. However, these models still exhibit process errors such as incorrect calculations, brittle logic, superficially plausible but invalid steps, and repetitive or incoherent reasoning. The broader research landscape has sought to address these limitations through several avenues.

One line of research involves model debate and collaboration, where LLMs engage in adversarial interactions either with each other (e.g., Du et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023) or with themselves (e.g., Kuba et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a) to refine reasoning. Another significant approach is the development of Process Reward Models (PRMs) (e.g., Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). PRMs provide finer-grained supervision by evaluating intermediate reasoning steps rather than solely relying on final answers, aiming to identify and mitigate errors throughout the reasoning process. While PRMs have shown promising results, they face challenges related to the high cost and subjective nature of human annotations for step-level feedback, as well as potential issues of reward mis-specification or over/under-rewarding (Wen et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2025).

As an alternative to human-annotated PRMs, prompt-based methods employ LLMs themselves as critics to provide stepwise judgments (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025). These methods offer lower annotation costs but can suffer from noisy, inconsistent, and less discriminative judgments. Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques, such as those used in DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) and FastCuRL (Song et al., 2025), also contribute to improving LLM reasoning by encouraging behaviors like self-reflection and verification. However, these often rely on static rewards or fixed critics, which may not adapt to the evolving capabilities of the LLM policy.

This work, Generative Adversarial Reasoner (GAR), positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of existing process-level feedback mechanisms. It aims to bridge the gap between costly human PRMs and noisy LLM-as-judge critics by introducing a co-evolutionary framework. GAR retains the concept of a stepwise critic but enables it to jointly train with the LLM reasoner in an adversarial reinforcement learning setup. This approach is intended to generate effective, well-calibrated, on-policy step-level signals at a reduced annotation cost, while maintaining robustness against label noise and reward mis-specification, thereby enhancing overall reasoning quality. The method also relates to research in self-play, multi-agent systems, and game-theoretic training by embedding adversarial dynamics directly within the training loop to provide fine-grained, on-policy credit assignment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the explicit reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs), particularly in mathematical contexts, by mitigating common process errors. These errors include incorrect calculations, brittle logical steps, and superficially plausible but invalid arguments that LLMs may generate despite extensive training.

The motivation for developing the Generative Adversarial Reasoner (GAR) stems from several observations regarding the limitations of current approaches to improve LLM reasoning:

1. **Persistent Reasoning Errors:** Despite their advancements, LLMs frequently commit process errors in complex reasoning tasks. Addressing these granular errors, rather than solely focusing on final answer correctness, is identified as crucial for more robust and reliable performance.
2. **Limitations of Existing Process-Level Supervision:**
 - **Process Reward Models (PRMs):** While effective, PRMs typically require expensive and time-consuming human annotation of intermediate reasoning steps. This cost makes scaling difficult and the annotations themselves can be subjective or prone to mis-specification, potentially leading to over- or under-rewarding.
 - **Prompt-Based LLM Critics:** Leveraging LLMs as critics offers a lower-cost alternative to human annotation. However, these LLM-generated judgments can be noisy, inconsistent, or lack sufficient discriminative power to provide precise feedback.
3. **Need for Adaptive Reward Signals:** Current reinforcement learning (RL) post-training methods often rely on static reward models or fixed critics. As the LLM reasoner improves, a static critic may become miscalibrated, providing less informative feedback or failing to detect more subtle errors that the reasoner starts to make. An adaptive, co-evolving critic is hypothesized to maintain alignment with the reasoner's evolving capabilities.
4. **Challenges of Joint Training:** Implementing joint training of a reasoner and a critic presents specific technical challenges. These include the high computational cost associated with analyzing long, intricate reasoning chains step-by-step; the requirement for critics to produce rigorous, interpretable, and concise judgments; and the risk of reward hacking, where the critic might drift towards overly positive judgments or the reasoner learns to produce superficially plausible but shallow reasoning.

To address these motivations, GAR aims to:
- Develop an on-policy joint training framework where an LLM reasoner and an LLM-based discriminator co-evolve.
- Generate dense, well-calibrated, and on-policy step-level rewards to improve credit assignment and sample efficiency, complementing sparse exact-match signals.
- Introduce a compute-efficient review schedule that partitions reasoning chains into logically complete slices, simplifying the discriminator's task and localizing errors.
- Design a reward system for the discriminator that encourages accurate error detection and distinction between generated and reference reasoning traces, while incorporating an alignment term to ensure consistency with overall task success.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Generative Adversarial Reasoner (GAR) is an on-policy joint training framework designed to enhance LLM reasoning through adversarial reinforcement learning. It comprises two main components: a Reasoner ($M_r$) and a Discriminator ($M_d$), both implemented as LLMs, which are trained together.

**Reasoner ($M_r$):**
The reasoner is a general-purpose LLM responsible for generating step-by-step reasoning processes and final answers based on user input. For experimental validation, the authors instantiate the reasoner with state-of-the-art open-source models, specifically variants of the DeepSeek-R1-Distill models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B). The reasoner generates outputs in a specific format, typically including an internal monologue (`<think>...</think>`) followed by the answer (`<answer>...</answer>`).

**Discriminator ($M_d$):**
The discriminator is also an LLM, implemented as a smaller, pre-trained variant of the reasoner (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B or DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B). Its function is to evaluate the quality and correctness of the reasoning process generated by the reasoner, assigning reward signals.

**Slice-Level Evaluation:**
To mitigate the difficulty LLMs face in evaluating long and complex reasoning chains holistically, GAR introduces a slice-level evaluation mechanism. Generated reasoning trajectories are partitioned into shorter, logically coherent "slices." This segmentation is achieved by identifying delimiters (e.g., line breaks, discourse cues) and merging adjacent segments until a new semantic beginning is identified or a predefined token length (L = 320 tokens) is reached, balancing semantic completeness with segment brevity. For each slice *i*, the discriminator assigns a binary slice reward $r_s_i \in \{0, 1\}$, where 1 indicates logical soundness. The overall slice reward for a complete reasoning chain, $R_s$, is calculated as the mean of all slice-level scores. This dense, slice-level feedback aims to improve reliability, provide a more informative training signal, and enhance credit assignment compared to sparse final-answer matching.

**Reward Functions:**
GAR employs distinct but complementary reward functions for the reasoner and the discriminator:

- **Reasoner Reward ($R_{rea}$):** The reasoner is optimized using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Its reward is a linear combination of two terms:
 - **Exact-match term ($R_m$):** A binary reward (0 or 1) based on the correctness of the final answer compared to the ground truth.
 - **Slice reward ($R_s$):** The continuous reward (mean of slice-level scores) provided by the discriminator.
 The total reasoner reward is $R_{rea} = \lambda_1 R_m + \lambda_2 R_s$, with $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \geq 0$ as hyperparameters.

- **Discriminator Reward ($R_{dis}$):** The discriminator's objective is to maximize two terms:
 - **Discriminator reward ($R_d$):** This term follows a standard Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) objective. It encourages the discriminator to accurately classify reference reasoning slices as "real" and model-generated reasoning slices as "fake." Formally, $R_d = E_{x \sim p_{ref}}[\log M_d(x)] + E_{x \sim p_{gen}}[\log(1 - M_d(x))]$.
 - **Alignment reward ($R_a$):** This term quantifies the mean agreement between the discriminator's slice-level scores ($r_s$) and the correctness of the final answer of the entire reasoning sequence. It promotes consistency, assuming that correct final answers are typically supported by logically sound reasoning.
 The total discriminator reward is $R_{dis} = \lambda_3 R_d + \lambda_4 R_a$, with $\lambda_3, \lambda_4 \geq 0$ as hyperparameters.

**Training Procedure:**
The training proceeds through an iterative, joint optimization process:
1. For each batch of questions, the reasoner generates detailed reasoning steps and final answers.
2. These generated reasoning steps are segmented into multiple slices.
3. The generated slices are mixed with an equal number of reference slices (from a pre-existing dataset) to form a balanced set.
4. The discriminator is trained to distinguish between generated and reference slices.
5. The discriminator then scores each slice of the reasoner's output, providing the $R_s$ signal for the reasoner and contributing to the discriminator's own objectives ($R_d$ and $R_a$).
6. Both the reasoner and the discriminator are updated using their respective objective functions. This co-adaptation aims to dynamically align the reward signal with the reasoner's evolving capabilities.

**Efficiency and Discriminator SFT:**
To enhance computational efficiency during training, the discriminator's workflow is modified. Instead of generating a full reasoning chain, it is prompted to first provide a brief analysis, then a binary evaluative judgment ($r_s$), and finally a concise rationale. The maximum generation length for the discriminator's output, particularly for the rationale, is limited to K=128 tokens during training to accelerate the process.

To improve the discriminator's accuracy and adaptability to this specific output format, a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage is implemented. During SFT, a small subset of the training data's reasoning steps, generated by a pre-trained LLM, are annotated by GPT-o4-mini with an analysis, judgment, and concise rationale. This SFT dataset is balanced (equal numbers of 'yes' and 'no' labels) to mitigate bias. The discriminator is fine-tuned on this data before the adversarial RL training begins.

At inference time, only the LLM reasoner is utilized to produce answers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The Generative Adversarial Reasoner (GAR) framework demonstrated consistent performance improvements across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks and extended capabilities to other tasks.

**Mathematical Reasoning Performance:**
GAR consistently improved the Pass@1 accuracy over strong baseline models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) across seven mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME24, AIME25, MATH500, GSM8K, AMC23, OlympiadBench, and LiveMathBench-Hard.
- **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B:** Accuracy on AIME24 improved from 54.0 to 61.3 (+7.3). On LiveMathBench-Hard, accuracy increased from 18.4 to 24.9 (+6.5). Overall, gains were observed across all seven benchmarks.
- **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B:** Accuracy on AIME24 improved from 43.7 to 53.7 (+10.0). Gains were also consistent across all other benchmarks, including a +5.9 on AIME25 (from 30.3 to 36.2) and +5.5 on AMC23 (from 84.5 to 90.0).
These results indicate GAR's effectiveness in enhancing reasoning performance, particularly on challenging mathematical tasks, by providing supervision on reasoning traces.

**Discriminator Efficiency:**
The modification to the discriminator's workflow, limiting its rationale generation to 128 tokens, proved effective. While maintaining comparable accuracy to an unrestricted discriminator (e.g., 61.3% vs. 60.8% on AIME24 for Qwen-7B), it significantly reduced training time. For instance, GAR with truncation completed AIME24 training in 19 hours, compared to 43 hours without truncation, and 16 hours for standard RL without a discriminator. This demonstrates that the analysis-score-rationale design, coupled with length constraints, delivers dense supervision efficiently.

**Entropy Analysis and Selective Entropy:**
GAR demonstrated an ability to improve model accuracy without the typical policy entropy collapse often observed in RL for LLMs.
- The overall mean-entropy distribution of GAR was comparable to the baseline, suggesting that it did not globally reduce exploration or diversity.
- Analysis of entropy by correctness showed that GAR's "wrong" case distribution was tighter with a shorter low-entropy tail, while its "correct" case entropy was lower than the baseline, indicating improved calibration and fewer high-uncertainty failures.
- When zero-entropy tokens were removed, the mean entropy over non-zero-entropy tokens was higher for GAR's correct cases than the baseline's, and comparable between GAR's correct and wrong groups. This suggests a "selective-entropy" mechanism, where GAR encourages low entropy on deterministic steps but sustains exploration on decision-critical tokens, contributing to the observed accuracy gains.

**Ablation Studies:**
Ablation studies systematically validated the contributions of GAR's components:
- **Discriminator Design:** Adding a fixed standard critic improved performance over standard RL with only exact-match rewards. Further improvement was observed by introducing a fixed GAR discriminator, which utilized the slice-level judgment format and compute-efficient review schedule (e.g., AIME24: 56.3 (standard RL) -> 56.7 (fixed standard critic) -> 58.6 (fixed GAR discriminator)). This highlighted the benefit of refined, slice-level feedback.
- **Discriminator Reward Functions:** Both the alignment reward ($R_a$) and the discriminator reward ($R_d$) individually contributed to performance gains. Their combination yielded the best results, indicating their complementary roles in providing calibrated and task-aligned feedback while steering the discriminator towards distinguishing real from generated traces.
- **Effectiveness of Joint Training:** Joint on-policy updates, allowing the discriminator to co-evolve with the reasoner, consistently outperformed a fixed discriminator. This demonstrated that dynamic adaptation of the reward signal is crucial for detecting subtler errors and raising the overall performance ceiling (e.g., AIME24: 58.6 (fixed GAR discriminator) -> 61.3 (trainable GAR discriminator)).
- **Slice Segmentation Design:** The proposed rule-based segmentation combining delimiters with a token-length constraint was found to be effective, balancing semantic completeness with computational cost. Alternative methods (pure fixed-length windows, LLM-based semantic segmentation) either performed worse or incurred higher training costs without significant performance gains. Performance was highest and most stable with slice lengths between approximately 320–560 tokens.

**New Applications and Capabilities:**
- **RL without Verifiable Final Answers:** GAR decouples RL post-training from the necessity of complete chain-of-thought generation and verifiable final answers. By evaluating partial reasoning traces (e.g., after three slices), it achieved higher accuracy (57.7% on AIME24) with significantly less training time (6 hours) compared to standard RL (56.3% in 16 hours). This opens possibilities for tasks with hard-to-evaluate outputs like mathematical proofs.
- **Reasoning Pattern Distillation:** GAR demonstrated the ability to distill reasoning patterns. In an experiment, training with a discriminator distinguishing between "gemini thinking trajectory" and "deepseek thinking trajectory" significantly reduced human experts' ability to differentiate GAR-generated reasoning from Gemini reasoning (distinguishability success rate dropped from 82.3% to 55.9%). This suggests GAR can align a student model's reasoning style with a teacher's.
- **Code Generation Performance:** The method yielded substantial improvements in coding-based reasoning tasks, with Pass@1 accuracy on LiveCodeBench for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B increasing from 37.4 to 43.6.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Generative Adversarial Reasoner (GAR) introduces a framework for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models, presenting several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Advancement in LLM Reasoning Robustness:** GAR consistently improves LLM performance on complex mathematical benchmarks and also shows gains in code generation. By explicitly targeting process errors through fine-grained, slice-level feedback, the method contributes to making LLM reasoning more robust, reliable, and less prone to common logical flaws or calculation mistakes.

2. **Addressing Limitations of Reward Models:** The approach mitigates key challenges associated with existing process reward models (PRMs) and LLM-as-judge critics. By co-evolving the discriminator with the reasoner, GAR generates well-calibrated, on-policy step-level rewards, reducing reliance on expensive human annotations and increasing robustness to label noise and potential reward mis-specification. This dynamic alignment ensures that the reward signal remains relevant as the reasoner's capabilities improve, allowing for the detection of progressively subtler errors.

3. **Improved Credit Assignment and Sample Efficiency:** The provision of dense, slice-level rewards offers a continuous learning signal that supplements sparse exact-match grading. This enhances credit assignment, allowing the model to differentiate between better and worse reasoning paths even when final answers are incorrect. The resulting improvement in sample efficiency could accelerate the development and fine-tuning of reasoning-capable LLMs.

4. **Computational Efficiency:** The introduction of a compute-efficient review schedule, which partitions reasoning chains into manageable slices and employs truncated, structured rationales from the discriminator, makes the adversarial reinforcement learning process feasible within a comparable computational budget to standard RL methods. This is critical for practical implementation and scalability.

5. **Expansion of Applicable RL Tasks:** GAR's ability to provide reward signals based on intermediate reasoning steps, independent of a final verifiable answer, significantly expands the range of tasks where RL post-training can be effectively applied. This is particularly impactful for domains like open-ended mathematical proofs or scientific discovery, where outputs may lack a clear, automatically verifiable ground truth.

6. **Enabling Reasoning Pattern Distillation:** The demonstrated capability of GAR to distill specific reasoning patterns opens new avenues for aligning LLMs with desired styles, whether derived from expert human explanations, specific problem-solving strategies, or even the stylistic traits of other advanced models. This has potential implications for creating more interpretable, human-aligned, or domain-specific reasoning agents.

In summary, GAR represents a step towards developing more autonomous and adaptive learning systems for LLMs. By combining adversarial learning with granular process-level feedback, it offers a method for continuous improvement in reasoning while addressing practical challenges of cost and calibration. Its potential impact extends to fostering the development of more accurate, reliable, and versatile AI systems capable of complex problem-solving.
