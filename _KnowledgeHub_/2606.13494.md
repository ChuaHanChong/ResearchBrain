# NavWAM: A Navigation World Action Model for Goal-Conditioned Visual Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> NavWAM introduces a navigation world action model that integrates visual foresight, goal-progress estimation, and action generation into a single diffusion-transformer policy for goal-conditioned visual navigation. This approach overcomes the need for external planning, improving real-robot success rates to 79.2% and significantly reducing inference latency compared to prior world models.

## Problem

- Direct navigation policies often lack explicit prediction of future visual consequences, acquiring foresight only implicitly through action supervision.
- Existing Navigation World Models (NWMs) provide explicit visual foresight but depend on computationally intensive external planners (e.g., CEM) for action selection, creating a bottleneck for real-time control.
- The separation between future prediction and action generation limits the direct utility of learned visual foresight for closed-loop control and introduces external dependencies.

## Method

- **NavWAM** represents the current observation, goal, robot state, action chunk, future egocentric observations, and goal-progress value within a shared latent canvas.
- It reformulates ==goal-conditioned== navigation as a joint denoising problem, solved by a pretrained diffusion-transformer video world model (Cosmos Predict2).
- A multi-mode training strategy (policy, world-model, value modes) allows a single model to learn all necessary aspects, complemented by a training curriculum involving simulator pretraining and real-robot fine-tuning.

## Results

- NavWAM achieved a **79.2%** task success rate in real-world closed-loop image-goal navigation on a Diablo mobile robot across 24 episodes, outperforming OmniVLA (**58.3%**) and NWM (**16.7%**).
- On the GO STANFORD offline benchmark, a fine-tuned NavWAM achieved an Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) of **0.192** and Relative Pose Error (RPE) of **0.070**, demonstrating improved trajectory accuracy over planning-based world models.
- Inference latency and computational cost per action were significantly reduced, operating at approximately **5 Hz** compared to sub-Hz rates for CEM-based baselines (e.g., NavWAM at **205.7 ms** latency versus NWM with N=120 candidates at 233,**831 ms**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating future observation prediction, goal-progress estimation, and action generation into a single, executable policy formulation enables explicit visual foresight to directly support closed-loop robot control without external planners.
> - A pretrained diffusion-transformer video world model can be effectively repurposed to jointly denoise diverse navigation-relevant information, including actions, future perceptions, and goal-progress values.
> - Robust navigation performance relies not only on predicting future images but also on explicit supervision for actions, states, and goal-progress values, which collectively contribute to improved control accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Goal-conditioned visual navigation requires robots to operate under partial observability, necessitating the anticipation of future egocentric views and assessment of progress toward a goal. The broader research landscape in this domain includes several categories of approaches:

- **Direct Policies:** These methods learn to map egocentric observations and goals directly to actions. They are typically efficient in closed-loop control but often acquire foresight implicitly through action supervision rather than explicit prediction. Examples include GNM, ViNT, NoMaD, and OmniVLA.
- **Visual Prediction Approaches (Navigation World Models - NWMs):** These approaches make visual foresight explicit by synthesizing future egocentric observations along potential paths. NWMs learn action-conditioned predictive models whose generated futures can be scored against the goal for planning. However, they function primarily as prediction modules and rely on external planners (e.g., Cross-Entropy Method, CEM) to convert predictions into executable actions. This separation can introduce computational bottlenecks and external dependencies for closed-loop behavior. Noteworthy examples include PathDreamer, Schrödiner’s Navigator, NWM, and NavDreamer.
- **Video-Based Robot Policies:** A related area uses generative modeling, particularly video prediction or generation, as a policy representation. Initially applied primarily to manipulation tasks, these models (e.g., Diffusion Policy, UniPi, Dreamitate, GR-1) leverage video models to generate intermediate plans or jointly predict future states and actions. Cosmos Policy, in particular, shares a similar model design by encoding actions, future states, and values as latent frames within a pretrained video model.

NavWAM positions itself at the intersection of navigation world models and video-based robot policies. It addresses a specific gap: while NWMs offer explicit visual foresight, their reliance on external planning for action selection separates prediction from control. NavWAM aims to bridge this gap by integrating future observation prediction, goal-progress estimation, and action generation within a single policy representation. This approach seeks to make visual foresight directly usable for closed-loop control in goal-conditioned navigation, distinguishing it from prior video policies predominantly focused on manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a fundamental challenge in robot learning for navigation under partial observability: how to effectively integrate explicit visual foresight into executable closed-loop control. Direct navigation policies, while efficient, do not explicitly predict the visual consequences of their actions. Conversely, Navigation World Models (NWMs) provide explicit visual foresight by synthesizing future egocentric observations. However, they function as prediction tools, requiring an external planner (such as one based on the Cross-Entropy Method, CEM) to evaluate these predicted futures against a goal and select an appropriate action.

This separation between future prediction and action selection presents several limitations:
- **Computational Bottleneck:** External planners often involve computationally intensive search over candidate actions and predicted trajectories at inference time, which can hinder real-time deployment.
- **Dependency on External Planning:** The robot's closed-loop behavior becomes dependent on the choices and efficacy of the external planner rather than solely on the learned world model.
- **Lack of Direct Integration:** The learned world model's visual foresight is not intrinsically linked to the mechanism that generates actions, potentially limiting its direct utility for control.

The key objective of NavWAM is to overcome these limitations by proposing a novel formulation that transforms navigation world-model prediction into an executable action policy. Specifically, NavWAM aims to:
- Develop a diffusion-transformer policy that integrates future egocentric observations, goal-progress values, and executable action chunks into a shared latent sequence.
- Reformulate goal-conditioned navigation as a joint denoising problem encompassing future perception, goal progress estimation, and executable motion.
- Enable visual foresight to directly support closed-loop robot control without requiring external CEM-style test-time trajectory optimization.
- Demonstrate improved navigation performance compared to planning-based world models and competitive performance against direct navigation policies, while additionally providing interpretable future-view and value predictions.

By achieving these objectives, NavWAM seeks to make explicit visual foresight an intrinsic component of a robot's control policy, rather than an auxiliary prediction module.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NavWAM's methodology centers on parameterizing a closed-loop navigation policy using a pretrained video world model. The core approach involves representing all necessary variables for navigation control—current observation, goal, robot state, action chunk, future egocentric observations, and goal-progress value—within a shared latent canvas. This transforms goal-conditioned navigation into a joint denoising problem.

**Backbone:**
NavWAM is instantiated using Cosmos Predict2, a pretrained diffusion-transformer video world model. This backbone provides the causal Variational Autoencoder (VAE), Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture, and a text-conditioning interface, which are utilized to encode and denoise the latent sequence. Non-visual robot variables are encoded as latent frames, maintaining compatibility with the pretrained video model interface while enabling a common latent representation for diverse navigation-related information.

**World-Action Latent Canvas:**
NavWAM employs a fixed nine-frame latent canvas, with each frame either observed (for conditioning) or generated (as a prediction target).
- **Observed/Conditioning Frames (Bottom Four):**
 - **Frame 0:** A blank frame, required by the causal VAE’s temporal compression.
 - **Frame 1:** Current robot state `s_t`, represented as a 3D vector `[x, y, ψ]` (2D position and yaw). This is normalized and spatially broadcast across the latent grid.
 - **Frame 2:** Goal specification `g`. For image-goal navigation, this is the target image. A variant uses `o_t-1` (previous observation) for world-model specific tasks.
 - **Frame 3:** Current egocentric observation `o_t` (RGB image, 3x224x224 pixels).
- **Generated/Prediction Target Frames (Top Five):**
 - **Frame 4:** Executable action chunk `a_t:t+H-1`, consisting of `H` local-frame waypoint increments `(Δx, Δy, Δψ)`. The chunk size `H` is set to 4 in the main paper. These are normalized, broadcast spatially, and decoded by spatial averaging.
 - **Frame 5:** Future state `s_t+H`, a 3D vector similar in representation to Frame 1.
 - **Frames 6 & 7:** Future egocentric observations `o_t+H-1` and `o_t+H` (RGB images).
 - **Frame 8:** Goal-progress value `v_t+H`, a scalar in `` estimating progress towards the goal. It is normalized, broadcast spatially, and decoded by spatial averaging.

This canvas structure integrates future-view prediction, progress estimation, and action generation into a unified denoising process.

**Training Objective:**
The diffusion transformer `F_θ` is trained to denoise the latent canvas using a standard diffusion loss, `L_diff`, applied only to the generated frames of the canvas. To prevent the high-dimensional image reconstruction loss from dominating, the action frame (Frame 4) is upweighted relative to the future-image frames by a multiplier (λ=5). The value frame (Frame 8) is specifically trained to represent goal progress, calculated as `clip(1 - (distance_to_goal_at_t+H - distance_to_goal_at_t) / (2 * d_max), 0, 1)`.

**Multi-mode Conditioning:**
A single set of model weights learns multiple aspects of the problem through a mixed training strategy. At each step, a sample is assigned to one of three modes:
- **Policy mode (50% of samples):** Conditions on Frames 0-3 and predicts Frames 4-8 (action, future state, future observations, value). This mode is used for inference.
- **World-model mode (25% of samples):** Additionally conditions on Frame 4 (the action chunk) and predicts Frames 5-8 (future state, future observations, value).
- **Value mode (25% of samples):** Conditions on Frames 0-7 and predicts Frame 8 (the scalar value).

**Training Curriculum:**
Training proceeds in three phases:
1. **HM3D Simulator Pretrain:** Initial training on a large dataset of success-only HM3D simulator trajectories, utilizing a longer action chunk size (H=16) to expose the model to longer-horizon visual foresight.
2. **Joint Fine-Tune on Real-Robot Data:** The Phase 1 checkpoint is fine-tuned on real-robot datasets (RECON, SACSON, SCAND). The action chunk size is reset to H=4. This phase creates distinct "WM" and "IG" (image-goal) variants of NavWAM based on the content of Frame 2.
3. **Optional Per-Dataset Fine-Tune:** For specific benchmarks (e.g., GO STANFORD), an additional fine-tuning pass is performed on the corresponding training trajectories using the Phase 2 IG checkpoint.

**Inference:**
At test time, NavWAM operates in its default policy mode. Given the current observation (`o_t`) and goal (`g`), it denoises the prediction frames and directly outputs an action chunk (`a_t:t+H-1`). The robot executes this chunk in a receding-horizon manner, re-querying the model with new observations. While future observations and the goal-progress value are predicted, they are not required for execution, serving instead as interpretable visual foresight. NavWAM also supports an optional value-guided best-of-N sampling mode, but this is not used for the reported main results.

**Robot Platform:**
Real-world evaluations are conducted on a Diablo mobile robot equipped with an egocentric RGB camera, Intel RealSense D455, Livox Mid-360 LiDAR, and an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin. Control is managed via ROS 2 `cmd_vel` commands.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of NavWAM encompassed both offline benchmarks and closed-loop real-robot deployment, comparing it against planning-based world models (NWM, Cosmos Predict2) and a representative direct navigation policy (OmniVLA).

**Offline Benchmarks:**
- **World Models as Policies (GO STANFORD):**
 - NavWAM, operating in its default policy mode without CEM-style action search, demonstrated improved navigation performance over planning-based world models. Without in-domain fine-tuning, NavWAM reduced Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) compared to Cosmos Predict2 and NWM.
 - With an additional fine-tuning pass on GO STANFORD (NavWAM w/ FT), it achieved the best performance, with an ATE of 0.192 and Relative Pose Error (RPE) of 0.070, indicating improved trajectory accuracy.
- **Preserving Visual Foresight:** NavWAM maintained or improved its ability to predict future egocentric observations. Without task-specific fine-tuning, NavWAM achieved a subject consistency of 0.668, higher than NWM's 0.524. After fine-tuning, consistency remained competitive at 0.635. Qualitative results illustrated that NavWAM predicted future views that aligned more closely with the goal scene compared to NWM.
- **Learning Useful Futures for Control (Ablation):** An ablation study confirmed that image-only future prediction was insufficient for robust navigation, even when coupled with CEM planning. Adding action and state supervision substantially improved trajectory accuracy. The inclusion of value supervision further enhanced performance, suggesting that the joint prediction of future images, actions, and goal progress is beneficial for control.
- **Predictive Policies vs. Direct Policies (SIT):** NavWAM, utilizing a 2B-parameter video backbone, remained competitive with OmniVLA, a larger 7B-parameter direct navigation policy. NavWAM achieved lower ATE and slightly higher observed success rates on the SIT benchmark, while additionally providing future observations and goal-progress values.

**Closed-Loop Real-Robot Deployment:**
- **Real-World Performance:** In a deployment on a Diablo mobile robot across 24 closed-loop image-goal navigation episodes in four indoor environments, NavWAM achieved the highest observed success rate, reaching the goal in 19 out of 24 episodes (79.2%). This compared favorably to OmniVLA (14/24 episodes, 58.3%) and NWM (4/24 episodes, 16.7%).
- **Qualitative Observations:** Real-world rollouts indicated that NavWAM exhibited more consistent goal-reaching behavior. Predicted future views during execution qualitatively resembled subsequent egocentric observations, demonstrating action-consistent visual foresight.
- **Failure Modes:** An analysis of failure modes in real-robot trials indicated NWM primarily suffered from drift (13 out of 20 failures), OmniVLA predominantly from collisions (7 out of 10 failures), while NavWAM had the fewest failures overall, with 3 drift and 2 collision failures. This pattern aligns with the different control structures of the methods.

**Inference Efficiency (Appendix C.1):**
- NavWAM demonstrated significantly lower computational cost per executed action compared to CEM-based baselines. For example, NWM with N=120 candidates required 14,521 TF FLOPs/action and had a latency of 233,831 ms, while NavWAM required 4.45 TF FLOPs/action and had a latency of 205.7 ms.
- NavWAM operated at approximately 5 Hz, meeting real-time control requirements, whereas CEM-based baselines ran at sub-Hz rates.
- The single-pass NavWAM outperformed all CEM budget settings, which showed accuracy saturation at higher N values.

**Component Ablation (Appendix C.2):**
- Adding future-view supervision to an action-state-value policy improved trajectory accuracy on GO STANFORD, particularly at the longer horizon of h=8, where ATE dropped by 27% and RPE by 32%. This indicates that future-view prediction contributes positively to closed-loop control accuracy, even when other control signals are present.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

NavWAM contributes to the field of robot navigation by addressing the integration of explicit visual foresight with actionable control. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Bridging the Foresight-Action Gap:** The primary significance lies in its novel approach to converting explicit visual foresight, typically provided by navigation world models, into a directly executable policy. This overcomes the reliance on computationally intensive external planners, which has been a bottleneck for deploying such models in real-time robot control.
- **Improved Navigation Performance:** By integrating future observations, goal-progress values, and action chunks into a joint denoising problem, NavWAM demonstrates superior navigation performance compared to planning-based world models and remains competitive with larger direct navigation policies. This suggests that a holistic learning approach can yield more effective navigation strategies.
- **Enhanced Efficiency for Real-Time Deployment:** The ability to generate actions from a single denoising chain, without CEM-style trajectory optimization, significantly reduces inference cost and latency. This makes NavWAM more suitable for real-time operation on robots with constrained computational resources.
- **Interpretable Robot Behavior:** NavWAM's design inherently provides interpretable outputs, including predicted future views and goal-progress values, alongside the actions. This offers a level of insight into the robot's anticipatory reasoning and goal-directed decision-making process, which can be valuable for debugging, monitoring, and building trust in autonomous systems.
- **Foundation for Embodied AI:** The methodology of repurposing powerful video world models for integrated perception, planning, and control in navigation tasks could serve as a foundation for developing more capable embodied AI agents. It highlights the potential of generative models to encapsulate complex spatio-temporal dynamics relevant for robot interaction with the physical world.
- **Potential for Broader Applications:** While evaluated on image-goal navigation, the underlying framework of a world-action model could potentially be extended to other goal specifications (e.g., language- or object-specified goals) by leveraging the text-conditioning interface of the video backbone, opening avenues for more versatile navigation policies.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the focus on image-goal navigation in indoor environments, a limited-scale real-world evaluation (24 episodes across four environments), and the primary use of the default policy mode without extensive study of value-guided best-of-N sampling. Future work could expand evaluation to broader goal specifications (language, object), conduct larger-scale real-world deployments across diverse environments and robot platforms, investigate navigation in dynamic environments with moving obstacles, and explore long-horizon multi-room or multi-floor navigation scenarios. Additionally, further research could optimize the diffusion chain length for latency-accuracy trade-offs and study the generalizability of the value function calibration across diverse datasets. The authors also emphasize that the released checkpoints and code are for research use and not safety-certified for autonomous operation in safety-critical environments.
