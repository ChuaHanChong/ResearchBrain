# LOPAL: Local Performance-Aware Active Learning from Imperfect Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> LOPAL is an active learning framework that allows robots to learn from imperfect human demonstrations by leveraging local performance information. It generates improved robot trajectories by intelligently combining high-quality segments from multiple demonstrations and actively guides users to provide targeted corrections in areas lacking good data, reducing teaching effort and enhancing task performance by up to 27.31%.

## Problem

- Robot performance in Learning from Demonstration (LfD) strongly depends on the quality of human demonstrations, which are often suboptimal and inconsistent, even within a single demonstration.
- Existing LfD methods often average behaviors or prune entire demonstrations, failing to address intra-demonstration variability and potentially discarding valuable local insights.
- Current active learning strategies do not explicitly incorporate local variations in demonstration quality to guide data acquisition, leading to inefficient data collection efforts.

## Method

- **LOPAL** encodes demonstrations using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) that includes local performance values (smoothed quality measures) and a phase variable to represent multimodal data.
- It generates reference trajectories via ==Gaussian Mixture Regression (GMR)==, conditioning the motion on a phase-dependent performance target set above the mean to selectively follow high-performing demonstration segments.
- An active data acquisition mechanism identifies low-performance regions, modulates the robot's motion (e.g., slowing down and interpolating), and uses a shared autonomy approach to facilitate targeted user corrections.

## Results

- In simulation, LOPAL (LP-GMR+I) significantly reduced penalties and enhanced learning efficiency compared to vanilla GMR and other LfD baselines, achieving performance comparable to RL methods without extensive exploration.
- During real-world pipe inspection, LP-GMR+I demonstrated superior task performance, achieving up to **27.31%** improvement over GMR and better performance with fewer demonstrations.
- LOPAL reduced perceived physical workload (lower user energy injected) and mental workload for participants compared to other shared autonomy teaching methods, by providing effective feedback and guiding corrections.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly encoding and leveraging local performance information enables the robot to synthesize higher-quality trajectories by combining optimal segments from various imperfect human demonstrations.
> - Performance-guided modulation of robot behavior (e.g., speed reduction and interpolation in low-quality regions) implicitly signals the need for user input, thereby reducing the user's physical and mental workload.
> - Active data acquisition, focused on areas lacking high-quality data, allows for more efficient learning by prioritizing user teaching effort where it is most critical.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD), an approach that enables robots to acquire skills by observing human examples. LfD offers an intuitive alternative to traditional manual programming, which can be time-consuming. A fundamental challenge in LfD is the inherent variability and potential suboptimality of human demonstrations. Existing LfD methods frequently overlook the fact that even within a generally high-quality demonstration, specific segments can exhibit lower performance due to factors such as skill level or human fatigue during extended tasks.

Prior research has addressed the imperfect nature of demonstrations through various strategies. Some methods classify demonstrations as either successful or unsuccessful, providing a binary signal for learning algorithms. Others employ continuous score values to rate overall demonstration quality, allowing for a more granular prioritization of data. While some work computes explicit local quality measures directly from demonstration data, these measures are often aggregated into global scores, obscuring intra-demonstration variability. Consequently, the ability of these methods to identify and integrate superior local behaviors from different demonstrations into a consolidated, improved behavior is limited.

Beyond pure imitation, another research direction combines LfD with Reinforcement Learning (RL), where demonstrations serve to bootstrap an RL agent, which then refines its policy through exploration. However, this fine-tuning can necessitate extensive exploration, posing challenges for real-world application in terms of time and safety. Human critique of generated trajectories can guide this exploration and refine learned rewards.

Active Learning (AL) represents another approach aimed at reducing the user's demonstration burden by enabling the robot to actively solicit additional, informative data. This often involves user-initiated interventions or active requests for corrections, frequently facilitated by shared autonomy (SA) mechanisms where control is dynamically distributed between human and robot. Robots can signal the need for input through various cues, such as speed adjustments or visual feedback. Existing active imitation learning approaches often query demonstrations based on uncertainty measures (aleatoric and epistemic), which primarily reflect ambiguity in action selection rather than uncertainty about the optimal task outcome.

The paper identifies a gap in the existing AL literature: current methods do not explicitly incorporate local variations in demonstration quality to guide data acquisition. This limitation can lead to inefficient data collection, where data might be over-collected in well-performing regions and under-collected in areas where high-quality demonstrations are critically needed to enhance robot performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from a fundamental challenge in Learning from Demonstration (LfD): the strong dependence of robot performance on the quality of human demonstrations. Specifically, the authors identify that human behavior is often suboptimal and inconsistent, leading to variations in demonstration quality even within a single demonstration. If a robot naively replicates these imperfect demonstrations, it risks reproducing the demonstrated flaws, including locally suboptimal segments. This issue is compounded in tasks requiring extended operation, where human performance can decline over time due to factors like fatigue.

The key objectives of this research are to address this challenge by developing a novel active learning framework, LOPAL (LOcal Performance-aware Active Learning), that leverages information about local demonstration quality. The framework aims to:

1. **Generate Trajectories Outperforming Imperfect Demonstrations:** Develop an LfD method that can synthesize a reference behavior by intelligently combining high-performance segments from multiple imperfect human demonstrations. This seeks to overcome the limitations of methods that average behaviors or prune entire demonstrations based on global quality, which might discard valuable local insights.
2. **Optimize Active Data Acquisition:** Design an active learning strategy that strategically requests additional human input in specific regions where high-quality demonstration data is currently lacking. The goal is to focus human teaching effort precisely where it is most needed, thereby reducing the overall burden on the user compared to collecting complete, new demonstrations or performing extensive autonomous exploration.
3. **Enhance Task Performance:** Ultimately, improve the robot's ability to execute tasks more effectively by learning from and improving upon imperfect human examples.
4. **Reduce User Effort:** Decrease the physical and mental workload required from human users during the teaching process.

By explicitly accounting for local performance variations and integrating this information into both the learning and data acquisition processes, LOPAL seeks to create a more efficient and robust LfD system for robot skill acquisition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LOPAL is an active learning framework composed of two interdependent components: a local performance-driven LfD method and an active data acquisition mechanism.

#### 3.1. Learning from Locally Imperfect Demonstrations

This component focuses on encoding demonstrated trajectories and their local quality information to generate improved behaviors.

##### 3.1.1. Encoding Demonstrations with Locally Varying Quality

The process begins by representing each of *D* human demonstrations, which consist of *N* samples of robot pose ($\xi \in R^6$), comprising position ($x \in R^3$) and orientation ($\gamma \in R^3$). For each sample, a local quality measure ($p$) is determined. This measure can be a binary penalty (e.g., for constraint violations) or a continuous score (e.g., motion smoothness). These raw quality measures are then transformed into a smoothed local performance value ($l \in$) for each sample using a moving average (Equation 1). This smoothing balances responsiveness with the ability to follow locally optimal segments. To represent the progression of the motion, a phase variable ($\phi \in$) is defined based on the normalized cumulative pose distance. Finally, the combined multimodal data (pose, phase, and local performance) from all demonstrations is approximated by training a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) with *K* Gaussian components using an iterative Expectation-Maximization (EM) procedure. The GMM parameters include priors ($\pi_k$), means ($\mu_k$), and covariances ($\Sigma_k$) for each component.

##### 3.1.2. Generating Trajectories Driven by Local Performance Information

After encoding the data, Gaussian Mixture Regression (GMR) is employed to generate a reference trajectory. For a sequence of *M* reference phase values ($\phi_{ref,m}$), a phase-dependent performance target ($l_{ref,m}$) is extracted. This target is set at two standard deviations above the phase-dependent mean of the local performance (Equation 2) to bias the generated motion towards high-performance regions. Using this reference phase and the elevated performance target, the corresponding reference pose ($\xi_{ref,m} \in R^6$) is regressed (Equation 3). This conditioning mechanism allows the generated movement to selectively follow the locally highest-performing demonstration segments, thereby mitigating the influence of suboptimal demonstrations. The use of phase based on cumulative pose distance ensures non-zero velocity even if demonstrations contain pauses. This LfD approach is designed to be trainable from a single demonstration, enabling immediate robot contribution in subsequent task iterations.

#### 3.2. Active Data Acquisition Guided by Local Performance Information

This component facilitates the incremental expansion of the demonstration dataset by actively soliciting user corrections where high-quality data is absent.

##### 3.2.1. Performance-Guided Local Motion Modulation

In areas identified as having low reference local performance ($l_{ref,m}$ below a threshold $l_{th}$), the robot's nominal behavior is modulated to potentially reduce the need for human corrections. The position ($x'_{ref,m}$) in these regions is replaced by a linear interpolation between the closest preceding and succeeding high-quality samples (Equation 4). This strategy ensures that motion in low-quality areas is improved using adjacent high-quality data, while preserving the original learned behavior in well-demonstrated areas. The resulting trajectory is then smoothed using a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter, and the reference twist ($\dot{\xi}'_{ref,m}$) is computed by numerical differentiation.

##### 3.2.2. Executing Motions with an Autonomous Dynamical System

The robot executes the modulated reference motion using an autonomous dynamical system. This system incorporates a proportional feedback control term to attract the robot's current pose to the reference pose, and a feedforward reference twist to drive the motion (Equation 5). The attraction stiffness ($K_{DS}$) defines the strength of this attraction. A crucial aspect is the speed scaling factor ($s \in [s_{min}, 1]$), which actively slows down the motion progression when the robot approaches regions of low local performance (Equation 6). This reduced velocity serves as an implicit indicator to the user that the robot requires assistance or correction in that specific segment, providing more reaction time. The speed factor can also be used to provide explicit visual cues (e.g., LED feedback). The coupling of motion progression to pose alignment is achieved by continuously identifying the closest reference pose using a k-d tree algorithm, ensuring the dynamical system is time-invariant.

##### 3.2.3. User Corrections through Autonomy Arbitration

To enable human intervention, LOPAL employs a shared autonomy (SA) approach where the robot dynamically adjusts its control compliance in response to human-applied forces and torques (wrench $\hat{w}$). The human autonomy level ($\alpha_h \in (0, 1)$) is derived from the wrench-based autonomy value ($\alpha_w$) through a recursive filter (Equations 7, 8, and 9). This filter is designed to increase human autonomy rapidly upon force application and decrease it more slowly when force is released, accommodating transient inputs versus persistent intent. The `tanh` function in Equation 7 determines $\alpha_w$ based on the magnitude of the applied wrench. The resulting $\alpha_h$ then modulates the stiffness ($K$) and damping ($D$) of a Cartesian variable impedance controller, with a lower $\alpha_h$ leading to higher robot stiffness and vice versa. An energy-tank-based approach is used to ensure the passivity and safety of the controller during human-robot interaction. During this interaction, the robot records the corrected pose and any incurred penalties, expanding the demonstration dataset for subsequent learning iterations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of LOPAL was evaluated through both a simulation study and a real-world experiment, with statistical analyses performed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and rank-biserial correlation ($r$) for effect sizes.

#### 4.1. Simulation Evaluation (Car Driving Task)

The simulation involved a 2D car driving task where the objective was to follow a track without incurring penalties for leaving the road. The evaluation focused on the ability to avoid local penalties and learn efficiently from an incrementally expanding set of imperfect demonstrations.

- **Comparison with TLFSD:** Trajectory Learning from Failed and Successful Demonstrations (TLFSD) often performed poorly, generating more penalties than the imperfect demonstrations if no entirely penalty-free demonstration was available. This was attributed to its strategy of maximizing distance from "failed" global demonstrations.
- **Performance of LfD Methods:**
 - **GMR (Vanilla):** Without leveraging performance information, GMR was prone to replicating low-performance behaviors present in demonstrations due to its averaging nature.
 - **P-GMR (Pruned):** Leveraging local performance by pruning penalized samples.
 - **LP-GMR (Local Performance-Aware):** Encoding local performance directly into the GMM.
 - **LP-GMR+I (LP-GMR with Interpolation):** Extending LP-GMR by interpolating low-performance regions.
 - Both P-GMR and LP-GMR significantly reduced penalties compared to GMR (p < 0.001, smallest $|r| = 0.98$). This was attributed to their capacity to utilize complementary high-quality local demonstration data.
 - LP-GMR+I further improved learning efficiency, particularly with only one or two demonstrations, by additionally reducing penalties through interpolation of low-quality regions (p < 0.006, smallest $|r| = 0.73$).
- **Comparison with RLPD:** Reinforcement Learning from Prior Data (RLPD) achieved penalty-free performance but necessitated extensive exploration, requiring 430 ± 140 additional exploration episodes (129,013 ± 46,852 steps). Such exploration is considered impractical and potentially unsafe for real-world deployments.

The simulation results indicated that the proposed LfD approach, particularly with local performance awareness and interpolation, enhanced learning efficiency from imperfect demonstrations by selectively leveraging high-quality local data.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiment (Pipe Inspection Task)

A real-world experiment was conducted on a Franka Research 3 robot, where participants kinesthetically taught the robot a pipe inspection task, requiring the end-effector-mounted probe to maintain contact with an inclined copper pipe without penalty.

##### 4.2.1. Comparison of LfD Approaches (Autonomous Execution)

The LfD methods (GMR, P-GMR, LP-GMR, LP-GMR+I) were compared based on their ability to generate high-performance behaviors as the number of available demonstrations increased incrementally (evaluated on six datasets of four demonstrations).

- **Task Performance:** LfD approaches incorporating local performance information (P-GMR, LP-GMR, LP-GMR+I) generally achieved higher task performance with fewer demonstrations compared to the GMR baseline.
- **LP-GMR+I Performance:** LP-GMR+I consistently demonstrated superior performance over GMR (p = 0.031, $|r| = 1$), yielding an average performance improvement of up to 27.31%. With only a single demonstration available, LP-GMR+I's interpolation mechanism provided further performance benefits by reducing low-performance areas compared to P-GMR and LP-GMR (p = 0.031, smallest $|r| = 1$).

##### 4.2.2. Comparison of Teaching Approaches (User Interaction)

Four kinesthetic teaching methods were compared: Gravity Compensation (GC), Shared Autonomy with GMR (SA + GMR), Shared Autonomy with P-GMR (SA + P-GMR), and LOPAL (ours).

- **Physical Workload:** Participants reported reduced perceived physical task load with SA + P-GMR and LOPAL (p < 0.028, smallest $|r| = 0.83$) compared to GC and SA + GMR. Quantitatively, GC and SA + GMR resulted in higher energy injected by the user (p < 0.023, smallest $|r| = 0.89$). This suggests that leveraging local performance information significantly reduced the physical burden on the user.
- **Force Application:** In the initial teaching trial, the maximum force applied by users was lower with LOPAL than with SA + P-GMR (p = 0.039, $|r| = 0.83$). This was attributed to LOPAL's speed modulation, which proactively slows the robot in low-quality regions, potentially reducing the need for users to apply large forces to slow the robot manually.
- **Mental Workload:** Participants reported lower mental demand with LOPAL compared to the other SA-based teaching variants (SA + GMR and SA + P-GMR) (p < 0.027, smallest $|r| = 0.90$). This was hypothesized to be due to LOPAL's active user feedback mechanisms (speed reduction, visual cues) guiding corrections to the most critical areas.

The real-world experiment corroborated the simulation findings, demonstrating that local quality information enhances LfD effectiveness and that interpolating low-performance regions further boosts performance. Furthermore, LOPAL was shown to significantly reduce physical teaching effort and, through active feedback, decrease the mental load for the user during incremental teaching.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents LOPAL, a local performance-aware active learning framework, which contributes to the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD) by addressing a core challenge: the inconsistent quality of human demonstrations, particularly the variability within individual demonstrations.

The significance of LOPAL lies in its dual approach:
1. **Enhanced Learning from Imperfect Data:** By explicitly encoding and leveraging local performance information, LOPAL enables the robot to synthesize trajectories that surpass the quality of any single human demonstration. It achieves this by intelligently combining high-performing segments from multiple imperfect examples, rather than merely averaging behaviors or discarding entire demonstrations. This capability allows for more robust skill acquisition in scenarios where entirely perfect demonstrations are difficult or impossible to provide.
2. **Reduced User Burden and Improved Teaching Efficiency:** LOPAL's active data acquisition mechanism reduces the physical and mental effort required from human users during the teaching process. By proactively identifying regions where high-quality data is missing and signaling the need for correction (e.g., through speed modulation and visual cues), the system guides the user to provide targeted input. This strategy minimizes redundant or unnecessary demonstrations, focusing human effort on critical areas and leading to more efficient learning.

The validation through both simulation and real-world robotic experiments, particularly the pipe inspection task, demonstrates the practical applicability and benefits of LOPAL. The observed improvements in task performance (up to 27.31%) and reductions in perceived physical and mental workload highlight its potential to foster more effective and intuitive human-robot collaboration. This approach is particularly relevant for complex, long-duration, or safety-critical tasks where human performance can naturally fluctuate, and extensive autonomous exploration is impractical or dangerous.

Potential future impacts include the broader adoption of LfD in real-world industrial and service robotics applications, where teaching new skills efficiently and safely is paramount. The framework's ability to learn from suboptimal examples also opens avenues for less skilled users to teach robots, lowering the barrier to entry for robot programming. Future research could explore the application of LOPAL with alternative quality measures to expand its applicability to a wider range of tasks or extend the approach to more complex state spaces, such as dual-arm manipulation.
