# Certified Self-Consistency: Statistical Guarantees and Test-Time Training for Reliable Reasoning in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Imperial College London developed a statistical framework providing formal guarantees for the reliability of majority voting in Large Language Models for reasoning tasks, while also explaining the mechanism of test-time training to enhance this reliability. The work introduces an adaptive stopping rule to reduce inference-time computation and demonstrates that internal uncertainty measures correlate with problem difficulty.

## Problem

- Existing empirical improvements in LLM reasoning reliability from methods like self-consistency and test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) lack thorough statistical understanding and formal guarantees.
- Quantifying the confidence that an LLM's aggregated output (via majority vote) truly represents the Bayes-optimal decision remains a challenge.
- Adaptive sampling strategies for LLM inference often use heuristic stopping rules, leading to suboptimal computational cost without principled statistical guarantees.

## Method

- A unified ==statistical framework== was developed to formalize LLM stochastic rollouts and their terminal answer distributions, enabling quantification of the error probability of ==majority voting==.
- The **MMC** (==Martingale Majority Certificate==), an ==anytime-valid sequential stopping rule==, was introduced to adaptively determine sufficient rollouts for majority voting while guaranteeing a specified error probability.
- It was formally proven that ==KL-regularized test-time training== objectives induce an ==exponential tilting== of the terminal answer distribution, sharpening the mode and improving sample efficiency, leading to new ==SNR- and entropy-based training rewards==.

## Results

- Formal finite-sample and asymptotic concentration bounds for majority voting were derived, accurately predicting the exponential decay of error probability.
- The Martingale Majority Certificate adaptively reduced the number of samples required for certification by up to **30%** (e.g., from **100 to 67.2 samples** for Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500) compared to fixed budgets, without accuracy degradation.
- Test-time training with new SNR-based and entropy-based objectives improved LLM pass@1 performance (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B on AIME improved from **10.6 to 38.3** with entropy reward) and further decreased adaptive sample requirements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The error probability of majority voting in LLMs decays exponentially with the number of samples, with the rate governed by the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of the mode margin.
> - Test-time training effectively improves reliability by exponentially tilting the LLM's terminal answer distribution, sharpening its mode and thus increasing the SNR.
> - The estimated SNR of the margin variable serves as an emergent, label-free proxy for an LLM's epistemic uncertainty and correlates with external measures of problem difficulty.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the increasing performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) on reasoning tasks, particularly those leveraging Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. While CoT and techniques like self-consistency (majority voting over multiple reasoning trajectories) and test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) have empirically improved LLM reliability and accuracy, their underlying statistical mechanisms and guarantees have not been thoroughly understood.

The existing landscape of test-time scaling strategies for LLMs includes:
- **External Verifier Approaches:** Methods like Best-of-N (BoN) that rank outputs using an external proof checker or another model.
- **Aggregation in Absence of Verifiers:** Self-consistency (majority voting) for combining multiple samples.
- **Trajectory Extension:** Budget forcing or multi-hop reasoning.
- **Self-Evaluation Strategies:** Tree of Thoughts, Beam Search, and Monte-Carlo Tree Search, where the model explores and self-evaluates reasoning branches.
- **Adaptive Self-Consistency:** Dynamic stopping rules based on model uncertainty or rollout agreement, aiming to reduce test-time compute. However, these often lack formal statistical guarantees.
- **Test-Time Training (TTRL):** Adapting model parameters online using self-generated rewards and KL-regularized objectives to sharpen predictions and improve reliability. The mechanism behind these empirical improvements has been largely opaque.

This paper positions itself by providing a unified statistical framework that connects and explains two central test-time scaling strategies: self-consistency and TTRL. It moves beyond empirical observations by deriving statistical guarantees for majority voting, introducing an anytime-valid stopping rule for certification, and formally explaining the mechanism of TTRL through the lens of exponential tilting and mode sharpening. The work aims to provide principled, certifiable reliability for reasoning LLMs, contrasting with heuristic adaptive-sampling strategies and clarifying the theoretical underpinnings of test-time adaptation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework for certifiable inference in LLMs, specifically for reasoning tasks, by providing statistical guarantees for their outputs. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations:

- **Understanding Reliability Mechanisms:** While methods like self-consistency and TTRL empirically enhance LLM reliability, the precise statistical mechanisms driving these improvements have been poorly understood. The authors aim to provide a theoretical foundation for these observed benefits.
- **Quantifying Confidence in LLM Outputs:** In reasoning tasks, LLMs often exhibit "epistemic uncertainty" over their final outputs, even if their intermediate reasoning steps are diverse. Recovering the Bayes-optimal decision (the mode of the terminal answer distribution) is crucial for reliable prediction under symmetric loss. The paper seeks to quantify the confidence that a model's aggregated answer (via majority vote) truly coincides with this mode, thereby providing a "statistical certificate of self-consistency."
- **Optimizing Inference-Time Compute:** Generating multiple LLM rollouts for aggregation (e.g., in self-consistency) incurs significant computational cost. A key motivation is to reduce this cost by adaptively determining when sufficient samples have been drawn to achieve a desired confidence level. This necessitates principled stopping rules rather than fixed sampling budgets.
- **Explaining and Improving Test-Time Adaptation:** Test-time training methods like TTRL have shown empirical success but their mechanism for improving reliability remained unexplained. The authors aim to formalize how such methods reshape the LLM's output distribution to enhance reliability and reduce the number of samples required for certification. This understanding also motivates the development of new, more targeted post-training objectives.
- **Developing Label-Free Reliability Indicators:** The ability for LLMs to "know when they do not know" without external supervision is valuable for dynamic resource allocation and understanding model behavior. The research is motivated by finding internal, label-free metrics that correlate with model uncertainty and task difficulty.

Specifically, the paper outlines four key contributions that address these motivations:
1. **Deriving Statistical Certificates:** To quantify the error probability of majority voting, providing explicit finite-sample and asymptotic concentration bounds.
2. **Introducing Anytime-Valid Stopping Certificates:** To enable adaptive sampling for majority voting, guaranteeing a specified error probability at the (random) stopping time.
3. **Explaining TTRL's Mechanism:** To formalize how KL-regularized TTRL objectives lead to exponential tilting of the terminal answer distribution, thereby improving reliability. This also inspires new post-training objectives.
4. **Identifying a Link between Uncertainty and Difficulty:** To demonstrate an empirical correlation between an internal statistical metric (Signal-to-Noise Ratio of the margin variable) and external measures of problem difficulty, suggesting emergent calibration in LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors develop a unified statistical framework to analyze and improve the reliability of LLM reasoning. The methodology encompasses theoretical derivations, algorithmic contributions, and empirical validation.

#### 3.1. Formalizing LLM Rollouts and Terminal Distribution

The work formalizes an LLM rollout as a stochastic decoding process $(Y_t)_{t \ge 0}$, where $Y_t$ is a token sampled from a conditional policy $\pi_\phi(\cdot | Y_{<t}, \text{pr})$. A deterministic extraction map $g$ is applied at a random stopping time $\tau$ to yield a canonicalized terminal answer $X = g(Y_{\tau:}) \in \mathcal{A}$. The induced terminal distribution $p = \text{Law}(X)$ over the answer set $\mathcal{A}$ quantifies the model's epistemic uncertainty. The Bayes-optimal decision, minimizing 0-1 loss, is identified as the mode $c_⋆ = \arg \max_j p_j$.

#### 3.2. Statistical Guarantees for Majority Voting

The paper views majority voting ($ \hat{c}_n = \arg \max_j \hat{p}_{n,j} $) over $n$ independent rollouts as the Bayes-optimal estimator of $c_⋆$ under 0-1 loss. It then derives statistical certificates by quantifying the error probability $P[\hat{c}_n \ne c_⋆]$.
- **Exact Error Probability:** For small sample sizes ($n \lesssim 50$), an exact multinomial expression for $P[\hat{c}_n \ne c_⋆]$ is provided, computable via dynamic programming.
- **Finite-Sample Certificates:** Leveraging Hoeffding, Bernstein, and Chernoff–Markov concentration bounds, the authors derive exponentially decaying error bounds valid for any finite $n$. These bounds highlight the crucial role of the `mode margin` $\delta = p_{c_⋆} - p_{j_⋆}$ (the gap between the top two answer probabilities) and the variance of margin random variables.
- **Asymptotic Consistency:** For large $n$, the paper employs two complementary asymptotic lenses:
 - **Gaussian/CLT Regime:** Using the Central Limit Theorem to approximate multinomial counts, yielding normal tail approximations for pairwise margins and highlighting the dependence on the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR).
 - **Large-Deviations (Sanov/Cramér) Regime:** Characterizing the exact first-order exponent of the error probability, $P[\hat{c}_n \ne c_⋆] = \exp(-nI_⋆(p) + o(n))$, where $I_⋆(p)$ is the minimal KL divergence to a distribution where a rival ties the leader. This exponent is shown to be related to the mode margin.
These theoretical bounds establish that majority voting acts as a statistical amplifier, with the error probability decaying exponentially in $n$, governed by the SNR of the margin variable $\Delta_{j_⋆}$.

#### 3.3. Martingale Majority Certificate (MMC): An Anytime-Valid Stopping Rule

To address the practical challenge of determining sufficient samples on the fly, the authors introduce the MMC. This sequential procedure adaptively decides when to stop sampling rollouts while controlling the error of the empirical majority vote.
- **Adaptive Testing:** MMC updates after each new sample and stops when empirical evidence is sufficient to guarantee $P[\hat{c}_{n_\tau} \ne c_⋆] \le \varepsilon$ at the (random) stopping time $n_\tau$.
- **e-Values and Ville's Inequality:** The procedure is based on e-values and Ville’s inequality, ensuring anytime-valid tests.
- **Two-Test Strategy:** Given the potentially large number of output classes, MMC aggregates votes into categories: the current leader, the top-($m-1$) runner-ups, and all others. It performs two types of tests: leader vs. top-($m-1$) runner-ups, and leader vs. aggregated others. The paper focuses on $m=2$ for simplicity but notes generalizability.
- **Priors for e-Processes:** Two practical priors are introduced for computing the e-values: a truncated Beta(a,b) prior and an updating plug-in point prior, which are designed to be predictable and data-dependent.

#### 3.4. Optimizing Sample Efficiency Through Test-Time Training

The paper investigates how test-time adaptation can reshape the terminal distribution to enlarge the mode margin $\delta$ and reduce the number of samples required for MMC stopping.
- **Explaining TTRL:** The authors prove that KL-regularized objectives used in methods like TTRL correspond to an exponential tilting of the terminal law $p$. This tilting sharpens the distribution around its mode, monotonically increasing the SNR of the margin variable $\Delta_{j_⋆}$ and thereby reducing the required samples for certification.
- **New Post-Training Objectives:** Building on this insight, two new label-free, group-level rewards are proposed for test-time training:
 - **SNR-based reward:** Directly maximizes the empirical SNR, aiming to minimize the expected number of samples for certification.
 - **Entropy-based reward:** Minimizes the Shannon entropy of the answer distribution, encouraging a sharper, lower-entropy distribution.
- **KL Regularization and Variance Reduction:** Both new objectives are used within a KL-regularized variational framework. To address high variance in gradient estimates for group-level rewards, a leave-one-out control variate approach is adopted. The GRPO algorithm is used for post-training.

#### 3.5. SNR as a Label-Free Estimator of Task Difficulty

The estimated SNR of the margin variable is proposed as an internal proxy for epistemic uncertainty and problem difficulty. This empirical link is investigated by correlating SNR values from model rollouts with external measures of problem difficulty.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

The methodology includes empirical evaluation using various LLMs (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct) across mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2024, AMC, MATH-500). Performance is measured using pass@1 and majority vote accuracy. The VERL framework with GRPO is used for test-time training, with specific hyperparameters detailed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several findings across theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation:

#### 4.1. Statistical Guarantees for Majority Voting

- **Formal Bounds:** The paper successfully derives finite-sample concentration bounds (Hoeffding, Bernstein, Chernoff-Markov) and asymptotic rates (CLT, large-deviations/Sanov) for the error probability $P[\hat{c}_n \ne c_⋆]$. These bounds quantify how reliability improves with ensemble size $n$ and how it depends on the mode margin $\delta$.
- **SNR as a Governing Rate:** Both the CLT and large-deviation analyses highlight that the exponential decay rate of the error probability is governed by the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the margin variable $\Delta_{j_⋆} = 1\{X = c_⋆\} - 1\{X = j_⋆\}$. A larger SNR implies faster error decay and higher reliability.
- **Numerical Validation:** Numerical experiments on synthetic data confirm that the derived bounds (especially the CLT bound with continuity and Berry–Esseen corrections, and Sanov with Bahadur-Rao correction) provide accurate estimates of the error probability, becoming tighter with increasing sample sizes.

#### 4.2. Efficacy of the Martingale Majority Certificate (MMC)

- **Anytime-Valid Certification:** The MMC provides a sequential stopping rule that adaptively determines when sufficient rollouts have been drawn to guarantee $P[\hat{c}_{n_\tau} \ne c_⋆] \le \varepsilon$. This offers formal, time-uniform error control, moving beyond heuristic stopping criteria.
- **Reduced Sample Requirements:** Empirical results on the MATH-500 dataset demonstrate that MMC adaptive sampling substantially reduces the number of samples ($N_{\text{adaptive}}$) required compared to using a fixed full sample budget ($N_{\text{budget}}$), without a noticeable degradation in majority vote accuracy (Table 2). For instance, for Qwen2.5-Math-7B, $N_{\text{adaptive}}$ was around 67.2 for a budget of 100 samples with $\varepsilon=0.1$ after test-time training, versus 74.9 for the pre-trained model.
- **Prior Performance:** Comparing different priors for MMC (truncated Beta vs. updating point priors on synthetic data), the updating point prior with shared parameters was found to require fewer votes to achieve statistical guarantees for the same probability gap.

#### 4.3. Explanation and Improvement of Test-Time Training (TTRL)

- **Mechanism Clarification:** The paper formally proves that KL-regularized TTRL objectives, including the proposed SNR-based and entropy-based rewards, lead to an exponential tilting of the terminal distribution. This tilting effectively sharpens the distribution around its mode, thereby increasing the mode margin and the SNR.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** This sharpening directly translates to a reduction in the number of samples required for the MMC to certify reliability. Table 2 shows that test-time training with SNR-based rewards further decreases $N_{\text{adaptive}}$ compared to the pre-trained model across different confidence levels $\varepsilon$. The estimated linear regression coefficient $\beta$ (representing $N_{\text{adaptive}}/N_{\text{budget}}$) was consistently lower for test-time trained models (Table 5).
- **Enhanced Performance:** The proposed SNR-based and entropy-based rewards, when used in test-time training, consistently improve pass@1 performance across various LLMs and benchmarks (Table 1). For example, Qwen2.5-Math-7B's pass@1 on AIME improved from 10.6 to 36.7 (SNR) and 38.3 (Entropy). These improvements were particularly pronounced for smaller models (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B).
- **Smoother Convergence:** The SNR- and entropy-based rewards provided smoother signals of consensus, leading to faster and more stable convergence of the RL-loss function compared to the original TTRL reward.
- **Targeted Sharpening:** The entropy reward is shown to temper only the distribution of final answers, preserving the reasoning trajectory law, which promotes certainty in the final answer while allowing for diverse reasoning paths.

#### 4.4. SNR as a Label-Free Estimator of Task Difficulty

- **Emergent Calibration:** The empirical signal-to-noise ratio (estimated SNR($\Delta_{j_⋆}$)) of the margin variable correlates strongly with externally defined problem difficulty levels on the MATH-500 benchmark (Figure 2c, 2d, 8, 9). Harder problems consistently exhibit lower SNR values and greater variability, while easier problems show high SNR.
- **Uncertainty Proxy:** This suggests an emergent form of calibration: LLMs appear to quantify their own epistemic uncertainty (via SNR) in a way that aligns with ground-truth difficulty, even without explicit supervision or difficulty labels. This can serve as a practical signal for compute-adaptive inference.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "CERTIFIED SELF-CONSISTENCY" contributes to the field by providing a principled, statistical foundation for understanding and enhancing the reliability of reasoning in Large Language Models. Its significance and potential impact span theoretical understanding, practical applications, and future research directions:

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

- **Unified Statistical Framework:** The paper unifies disparate strands of work—classical concentration theory, martingale testing, and test-time reinforcement learning—under a common statistical lens. This provides a coherent framework for analyzing self-consistency and test-time adaptation.
- **Formal Guarantees for Reliability:** By deriving explicit finite-sample and asymptotic concentration bounds, the work offers rigorous statistical certificates for the reliability of majority voting in LLMs. This moves beyond empirical observations to provide quantitative, confidence-backed guarantees that the aggregated answer coincides with the model's most probable prediction.
- **Mechanistic Explanation for TTRL:** The formal proof that KL-regularized TTRL objectives induce an exponential tilting of the terminal distribution provides a theoretical explanation for the observed empirical improvements in reliability. This clarifies *why* these methods work, by showing they sharpen the answer distribution and increase the mode margin.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact

- **Compute-Adaptive Inference:** The Martingale Majority Certificate (MMC) offers a practical, anytime-valid stopping rule for self-consistency. This allows dynamic allocation of computational resources at inference time, stopping when sufficient statistical confidence is reached, rather than relying on fixed or heuristic sampling budgets. This can significantly reduce inference costs for LLMs, especially on easier problems.
- **Improved Reliability and Accuracy:** The proposed SNR-based and entropy-based test-time training objectives directly target the statistical properties that govern certifiable reliability. Empirical results demonstrate that these objectives lead to improved accuracy (pass@1 and majority vote) and reduce the number of samples required for certification, particularly for smaller models, making LLMs more reliable in practice.
- **Label-Free Difficulty Estimation:** The discovery that the SNR of the margin variable correlates with external measures of problem difficulty provides a novel, label-free proxy for epistemic uncertainty and task difficulty. This enables LLMs to internally assess problem hardness and adapt their inference strategies accordingly (e.g., triggering more rollouts or external checks for low-SNR cases).

#### 5.3. Future Research Directions

- **Robustness to Dependencies:** The current analysis assumes conditionally independent rollouts. Future work can extend the anytime-valid certificates to scenarios with correlated rollouts, which might arise from adaptive or verifier-guided sampling strategies.
- **Generalized Output Spaces:** The current application focuses on discrete, multiple-choice outputs. Extending MMC to handle free-text answers or structured prediction would require robust response canonicalization or alternative reformulations of the certificate.
- **Improved Calibration:** Further research on calibration corrections could enhance the robustness of the SNR and entropy-based quantities, ensuring they accurately reflect true epistemic uncertainty across diverse models and decoding temperatures.
- **Integration with Learned Difficulty Estimators:** Combining MMC with more sophisticated learned difficulty estimators or hierarchical extensions could lead to more robust and scalable dynamic compute scheduling.
- **Multi-Agent and Verifier-Generator Systems:** The framework could naturally extend to more complex scenarios involving multiple agents, or systems with explicit verifiers and generators, furthering certifiable reliability in advanced LLM applications.

In summary, this paper provides a robust statistical foundation for LLM self-consistency and test-time training, offering both theoretical clarity and practical tools for building more reliable and computationally efficient reasoning systems.
