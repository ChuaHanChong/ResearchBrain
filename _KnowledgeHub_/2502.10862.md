# Accelerated co-design of robots through morphological pretraining

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework from Northwestern University accelerates robot co-design by pretraining a universal, morphology-agnostic controller using differentiable simulation. This approach enables rapid, zero-shot and few-shot exploration of morphological design spaces, facilitates successful genetic crossover, and addresses 'diversity collapse' in co-optimization.

## Problem

- Traditional robot co-design methods are computationally intensive, requiring a unique control policy to be trained for each distinct morphological design, which limits the complexity and scale of robots that can be developed.
- Prior work in co-design was often restricted to simplified robots with limited morphological complexity and behaviors due to the high computational costs of policy training without gradient information.
- Genetic crossover, a common method for innovation in biology and human engineering, has historically struggled to produce viable or superior offspring in evolutionary robotics.

## Method

- A large-scale morphological pretraining phase is introduced, where a universal, ==morphology-agnostic controller== (a Multi-Layer Perceptron) is trained using gradient information from a 3D differentiable mass-spring simulator.
- The simulator enables end-to-end backpropagation through physics and neural control, accelerating gradient-based optimization for adaptive, sensor-guided behaviors like phototaxis.
- Three evolutionary paradigms (zero-shot, few-shot, and simultaneous co-design) are employed to evaluate the pretrained controller's impact on morphological evolution and performance.

## Results

- The morphological pretraining achieved a **70%** reduction in relative distance to the target light source in diverse environments, converging in approximately 1,400 learning steps (56 minutes).
- Zero-shot evolution, using the frozen pretrained controller, converged to near-optimal performance within 100 generations (17 minutes) but exhibited morphological 'diversity collapse'. Few-shot evolution, with generational fine-tuning, preserved and increased diversity while achieving superior performance.
- The universal controller enabled successful genetic crossover, particularly with pretraining, and discovered novel locomotion patterns such as saltation (hopping) gaits, which differ from behaviors typically seen with individually optimized controllers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large-scale morphological pretraining can effectively create a universal controller capable of governing diverse robot morphologies, significantly reducing the computational bottleneck in co-design.
> - Generational fine-tuning of the pretrained controller (few-shot evolution) is crucial for maintaining and increasing morphological diversity within an evolving robot population, mitigating the 'diversity collapse' observed in fixed-controller or simultaneous co-design scenarios.
> - A robust, pretrained universal controller fundamentally changes the efficacy of genetic crossover in evolutionary robotics, enabling the recombination of designs to produce functional and sometimes superior offspring.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The co-design of robot morphology and control policies is a long-standing challenge in robotics. The objective is to develop robots whose physical structure is optimized in conjunction with their behavioral control, allowing for enhanced performance in specific environments or tasks. Historically, progress in this area has been limited due to the computational demands associated with training a unique control policy for each distinct morphological design. This process typically involves reinforcement learning (RL) in non-differentiable simulations, requiring substantial data and computational resources to evaluate each design iteration. As a result, previous work often focused on simplified robots with limited morphological complexity, simple behaviors, and often restricted to two-dimensional environments.

Existing attempts to address this challenge include approaches where RL was used to approximate universal control policies for small datasets of pre-designed or simultaneously co-designed morphologies. While these methods aimed for greater generalizability, the inherent inefficiencies of policy training without gradient information constrained their scalability. More recent research has leveraged first-order gradients from differentiable simulations to accelerate co-design, but these methods generally still required learning a custom controller for each morphology, limiting behaviors to rote actions like straight-line locomotion.

This work positions itself within this landscape by introducing a scalable approach inspired by large-scale pretraining models in fields like computer vision and natural language processing. It seeks to overcome the computational bottleneck by pretraining a *universal, morphology-agnostic controller* using gradient information derived from differentiable simulation. This strategy aims to enable rapid exploration of morphological design spaces, including non-differentiable changes such as adding or removing discrete body parts, and to facilitate successful genetic crossover, a concept that has been difficult to implement effectively in evolutionary robotics. Furthermore, the paper identifies and addresses a new challenge termed "diversity collapse," where simultaneous co-optimization of morphology and universal control can lead to a convergence of designs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to accelerate the co-design of robot morphology and neural control. This overarching goal is supported by several specific motivations and sub-objectives:

- **Overcoming Computational Bottlenecks in Co-design:** Traditional co-design methods, particularly those relying on reinforcement learning to approximate control policy gradients for each unique body plan, are computationally intensive. This "nested complexity" requires vast amounts of training data and repeated controller relearning as morphologies evolve, which has significantly hampered progress over the past three decades. The authors aim to circumvent this by leveraging differentiable simulation for gradient-based optimization.
- **Developing a Universal, Morphology-Agnostic Controller:** The core motivation is to create a single controller capable of effectively governing a wide range of robot morphologies. This universal controller would eliminate the need to train a bespoke controller for every new design, thereby drastically reducing the computational cost of evaluating different body plans. The inspiration for this comes from the success of large-scale pretrained models in other AI domains.
- **Enabling Rapid Exploration of Non-Differentiable Morphological Changes:** With a pretrained universal controller, designers could explore substantial, non-differentiable alterations to a robot's physical layout—such as adding, removing, or recombining discrete body parts—and immediately assess their impact without incurring the cost of new controller training. This approach, termed "zero-shot evolution," seeks to make morphological exploration more efficient and practical.
- **Facilitating Successful Genetic Crossover:** Biological evolution and human engineering frequently benefit from the recombination of successful designs (crossover). In evolutionary robotics, however, crossover has rarely been shown to produce offspring with equal or superior fitness, often resulting in non-viable or inferior designs. A key motivation is to demonstrate how a pretrained universal controller can enable successful crossover, leading to more effective design innovation.
- **Addressing "Diversity Collapse" in Co-optimization:** The paper identifies a previously uncharacterized pathology in the simultaneous co-optimization of a universal controller alongside an evolving population of designs: "diversity collapse." This phenomenon causes the design population to converge to morphologically similar designs that are easier for a shared controller to manage, thereby limiting the exploration of the design space. A critical objective is to understand and provide a solution for maintaining morphological diversity during co-design.
- **Scaling Co-design to Complex Robots:** Most prior work was limited to simple robots due to computational constraints. This research is motivated by the desire to apply co-design to complex robots with thousands of independent motors, enabling adaptive, sensor-guided behaviors in challenging, varied environments (e.g., phototaxis across rugged terrains).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a pipeline for accelerated robot co-design centered on morphological pretraining and differentiable simulation, evaluated through various evolutionary strategies.

#### 3.1. Morphospace and Robot Representation

Robot morphologies are encoded as contiguous collections of voxels within a 6x6x4 binary genotype workspace. This genotype maps to a phenotype of masses and springs arranged in a cubic lattice (10 cm unit cells). Each active voxel expresses eight masses at its corners and springs along its edges and planar diagonals. Adjacency rules ensure cohesive structures. The workspace supports up to 245 mass positions and 1648 springs. Robots are centered by mass and shifted to the bottom for consistent ground contact. An equivalence relation identifies unique morphologies by accounting for translations, 90° z-axis rotations, and x/y-axis reflections.

#### 3.2. Differentiable Simulation

The 2D differentiable mass-spring simulator (Hu et al., 2020; Strgar et al., 2024) is extended to three dimensions and includes exteroception for light sensing.
- **Actuation:** Spring rest lengths are continuously actuated within ±20% of their initial values. Forces are computed using Hooke’s law ($F = k(L - L_0)$, with $k = 1.5 \times 10^4$ N/m).
- **Environment:** Terrains are modeled using randomly sampled 8x8 height maps with Gaussian-distributed values for varied ruggedness. Light sources are randomly positioned.
- **Physics Engine:** The simulator calculates impulses, damping, and gravitational forces to update mass velocities and positions.
- **Collision Handling:** When a mass's z-coordinate falls below terrain height, impact time is estimated via iterative bisection, velocity is projected onto the surface normal, and constrained motion is applied. Friction is modeled by negating and clamping tangential velocity.
- **Implementation:** Developed in Taichi, enabling GPU acceleration for parallel simulation and automatic differentiation. This allows end-to-end backpropagation through 1000 timesteps (0.004s each) of physics simulation and neural control for gradient-based optimization.

#### 3.3. Universal Controller

A simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) acts as the universal controller for adaptive phototaxis (movement towards a light source).
- **Inputs:** Two streams: (1) Photosensor readings from masses (light intensity, inverse square law) normalized by the mean of active sensors to provide a zero-centered irradiance gradient. (2) Central Pattern Generator (CPG) inputs (five sinusoidal waves, $\omega = 10$ rad/s, evenly spaced phase offsets).
- **Architecture:** Input layer (250 dimensions: 245 max masses + 5 CPG inputs), three hidden layers (256 dimensions each with layer normalization and ReLU), and an output layer (1648 dimensions: max springs, with tanh activation).
- **Morphological Conditioning:** Sensors and actuators not present in a specific robot's body have their corresponding input/output signals masked to zero.
- **Parameters:** 620,912 learnable parameters, initialized with Xavier uniform distribution, optimized using Adam (learning rate scheduled with cosine annealing with restarts), and gradient norm clipping.

#### 3.4. Morphological Pretraining

The universal controller is initially pretrained on a dataset exceeding 10 million distinct robot morphologies, each paired with a randomly generated terrain and light source.
- **Objective:** Minimize the batch mean of $d_1/d_0$, where $d_1$ is the final distance and $d_0$ is the initial distance from the robot to its target light source. This relative formulation rewards both large-scale movement and fine-grained control.
- **Training Details:** 1400 learning steps with a batch size of 8192 (1024 per H100 GPU). Each sample (robot, terrain, light source) is unique and seen once. Cosine annealing with restarts is used for the learning rate.

#### 3.5. Evolutionary Algorithms

Three distinct evolutionary paradigms are employed:

- **Zero-shot Evolution:**
 - Utilizes the *frozen* pretrained universal controller.
 - Population: 8192 random morphologies, evaluated on a fixed set of 10 test environments.
 - Genetic Algorithm: Offspring are produced, evaluated, and the top 50% (parents + offspring) form the next generation.
 - Variation Operators:
 - **Mutation:** 25% of population mutate via random bit flips (p=1/N) on their genotype, followed by post-processing to retain the largest connected component, center, and ensure uniqueness.
 - **Recombination (Crossover):** 75% of the population are paired, and offspring genotypes are created using a bitwise XOR operation, followed by similar post-processing and uniqueness checks.

- **Few-shot Evolution:**
 - Extends zero-shot by *fine-tuning* the pretrained controller for the current population at every generation.
 - Procedure: Each generation receives 60 fine-tuning steps (30 for parents, 30 for offspring) *before* evaluation.
 - Controller State: Weights are reset to their pretrained values, and the optimizer state is reinitialized at the start of each generation.
 - Learning Rate: Cosine annealing schedule for fine-tuning.

- **Simultaneous Co-design (from scratch):**
 - Removes morphological pretraining.
 - The robot population and universal controller are co-optimized from scratch.
 - Controller State: Parameters and optimizer state are *inherited* across generations (not reset).
 - Training: Only 2 learning steps per generation (1 for parents, 1 for offspring) to maintain parity with pretraining batch uniqueness.
 - Learning Rate: Modified cosine annealing schedule.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Environments

A fixed set of 10 challenging test environments is used for evaluating evolutionary runs. These environments feature light sources placed in two concentric rings (radii 1.5m and 2.0m) around the robot's starting position, combined with terrains of five uniformly spaced difficulty levels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the performance and diversity outcomes of morphological pretraining and three distinct evolutionary co-design paradigms.

#### 4.1. Pretraining Performance

The morphological pretraining phase demonstrated stable learning, achieving convergence in approximately 1,400 learning steps (56 minutes). The initial loss ratio of 1.0 (indicating no movement toward the target) improved to approximately 0.3, representing a 70% reduction in relative distance to the light source. This indicated that the pretrained universal controller could enable robots to traverse 70% of their initial distance to a light source in diverse, randomly sampled training environments. The low variance across independent trials suggested robust learning. The pretrained controller exhibited generalization capabilities across a wide range of previously unseen and morphologically diverse robot designs.

#### 4.2. Zero-shot Evolution (with Pretraining)

Zero-shot evolution, which utilized the frozen pretrained universal controller, achieved rapid convergence to near-optimal performance within 100 generations (17 minutes). However, this improvement in performance was accompanied by a decrease in morphological diversity within the population. This phenomenon, termed "diversity collapse," was measured by the mean pairwise Hamming distance of genotypes. The population gradually homogenized, suggesting that evolution converged to designs that were most compatible with the fixed pretrained controller.

#### 4.3. Few-shot Evolution (with Pretraining and Generational Finetuning)

Few-shot evolution, which involved fine-tuning the pretrained controller at each generation, demonstrated a different dynamic. This approach not only preserved morphological diversity but significantly increased it over time, even in the absence of an explicit selection pressure for diversity. This suggested that adaptive finetuning of the controller allowed the evolutionary algorithm to explore a broader range of morphological innovations without compromising functionality. The process achieved superior performance compared to both zero-shot evolution and simultaneous co-design.

#### 4.4. Simultaneous Co-design (without Pretraining)

Simultaneous co-design, which involved co-optimizing morphology and a universal controller from scratch without pretraining, also resulted in rapid diversity collapse. Performance plateaued in fewer than 180 generations (109 minutes), with the evolved designs exhibiting morphological homogeneity.

#### 4.5. Success of Crossover and Discovered Gaits

Universal control, particularly when aided by pretraining, was shown to enable successful crossover operations. In zero-shot and few-shot evolution, crossover initially proved more successful than mutation in producing viable offspring. In simultaneous co-design, while crossover did produce offspring, their performance was not demonstrably better than random designs due to the initially unoptimized controller. The universal controller also discovered novel locomotion patterns, specifically saltation (hopping) gaits, which differed from typical walking or ambling behaviors observed in individually optimized controllers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotics and artificial intelligence:

- **Accelerated and Scalable Robot Co-design:** The introduction of large-scale morphological pretraining combined with differentiable simulation provides a framework for dramatically accelerating the co-design process. This approach bypasses the need for computationally expensive, morphology-specific controller training, enabling the design of complex robots with thousands of motors and adaptive, sensor-guided behaviors in minutes or hours, rather than days or weeks. This represents a significant advancement over previous limitations in robot complexity and behavioral sophistication.
- **Enabling Successful Crossover:** The work demonstrates a method for achieving successful genetic crossover in robots, a capability that has been a long-standing challenge in evolutionary robotics. By pretraining a robust universal controller, the probability of creating functional offspring from recombined parent morphologies is increased, which could lead to more efficient and innovative exploration of the design space, akin to biological evolution and human engineering practices.
- **Identification and Solution for Diversity Collapse:** The identification of "diversity collapse" as a pathology in universal control co-design is a novel theoretical contribution. The proposed solution of generational fine-tuning not only preserves but actively increases morphological diversity, which is crucial for fostering robust and adaptable robot populations. This finding informs future research on maintaining diversity in co-evolutionary systems.
- **A New Design Paradigm:** The "zero-shot" and "few-shot" evolution paradigms offer designers a new way to interact with robot design. By leveraging a pretrained model, designers can rapidly evaluate the impact of substantial morphological changes without the need to re-engineer control policies. This allows for more intuitive and iterative exploration of non-differentiable design changes.
- **Leveraging Deep Learning Advancements:** The approach demonstrates how infrastructure and theoretical advancements from deep learning, particularly large-scale pretraining, can be effectively leveraged in robot co-design. This suggests a broader integration of deep learning methodologies into robotics for enhanced design and control capabilities.
- **Future Research Avenues:** The work opens multiple avenues for future research, including extending the approach to multi-material robots, multi-modal sensing, and multi-task behaviors. It also highlights the potential for more sophisticated genotype-to-phenotype mappings, the development of diverse morphological and behavioral metrics, and strategies for bridging the simulation-reality gap, such as incorporating noise models or learned residual physics. The concept of a universal controller inherently being robust to variations in morphology and environment also suggests a potential self-correction mechanism for sim-to-real transfer.
