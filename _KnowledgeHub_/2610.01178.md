# Recova: Agent-Guided Failure Recovery for Autonomous Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Recova is an agent-guided framework that learns failure recovery for robot manipulation. A coding agent builds a MuJoCo digital twin of the workstation, finds failures there, and writes recovery programs and rollouts that train a separate recovery policy. During real deployment, a VLM monitor detects failures, invokes and verifies recoveries, and asks a human only when needed. It reaches 78.8% on LIBERO-Pro and 87.5% on real robots.

## Problem

- VLA training data mostly holds successful trajectories from standard initial states, so policies get stuck when a failure leaves objects in unfamiliar, blocking configurations.
- Exploring failures directly on robot hardware is costly, because it needs frequent human resets and risks damaging objects, so recovery data is hard to scale.
- Prior failure-detection, recovery, and DAgger systems do not connect diagnosis, correction, verification, skill retention, and routing new data to the right policy in one loop.

## Method

- Agent-built ==digital twin==: a ==coding agent== fits object poses and contact parameters in MuJoCo to recorded real trajectories, then attempts tasks and diagnoses failures there.
- Decoupled ==recovery skills==: each skill is keyed to a natural-language recovery instruction and runs as either a learned ==language-conditioned recovery policy== or a ==code-as-policy== program, so skills can be reused across tasks.
- Parallel ==DAgger== loop: a ==VLM monitor== runs monitor, detect, decide and verify steps across four stations, sending successes and human demos to the task dataset or the recovery dataset.

## Results

- LIBERO-Pro: **78.8%** mean success over **six** perturbation settings versus **71.7%** for ASPIRE, with gains of **22** and **23 points** on Goal and Spatial task perturbations.
- MolmoSpaces: **64.9%** mean across pick, pick-and-place, open and close versus **38.0%** for RATs and **36.0%** for MolmoAct2-DROID, best in all **four** categories.
- Real robots: DAgger lifts mean success from **23.8%** to **77.5%**, and recovery skills raise it to **87.5%**. On one task, human intervention falls from **87.5%** to **0%** over **four** rounds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating recovery as its own objective, conditioned on a recovery instruction instead of the task goal, lets corrective skills specialize and transfer wherever the same failure recurs.
> - Simulation is best used to find and rehearse failures safely, while real deployment verifies and refines them. Each stage covers gaps the other cannot.
> - Asking for human help only at failure handoffs, not labeling every state, lets one operator supervise several robots, and each intervention becomes a reusable skill.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Packing a pencil box can stall on a small error: a pencil lands in the wrong place, or a tray will not slide back. A robot that simply retries may leave that obstruction in place. Ordinary task demonstrations teach what to do from expected starting states. They do not teach how to undo a mistake. Even a small mistake can halt an otherwise capable policy, and the paper describes recovery as restoring a workable scene before task execution resumes.

Recova treats the blockage as a recovery problem instead of asking the task policy simply to try again. In the pencil-box task, a recovery skill can reinsert the pencil or slide the tray back into its sleeve. The authors' key move is to discover corrections in a reconstructed digital twin, then verify and improve recovery on real robots.

### 2. Give recovery its own job

Recova uses two separate policies:

- The **task policy** tries to complete the language-instructed task.
- A **recovery skill** is a distinct correction, specified by a recovery instruction.

To find corrections, Recova's coding agent reconstructs a digital twin from robot recordings. These recordings include camera observations, trajectories and, when available, camera calibration. In simulation, the agent collects successful task rollouts. When an attempt fails, it diagnoses the failure, names a recovery instruction, and writes a reusable program. It then collects recovery rollouts for a separate recovery policy. The two policies therefore have distinct roles and data.

A recovery can restore a workable scene, prepare the next step, or change the approach rather than repeat a failed one. The paper gives the example of a ring caught at an angle on a peg. The authors explicitly allow any workable scene rather than exact pose restoration. On real robots, the learned recovery policies perform autonomous recoveries, while the agent develops and tests new programs in the twin.

### 3. Does recovery change outcomes?

A skeptic might ask whether recovery adds value beyond improving the task policy with deployment data. The real-robot comparison addresses this question. It covers four tasks: pencil-box packing, ring stacking, drawing a tile, and discarding a tile. Each configuration has 20 trials per task. The reported metric is mean success, the unweighted average across the four task success rates.

- The base task policy reaches 23.8%.
- After DAgger fine-tuning, the task policy reaches 77.5%.
- With recovery skills enabled at deployment, success reaches 87.5%.

The final configuration is best on every task. The largest per-task gains are 15 points for pencil-box packing and ring stacking, while the two mahjong tasks each gain five points. This comparison tests the added recovery deployment. It does not test each component of the full framework independently.

Simulation offers corroboration. The LIBERO-Pro benchmark has six settings: Object, Goal and Spatial, each with position swaps and task perturbations. Averaged unweighted across these settings, Recova's reported mean task success is 78.8%. ASPIRE, the strongest baseline on average, reaches 71.7%. ASPIRE also discovers and reuses robot-program repairs. ([[2607.00272|ASPIRE paper]]) The baseline figures are published results, while Recova's come from its own evaluation. ASPIRE remains stronger on the Object suite, and Recova leads in all four Goal and Spatial settings.

### 4. Learning from handoffs

During deployment, a vision-language monitor checks task completion. It also compares recent scenes to detect a stalled or disturbed rollout.

1. If a trained recovery instruction applies, the recovery policy gets a bounded attempt.
1. A restoration check accepts a workable scene, which need not be the exact starting layout, before the task policy resumes.
1. If the scene is intact but the task has stalled, or recovery is unavailable or unsuccessful, a human supplies the relevant task or recovery demonstration.

The two policies learn from separate data. Successful task rollouts and task demonstrations update the task policy. Human recovery demonstrations and verified autonomous recoveries update the recovery policy. Failed policy segments are excluded from training.

On one task, drawing a mahjong tile, human takeovers fell over four collection rounds. Task success rose from 12.5% to 85.7%, and the fraction of episodes involving human intervention fell from 87.5% to 0%. That trend was observed on this task only. It is not evidence of a general intervention-free robot.

### 5. A failure becomes data

A blocked pencil box calls for more than a rescue in the moment. When a correction is verified or demonstrated, it can train the recovery capability used on later attempts. The evidence supports a benefit from adding recovery in this evaluation. Physical validation still matters, because real contacts, sensing and unanticipated failures remain.
