# VMTS: Vision-Assisted Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Terrain Locomotion in Bipedal Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Southeast University developed VMTS, a vision-assisted teacher-student reinforcement learning framework that enables bipedal robots to navigate diverse and complex terrains. This method integrates a Mixture of Experts model for terrain-adaptive control and uses contrastive learning for robust knowledge transfer, achieving superior terrain traversal and lower locomotion errors in simulation and successful real-world deployment on a Limx Dynamics P1 robot.

## Problem

- Bipedal robots face significant control complexity, especially when integrating visual perception for navigating human-centric environments.
- Existing proprioception-based reinforcement learning methods for legged robots are insufficient for effective locomotion on complex, varied terrains.
- Current vision-based teacher-student RL frameworks suffer from student-teacher input discrepancies and a lack of terrain-specific adaptation, leading to suboptimal performance across diverse conditions.

## Method

- A Vision-assisted Teacher-Student (**VMTS**) ==reinforcement learning== framework is proposed, incorporating a Mixture of Experts (MoE) model into the teacher policy for terrain-adaptive guidance.
- The student policy's learning is enhanced through implicit feature space alignment using the Barlow Twins method, facilitating robust knowledge transfer from the teacher.
- A comprehensive reward function, including a novel smooth foot-trajectory tracking component, is designed to improve stability and naturalness of locomotion and mitigate the sim-to-real gap.

## Results

- The VMTS student policy achieved superior terrain traversal (higher terrain levels) and lower velocity (**0.535**) and height (**0.071**) errors in simulation compared to Teacher-Student (TS) and PIE methods.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the Mixture of Experts and Barlow Twins modules are crucial, with their removal leading to significant performance degradation.
- Real-world experiments on a Limx Dynamics P1 robot demonstrated stable locomotion on grassland and autonomous, adaptive traversal of obstacles like stairs, showcasing the practical feasibility and robustness of the vision-assisted approach.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a Mixture of Experts model into the teacher policy allows for adaptive, terrain-specific control strategies, which significantly improves the robot's ability to handle diverse environments compared to a single generalized policy.
> - Employing contrastive learning (Barlow Twins) for implicit feature space alignment between teacher and student observations enables more robust knowledge transfer and student performance without requiring strict input similarity.
> - Optimizing visual data acquisition in simulation using NVIDIA Warp and applying extensive domain randomization are critical for efficient training and successful sim-to-real transfer for vision-assisted bipedal locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bipedal robots, by virtue of their anthropomorphic design, hold considerable potential across various applications. However, their inherent structural complexity poses significant control challenges. Furthermore, effective operation in human-centric environments necessitates visual perception, the integration of which further complicates the control task.

Historically, advanced methods for bipedal control, exemplified by Boston Dynamics' Atlas robot, often relied on odometry for online mapping and extensive offline planning. While effective for complex movements, these approaches typically exhibit limited generalization performance across diverse environments.

More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for controlling legged robots. Much of the initial success in this area has focused on proprioception-based methods. A notable development is the teacher-student network approach, where a teacher policy, trained with privileged information, guides a student policy that operates solely on proprioceptive data. This methodology enables students to achieve performance levels similar to teachers with reduced information requirements and has been widely adopted. Concurrent single-phase training methods, such as those employing Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) for implicit state estimation or incorporating privileged learning of encoded proprioceptive information, have also been explored. Despite these advancements, proprioception-based methods face a fundamental limitation: they struggle to effectively navigate complex terrains, often restricting research on bipedal robots to flat-terrain scenarios.

The incorporation of external perception, particularly vision, is considered crucial for overcoming terrain challenges. However, this also escalates the difficulty of training legged robots. In response, several vision-assisted RL solutions have been developed, broadly categorized into teacher-student and single-phase training frameworks. Single-phase methods have extended concepts like DreamWaQ by integrating depth images and VAEs or explicitly estimating height maps from visual and historical data. However, these often encounter difficulties in visual perception acquisition within simulation environments like Isaac Gym and the direct learning of environmental maps. Consequently, the teacher-student framework remains prevalent in vision-based RL for legged robots, with approaches utilizing online-generated height maps or direct depth images as input, sometimes combined with waypoints or specialized parkour techniques.

Despite these developments, existing teacher-student visual RL methods have limitations. Discrepancies between teacher and student input information can prevent the student from fully imitating the teacher's behavior. Additionally, training a single policy network for diverse terrains often overlooks potential conflicts arising under varying conditions, potentially degrading overall performance. This paper aims to address these specific challenges within the context of vision-assisted bipedal locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and adaptable control strategy for bipedal robots, enabling them to navigate diverse and complex terrains effectively using visual perception. This work is motivated by several identified challenges in the current state of bipedal robot locomotion control:

1. **Complexity of Bipedal Control:** Bipedal robots, despite their anthropomorphic advantages, present inherent control complexities. The integration of visual perception, which is essential for operation in human-centric environments, further exacerbates this difficulty, requiring sophisticated control strategies.
2. **Limitations of Proprioception-Based Reinforcement Learning:** Current reinforcement learning approaches for legged robots predominantly rely on proprioceptive feedback. While successful in various scenarios, these methods are generally insufficient for overcoming complex terrains and often limit research to flat-ground environments. This highlights a critical need for external perception to enhance terrain adaptability.
3. **Challenges in Vision-Based Reinforcement Learning Frameworks:**
 - **Single-Phase Training Limitations:** Single-phase vision-based RL approaches face substantial hurdles, including the high computational cost and difficulty of visual perception acquisition in simulation environments like Isaac Gym, as well as the inherent challenge of directly learning robust environmental maps from visual input.
 - **Teacher-Student Discrepancies:** While the teacher-student paradigm offers a structured approach, existing methods often suffer from an inability of the student policy to fully replicate the teacher's behavior due to fundamental differences in their input information. This can lead to suboptimal student performance.
 - **Lack of Terrain-Specific Adaptation:** Many current RL training methods for multi-terrain locomotion treat all terrains as a singular task, employing a uniform policy network. This overlooks the potential for performance conflicts or degradation when a single policy attempts to generalize across vastly different terrain conditions (e.g., slopes, stairs, rough ground).

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of this paper are:
- To propose a novel vision-assisted reinforcement learning framework that integrates a mixture of experts model with a teacher-student architecture. This aims to allow the teacher policy to adapt its strategy based on terrain type, thereby providing terrain-specific guidance.
- To improve the student policy's ability to navigate diverse terrains by introducing an implicit alignment mechanism between the encoded feature spaces of the teacher and student networks, rather than enforcing strict similarity. This is hypothesized to enable more effective knowledge transfer.
- To design a comprehensive reward function, including a smooth foot-trajectory tracking component, to mitigate the sim-to-real gap and enhance the stability and naturalness of robot locomotion.
- To experimentally validate the proposed approach in both simulation and real-world environments using a physical bipedal robot, demonstrating its feasibility and robustness across multiple terrain types.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces VMTS, a novel vision-assisted teacher-student reinforcement learning strategy designed for multi-terrain locomotion in bipedal robots. The framework employs a two-phase training process due to the computational demands of visual feature learning.

**A. Overall Framework Overview:**
The proposed framework comprises three main components: the teacher policy, the student policy, and a mixture of experts (MoE) model. The teacher policy is trained with privileged information from the simulation, while the student policy operates with deployable inputs (depth images and proprioception).

**B. Teacher Policy:**
1. **Inputs (`s_t`):** The teacher policy receives a comprehensive set of inputs:
 - Proprioceptive observation (`o_t`): A 29-dimensional vector including angular velocity (`w_t`), gravity vector (`g_t`) from an IMU, joint positions (`θ_t`), joint velocities (`˙θ_t`), previous action (`a_t-1`), user commands (`c_t`), and a reference contact phase (`p_t`) for the foot. The reference contact phase is a square wave function designed to aid balance in point-foot bipedal robots.
 - Surrounding height map (`m_t`): A detailed 1-meter radius height map around the robot.
 - Physical parameters (`o_p`): Accurate physical properties obtained directly from the simulator, such as mass, velocity, and friction coefficients.
2. **Optimization:** The teacher policy is optimized using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.
3. **Value Network:** A separate value network shares the same input (`s_t`) as the teacher policy, and its loss (`L_value`) is combined with the PPO loss (`L_ppo,s`) for optimization.

**C. Student Policy:**
1. **Inputs:** The student policy uses only the information available during real-world deployment:
 - Historical proprioceptive observations (`o_H_t`).
 - Depth information (`d_t`), which is processed through convolutional neural networks (CNN) and recurrent neural networks (RNN).
2. **Training:** The student policy is trained to approximate the teacher's behavior through two primary mechanisms:
 - **Supervised Learning:** The student policy's output is supervised by the teacher policy's actions (`a_t`), using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss (`MSE(ˆa_t, a_t)`).
 - **Feature Space Alignment:** Instead of directly matching encoded information, the Barlow Twins method is employed to align the encoded feature spaces of the student (`Z_a`) and teacher (`Z_b`). This contrastive learning approach focuses on reducing redundancy and achieving invariance across different views of the same underlying information.

**D. Mixture of Experts (MoE):**
1. **Integration:** A mixture of experts model is integrated into the teacher policy to enhance its performance across various terrains.
2. **Curriculum Training:** Terrains are categorized (e.g., flat ground, slopes, rough terrain, stairs) and introduced progressively in terms of difficulty during training.
3. **Expert Selection:** The system uses 4 experts. An encoder (three-layer fully connected network) processes the current state (`s_t`) and terrain category (`t_p`). Multi-head attention and a Softmax function are applied to its output to determine the weights (`a_1` to `a_4`) for each expert. These weights then modulate the outputs of the expert mixture model to derive the final teacher policy encoding.

**E. Reward Function Design:**
A comprehensive reward function, building on previous work, is designed to ensure stable and desired locomotion:
- **Primary Objectives:** Rewards for linear and angular velocity tracking, orientation stability, and maintaining a target base height.
- **Locomotion Quality:** Penalties for joint acceleration, action rate, joint torque, and collision. Rewards for foot airtime.
- **Balance and Gait:** Penalties for feet contact force (supplemented by a vertical foot velocity penalty near the ground for smooth landing), foot distance, unbalanced feet height and airtime. A reward for correct foot contact number, guided by a reference foot phase.
- **Foot Trajectory Control:** To prevent excessive foot lifting and promote smooth trajectories, a reward function component for target foot height (`r_f eet`) is introduced, defined by a sinusoidal reference trajectory.
- **Survival:** A continuous penalty for survival time.

**F. Environment Setup and Optimization:**
1. **Simulation Platform:** NVIDIA Isaac Gym is used for massively parallel training (8192 robots concurrently for both teacher and student policies).
2. **Training Time Optimization:** To address the long visual data acquisition times in Isaac Gym for student policy training, NVIDIA Warp is leveraged to store terrain mesh data. Ray-casting, based on the robot's camera pose and physical properties, is used for depth image retrieval, reducing student policy training from approximately 120 hours to 16 hours on an RTX 3090 GPU.
3. **Robot Parameters:** Key robot parameters such as PD gains, target base height, and foot end height are configured.

**G. Domain Randomization:**
To improve the model's generalization and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, threshold randomization is applied to several physical parameters during simulation training. These include PD controller gains, friction coefficients, joint masses, restitution, step delay, push velocity, and camera parameters (position, angle, FOV).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
The proposed VMTS method was evaluated against several alternative approaches in a simulation environment, including:
- **Blind:** A proprioception-only policy trained with PPO.
- **Teacher-Student (TS):** A two-phase teacher-student strategy using depth images and proprioception, as described in.
- **PIE:** A single-phase strategy that simultaneously trains the model while implicitly or explicitly predicting state information like velocity and height maps, as referenced in.

1. **Training Progress (Reward and Terrain Level):**
 - **Reward Curves (Fig. 5):** The teacher policy of VMTS (Ours-teacher) consistently achieved higher rewards during training compared to the TS-teacher policy. This improvement was propagated to the student policy, where Ours-student also showed higher rewards than TS-student. The final reward of VMTS closely approximated that of PIE, although PIE's reward emphasis was noted to be stronger on velocity terms due to its predictive nature.
 - **Terrain Level Curves (Fig. 6):** The Ours-student policy demonstrated superior terrain traversal capability, achieving higher terrain levels than both TS-student and PIE. While PIE performed better than the Blind policy, it still struggled to effectively overcome challenging terrains compared to VMTS. The Blind policy showed the lowest terrain level.

2. **Effect of Alignment Module (Barlow Twins):**
 - A specific comparison (Fig. 7) showed that integrating the Barlow Twins alignment module into the TS strategy improved its terrain level compared to the original TS strategy. However, this configuration still did not reach the performance level of the full VMTS method, indicating the distinct contribution of the MoE component in VMTS.

3. **Quantitative Evaluation (Table IV):**
 - Deployment of 128 robots across fixed proportions of terrains allowed for quantitative assessment of velocity error, height error, and survival time (within 100 seconds).
 - **VMTS (Ours)** achieved the lowest velocity error (0.535) and height error (0.071) among all compared methods, while maintaining a high survival time (88.485 s), slightly lower than "Ours w/o barlow" and "TS". This indicates superior basic user command tracking and stability.
 - **PIE** had a velocity error of 0.546 and a survival time of 87.111 s.
 - **TS** exhibited higher velocity (0.673) and height (0.097) errors, with a survival time of 88.601 s.
 - **Blind** showed the highest velocity (0.929) and height (0.076) errors, and the lowest survival time (84.675 s).
 - **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Ours w/o Moes:** Performance significantly degraded (velocity error 0.593, height error 0.093, survival time 87.011 s) compared to the full VMTS, indicating the critical role of the Mixture of Experts in handling diverse terrains.
 - **Ours w/o Barlow Twins:** Showed improved performance over the base TS policy but was slightly inferior to the full VMTS in velocity tracking (0.554 vs 0.535). Its height error was higher (0.079 vs 0.071), further supporting the positive impact of the Barlow Twins module on student policy performance and robustness.

**B. Real-World Experiments:**
The VMTS strategy was deployed on a Limx Dynamics P1 bipedal robot equipped with an IMU and a D435i depth sensor.

1. **Grassland Navigation (Fig. 8):** The robot demonstrated stable locomotion and turning on grassland. This indicates the policy's robustness to terrains that lack the consistent friction and elasticity of flat, rigid surfaces.
2. **Obstacle Traversal (Fig. 9):** Upon detecting obstacles such as stairs or elevated surfaces, the robot autonomously increased its foot lift height to clear them. After successfully traversing the obstacle, the robot lowered its foot height to restore stability. This demonstrates the vision-assisted ability to recognize and adapt gait for obstacle negotiation.
3. **Comparison with Blind Walking (Fig. 10):** Experiments illustrated that a robot operating without camera depth images (blind walking) was highly susceptible to falling when encountering unknown terrain. Specifically, the robot failed to autonomously raise its foot height, leading to unintended contact and instability. This highlights the indispensable role of visual perception, enabled by VMTS, for robust multi-terrain locomotion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of bipedal robot locomotion, particularly concerning their ability to navigate diverse and complex environments.

**1. Enhanced Bipedal Locomotion on Complex Terrains:** The VMTS framework offers a method for bipedal robots to move beyond flat-terrain limitations and successfully traverse varied obstacles like slopes, rough ground, stairs, and grasslands. This significantly broadens the operational scope of bipedal robots, making them more suitable for unstructured or human-centric environments.

**2. Improved Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning:** The work introduces innovations within the teacher-student RL paradigm:
 - **Adaptive Policy via Mixture of Experts:** By integrating a Mixture of Experts model with terrain selection, the teacher policy can adapt its behavior to specific terrain types. This addresses the limitations of a single, generalized policy, allowing for more specialized and effective control in multi-terrain scenarios.
 - **Robust Student Learning through Contrastive Alignment:** The use of Barlow Twins for implicit alignment and redundancy reduction in the observation feature space between the teacher and student is a novel contribution. This approach facilitates a more robust and effective transfer of knowledge, improving the student policy's performance on diverse terrains without requiring strict input similarity.

**3. Practical Feasibility and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The comprehensive reward function design, coupled with efficient simulation techniques like leveraging NVIDIA Warp for optimized depth image acquisition and extensive domain randomization, contributes to a reduced sim-to-real gap. The successful deployment and validation on a physical Limx Dynamics P1 bipedal robot across multiple real-world terrains underscore the practical feasibility and robustness of the proposed method.

**4. Foundation for Future Robotic Applications:** The demonstrated capability of vision-assisted, adaptable locomotion has implications for various applications, including disaster response, inspection in challenging industrial settings, and assistance in human environments where navigating diverse terrains is paramount. The ability to autonomously adapt gait based on visual input enhances robot autonomy and operational reliability.

**5. Guidance for Future Research:** The paper identifies areas for further development, such as enhancing the inherent stability of the bipedal platform and integrating dynamic trajectory planning. Addressing these aspects could lead to even more agile and capable bipedal robots, potentially enabling complex tasks like dynamic obstacle avoidance and more sophisticated interactions with the environment.
