# Route by Kinematics, Act by Observation: Kinematics-Supervised Expert Routing in MoE-Augmented VLA

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University introduce KinRT, a framework for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that uses action kinematics to supervise expert routing in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures during training. This approach led to a 23.26% improvement in average success rate on the RoboTwin simulation benchmark and a 20.27% improvement on a new real-world DIYRobot benchmark compared to the strongest dense baselines.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models face challenges generalizing across diverse manipulation tasks due to heterogeneous underlying kinematics, causing gradient interference in dense architectures.
- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routers in VLAs, when relying solely on visual-linguistic observations, often make suboptimal expert assignments because observation similarity does not always correlate with necessary action kinematics.
- An information asymmetry exists where crucial action kinematic data is available during VLA model training but not during real-world inference, preventing direct kinematic guidance for expert selection.

## Method

- A ==Kinematics-supervised explicit routing== (**KinRT**) paradigm is introduced, leveraging an asymmetric bridge to supervise MoE routers with action kinematics during training.
- Action trajectories are clustered into ==kinematic archetypes== offline, providing ground truth labels for a global router that learns to map visual-linguistic observations to specific experts.
- A ==residual Mixture-of-Transformers== architecture, with shared and expert FFNs, integrates this routing for action generation via a ==flow-matching objective==, enabling observation-only inference.

## Results

- KinRT-LoRA achieved an average success rate of **40.8%** on the RoboTwin simulation benchmark, a **23.26%** improvement over the best dense baseline, and KinRT-Full achieved **35.6%** on the DIYRobot real-world benchmark, improving by **20.27%** over its dense counterpart.
- The kinematics-supervised explicit routing consistently surpassed MoE models relying on implicit, observation-driven routing by significant margins (e.g., **+8.7** to **+14.2 percentage points** on RoboTwin).
- KinRT demonstrated substantial gains on kinematically complex and rare tasks like bimanual coordination and precise contact-based manipulation, indicating effective expert specialization for challenging motion patterns.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diverse robot manipulation tasks can be effectively categorized into a small, discrete set of "kinematic archetypes," suggesting an underlying structured commonality in robot movements.
> - Kinematic similarity, rather than visual-linguistic observation similarity, is the essential criterion for effective and meaningful expert specialization within Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) for robot manipulation.
> - Explicitly supervising the MoE router with privileged kinematic information during training can effectively distill kinematic understanding into observation-driven decisions, overcoming the inference-time information asymmetry.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models as a unified framework that integrates visual perception, language understanding, and action generation. Models such as RT-1, RT-2, VIMA, and OpenVLA aim to generalize across various manipulation tasks by training on large-scale robotic datasets. However, a challenge arises as the complexity and diversity of tasks increase: the underlying kinematics across different tasks become increasingly heterogeneous. This heterogeneity, if compressed into a single, shared model space, can lead to issues like gradient interference and reduced generalization capabilities, as observed in multi-task learning contexts.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have been proposed as a method to address this scaling challenge by allowing different "experts" within a model to specialize in distinct data regimes. This approach helps to mitigate parameter competition while maintaining a unified architectural framework. While MoE has demonstrated success in large language models and other domains, its application to VLA models for robot manipulation introduces specific challenges. The effectiveness of MoE-augmented VLAs relies heavily on the router's ability to assign appropriate experts. Conventional MoE routers typically make decisions based on input observations (e.g., visual and linguistic cues), learning these assignments implicitly through backpropagation.

This paper identifies a fundamental gap in this conventional approach: the potential misalignment between visual-linguistic observations and the underlying action kinematics. For instance, two visually similar scenes might require entirely different kinematic actions (e.g., "lift cup" vs. "unscrew cap"), while visually dissimilar scenes might share similar kinematic patterns (e.g., "push plate" vs. "push book"). This semantic misalignment can result in a router making physically inappropriate expert assignments. Furthermore, a critical information asymmetry exists: while action kinematics are available during training, they are fundamentally unavailable at inference time, meaning the most direct signal for kinematic specialization cannot be used directly for deployment.

The work builds upon the concept of "learning using privileged information" (LUPI), where information available exclusively during training can enhance generalization. Prior LUPI applications in robotics often use privileged information to supervise the policy directly. This paper shifts this paradigm by using action trajectory kinematics as privileged information to supervise the *router* instead of the policy itself. This approach aims to inject task-relevant kinematic understanding into the routing mechanism while preserving an observation-only inference pipeline. By proposing an explicit, kinematics-supervised routing mechanism, this research aims to overcome the limitations of implicit, observation-driven routing in MoE-augmented VLA models, particularly in the context of diverse robot manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel expert routing paradigm, named Kinematics-supervised explicit routing (KinRT), for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)-augmented Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This paradigm aims to enhance the specialization and performance of VLA policies by explicitly leveraging kinematic information during training to guide expert selection, even though this information is unavailable during inference.

This objective is driven by several detailed motivations:

- **Addressing Kinematic Heterogeneity in VLA Models:** As the number and diversity of robot manipulation tasks increase, the underlying action kinematics become increasingly varied. Existing VLA models, particularly dense architectures, struggle to compress these disparate kinematic patterns into a single model space, leading to issues such as gradient interference and poor generalization. MoE offers a potential solution by allowing experts to specialize, but its effectiveness depends on proper expert assignment.
- **Overcoming Observation-Action Misalignment in MoE Routing:** Traditional MoE routers in VLAs typically rely solely on visual and linguistic observations to determine expert activation. However, these observations do not always align with the required action kinematics. Visually similar scenes can demand kinematically distinct actions, while visually distinct scenes might share isomorphic kinematic patterns. This fundamental semantic misalignment leads to routers making suboptimal or physically inappropriate expert assignments when based purely on visual-linguistic inputs.
- **Mitigating Failures of Implicit Routing:** In conventional MoE setups, routing decisions are often learned implicitly through backpropagation without explicit guidance regarding the underlying task structure. This implicit learning has been observed to result in uneven expert utilization and semantically ambiguous specialization, even in domains like natural language processing. In embodied manipulation, where kinematic differences are pronounced, implicit routing exacerbates these issues, as the true criterion for expert assignment is kinematic semantic isomorphism, not superficial observation similarity.
- **Bridging the Information Asymmetry Gap:** A critical challenge is that detailed action kinematic information, which is highly relevant for expert specialization, is available during the training phase (from demonstration trajectories) but is absent during the deployment or inference phase (when the robot operates based on real-time observations). This constitutes an intrinsic information asymmetry that conventional routing mechanisms fail to bridge effectively.
- **Leveraging the "Kinematic Prototype Collapse" Phenomenon:** The authors observed through initial experiments that despite the diversity of manipulation tasks, most ultimately reduce to a manageable number of distinct "kinematic archetypes" or prototypes. This finding suggests that a discrete, archetypal structure exists within the continuous action space, which could serve as an effective basis for expert specialization.
- **Developing an Accessible Robot Platform:** To facilitate research and provide a real-world evaluation benchmark for generalizable robot manipulation policies, especially for smaller research teams, there is a motivation to create an economical and practical robot platform. This platform would allow for the collection of novel real-world datasets and robust testing of methods like KinRT across the simulation-to-reality gap.

In summary, the motivation is to address the core problem of ineffective expert routing in MoE-augmented VLAs by explicitly supervising the router with kinematically derived labels during training, thereby enabling robust, kinematically-aware expert dispatching at inference time without requiring kinematic inputs at deployment. This approach aims to achieve better specialization, improved generalization, and higher success rates across diverse manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Kinematics-supervised explicit routing (KinRT) paradigm introduces an asymmetric bridging mechanism where action kinematics are utilized during training to supervise expert routing, while inference relies solely on visual-language observations. The methodology can be broken down into architectural design, a three-stage bridging mechanism, and the introduction of a new robot platform and benchmark.

#### 3.1. KinRT's Architecture and Design Philosophy

KinRT employs a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture, comprising 18 Transformer blocks. This architecture is designed with two streams:
- **Prefix Stream:** Encodes multi-view images (I) using a frozen SigLIP model into vision tokens and a language instruction (ℓ) using a frozen PaliGemma-2B into language tokens.
- **Suffix Stream:** Generates action sequences by integrating a Gemma-300M model and the MoE extension.

The two streams interact via attention over a concatenated prefix-suffix sequence, while maintaining stream-specific attention projections and feed-forward network (FFN) parameters. Prefix tokens attend bidirectionally within the visual-language context, and action tokens attend to the full prefix and the action chunk.

A key modification is that each FFN within the action head of a Transformer block is replaced with a parallel MoE composition:
$$ \text{FFN}_{\text{MoE}}(x) = \frac{1}{2} \text{FFN}_{\text{shd}}(x) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^K \tilde{w}_k \text{FFN}_{e_k}(x) $$
where $\text{FFN}_{\text{shd}}(x)$ is a shared, pre-trained FFN, and $\text{FFN}_{e_k}(x)$ are the Top-K selected expert FFNs. This residual design ensures a stable base through the shared branch while experts learn incremental specialization. The expert FFNs match the backbone width ($d_{\text{model}}=1024, d_{\text{mlp}}=4096$).

The core design philosophy addresses the information asymmetry where task-relevant kinematic structure is available during training but absent during inference. KinRT bridges this gap through a three-stage process.

#### 3.2. Three-Stage Asymmetric Bridge

1. **Kinematic Archetype Clustering:**
 - **Action Chunk Representation:** To capture both "where the arms go" and "how they get there," a future H-steps' action is combined into an action chunk $a_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times D}$, where H is the horizon (set to 50) and D is the action dimension (14 for a 14-DoF robot: Left Arm $\times$ 6, Left Gripper $\times$ 1, Right Arm $\times$ 6, Right Gripper $\times$ 1). This results in a $50 \times 14 = 700$-dimensional action chunk.
 - **Feature Extraction:** Position features are derived by flattening the action chunk ($\text{vec}(a^{(i)}_{0:H})$). Velocity features are derived by flattening the temporal difference between adjacent actions ($\text{vec}(a^{(i)}_{1:H} - a^{(i)}_{0:H-1})$), resulting in $(H-1) \times D = 686$ dimensions. These are concatenated to form a 1386-dimensional descriptor $\phi_i$.
 - **Preprocessing and Clustering:** Features are standardized to remove disparities. Dimensionality is reduced to 64 using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for scalability. Finally, frame-level K-means clustering is applied to these descriptors to obtain integer prototype labels $y_i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, K\}$, which are stored offline.
 - **Kinematic Prototype Collapse:** The clustering process empirically reveals a "kinematic prototype collapse" phenomenon, where diverse manipulation tasks converge to a small number of kinematic archetypes. For instance, on the RoboTwin benchmark, four such archetypes were identified, leading to setting the number of MoE experts N=4 to align with this granularity.

2. **Kinematics-Supervised Global Router:**
 - **Router Input:** Raw visual-language embeddings were found to be non-discriminative. Instead, the aggregated prefix context `c` is used, formed by masked mean pooling of all valid output tokens from the prefix PaliGemma. This summary vector $c \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}$ serves as the input to the router.
 - **Global Routing:** Routing decisions are made at the observation level, not per-layer, to ensure stability and efficiency. The router maps the context vector `c` through a single linear layer to obtain logits `g`. During training, exploration noise $\eta \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$ is added to `g` (disabled at inference), and a temperature-scaled softmax is applied to yield probabilities $p = \text{softmax}((g + \eta)/\tau)$. The Top-K experts are selected based on `p`, and their probabilities are renormalized into $\tilde{w}_k$. This final assignment of experts and weights is shared across all 18 Transformer layers.
 - **Supervised Routing Loss:** This is the core of the asymmetric bridge. The kinematic prototype labels $y_b$ obtained from clustering serve as ground truth to supervise the router's training using a cross-entropy objective: $L_{\text{sup}} = - \sum_b y_b \log \hat{y}_b$. This forces the router to infer kinematic structure from observations alone.
 - **Sample-Balancing Strategy:** To counter class imbalance among kinematic archetypes, a resampling strategy is employed during training. Each sample $i$ with prototype label $y_i$ is assigned a sampling weight $w_i = n_{y_i}^{-\alpha}$, where $n_k$ is the number of samples in prototype $k$. Mini-batches are drawn with replacement according to $p_i = w_i / \sum_j w_j$. The coefficient $\alpha$ interpolates between empirical sampling ($\alpha=0$) and uniform sampling ($\alpha=1$). $\alpha=0.5$ is used as a default to ensure minority-prototype exposure without over-distorting the data distribution.

3. **Action Generation by Observation at Deployment:**
 - **Flow-Matching for Action Generation:** The action generator is trained using a flow-matching objective. Clean actions $a_0$ are noised ($x_t = t \cdot \varepsilon + (1-t) a_0$) by drawing noise $\varepsilon \sim N(0, I)$ and a time step $t \sim \text{Beta}(1.5, 1)$. The model learns to predict the target velocity $v_t = \varepsilon - a_0$ (which is constant). This is optimized via an MSE loss $L_{\text{act}} = E_{t, \varepsilon, a_0} \|\hat{v}_\theta(x_t, t, o) - v_t\|^2$.
 - **Inference Pipeline:** At deployment, the router operates solely on the visual-linguistic observation $o$. A one-time precomputation passes the input through the prefix stream to generate the pooled context $c$ and yield the global routing decision ({experts, weights}). Subsequently, a denoising loop runs for T steps, where routed action experts predict $\hat{v}_t$. The action is iteratively updated using $x_{t-\Delta t} = x_t - \Delta t \hat{v}_t$, converging to the clean action sequence $x_0$. This completes the bridge, allowing kinematic understanding from training to guide observation-driven action generation.

#### 3.3. DIYRobot Platform and Benchmark

To evaluate cross-platform generalization and provide an accessible research tool, the authors constructed a 14-DoF Do-It-Yourself robot (DIYRobot) platform using 3D-print technology. This platform is designed to be economical (< 2,000 USD) and practical. A corresponding real-world benchmark, also named DIYRobot, was collected using this platform. It consists of five manipulation tasks (handover pen, pick box, rotate screwdriver, pull bottle, press button), each with 100 demonstrations, totaling 500 demonstrations. This benchmark focuses on long-tail demonstrations and operates in a clean setting. The platform's design files, driver code, and benchmark data are planned for public release.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive experimental results on two benchmarks: RoboTwin (simulated) and the newly introduced DIYRobot (real-world). The findings address several research questions and include ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparisons (RQ1-RQ3)

**RQ1: KinRT vs. SOTA Dense VLAs:**
KinRT demonstrated superior performance compared to state-of-the-art dense VLA models.
- **RoboTwin:** KinRT-LoRA achieved an average success rate of 40.8% (clean) / 38.8% (random), surpassing the strongest dense baseline, $\pi_{0.5}$-LoRA (33.1% / 34.1%), by +7.7 / +4.7 percentage points, representing a 23.26% improvement in the clean setting. KinRT-Full (36.0% / 28.9%) also outperformed $\pi_{0.5}$-Full (24.6% / 23.1%).
- **DIYRobot:** KinRT-Full achieved an average success rate of 35.6%, outperforming the best dense model, $\pi_{0.5}$-Full (29.6%), by +6.0 percentage points (20.27% improvement). Dense models like OpenVLA exhibited severe degradation (e.g., 4.1% on RoboTwin, 3.4% on DIYRobot) with increasing task kinematic heterogeneity, supporting the motivation that compressing diverse kinematics into a shared space causes issues.

**RQ2: KinRT's Explicit Routing vs. Implicit, Observation-Driven Routing in MoE VLAs:**
KinRT's explicit kinematics-supervised routing outperformed MoE models with implicit routing.
- The best implicit routing competitor, AdaMoE, achieved 32.1% / 29.4% on RoboTwin and 21.4% on DIYRobot. KinRT-LoRA and KinRT-Full surpassed AdaMoE by +8.7 / +9.4 and +14.2 percentage points, respectively.
- Hi-MoE, another implicit MoE model, showed significant failure on many tasks (e.g., 0% on "Move Can"), yielding low overall averages (6.8% / 6.4% on RoboTwin, 8.0% on DIYRobot). This collapse validates the paper's hypothesis that implicitly learned routers struggle with kinematic heterogeneity, whereas explicit kinematic supervision leads to more meaningful expert specialization.

**RQ3: KinRT's Architecture-Agnostic Plug-and-Play Capability:**
KinRT consistently provided performance gains when integrated into various backbone architectures, demonstrating its architecture-agnostic nature.
- KinRT-LoRA($\pi_0$) improved $\pi_0$-LoRA from 13.9% / 12.6% to 15.8% / 14.4% on RoboTwin.
- KinRT-Full($\pi_0$) improved $\pi_0$-Full from 8.9% / 8.8% to 11.1% / 10.8%.
- KinRT-OpenVLA lifted OpenVLA from 4.1% / 4.4% to 7.6% / 6.5%.
- KinRT-AdaMoE boosted AdaMoE from 21.4% to 28.4% (+7.0 percentage points) on DIYRobot. These consistent improvements suggest that the gains are attributable to the kinematics-supervised routing paradigm rather than specific architectural choices.

#### 4.2. Cross-Platform Generalization and Fine-tuning Strategy (RQ4)

**RQ4: Simulation-to-Reality Gap and LoRA vs. Full Fine-tuning Trade-off:**
A dichotomy was observed between the two benchmarks regarding fine-tuning strategies:
- **Simulation (RoboTwin):** KinRT-LoRA generally outperformed KinRT-Full (40.8% / 38.8% vs. 36.0% / 28.9%).
- **Real-world (DIYRobot):** KinRT-Full achieved superior results (35.6%) compared to KinRT-LoRA (23.8%).
This pattern was also consistent with $\pi_{0.5}$ baselines. The findings suggest that real-robot data from DIYRobot deviates significantly from pre-training distributions, necessitating the larger adaptation capacity of full fine-tuning to absorb the "embodiment gap." In contrast, the parameter-efficient LoRA regularization was more effective in simulation by mitigating overfitting to limited demonstrations. KinRT maintained its leading performance in both regimes, indicating genuine cross-platform generalizability.

#### 4.3. Performance on Specific Kinematic Regimes (RQ5)

**RQ5: KinRT's Impact on Specific Kinematic Regimes:**
KinRT demonstrated its most substantial gains on kinematically complex and rare tasks:
- **Bimanual Coordination:** On tasks like "Handover Block" (RoboTwin), KinRT-Full achieved 34%/22%, significantly outperforming all dense and MoE baselines (below 19% in clean setting). For "Handover Pen" (DIYRobot), KinRT-Full achieved 26 successes, surpassing most baselines except $\pi_{0.5}$-Full (19).
- **Contact-Precise Tasks:** On "Press Button," where many $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$-LoRA variants largely failed (0-1 success), KinRT-Full achieved 28 successes.
These tasks align with the rare, large-amplitude bimanual, and fine-positioning kinematic archetypes identified by the clustering. KinRT's explicit supervision allocated dedicated expert capacity to these minority regimes, which are often overwhelmed in implicit routing MoEs or dense models. KinRT also remained competitive on common kinematic regimes (e.g., "Open Laptop": 84%/83%), indicating that specialization did not sacrifice performance on frequent motion patterns.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Balanced Sampling Coefficient $\alpha$ (Table 2):** An intermediate $\alpha=0.5$ yielded the best average success (40.8%). $\alpha=0$ (no balancing) led to under-training of experts for rare prototypes (34.4%), while $\alpha=1$ (full uniform sampling) over-distorted the data distribution, sacrificing performance on dominant patterns (36.6%).
- **Source for Clustering Archetype Labels (Table 3):**
 - Clustering based on VLM embeddings (semantic similarity) performed worst (21.8%), confirming the hypothesis that semantic proximity does not equate to kinematic isomorphism.
 - Coarse kinematic abstractions (action range, arm presence) showed limited improvements. Manual task-identity labels also failed to capture nuanced kinematic equivalence or heterogeneity.
 - Fine-grained kinematic signals, particularly combining action trajectories and velocity profiles, yielded the best performance (40.8%). This suggests that action and velocity encode complementary spatial configuration and motion tempo information, producing the most physically coherent expert partition.
- **VLM vs. Kinematic Similarity Visualization (Figure 3):** This visualization provided a mechanistic explanation for the ablation results. VLM similarity was highly collapsed (most pairs within [0.96, 1.00]), demonstrating its non-discriminative nature for kinematics. In contrast, at any fixed VLM similarity, kinematic similarity spanned a broad range, indicating poor correlation. Per-cluster panels showed compacted kinematic similarity distributions, confirming that kinematic clustering creates internally coherent motion groups, making it an appropriate supervisory signal.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Case Studies (Figure 4)

Qualitative demonstrations on the DIYRobot platform showcased KinRT's ability to produce smooth, temporally coherent trajectories for various tasks, including bimanual handover, grasp-and-reorient, precise vertical positioning, horizontal dragging, and fine rotational manipulation. These case studies further supported the effectiveness and robustness of kinematics-guided expert routing in real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, through the introduction of Kinematics-supervised explicit routing (KinRT), holds several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of robot manipulation and VLA models.

Firstly, **KinRT offers a novel solution to a fundamental problem in MoE-augmented VLA models:** the misalignment between observation-driven expert routing and the underlying kinematic requirements of diverse manipulation tasks. By explicitly supervising the router with kinematic archetype labels derived from action trajectories during training, the framework effectively distills privileged kinematic information into the visual-linguistic observation space, enabling kinematically-informed expert dispatching at inference time without requiring kinematic inputs. This asymmetric bridging mechanism directly addresses the information asymmetry problem, providing a more principled approach to expert specialization.

Secondly, the empirical results demonstrate **substantial performance improvements** over existing state-of-the-art dense and MoE-featured VLA models across both simulated (RoboTwin) and real-world (DIYRobot) benchmarks. The consistent gains across diverse backbones (OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, AdaMoE) highlight KinRT's **architecture-agnostic and plug-and-play generalizability**. The framework's particular effectiveness in handling kinematically rare and demanding tasks, such as bimanual coordination and precise contact tasks, indicates its ability to allocate dedicated expert capacity to complex motion patterns that are often neglected by implicit routing or dense models. This suggests that KinRT could unlock more dexterous and robust robot behaviors.

Thirdly, the research provides **empirical validation for the hypothesis that kinematic isomorphism, rather than observation similarity, is the crucial criterion for effective expert routing** in robot manipulation. The ablation studies compellingly show that clustering based on rich kinematic descriptors (action and velocity) is far more effective than relying on visual-linguistic features, which are prone to collapse and lack discriminative power for motion patterns. This insight could guide future research in designing more intelligent routing mechanisms for embodied AI.

Fourthly, the introduction of the **economical and 3D-printable DIYRobot platform and its corresponding benchmark** represents a significant contribution to the robotics research community. By making a real-world robot platform and dataset accessible (costing less than 2,000 USD), the authors lower the barrier to entry for smaller academic teams and resource-constrained labs. This accessibility fosters broader participation in robot learning research, facilitates the collection of diverse real-world data, and enables more comprehensive evaluation of algorithms across the simulation-to-reality gap, ultimately accelerating progress in the field.

Finally, the study's findings regarding the trade-off between LoRA and full fine-tuning in simulated versus real-world environments offer practical guidance for deploying VLA models. This nuanced understanding of adaptation strategies contributes to the robustness and applicability of VLA models in practical robotic settings. In the future, the exploration of adaptive expert activation and the extension of kinematic routing to larger-scale VLA models could further enhance the capabilities and impact of this paradigm. Overall, KinRT represents a step towards more intelligent, specialized, and adaptable robot control policies.
