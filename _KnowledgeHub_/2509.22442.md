# Learning to Ball: Composing Policies for Long-Horizon Basketball Moves

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed a novel framework for composing diverse motor skills in physics-based characters, specifically addressing complex, long-horizon tasks like basketball maneuvers with ill-defined intermediate phases. The system learned intricate actions such as shoot-off-the-dribble with a 98.3% ball catching rate and 91.8% shot accuracy, leveraging heterogeneous and unstructured motion data.

## Problem

- Composing drastically different primitive motor skills into coherent, long-horizon behaviors for physics-based characters remains challenging for existing methods like Mixture of Experts or skill chaining.
- Intermediate subtasks often lack clearly defined initial or terminal states (e.g., 'gathering' a basketball), making them difficult for traditional policy training and chaining approaches.
- Acquiring high-fidelity, structured motion capture data with synchronized full-body, hand, and ball trajectories is expensive and limits the scalability of learning complex skills.

## Method

- A multi-stage framework that trains primitive policies from unstructured and heterogeneous motion data (2D video, 3D mocap, hand-only data) using adversarial imitation learning.
- A policy integration framework categorizes and handles three types of skill transitions: direct execution (Type A), mutual adaptation (Type B), and, crucially, intermediate policies (Type C) which leverage the preceding policy for initial states and the succeeding policy's value function for reward shaping.
- A high-level 'soft-routing' policy uses learned weights to orchestrate transitions between primitive policies, achieving smooth action blending while maintaining dominance of a single skill, guided by user commands.

## Results

- The system achieved a **98.3%** ball catching rate and **91.8%** overall shot accuracy for the 'shoot-off-the-dribble' task, significantly outperforming baselines which failed to integrate skills effectively (e.g., DirectExecution had **0.7%** catching rate).
- Generated motions exhibited dynamic dribbling, intelligent ball gathering that adapts posture to align with the hoop, and seamless transitions between various basketball actions, including cooperative and competitive team play.
- Ablation studies confirmed that value-function-based reward shaping for intermediate policies and the soft-routing approach were critical for robust performance and smooth transitions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning intermediate skills with ill-defined goals can be effectively achieved by shaping their reward function using the state value function of the subsequent, well-defined skill.
> - Simultaneous mutual adaptation, where both the intermediate policy and the succeeding pre-trained policy are fine-tuned, is crucial for robust skill integration and handling varied states.
> - A soft-routing mechanism, which linearly combines actions from primitive policies with learned weights, allows for seamless and responsive transitions between diverse motor skills, outperforming hard-switching heuristics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of physics-based character control, particularly for long-horizon, multi-phase tasks, represents a significant challenge in both reinforcement learning (RL) and computer animation. Traditional methods for character control often rely on meticulously designed trajectory optimization or heuristic rules, which struggle to adapt to dynamic, unscripted scenarios. While deep RL has shown remarkable success in training policies for individual motor skills (e.g., locomotion, manipulation), composing these disparate skills into a coherent, robust framework for complex tasks remains an open problem.

Existing approaches to policy composition primarily fall into two categories:
1. **Mixture of Experts (MoE):** This technique combines multiple specialized policies, but it typically requires these individual policies to share a significant overlap in explored states to function effectively. For tasks involving drastically different physical interactions, such as dribbling a basketball versus shooting it, the state spaces may be too disjoint for MoE to handle seamlessly.
2. **Skill Chaining:** This approach allows for the concatenation of policies by transitioning from the terminal state of one skill to the initial state of the next. However, skill chaining typically demands that each subtask has a clearly defined set of terminal and initial states. This limitation proves problematic for "intermediate" subtasks, where the primary goal is to bridge two other skills, and their own start/end conditions are ambiguous or context-dependent. For example, the "gathering" motion between dribbling and shooting doesn't have a singular, well-defined goal state; rather, its success depends on preparing the character for the *subsequent* shooting action.

This paper directly addresses these critical limitations. It recognizes that many real-world tasks, like basketball maneuvers (e.g., "shoot-off-the-dribble," "catch-and-shoot"), inherently involve distinct subtasks with varied goal definitions. The authors highlight the challenge posed by transitional subtasks, such as gathering the ball, which have "unclear goals but critical to the success of the entire task." This inability of existing methods to handle ill-defined intermediate states and drastically different skills represents a significant gap in the current research landscape of physics-based character control.

Furthermore, a secondary but important challenge in this domain is the acquisition of suitable training data. High-fidelity, structured motion capture data, encompassing full-body, detailed finger movements, and corresponding ball trajectories, is incredibly difficult and expensive to collect at scale. Existing work often relies on such specialized datasets. This paper differentiates itself by demonstrating the ability to learn complex, human-like basketball skills from *unstructured* and *heterogeneous* data sources, including 2D videos and 3D mocap, without requiring explicit ball trajectory references or perfect correspondence between different body parts. This approach significantly broadens the applicability of RL to motion synthesis by reducing the reliance on meticulously curated datasets.

In essence, "Learning to Ball" pushes the boundaries of RL for character animation by tackling the sophisticated problem of composing diverse and ill-defined motor skills for long-horizon tasks, while simultaneously addressing the practical challenges of data acquisition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel policy integration framework that enables the seamless composition of drastically different motor skills in multi-phase, long-horizon tasks, particularly those characterized by ill-defined intermediate states. The authors explicitly set out to overcome the shortcomings of existing reinforcement learning approaches, such as mixture of experts and skill chaining, which struggle with such complex scenarios.

The motivations driving this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Addressing Policy Composition Challenges:** The core motivation stems from the inherent difficulty in composing heterogeneous primitive policies for complex, long-horizon tasks. Many real-world activities, like playing basketball, involve sequences of distinct subtasks (e.g., dribbling, gathering, shooting) that require fluid transitions. Current methods often fail when individual policies do not share significant common states or when intermediate subtasks lack well-defined initial and terminal conditions. The authors aim to bridge these gaps by providing a robust solution for skill integration.
2. **Handling Ill-Defined Intermediate Subtasks:** A key inspiration comes from challenging transitional skills like "gathering" the basketball between a dribble and a shot. This subtask is critical for success but lacks clear, standalone goals, making it unsuitable for traditional, independently trained policies. The objective is to devise a method that can learn such ambiguous intermediate behaviors by leveraging the context provided by well-defined preceding and succeeding policies.
3. **Achieving Seamless and Robust Transitions:** Beyond merely concatenating skills, the goal is to enable transitions that are both smooth and adaptive. The authors seek to avoid abrupt changes or "stalling" behaviors that can arise from heuristic-based policy switching. This motivates the development of a high-level "soft router" that can intelligently orchestrate policy execution based on real-time commands and environmental context.
4. **Leveraging Unstructured and Diverse Data:** A significant practical motivation is to reduce the reliance on scarce and expensive structured motion data. The authors aim to demonstrate that complex, physics-based character control can be learned from heterogeneous sources, including 2D videos and 3D motion capture, even without perfectly synchronized full-body, hand, and ball trajectory information. This objective enhances the scalability and applicability of their approach.
5. **Enabling Real-time Interactive Control and Team Play:** Ultimately, the researchers are motivated to create physically simulated characters capable of performing dynamic, unscripted basketball behaviors in real-time. This includes responding adaptively to user commands, executing complex maneuvers like shoot-off-the-dribble, and even demonstrating cooperative and competitive team play with multiple agents. The vision is to push the capabilities of physically-based characters in dynamic, interactive virtual environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in "Learning to Ball" is a sophisticated, multi-stage framework designed to enable the composition of diverse and often ill-defined motor skills for long-horizon tasks. It combines adversarial imitation learning, value-function-based reward shaping, and a novel soft-routing mechanism.

#### 3.1. Primitive Policy Learning from Unstructured Motions

The foundation of the framework involves training individual "primitive" policies for well-defined subtasks using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) within an adversarial imitation learning framework. A key innovation here is the ability to learn from heterogeneous and unstructured motion data:
- **Data Sources:** The system integrates 3D motion capture data (full-body without hands, locomotion from LAFAN1 and CMU Mocap), 2D video data (processed by ExAvatar and TRAM for body and hand poses), and custom-recorded hand-only motions (Rokoko Smartgloves). Crucially, the method *does not* assume access to corresponding ball trajectories, allowing for greater data flexibility.
- **Decoupled Motions:** To handle diverse data, full-body motions are decoupled into lower body, upper body, and *separate hand groups* (wrists/fingers with elbows as roots). This enables the use of hand-only motion data and enhances coordination.
- **Adversarial Imitation Learning:** A GAN-like architecture is used, where discriminators process partially observable motions (e.g., hand-only or body-only) to provide imitation rewards. This is combined with goal-directed task rewards (e.g., for navigation, dribbling, shooting accuracy).
- **Dribbling Policy:** This policy learns to control the character for velocity-controlled navigation while dribbling. It includes two imitation objectives and two task rewards, with specific violation detection for invalid ball contact and traveling. A dynamic weighting strategy is used to balance navigation and dribbling objectives throughout training.
- **Shooting Policy:** Trained using full-body (including hands) jump-shooting demonstrations. It combines full-body imitation with a task reward measuring shot accuracy and ball-holding performance before release.
- **Other Primitive Policies:** Policies for Passing, Catching, Rebounding, and Locomotion/Defending are also trained with similar principles, adapting their reward functions and goal states to their specific tasks.

#### 3.2. Policy Integration Framework for Skill Transitions

The core contribution is a novel policy integration framework that categorizes and handles three types of transitions between skills, with a focus on the most challenging:
- **Type A: Direct Execution:** For skills with significant shared states (e.g., Shoot to Locomotion), the succeeding policy can be executed directly.
- **Type B: Mutual Adaptation:** For skills requiring some alignment but without drastically incompatible states (e.g., Catch to Shoot), the preceding policy's reward is shaped by the succeeding policy's value function, and the succeeding policy is adapted to the states generated by the preceding one. This also includes co-adaptation for interactive skills like Catching and Passing between two agents.
- **Type C: Intermediate Policy (Most Challenging):** This is the key innovation for transitions like Dribble to Shoot, which require an intermediate, ill-defined subtask (Gather).
 - **Initial State Distribution:** The "Gather" policy is trained to take over from *any* terminal state generated by the preceding "Dribble" policy, effectively using the Dribble policy as an initial state generator.
 - **Reward Shaping via Value Function:** The "Gather" policy's reward function is shaped by the state value function ($\bar{V}_{shoot}$) of the *succeeding*, well-defined "Shoot" policy. This teaches Gather to produce states that are advantageous for shooting.
 - **Simultaneous Mutual Adaptation:** Critically, the *pre-trained Shoot policy is simultaneously adapted* during the training of the Gather policy. States encountered during Gather's rollouts (especially "good" ones, where $\bar{V}_{shoot}$ is above a threshold) are used to fine-tune the Shoot policy. This co-adaptation ensures that Gather leads to states manageable by Shoot, and Shoot becomes robust to the varied states produced by Gather.
 - **Pivoting Foot Control:** To prevent "traveling" violations during gathering and shooting, an additional variable indicating the pivoting foot is incorporated into the goal state during adaptation.

#### 3.3. High-Level Soft Routing Policy

To automate and provide seamless transitions across these composed policies, a high-level "soft-routing" policy is introduced:
- **Input and Output:** The router takes user commands (e.g., "dribble," "shoot"), current state, and goal as input. It outputs *weights* for a linear combination of actions from the primitive policies (e.g., Dribble, Gather, Shoot).
- **Soft Merging:** Unlike "hard routing" (which activates only one policy), soft routing allows for a weighted average of actions. To prevent unnatural blended motions while ensuring responsiveness, the router's reward function encourages one primitive policy to dominate control at any given moment, effectively achieving a smooth transition while avoiding excessive averaging.
- **Guidance from Reference Commands:** The router's training is bootstrapped by heuristically defined reference commands, guiding its exploration.
- **Policy Distillation:** After training, the hierarchical policy can be compressed into a single neural network via supervised learning to reduce inference time without significant performance loss.

This comprehensive approach allows the system to build complex, long-horizon behaviors by training modular primitive skills, intelligently composing them through adaptive integration, and orchestrating them via a flexible high-level controller, all while being robust to limitations in motion data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents compelling quantitative and qualitative results that validate the efficacy of their policy integration framework, particularly for complex, long-horizon basketball maneuvers.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Evaluation (Shoot-off-the-Dribble Task)

The primary quantitative analysis focuses on the most challenging Type C transition: "shoot-off-the-dribble," evaluated through two key metrics:
- **Ball Catching Rate:** This measures the success rate of the character gathering and holding the ball after a shooting command and before the shot is taken.
 - **Our Method:** Achieved a remarkably high **98.3% ball catching rate** across all dribbling speeds, demonstrating robust performance even in dynamic scenarios.
 - **Baselines:**
 - **DirectExecution (Dribble→Pretrained Shoot):** Failed almost entirely with a **0.7%** catching rate, highlighting the incompatibility of unadapted policies for dynamic transitions.
 - **NoAdapt (Dribble→Gather→Pretrained Shoot):** While its gathering policy showed high success in catching, the *pretrained shooting policy* failed to generalize, leading to a significant performance drop during the shooting phase.
 - **SequentialChaining (Dribble→Shoot):** Performed better than DirectExecution but significantly underperformed compared to the NoAdapt's dedicated gathering policy, suggesting that treating gathering and shooting as a single phase hinders effectiveness.
- **Shot Percentage:** This measures the ratio of successful field goals to attempted ones, including failures from unsuccessful ball catching.
 - **Our Method:** Achieved an impressive **91.8% overall shot accuracy**. This nearly matches the performance of the vanilla pretrained shooting policy (93.0%) which operates from ideal, reference-motion-initialized states. This result is particularly significant as it demonstrates the system's ability to adjust dynamic poses during gathering and execute accurate shots from challenging dribbling scenarios.
 - **Baselines:** All baselines performed poorly: DirectExecution (1.3%), NoAdapt (6.1%), SequentialChaining (12.7%), underscoring their inability to handle the complex, dynamic states arising from dribbling.
 - **Adaptability:** The method showed high shot percentages even when the character approached the hoop from challenging directions, such as facing opposite (95.4%), left orthogonal (90.4%), or right orthogonal (92.7%), showcasing its adaptability.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Analysis

The qualitative results emphasize the smoothness, coordination, and adaptability of the generated motions:
- **Dynamic Dribbling:** Despite using unstructured data and no ball trajectories, the dribbling policy synthesizes diverse motions, enabling sharp turns, abrupt stops, and effective ball control across a wide range of speeds (0-5 m/s).
- **Intelligent Gathering:** The character dynamically adjusts its posture during gathering, including spinning, pivoting, or turning to align with the hoop, even when initially facing away.
- **Seamless Transitions:** The system demonstrates smooth transitions across all three types (A, B, C). For example, locomotion to rebounding (Type A) is direct, while passing and catching between agents (Type B) are achieved through mutual adaptation and co-reward shaping, leading to catchable throws and ready receiving.
- **Team Play:** The framework successfully supports cooperative and competitive interactions, including passing-off-the-dribble, catch-and-shoot, and defensive actions like blocking and screening, with multiple agents playing in real-time.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies provided critical insights into the framework's design choices:
- **Intermediate Policy Training:** Using the succeeding policy's state value function for reward shaping (`Value`) was crucial for the gathering policy to achieve high ball-catching rates, proving more effective than random state transfer (`Random`). Fine-tuning a pretrained shooting policy (`Adapted Shooting`) also outperformed training from scratch (`Scratch`). The combination of `Value` and `Random` state transfer for adaptation, as in the full proposed method, proved to be the most robust. Critically, separating gathering and shooting into distinct phases outperformed `Gather+Shoot` (SequentialChaining), confirming the necessity of explicit phase division for disparate subtasks.
- **High-Level Routing Policy:** The soft-routing policy significantly improved performance compared to heuristic rule-based policy switching. It achieved better ball-catching rates (98.3% vs. 86.0%) and shot percentages (91.8% vs. 67.4%), while also automating transitions. The `Soft Router` strategy outperformed `Hard Router` (which struggled with exploration) and even the simple heuristic baseline, demonstrating the benefits of its flexible, guided composition mechanism.

In summary, the main findings robustly demonstrate that the proposed framework effectively addresses the challenges of composing policies for long-horizon tasks with ill-defined intermediate states, yielding highly realistic, interactive, and robust physics-based character control for complex skills like basketball.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Learning to Ball" marks a significant advancement in the fields of reinforcement learning, computer graphics, and character animation, with far-reaching potential impacts.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Novel Policy Integration Framework:** The introduction of a structured policy integration framework that systematically addresses different types of skill transitions (Type A, B, C) is a major conceptual leap. The core idea of leveraging well-defined policies to shape the learning of ambiguous, intermediate subtasks (like "gathering") by using the preceding policy for initial state distribution and the succeeding policy's value function for reward shaping is highly novel. This provides a robust solution for a long-standing challenge in hierarchical RL and skill chaining.
- **Robustness to Ill-Defined Intermediate States:** The ability to effectively train policies for subtasks with unclear goals, which traditionally stump existing methods, opens up new possibilities for modeling complex human and animal behaviors. This moves beyond simply concatenating discrete skills to intelligently blending and transitioning between them.
- **Learning from Unstructured, Heterogeneous Data:** Demonstrating that complex, physics-based motor skills can be learned from a mix of 2D videos and 3D motion capture, crucially without requiring corresponding ball trajectories or perfect synchronization, significantly lowers the data collection barrier. This enhances the scalability and practical applicability of RL for motion synthesis, allowing researchers and practitioners to leverage readily available data.
- **Advanced Control and Adaptability:** The system's capacity to generate smooth, coordinated, and highly adaptive motions in response to real-time user commands, including complex maneuvers and dynamic adjustments (e.g., pivoting while gathering), showcases a high level of control. The achieved shot accuracy of 91.8% from diverse dribbling states is a testament to this robustness.
- **Soft Routing for Seamless Transitions:** The high-level soft-routing policy provides a flexible and effective mechanism for orchestrating multiple primitive policies. By encouraging dominance while allowing for subtle blending, it ensures both naturalness in motion and responsiveness in control, addressing limitations of prior hard-routing or pure averaging approaches.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Realistic Character Animation:** The framework can dramatically enhance the realism and interactivity of virtual characters in video games, virtual reality (VR), and augmented reality (AR) applications. Imagine AI-controlled teammates or opponents in sports games that exhibit human-like fluidity and adaptability, responding dynamically rather than following rigid scripts.
- **Robotics and Humanoid Control:** The principles of composing complex, multi-phase motor skills with ill-defined transitions are highly relevant to humanoid robotics. Robots could learn to perform intricate tasks by integrating various sub-skills, especially when intermediate steps are hard to program explicitly. The data-efficient learning from unstructured human demonstrations also aligns with goals in imitation learning for robotics.
- **AI for Sports Simulation and Training:** Beyond entertainment, this research could be used to create sophisticated sports simulations for training athletes or analyzing tactics. The ability to simulate realistic player behaviors and interactions in physics-driven environments offers a powerful tool for performance analysis.
- **Generalizable RL Frameworks:** The core policy integration and adaptation strategies are highly generalizable beyond basketball. They could be applied to other domains involving sequential, heterogeneous skills, such as complex industrial manipulation tasks, dexterous object interaction, or even simulating daily human activities.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors openly acknowledge several limitations:
- **Motion Quality:** Despite the advancements, the synthesized motion quality, particularly in terms of fluidity and fine details (e.g., dribbling posture, vertical lift in rebounding), still falls short of expert human players due to limited reference data.
- **Biomechanically Accurate Hand Models:** Incorporating more sophisticated hand models could further enhance the naturalness of ball interactions.
- **Large Language Models for Tactical Planning:** A promising future direction involves integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate high-level tactical plans for multiple simulated players. This would enable the training of more sophisticated autonomous agents in complex cooperative and adversarial team settings, moving beyond individual skill composition to strategic team play.

In conclusion, "Learning to Ball" provides a robust, adaptable, and generalizable framework for generating complex, long-horizon behaviors in physics-based characters. Its innovative solutions for policy composition and data utilization hold significant promise for advancing AI in interactive environments, with broad implications across computer graphics, robotics, and sports simulation.
