# Inverse Optimization Latent Variable Models for Learning Costs Applied to Route Problems

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel latent variable model, IO-LVM, learns representations for unknown cost functions of constrained optimization problems directly from observed optimal solutions, effectively disentangling diverse agent behaviors and guaranteeing feasible outputs by integrating a black-box solver. It consistently reconstructs complex structured solutions like Hamiltonian cycles more accurately than VAEs and identifies interpretable patterns in real-world datasets such as ship trajectories and taxi routes.

## Problem

- Traditional latent variable models struggle to generate feasible, structured solutions for discrete constrained optimization problems due to difficulty enforcing complex constraints during decoding.
- Inverse Optimization (IO) and Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods typically infer a single cost function, requiring explicit labeling to model diverse agent behaviors or contexts.
- The non-differentiability of black-box combinatorial optimization solvers prevents end-to-end gradient-based learning in generative models.

## Method

- The Inverse Optimization Latent Variable Model (**IO-LVM**) combines ==variational inference== with a black-box COP solver, where an encoder maps observed solutions to a latent space, and a decoder maps latent codes to cost functions which the solver then uses to reconstruct a guaranteed feasible solution.
- A Fenchel-Young loss, modified with input perturbations, is used to estimate gradients through the non-differentiable black-box solver, enabling end-to-end training.
- The model learns a distribution over cost functions in the latent space, capturing diverse underlying preferences without requiring explicit supervision of agent identities.

## Results

- IO-LVM disentangled distinct agent cost functions into separate latent space regions on synthetic data, outperforming a standard VAE which clustered by geometric similarity.
- The model achieved **31.9%** perfect Hamiltonian cycle reconstruction on the bayg29 dataset with 50 features, significantly surpassing the VAE's **3.4%** perfect reconstruction.
- Learned latent spaces correlated with unobserved factors like ship width on real-world ship path data, showing that certain latent regions inferred costs leading to avoidance of narrower passages for larger vessels.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a black-box optimization solver directly into the generative process ensures that all reconstructed or generated outputs are inherently feasible and satisfy problem-specific constraints.
> - The Fenchel-Young loss, combined with small input perturbations, provides a robust and practical method for obtaining informative gradients through non-differentiable discrete optimization problems.
> - Learning a distribution of cost functions in an interpretable latent space allows for discovering and modeling distinct behavioral patterns and hidden factors (e.g., agent preferences, physical attributes) from unlabeled observational data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the challenge of learning latent generative representations for solutions of constrained optimization problems (COPs), particularly when the underlying cost functions are unknown and the solutions are discrete. Traditional latent variable models, such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), often struggle to enforce complex structural constraints (e.g., forming connected paths in a graph) when decoding discrete outputs, leading to infeasible or unreliable samples.

Existing approaches to recovering underlying cost functions from observed solutions, often found in fields like Inverse Optimization (IO) and Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL), typically assume a single, global, or context-specific cost function. This limitation necessitates explicit labeling of agents or contexts when dealing with data exhibiting diverse behaviors, which is a common scenario in real-world datasets. Methods for differentiating through non-differentiable or discrete optimization problems include smoothing solver operations, interpolating solutions, perturbing costs, or relaxing the COP. However, these techniques often focus on single cost recovery or face scalability issues with combinatorial problems. For instance, differentiable dynamic programming methods, while useful, can struggle with scalability as graph size increases.

This work positions itself by combining elements of variational inference (similar to VAEs) with robust gradient estimation for black-box COP solvers. It distinguishes itself from standard IO/IRL by aiming to capture a *distribution* over cost functions rather than a single instance, thereby accommodating diverse agent behaviors without requiring explicit supervision (e.g., agent labels). Unlike VAEs, which may produce infeasible solutions, this approach guarantees feasibility by incorporating an optimization solver directly into the generative process. This integration of a solver addresses the fundamental challenge of ensuring that generated structured outputs adhere to problem-specific constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel latent variable model capable of learning representations for the underlying cost functions of Constrained Optimization Problems (COPs) from observed optimal solutions. This model aims to characterize the distribution of these cost functions, rather than inferring a single cost, which is crucial for understanding diverse behaviors.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and limitations in current machine learning and optimization paradigms:

- **Handling Discrete and Constrained Outputs:** Many real-world problems, particularly in domains like path planning and logistics, involve structured, discrete outputs (e.g., paths, cycles) that must satisfy specific constraints (e.g., connectivity, visiting all nodes). Standard deep generative models like VAEs are often ineffective in generating such feasible solutions due to their continuous nature and difficulty in enforcing hard constraints during decoding. The paper aims to overcome this by integrating a black-box COP solver into the generative process, ensuring reconstructed outputs are always feasible.
- **Addressing Non-Differentiability in COPs:** Learning end-to-end through a COP solver presents a significant challenge because the solver's operations are typically non-differentiable, particularly for discrete optimization problems. This leads to "non-informative gradients" that hinder the application of standard gradient-based optimization. The research is motivated by the need for a robust gradient estimation technique that can effectively bridge this gap, allowing the entire model to be trained end-to-end. The proposed solution leverages a Fenchel-Young loss with input perturbations to achieve this.
- **Modeling Diverse Agent Behaviors:** Traditional Inverse Optimization or Inverse Reinforcement Learning methods typically infer a single, static cost function or require explicit contextual information to model different behaviors. This limits their applicability to datasets comprising observations from multiple agents or varying conditions where the underlying cost functions differ but are not explicitly labeled. The paper is motivated to develop a model that can intrinsically learn and represent a *distribution* of cost functions, enabling the identification and modeling of distinct behavioral patterns (e.g., different agents' preferences) from unlabeled data.
- **Creating Interpretable Latent Representations:** The ability to map complex, high-dimensional solution spaces into a low-dimensional, interpretable latent space is a significant motivation. Such a space can facilitate analysis tasks, including clustering similar cost functions, denoising noisy observed solutions, and detecting anomalous behaviors, by providing a concise and meaningful summary of the underlying decision-making principles.
- **Generating Valid Solutions for Unseen Scenarios:** By learning a latent space of cost functions, the model aims to enable the generation of novel, feasible solutions for previously unobserved problem requirements (e.g., new start and target nodes). This capability allows for predicting how different "agents" (represented by points in the latent space) would behave in new situations, based on their learned cost preferences.

In summary, the core motivation is to develop a robust, flexible, and interpretable framework for learning the latent structure of COP costs from observed solutions, addressing key limitations of existing generative models and inverse optimization techniques in handling discrete constraints, non-differentiability, and diverse behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Inverse Optimization Latent Variable Model (IO-LVM)**, combines variational inference techniques with a black-box constrained optimization problem (COP) solver to learn latent representations of COP cost functions.

The IO-LVM architecture functions similarly to a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) but with significant modifications to handle discrete and constrained outputs:
1. **Encoder ($h_\phi$):** An encoder network $h_\phi$ maps an observed structured decision vector $x_i \in \mathcal{X}$ and its associated problem requirements $p_i \in \mathcal{P}$ to the parameters (mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$) of a variational distribution $q_\phi(z|x)$. This $q_\phi(z|x)$ approximates the intractable posterior $P(z|x)$, where $z \in \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ is a low-dimensional latent vector.
2. **Latent Space Sampling:** A latent variable $z$ is sampled from $q_\phi(z|x)$ using the re-parametrization trick ($z = \mu + \sigma \cdot \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}$).
3. **Decoder ($g_\theta$) and Cost Mapping ($\Phi$):** A decoder network $g_\theta$ maps the latent variable $z$ to a raw cost vector. This raw cost is then passed through a transformation $\Phi$ (e.g., an activation function like Softplus) to ensure it fits the input space of the COP solver (e.g., ensuring positive edge costs). The result is the inferred cost vector $y_\theta := \Phi(g_\theta(z))$.
4. **Black-box COP Solver ($\omega$):** The core distinguishing feature is the integration of a black-box COP solver $\omega$. This solver takes the inferred cost vector $y_\theta$ and the problem requirements $p_i$ to produce an optimal and *feasible* COP solution, $\hat{x}_i = \omega(y_i, p_i)$. This step guarantees that all generated solutions adhere to the problem's structural constraints (e.g., forming a connected path or a valid Hamiltonian cycle).

**Learning Process:**
The learning objective of IO-LVM adapts the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) used in VAEs. It replaces the standard reconstruction loss with a Fenchel-Young loss to address the non-differentiability introduced by the black-box solver:
$l(\theta, \phi) = E_{q_\phi(z|x)} [l_{FY}(y_\theta, x)] + \beta D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \parallel P(z))$

- **Fenchel-Young Loss ($l_{FY}$):** The chosen variant of the Fenchel-Young loss is defined as $l_\epsilon^{FY}(y, x) = \langle y, x \rangle - E_\epsilon[\min_{x' \in \mathcal{X}} \langle y + \epsilon, x' \rangle]$. This loss measures the suboptimality of the observed solution $x$ under the inferred cost $y$. Its key property is that its gradient with respect to $y$ is given by $\nabla_y l_\epsilon^{FY}(y, x) = x - \hat{x}_\epsilon$, where $\hat{x}_\epsilon = \omega(y + \epsilon, p)$. This formulation allows for gradient estimation even with a non-differentiable solver by introducing random perturbations $\epsilon$ to the input cost vector $y$.
- **Gradient Estimation:** The gradient of the Fenchel-Young loss with respect to the decoder parameters $\theta$ can be computed via the chain rule: $\nabla_\theta l_\epsilon^{FY}(y_\theta, x) = (x - \hat{x}_\theta^\epsilon) \frac{\partial y_\theta}{\partial \theta}$. The expectation over $\epsilon$ in the loss formulation allows for an unbiased stochastic gradient estimator. Critically, the linearity of expectation enables the use of a single perturbation per training step (instead of multiple Monte Carlo samples), significantly reducing computational cost. This makes the training practical using stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
- **KL Divergence Term:** The second term, $D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x) \parallel P(z))$, regularizes the latent space, encouraging the learned variational posterior to stay close to a prior distribution $P(z)$ (typically a standard Gaussian). The hyperparameter $\beta$ controls the balance between reconstruction accuracy and prior adherence.

**Assumptions and Applications:**
The methodology assumes that observed structured decisions are optimal solutions to a COP under some underlying, unobserved cost functions.
- **Path Planning:** For problems like Shortest Path Problems (SPP), the solver $\omega$ is Dijkstra's algorithm. Edge costs $y$ are inferred for a given graph, and problem requirements $p$ include start and target nodes.
- **Hamiltonian Cycles:** For problems like the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), a TSP solver is used for $\omega$. Edge costs $y$ are inferred for a complete graph.
- **Proposition 1:** A key assumption for the chosen Fenchel-Young loss variant is that the perturbation $\epsilon$ has independent, zero-mean components with identical variance. For this to work effectively, it is beneficial if all feasible solutions $x \in \mathcal{X}$ have approximately equal "length" (number of edges), as this ensures uniform expected cost and variance across solutions under perturbation, preventing bias. This holds exactly for Hamiltonian cycles and is considered a reasonable approximation for many real-world path planning scenarios.

**Inference Tasks:** Once trained, IO-LVM can perform various tasks:
- **Reconstruction:** Generate feasible paths/cycles from a latent code.
- **Path Distribution Prediction:** Sample from the learned latent space (e.g., using Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)) to infer distributions of paths/cycles.
- **Denoising:** Encode a noisy path and decode it to obtain a "cleaned" or more typical version.
- **Outlier Detection:** Identify anomalous paths by measuring their distance from the inferred path distribution in the latent or cost space.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of IO-LVM utilized both synthetic and real-world datasets across path planning and Hamiltonian cycle problems.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Latent Space Analysis

- **Synthetic Waxman Graph (Mult. S&T):** IO-LVM successfully disentangled the underlying cost functions of three distinct agents, mapping their varied paths (with different start/target nodes) to separate, distinct regions in the 2D latent space. This occurred even though agent identity was not provided during training. For example, paths generated by "Agent 1" (avoiding southern edges) consistently clustered together. In contrast, a standard VAE failed to capture this cost-based structure, instead clustering paths by their geometric similarity, leading to a scattered representation of agent-specific behaviors.
- **Ship Paths Dataset:** The learned 2D latent space for ship trajectories revealed interpretable correlations with unobserved factors, such as ship width. Regions of the latent space were found to correspond to the average width of ships whose paths encoded there. Specifically, larger ships were less frequently observed in a particular region (top-right corner). Furthermore, sampling from different latent regions and reconstructing paths for hypothetical start/target nodes showed that certain latent areas, likely associated with wider ships, inferred costs that led to avoidance of the narrower Öresund Strait in favor of the Great Belt, even if the latter was geometrically longer. This indicates that the latent space captured implicit navigation preferences.
- **Hamiltonian Cycles (TSPLIB):** An analysis of the latent space for Hamiltonian cycles demonstrated that paths with higher edge intersection (i.e., more similar usage of edges) were mapped closer to each other in the latent space. This was quantified by showing a positive correlation between decreasing Manhattan distance (edge usage similarity) and decreasing Euclidean distance in the latent space.

#### 4.2. Validating IO-LVM Reconstruction Quality

- **Hamiltonian Cycles (TSPLIB):** IO-LVM consistently outperformed the VAE baseline in the task of "full reconstruction match" for Hamiltonian cycles on burma14 and bayg29 datasets, especially with more complex underlying features (50 features). For instance, on bayg29 with 50 features, IO-LVM achieved 31.9% perfect cycle reconstruction, compared to 3.4% for VAE (both with 10 latent dimensions). The VAE often failed to produce valid Hamiltonian cycles, whereas IO-LVM, by design, guarantees feasible outputs even when the reconstruction is not perfect.
- **Impact of Training Data Size and Latent Dimensions:** IO-LVM maintained a larger performance advantage over VAEs when training data was scarcer (e.g., 20.8% vs. 0.8% for bayg29 with 50 features and 1000 training samples). While increasing latent dimensions could improve reconstruction recall for both models, IO-LVM's ability to ensure feasibility was a consistent advantage.

#### 4.3. Effect of Varying $\beta$ (Reconstruction vs. Denoising)

- In the synthetic Waxman dataset, increasing the $\beta$ parameter (weighting the KL divergence term) led to a decrease in the number of distinct paths reconstructed and a reduction in the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric between observed and inferred edges. This indicates a trade-off: higher $\beta$ promotes adherence to the prior distribution, leading to a denoising effect (less diverse, more "average" paths), while lower $\beta$ allows for more distinct and faithful reconstruction of individual paths. This capability was illustrated qualitatively by showing how IO-LVM could "denoise" real-world taxi paths by generating slightly modified, more typical routes.

#### 4.4. Inference Results

- **Path Distribution Prediction:** IO-LVM demonstrated superior performance in predicting the overall distribution of paths compared to VAE and a Perturbed Optimizer (PO) baseline. On synthetic, Ship, and Taxi datasets, IO-LVM achieved lower Jensen-Shannon divergence (D_JS) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) on edge usage frequencies. Notably, VAE struggled with sparse datasets like the Ship dataset, failing to converge. PO, which relies on naive sampling with noise, also underperformed IO-LVM, indicating the benefit of learning a structured latent space of costs.
- **Outlier Detection:** IO-LVM was capable of identifying artificially generated outlier paths in the taxi dataset. By computing a low-quantile score based on a distance metric derived from the learned costs, outlier paths consistently exhibited higher anomaly scores compared to typical observed paths. This suggests the model's potential for identifying unusual or unexpected behavior in route data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Inverse Optimization Latent Variable Model (IO-LVM) introduces a significant advancement in learning generative representations for constrained optimization problems, particularly those with discrete solution spaces like route planning. Its core significance lies in its ability to bridge the gap between deep generative models and combinatorial optimization solvers.

- **Guaranteed Feasibility of Generated Solutions:** By integrating a black-box COP solver into the decoding process, IO-LVM intrinsically guarantees that all generated samples are valid and feasible solutions to the underlying optimization problem. This addresses a major limitation of traditional generative models (like VAEs) that often produce infeasible or ill-structured outputs for complex constrained problems, enhancing the practical utility and reliability of generated data.
- **Modeling Diverse Decision-Making Behaviors:** Unlike most Inverse Optimization (IO) or Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods that typically infer a single cost function or require explicit context labels, IO-LVM learns a *distribution* over cost functions. This enables the model to capture and disentangle diverse behaviors arising from different agents or conditions within a dataset without prior supervision. This capability is critical for understanding heterogeneity in human or robotic decision-making processes.
- **Interpretable Latent Space for Cost Functions:** The model learns a low-dimensional latent space that represents the underlying cost functions, rather than the geometry of the solutions themselves. This leads to a more interpretable representation, as demonstrated by the ability to cluster agents with similar preferences or reveal hidden factors (like ship width) in the latent space. Such interpretability facilitates deeper insights into decision drivers and allows for intuitive analysis of complex structured data.
- **Versatile Applications in Path Analysis:** The demonstrated capabilities of IO-LVM – including accurate path reconstruction, effective prediction of path distributions, denoising of observed routes, and detection of anomalous behavior – indicate its broad applicability in various domains. From understanding shipping logistics and taxi movements to analyzing complex networks, the model offers tools for both generative tasks and data analysis.
- **Overcoming Non-Differentiability Challenges:** The use of a Fenchel-Young loss with perturbed inputs provides a robust mechanism for estimating gradients through non-differentiable black-box solvers. This technique extends the applicability of end-to-end deep learning to a broader class of combinatorial optimization problems where efficient solvers exist, opening avenues for future research in differentiable optimization.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework is generalizable to other COPs, provided that fast solvers and gradient estimation techniques are available. This sets a precedent for developing similar models for a wider array of constrained decision-making problems, fostering advancements in areas such as resource allocation, scheduling, and network design based on observed data.

In conclusion, IO-LVM represents a significant step towards learning meaningful, constrained generative models from observed optimal decisions, offering a powerful tool for analyzing, understanding, and predicting complex behaviors in structured environments.
