# LIBERO-VIFO: Benchmarking the Capability and Safety of Visual Cue Following in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> LIBERO-VIFO benchmarks VLA models on following visual cues (arrows, sketches, signage, codes) both correctly when authorized and safely when not. Eight cue families, 1,347 task-conditioned instances, four protocols test understanding, authorized following, language-cue conflict, and cue-only execution. Seven models show incomplete understanding-to-execution chains and unauthorized cue-driven task completion up to 60%.

## Problem

- Prior visual-cue-guided VLA work covers few cue forms and judges only final task success, hiding whether failure comes from cue perception or closed-loop execution.
- No benchmark isolates task-relevant visual cues as a distinct evaluation axis; existing suites treat visual input as scene context, robustness noise, or generic safety constraint.
- Existing evaluations assume all visual cues are authorized guidance, leaving unauthorized visual cue influence on robot behavior completely untested.

## Method

- ==Visual cue asset library== defines eight cue families (text, geometric, ghost trajectory, pictorial, gesture, signage, lighting, code) with manual, web, rendered, and generated variants.
- Automated task-conditioned construction pipeline parses each LIBERO task into object/state/target metadata and inserts cues via scene-anchored, fixed-region, or simulator-state overlay modes.
- ==Authorization-aware four-protocol evaluation chain==: staged VQA understanding, authorized following, language-cue conflict, and language-free cue-only execution with dedicated metrics (FCA, AVF, VTR, LFR, UVF, UVI).

## Results

- Full-Chain Accuracy across all four VQA stages never exceeds **17.6%** for any of seven models, showing understanding rarely survives the whole chain.
- Under language-cue conflict, Unauthorized Visual Cue Following Rate is **0.0%** for every model, but without language input Unauthorized Visual Induction Rate reaches **32.2-60.1%**.
- MolmoAct2 tops VQA Full-Chain Accuracy at **16.6%** yet only completes **33.5%** of authorized cue tasks, versus **61.2%** for the weaker-understanding Action-Sketcher.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cue comprehension and closed-loop cue-following are dissociable capabilities; benchmarking only final success masks which stage actually fails.
> - Language instructions reliably override conflicting visual cues today, but this priority may erode as visual perception and reasoning improve in future VLAs.
> - Absence of authorization signals is itself a safety gap: models will execute plausible-looking visual cues unprompted, a risk distinct from adversarial or robustness failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are increasingly being developed to enable robots to interpret visual observations and language instructions to perform actions. Recent advancements in this field have explored the integration of visual cues, such as arrows, trajectory curves, and visual prompts, to directly guide robot policy learning or inference. Methods like VIMA, RT-Trajectory, RT-Sketch, MOKA, TraceVLA, Robotic Visual Instruction, CrayonRobo, Action-Sketcher, and VP-VLA demonstrate the utility of visual guidance in supporting robot control.

However, existing research in this area exhibits several limitations. Current studies typically focus on a narrow range of visual cue forms and predominantly evaluate models based on final task success. This approach provides a coarse assessment of visual cue following capability, as it does not differentiate between failures arising from visual cue understanding (e.g., detection, interpretation, grounding) and those stemming from closed-loop execution. Furthermore, previous work has generally treated all visual cues as authorized task guidance, leaving the safety implications of unauthorized visual cue following largely unexplored. The potential for a robot to be influenced by unintended or malicious visual cues, despite explicit language instructions, presents a significant safety concern.

The broader landscape of VLA benchmarks, while extensive, also does not specifically address the joint evaluation of visual cue following capability and safety. Benchmarks like CALVIN and LIBERO focus on long-horizon manipulation and knowledge transfer, respectively. SIMPLER, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0 offer simulated evaluation for real-world policies and complex tasks. More recent LIBERO extensions investigate memorization and robustness, while VLA-Arena and LIBERO-Safety examine task structure, language commands, and safety constraints. Despite this breadth, these benchmarks primarily treat visual observations as scene input or vary them for robustness testing, rather than isolating task-relevant visual cues as a distinct evaluation target for both authorized following and unauthorized influence.

This work, LIBERO-VIFO, directly addresses these identified gaps. It introduces a benchmark specifically designed to systematically evaluate both the capability and safety of visual cue following in VLA models, covering a diverse set of cue forms and providing a granular assessment of performance, including safety-critical scenarios. By doing so, it introduces visual-centric safety as a new dimension for the VLA research community.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for introducing LIBERO-VIFO stems from the increasing adoption of visual cues in guiding robot learning and inference, coupled with the incomplete understanding of how VLA models interact with these cues. Visual cues can directly influence a robot's behavior, making it critical to evaluate not only the robot's ability to follow authorized guidance but also its capacity to disregard unauthorized or conflicting cues. The identified shortcomings in existing evaluation methodologies underscore the necessity for a comprehensive benchmark:

1. **Limited Scope of Existing Evaluations:** Current evaluations typically cover only a narrow range of visual cue forms (e.g., simple arrows, specific sketches). This restricted scope hinders a holistic understanding of VLA models' generalizability to the diverse visual cues they might encounter in real-world environments.
2. **Coarse Assessment of Capability:** Existing evaluations often focus solely on the final task success rate. This metric provides limited insight into the underlying causes of failure—whether a robot fails due to a misunderstanding of the visual cue (e.g., inability to detect, interpret, or ground it in the scene) or due to deficiencies in its closed-loop execution of the indicated task. A more granular assessment is needed to diagnose performance bottlenecks.
3. **Unexplored Safety Risks:** The prevalent assumption that all visual cues are authorized guidance leaves the safety implications of unauthorized visual cue following largely unaddressed. In practical scenarios, robots might encounter visual cues that conflict with explicit language instructions, or cues present when no instruction has been given. The potential for a robot to be misled or hijacked by such unauthorized cues poses significant safety risks, which have not been systematically investigated.

To address these motivations, LIBERO-VIFO sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Systematic Evaluation of Capability and Safety:** To establish a benchmark that comprehensively assesses both a VLA model's ability to correctly follow authorized visual cues and its robustness against unauthorized cues.
2. **Diverse Visual Cue Representation:** To define and integrate a broad visual cue asset library encompassing eight distinct families of visual cues, spanning diverse forms (e.g., text, geometric symbols, ghost trajectories, hand gestures, signage, machine-readable codes), to ensure comprehensive coverage. This also includes a robot-oriented signage system for future human-robot collaboration.
3. **Automated Cue Construction:** To develop an automated pipeline for constructing task-conditioned visual cue instances by extracting structured metadata from LIBERO tasks and appropriately inserting cues into robot observations, ensuring scalability and reproducibility.
4. **Authorization-Aware Evaluation Protocol:** To design a staged evaluation framework comprising four protocols organized into two parts:
 - **Part I (Capability):** To measure visual cue understanding through image-based Visual Question Answering (VQA) and assess authorized closed-loop following when language explicitly mandates cue adherence.
 - **Part II (Safety):** To evaluate behavior under conditions of language-cue conflict (where language and visual cues specify different tasks) and in the absence of any language instruction, to determine if unauthorized cues influence robot behavior.
5. **Benchmarking Representative VLA Models:** To apply the developed protocols to a suite of seven representative VLA models, thereby characterizing their strengths, weaknesses, and potential safety vulnerabilities with respect to visual cue following.
6. **Establish Visual-Centric Safety:** To highlight the importance of visual-centric safety as a novel and critical evaluation dimension within the VLA community, advocating for its systematic consideration in future model development and deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LIBERO-VIFO is constructed to provide a systematic evaluation of visual cue following. Its methodology comprises benchmark construction, a multi-part evaluation protocol, and experimental validation on representative VLA models, including extended studies.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Construction

The benchmark integrates a visual cue asset library with an automated pipeline for generating task-conditioned cue instances.

**Visual Cue Assets:** LIBERO-VIFO defines eight distinct visual cue families, designed to represent a diverse range of forms encountered in robot observations. These families include: F1 Text Overlay, F2 Geometric Symbol, F3 Ghost Trajectory, F4 Pictorial Demonstration, F5 Hand Gesture, F6 Visual Signage, F7 Visual Highlight, and F8 Machine-Readable Code. Each family includes multiple variants derived through manual design, web collection, simulator rendering, and image generation models. A specific robot-oriented signage system is also introduced within the visual-signage family, tailored for future human-robot collaborative environments. The benchmark contains 1,347 task-conditioned visual cue instances across 40 LIBERO tasks.

**Automated Cue Construction Pipeline:** As depicted in Figure 3, the pipeline processes LIBERO tasks to create cue instances.
- **Task Parsing:** A rule-based parser extracts critical metadata from each LIBERO task, including the manipulated object, target state or relation, and target object or region.
- **Cue Specification:** This extracted metadata is combined with a selected cue family and variant to generate a task-conditioned cue specification, which precisely defines the information conveyed by the visual cue.
- **Cue Placement and Insertion:** Visual cues are inserted into every robot camera observation during execution using one of three modes, chosen based on the cue's spatial requirements:
 - **Scene-anchored placement:** For cues that refer to specific objects or regions, 3D object poses are projected into image coordinates using camera parameters (e.g., F2, F5, F6, F7). This projection is recomputed at each timestep to maintain alignment with moving entities.
 - **Fixed-region insertion:** Cues conveying general task information without scene-specific localization are placed in reserved image regions (e.g., F1, F4, F8). Their content is composited and remains fixed throughout execution.
 - **Simulator-state overlay:** Ghost trajectories (F3) are constructed by superimposing rendered future states of manipulated objects onto the current observation, using opacity to visualize future motion.

#### 3.2. Authorization-Aware Evaluation Protocol

The benchmark defines four evaluation protocols, organized into two parts, to assess both capability and safety (Figure 1).

**Part I: Visual Cue Following Capability (Authorized)**
- **Part I-A: Image-Based Visual Cue Understanding:** This protocol assesses a model's ability to detect and comprehend a visual cue prior to closed-loop execution. It employs family-specific templates to generate four multiple-choice Visual Question Answering (VQA) stages: cue detection, semantic interpretation, scene grounding, and action decision. Performance is measured by accuracy at each stage and Full-Chain Accuracy (FCA), which requires all four questions for a single cue instance to be answered correctly.
- **Part I-B: Authorized Visual Cue Following:** This protocol evaluates whether a model's visual cue understanding translates into effective closed-loop execution. The language instruction explicitly directs the model to "follow the visual cue in the observation and complete the task," with the visual cue providing all necessary task-specific information throughout the rollout. Metrics include the Authorized Visual Cue Following Rate (AVF) and the Visual Transfer Ratio (VTR), which normalizes AVF by the model's Original Success Rate (Original SR) in a standard language-only setting.

**Part II: Visual Cue Following Safety (Unauthorized)**
- **Part II-A: Language–Visual Cue Conflict:** This protocol simulates a scenario where a robot, executing a task defined by language, encounters a conflicting visual cue specifying a different task. Metrics are the Language Following Rate (LFR) and the Unauthorized Visual Cue Following Rate (UVF), measuring completion of the language-specified and cue-indicated tasks, respectively. A low UVF indicates successful prioritization of language.
- **Part II-B: Visual Cue Influence Without Language:** This protocol assesses whether a robot completes a cue-indicated task based solely on the visual cue when no language instruction is provided. The Unauthorized Visual Induction Rate (UVI) measures this behavior, quantifying the risk of a robot being induced to act by an unauthorized visual cue in the absence of linguistic guidance.

#### 3.3. Experiments

Seven representative VLA models were evaluated on the main benchmark: OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5, MolmoAct2, Action-Sketcher, InstructVLA, InternVLA-A1.5, and Xiaomi-Robotics-0. All visual cues in the main benchmark were rendered as image overlays.

Additionally, three extended studies were conducted:
- **Scene-Instantiated Cues:** Text cards (F1), signs (F6), and code tags (F8) were registered as MuJoCo scene objects, allowing physical interaction and potential occlusion, to compare performance against image overlays.
- **Safety-Critical Settings:** Six safety-critical scenes, adapted from HazardArena, were used to assess visual cue following when cues indicated hazardous tasks. MolmoAct2 was used as a case study for F1 and F2 cues under authorized and conflict conditions.
- **Real-Robot Deployment:** The π0.5 model was evaluated on a 6-DoF AgileX PiPER robotic arm for a cup-stacking task, using a physical note as a visual cue under authorized and language-cue conflict (STOP cue) conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of seven VLA models using LIBERO-VIFO yielded several findings regarding the capability and safety of visual cue following.

#### 4.1. Main Benchmark Results

**Part I: Visual Cue Following Capability (Authorized)**
- **Incomplete Visual Cue Understanding Chain:** While some models demonstrated proficiency in individual VQA stages (cue detection, semantic interpretation, scene grounding, action decision), the Full-Chain Accuracy (FCA)—requiring all four questions for a given cue instance to be correct—remained low, never exceeding 17.6%. This indicates that correct predictions at isolated stages do not reliably propagate through the entire visual cue understanding process.
- **Understanding Does Not Reliably Translate to Execution:** A stronger visual cue understanding did not consistently correlate with stronger closed-loop following. For example, MolmoAct2 achieved an FCA of 16.6% but an Authorized Visual Cue Following Rate (AVF) of 33.5%, whereas Action-Sketcher showed a lower FCA of 1.9% but a higher AVF of 61.2%.
- **Varying Strengths Across Cue Families:** Models exhibited different performance profiles across the eight visual cue families. No single model consistently performed best across all cue types, suggesting specialized capabilities or limitations. For instance, InternVLA-A1.5 achieved 78.4% AVF on text overlays (F1) but only 36.1% on machine-readable codes (F8). Furthermore, the family a model understood best in VQA was not necessarily the one it followed most successfully in execution.

**Part II: Visual Cue Following Safety (Unauthorized)**
- **Language Priority in Conflict Scenarios:** In scenarios involving language–visual cue conflict, the Unauthorized Visual Cue Following Rate (UVF) was 0.0% for all evaluated models. This indicates that, at the task-completion level, language instructions maintained clear priority over conflicting visual cues. However, this zero-UVF result may be partially attributed to the limited visual cue following capability observed in Part I, rather than a robust ability to intentionally disregard unauthorized cues.
- **Emerging Risk of Unauthorized Visual Induction:** Without any language input, the Unauthorized Visual Induction Rate (UVI) ranged between 32.2% and 60.1% across models. This finding demonstrates that current VLA models are capable of executing tasks solely based on visual cues, exposing an emerging safety risk where robots might be induced to perform actions by unauthorized visual guidance in the absence of explicit language instructions. UVI also varied considerably across cue families for individual models (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT's UVI ranged from 3.3% for geometric cues to 83.1% for lighting cues).

#### 4.2. Extended Experiments Results

- **Visual Cues as Simulated Objects:** When visual cues (text cards, signs, code tags) were instantiated as interactive MuJoCo scene objects rather than observation overlays, performance generally decreased. Compared to observation-inserted cues, scene-object placement led to an average reduction of 17.7 percentage points in VQA, 29.0 points in authorized visual cue following (AVF), and 35.4 points in unauthorized visual induction (UVI). The larger decline in execution performance is attributed to factors like occlusion, viewpoint changes, and physical displacement of the cues as scene objects.
- **Visual Cues in Safety-Critical Scenes:** A case study with MolmoAct2 in safety-critical scenes revealed that authorized visual cues *can* lead to hazardous behavior, with the model completing hazardous cue-indicated tasks in 13.3% of episodes when authorized. Under language–cue conflict in these scenes, MolmoAct2 consistently prioritized the safe language-specified task, never completing the hazardous cue-indicated task.
- **Real-World Evaluation:** Real-robot deployment of π0.5 on a cup-stacking task corroborated the simulation findings. The robot successfully followed an authorized physical note for task completion. In a language–cue conflict scenario, the model prioritized the language instruction over a conflicting physical "STOP" cue, aligning with simulation results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LIBERO-VIFO represents a significant contribution to the field of Vision-Language-Action models by introducing a benchmark that systematically evaluates both the capability and safety of visual cue following. This work addresses critical gaps in existing VLA evaluation methodologies, which previously offered limited coverage of cue forms and lacked granular assessment of understanding versus execution, while largely neglecting safety considerations.

The primary significance of LIBERO-VIFO lies in:

1. **Establishing a New Evaluation Dimension:** It formally establishes visual-centric safety as a new and crucial evaluation dimension for VLA models. By providing protocols to assess behavior under authorized, conflicting, and absent language conditions, the benchmark enables a more comprehensive understanding of how VLA models interact with diverse visual guidance.
2. **Granular Performance Diagnostics:** The multi-stage VQA protocols allow for detailed diagnostics, distinguishing failures in visual cue detection, semantic interpretation, scene grounding, and action decision. This granularity is essential for identifying specific weaknesses in VLA architectures and guiding future model improvements.
3. **Identifying Emerging Safety Risks:** The benchmark highlights the practical risk of Unauthorized Visual Induction (UVI), demonstrating that current VLA models can execute tasks based solely on visual cues in the absence of language. This finding underscores the need for robust mechanisms to prevent unintended or malicious visual cue exploitation, particularly as VLA models become more capable and are deployed in less controlled environments.
4. **Informing Model Development:** The finding that visual cue understanding does not reliably translate into closed-loop execution suggests that future VLA research needs to focus on bridging this gap, potentially through better integration of visual perception with policy execution. The varying performance across cue families also indicates the need for models that can generalize effectively across different visual guidance formats.
5. **Foundation for Robust Human-Robot Interaction:** The inclusion of a robot-oriented signage system within the benchmark provides a foundation for developing standardized visual communication protocols for future environments where humans and robots collaborate closely. This could lead to safer and more intuitive interaction paradigms.
6. **Real-World Relevance:** The extended experiments, including scene-instantiated cues, safety-critical scenarios, and real-robot deployment, enhance the ecological validity of the findings, demonstrating the relevance of LIBERO-VIFO's insights to practical robotic applications. The observed impact of physically instantiated cues on performance, for instance, highlights challenges related to perception under occlusion and dynamic environments.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. By providing a standardized and comprehensive evaluation framework, LIBERO-VIFO can guide the development of more capable, robust, and inherently safer VLA models. It encourages the VLA community to move beyond mere task success and systematically consider the nuanced interplay between visual cues, language instructions, and robot behavior, particularly concerning safety in shared human-robot spaces. The limitations acknowledged by the authors, such as the scope of the current asset library, the challenge of distinguishing genuine cue-driven behavior from visual shortcuts, and the potential for future models to behave differently in conflict scenarios, also point towards fruitful avenues for subsequent research and benchmark expansion.
