# HumanPlus: Humanoid Shadowing and Imitation from Humans

> [!summary] Summary
> The HumanPlus system, developed at Stanford University, enables humanoid robots to learn complex skills from human demonstrations via real-time motion shadowing using a single RGB camera. It successfully learns tasks like folding clothes and unloading racks from 20-40 demonstrations, achieving success rates between 60% and 100% in autonomous operation.

## Problem

- Programming complex humanoid robots is challenging due to their high degrees of freedom and intricate dynamics.
- A scarcity of robot-specific training data exists, despite the abundance of human motion and skill data.
- Existing teleoperation methods for humanoids often require specialized equipment, leading to high cognitive load and a steep learning curve.

## Method

- Developed a real-time humanoid shadowing system using a single RGB camera, where a ==Humanoid Shadowing Transformer (HST)== retargets human poses to robot joint setpoints.
- Introduced the Humanoid Imitation Transformer (HIT), a vision-based policy that learns complex tasks from a small number of human demonstrations by processing egocentric images and proprioception.
- Utilized a custom humanoid robot with 33 degrees of freedom and a robust control architecture that combines high-level policy outputs with low-level PD control.

## Results

- The system successfully learned and performed complex tasks (e.g., wearing a shoe, folding a sweatshirt, unloading objects) from 20-40 demonstrations per task, achieving 60-**100%** success rates in autonomous operation.
- The HumanPlus controller demonstrated enhanced robustness, withstanding up to 100N of force in the y-direction and 44N in the x-direction, outperforming the default robot controller.
- User studies showed that the HumanPlus shadowing system achieved lower time-to-completion and higher success rates for stable standing compared to kinesthetic teaching, ALOHA, and Meta Quest teleoperation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Humanoid robots can effectively leverage abundant human motion data for learning complex skills, bypassing the need for extensive robot-specific data.
> - Combining real-time human motion shadowing with a vision-based imitation learning policy enables sample-efficient learning of diverse tasks from limited demonstrations.
> - A hierarchical control structure (50Hz high-level policy, 1000Hz low-level PD controller) and integrated forward dynamics prediction contribute to robust and generalizable motion execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Humanoid robots have long been a focus of robotics research due to their potential to operate in human-designed environments and perform tasks similar to humans. However, programming these complex machines to perform useful tasks remains challenging due to their high degrees of freedom and complex dynamics. The HumanPlus system, developed by researchers at Stanford University, presents an innovative approach to addressing this challenge by enabling humanoid robots to learn directly from human demonstrations.

The fundamental premise of HumanPlus is that humanoid robots can leverage the vast amounts of human motion and skill data available for training. This approach bypasses the scarcity of robot data by enabling robots to mimic humans, tapping into the rich repertoire of skills and motion exhibited by people in everyday activities.

### 2. Hardware and System Design

The HumanPlus system utilizes a custom humanoid robot with 33 degrees of freedom (DoFs), including 7 DoFs in each hand and wrist. As shown in Figure 1, the robot stands 180 cm tall, weighs 60 kg, and can carry payloads of up to 7.5 kg with its arms. The robot features binocular RGB cameras for vision and has the mechanical capability to perform a wide range of human-like movements.

A key aspect of the hardware design is its emphasis on practicality and reliability. The robot is powered by a battery that provides approximately 2 hours of operation time while standing, making it suitable for real-world deployment scenarios. The mechanical design allows for precise motion control with arm repeatability of 5mm and a maximum pulling force of 32N, enabling interaction with various objects in the environment.

### 3. Humanoid Shadowing Approach

The HumanPlus system's central innovation is its approach to real-time humanoid shadowing using only a single RGB camera. As depicted in Figure 2, the process involves several key steps:

1. **Human Pose Estimation**: A standard RGB camera captures human motion at 60Hz, which is then processed to estimate body and hand poses at 25Hz.

2. **Pose Retargeting**: The estimated human poses are retargeted to the humanoid's morphology, accounting for differences in proportions and joint configurations.

3. **Humanoid Shadowing Transformer (HST)**: The core of the control system is the HST, a transformer-based policy that takes the retargeted poses as input and generates joint position setpoints at 50Hz.

The HST is trained using reinforcement learning in simulation, conditioned on target humanoid poses derived from the AMASS dataset, which contains 40 hours of human motion data. This approach allows the policy to generalize across a wide range of motions without requiring additional real-world training.

The control architecture is designed to be robust to the challenges of real-world operation:

```python
# Simplified pseudocode for the HST control loop
def humanoid_shadowing_control(proprioception, target_pose):
    # Process inputs through the transformer architecture
    token_embeddings = embed_inputs(proprioception, target_pose)
    for i in range(n_layers):
        token_embeddings = transformer_layer(token_embeddings)

    # Generate joint position setpoints
    joint_setpoints = output_head(token_embeddings)

    # Send to low-level PD controller
    torques = pd_controller(joint_setpoints, proprioception.joint_positions)

    return torques
```

The HST operates at 50Hz, generating joint position setpoints that are passed to a PD controller running at 1000Hz. This hierarchical control structure enables smooth and stable motion execution.

### 4. Imitation Learning Architecture

Once the humanoid can shadow human motion, the next step is to enable it to learn tasks autonomously from demonstrations. HumanPlus introduces the Humanoid Imitation Transformer (HIT), a vision-based policy that allows the robot to learn from a small number of human demonstrations.

The HIT architecture, shown in Figure 3, has several innovative aspects:

1. **Vision-Based Processing**: The policy takes egocentric binocular RGB images from the robot's cameras as input, along with proprioceptive information.

2. **Forward Dynamics Prediction**: To prevent overfitting to proprioception and encourage the use of visual information, HIT incorporates a forward dynamics prediction component that predicts future camera images.

3. **Transformer-Based Architecture**: The policy uses a transformer architecture to process temporal sequences of visual and proprioceptive information, enabling it to capture complex patterns in the data.

The mathematical formulation for the HIT loss function combines action prediction with forward dynamics prediction:

$$L_{HIT} = L_{action} + \lambda L_{feature}$$

Where $L_{action}$ is the standard behavior cloning loss (mean squared error between predicted and demonstrated actions), and $L_{feature}$ is a feature-space loss that regularizes the policy by predicting future visual features. This combined approach helps the policy generalize better from limited demonstration data.

### 5. Performance Evaluation

The HumanPlus system demonstrates impressive capabilities across a range of complex tasks. With only 20-40 demonstrations per task, the robot successfully learns to perform activities such as:

- Wearing a shoe then standing up and walking
- Unloading objects from warehouse racks
- Folding a sweatshirt
- Rearranging objects on a table
- Typing on a keyboard
- Greeting another robot

These tasks achieve success rates ranging from 60% to 100% in autonomous operation, demonstrating the system's ability to learn practical skills from limited data.

The robustness of the low-level policy is quantitatively evaluated by measuring its ability to withstand external disturbances. As shown in Figure 6, the HumanPlus controller can withstand significantly larger disturbance forces compared to the manufacturer's default controller:

The experiments demonstrate that the HumanPlus controller can withstand up to 100N of force in the y-direction and 44N in the x-direction, compared to 40N and 36N respectively for the default controller. This enhanced robustness is crucial for reliable operation in unstructured environments.

### 6. Comparison with Other Methods

A key advantage of the HumanPlus system is its accessibility and ease of use compared to alternative teleoperation methods. The paper evaluates three alternative approaches:

1. **Kinesthetic Teaching**: Physically guiding the robot by hand, which requires direct physical interaction with the robot.

2. **ALOHA**: A teleoperation system that uses a custom exoskeleton interface.

3. **Meta Quest**: A VR-based teleoperation system using commercial VR controllers.

User studies show that the HumanPlus shadowing system achieves the lowest time-to-completion and highest success rate for stable standing compared to these alternatives. This suggests that the natural interface provided by HumanPlus—simply performing the desired motion in front of a camera—reduces the learning curve and cognitive load for operators.

### 7. Key Innovations and Contributions

The HumanPlus system makes several significant contributions to the field of humanoid robotics:

1. **End-to-End Learning Framework**: HumanPlus presents a complete pipeline from human demonstration to autonomous robot execution, integrating perception, control, and learning.

2. **Low-Cost Teleoperation**: By using only a standard RGB camera for teleoperation, HumanPlus makes humanoid robot control accessible without specialized equipment.

3. **Task-Agnostic Low-Level Control**: The HST provides a robust foundation for a wide range of motions without task-specific reward engineering.

4. **Sample-Efficient Learning**: The HIT architecture enables learning complex skills from a small number of demonstrations, making the approach practical for real-world applications.

5. **Real-World Validation**: The system demonstrates successful performance on diverse tasks in unstructured environments, showing its practical utility.

### 8. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive capabilities, the HumanPlus system has several limitations that present opportunities for future research:

1. **Visual Perception**: The current system primarily relies on proprioception for shadowing, with vision mainly used for autonomous skill execution. Integrating visual feedback into the shadowing process could enhance precision.

2. **Physical Interaction**: While the robot can manipulate objects, its interaction capabilities are limited by factors such as sensing resolution and control bandwidth. Improving tactile sensing could enable more dexterous manipulation.

3. **Task Complexity**: The demonstrated tasks, while impressive, are still relatively structured. Extending to more complex, multi-stage tasks requiring reasoning and planning represents an important direction for future work.

4. **Generalization**: The current system requires separate policies for different tasks. Developing more general-purpose policies that can perform a variety of tasks from the same training data would increase utility.

5. **Safety**: As with any large, powerful robot, ensuring safe operation around humans remains a critical consideration for real-world deployment.

### 9. Conclusion

The HumanPlus system represents a significant advancement in enabling humanoid robots to learn from human demonstrations. By combining reinforcement learning for low-level control, imitation learning for skill acquisition, and a simple teleoperation interface for data collection, HumanPlus creates a comprehensive approach to humanoid robot programming.

The demonstrated ability to learn complex tasks from a small number of demonstrations suggests that this approach could make humanoid robots more practical for real-world applications. The natural interface for teleoperation reduces the barrier to entry for non-expert users, potentially expanding the use cases for humanoid robots.

As research in this field continues, we can expect further improvements in the robustness, versatility, and intelligence of humanoid robots. The HumanPlus system provides a solid foundation for these future developments, bringing us closer to the vision of general-purpose humanoid robots that can seamlessly integrate into human environments.
