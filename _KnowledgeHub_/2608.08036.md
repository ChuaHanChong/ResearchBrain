# Compiling and Benchmarking Task-State Horizons for Embodied Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboGraph is a task compiler that turns spatial and temporal state-transition dependencies into executable symbolic scene graphs, letting benchmark designers control a new difficulty axis called task-state horizon (TSH): how far agents must maintain, explore, and update task-relevant world state. The released RoboGraphBench has 588 episodes across 84 scenes; across 8820 rollouts GPT-5.5 leads at 78.4% tabletop success while open-source models score near zero.

## Problem

- Existing long-horizon robotic benchmarks characterize difficulty mainly through action-sequence length and subgoal count, overlooking how far task-relevant world state must be tracked.
- Prior embodied memory and dynamic-scene benchmarks entangle state dependencies inside subtasks, so failures cannot be attributed to forgetting, missing exploration, or stale beliefs.
- Procedural task generators scale scenes and demonstrations but never control hidden-state or exogenous-change dependencies, leaving state-management difficulty uncontrolled and unmeasurable across generated tasks.

## Method

- ==Task-state horizon (TSH)==, a triple of maintenance, exploration, and update spans computed on the oracle trajectory, making state-management burden an instance property independent of the agent.
- **RoboGraph** compiler emits executable semantic scene graphs with an ==observation operator== projecting partial view graphs, plus an ==event schedule== of trigger-conditioned graph rewrites for exogenous change.
- **RoboGraphBench**: 84 auto-generated tabletop and indoor scenes, each with baseline plus six interventions, and five ==state-management metrics== (AER, RAR, SEG, ADR, IRR) in a unified closed-loop harness.

## Results

- GPT-5.5 leads with **78.4%** tabletop and **82.5%** indoor success plus best SSAL; open-source models collapse, Qwen3-VL-32B at **9.3%** and RoboBrain2.0-7B at **0%**.
- State-management metrics track success better than conventional proxies: AER correlates **0.69** between tabletop scenes, RAR **-0.71** indoors, exploration SEG/ADR reach **0.72/0.74** within scenes.
- Added occlusion is the most disruptive intervention, cutting success **10.4** points tabletop and **4.4** indoors; injected action failures need roughly **36** steps of recovery latency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Satisfying preconditions is easy, using history is hard: 93.6% executability coexisted with 47.7% redundant actions, so executability alone overstates state maintenance.
> - Difficulty axes separate: between-scene correlations expose task hardness, within-scene correlations expose model capability, so report both before claiming a benchmark measures reasoning.
> - Symbolic view graphs isolate planning and state management; simulator backends add perception and execution error, and their metrics correlate only moderately with symbolic ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of embodied AI has seen advances in models capable of interpreting language, grounding visual observations, and generating action plans for long-horizon tasks. Existing robotic benchmarks contribute to the evaluation of these tasks by increasing the complexity of action sequences, subtasks, and environmental conditions. For instance, benchmarks like VirtualHome, ALFRED, ALFWorld, BEHAVIOR, and BEHAVIOR-1K have expanded task composition, memory requirements, and adaptation to changing environments by focusing on aspects such as traversing multiple rooms, manipulating numerous objects, and satisfying multiple subgoals. These benchmarks primarily characterize task difficulty based on the length and structural complexity of action sequences and subgoals.

However, the authors identify a limitation in this approach: existing benchmarks tend to implicitly entangle task-relevant state dependencies within subtasks, overlooking the explicit challenge of an agent needing to track evolving task-relevant world states. This includes information that was initially hidden and required exploration, or state changes due to environmental dynamics or interventions. Research in long-term memory (e.g., FindingDory, LMEE-Bench, 3DMem-Bench, MIKASA-Robo, RoboMME, RoboMemArena) and tracking changing world states (SpaMEM, WorldLines) addresses aspects of retaining and updating information. Similarly, procedural task generation (ProcTHOR, GenSim, RoboGen, PARTNR, ManiTaskGen) has expanded the scale and diversity of environments and tasks.

This work contributes to the landscape by formalizing a distinct dimension of long-horizon task difficulty: the "task-state horizon" (TSH). TSH specifically quantifies the demands on an agent to maintain, explore, and update task-relevant world state over time, irrespective of the action-sequence length or subgoal count. By introducing TSH, the paper aims to provide a complementary framework for systematically evaluating and training embodied agents, moving beyond a sole focus on action-centric metrics to explicitly assess an agent's state-management capabilities in dynamic and partially observable environments.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while frontier agentic models are increasingly used as high-level planners for long-horizon embodied tasks, existing benchmarks do not adequately characterize a critical challenge: the agent's need to track evolving task-relevant world states. This deficiency is noted even in tasks with identical action sequences but differing state-management demands, such as when an object's location is initially hidden or subject to change.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Formalize the Task-State Horizon (TSH):** To introduce a quantitative definition for the span over which an agent must maintain, explore, and update task-relevant world state. This formalization decomposes state-management demands into three distinct dimensions:
 - **State maintenance:** The ability to retain and retrieve previously observed state information.
 - **State exploration:** The capacity to actively resolve hidden or uncertain state through deliberate actions.
 - **State updating:** The mechanism to detect environmental changes and revise outdated beliefs about the world state.
 The goal is to enable fine-grained attribution of task failures to specific state-management errors.

2. **Develop RoboGraph, a Robotic Task Compiler:** To design and implement a system that translates spatial and temporal causal dependencies among task-relevant state transitions into executable symbolic graphs. This compiler is intended to automatically generate numerous task instances with varying TSHs, including those induced by unexpected failures and interventions during task execution. RoboGraph aims to serve as an intermediate representation executable across various backends, from symbolic environments to simulators and real-world robots.

3. **Construct RoboGraphBench, a Benchmark Dataset:** To create a benchmark using RoboGraph that comprises a diverse set of robotic tasks with controlled TSHs. This benchmark is designed to facilitate systematic measurement and controlled variation of TSH for evaluating agent performance. The goal is to include a variety of task families and scenes, incorporating external interventions to introduce dynamic changes and test state-updating capabilities.

4. **Evaluate Advanced Agentic Models:** To benchmark the performance of a range of advanced agentic models (including LLMs and VLMs) using RoboGraphBench in both semantic and visual closed-loop environments. This evaluation aims to identify the current capabilities and limitations of these models concerning demanding TSHs and to reveal gaps in their ability to maintain, explore, and update task-relevant state over long horizons.

5. **Introduce Novel Metrics:** To propose and utilize new metrics that specifically assess agents' state-management capabilities, complementing traditional task success rates and action-centric measures. These metrics aim to disentangle TSH difficulty from model capabilities and provide deeper insights into an agent's performance in state-aware tasks.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology centers on formalizing the Task-State Horizon (TSH) and developing a compiler, RoboGraph, to systematically generate and evaluate tasks with controlled TSHs.

**Problem Formulation:**
Embodied interaction is framed as a partially observable decision process. The task-relevant world state at time $t$ is represented by a scene graph $G_t = (V_t, E_t, X_t)$, where nodes $V_t$ are entities, edges $E_t$ are relations, and $X_t$ are node attributes. A base task is defined by an initial state ($G_0$), a goal predicate ($g$), and a set of task-level action schemas ($A$). The agent operates under partial observation, receiving a task-conditioned view graph $^eG_t = O(G_t, h_t)$ at each step, where $h_t$ is the interaction history and $O$ exposes currently observable facts. The world state $G_{t+1}$ evolves based on the agent's action $a_t$ and an optional exogenous event $e_t \in \mathcal{E} \cup \{\emptyset\}$. A rollout succeeds if the goal is met within a step budget.

The paper defines **state dependency** where a decision at step $t$ relies on a fact $f$ not recoverable from the current view graph. These dependencies are categorized as:
- **Spatial dependency:** $f$ is hidden (e.g., occluded, inside a container) and requires view-exploring actions to expose.
- **Temporal dependency:** $f$, observed at $t' < t$, remains necessary for an action at $t$. An exogenous event can make $f$ stale, requiring updating.

The **Task-State Horizon (TSH)** for a task instance $I$ is defined as a tuple $H = (H_{maintain}, H_{explore}, H_{update})$, computed on an oracle action sequence $\tau^*$:
- $H_{maintain}$: Maximum steps between acquisition of a temporally-dependent fact and its last constraining decision.
- $H_{explore}$: Maximum minimal view-expanding actions needed to expose a spatially-dependent fact in $G_0$.
- $H_{update}$: Number of facts invalidated by exogenous events along $\tau^*$.

**RoboGraph: A Task-State Horizon Compiler:**
RoboGraph is designed to transform a task-family specification and a source semantic scene graph into an executable task instance $I = (G_0, g, A, O, \Lambda)$.
- **Executable Scene Graph:** Task instances are represented as executable semantic scene graphs. Nodes possess affordances and mutable states (e.g., openness, location), while edges represent spatial/functional relations (e.g., containment, support, occlusion). Visibility is dynamically determined; entities are hidden by containment or occlusion, or restricted by room boundaries in indoor scenes.
- **Compiling Partial Observations:** The observation operator $O(G_t, h_t)$ generates the view graph $^eG_t$. It identifies visible nodes ($V_{vis_t}$) as entities in the current room reachable without crossing active visibility constraints ($E_{block_t}$). $^eG_t$ includes visible nodes, their relations, observable attributes, and feedback from the preceding action. Hidden changes remain unobserved until the affected region is revealed.
- **Compiling State Transitions:** RoboGraph compiles rules for world graph updates. Agent actions $a_t$ are checked against $G_t$'s preconditions and apply effects as graph rewrites if executable. Exogenous changes are specified by an event schedule $\Lambda = \{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_K\}$, where each $\lambda = \langle\psi_\lambda, \delta_\lambda, \kappa_\lambda\rangle$ defines a trigger ($\psi_\lambda$), graph rewrite ($\delta_\lambda$), and application time ($\kappa_\lambda$). Triggers are based on semantic task progress rather than fixed time steps.

**RoboGraphBench:**
The benchmark dataset is constructed from 84 source scenes across 7 task families (57 tabletop, 27 indoor). Each scene yields one baseline and six intervention conditions, totaling 588 episodes (399 tabletop, 189 indoor).
- **Intervention Types:** Six types of interventions ($I_1$ to $I_6$) are incorporated: action failure, state regression, completed-subgoal rollback, wrong relocation, added occlusion, and an added-object subgoal. These are triggered dynamically based on task progress to introduce controlled state changes.
- **Scene Statistics:** Scene graphs average 18.4 nodes, 21.8 edges, 6.1 occluded nodes, and 14.3 subgoals. The average symbolic oracle action-sequence length is 21.7 actions.

**Experiments Setup:**
- **Base Models:** 15 advanced agentic models are evaluated, including 8 closed-source (LLMs: DeepSeek-V4 Pro, GLM-5.2; VLMs: GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen3.6-Plus, Qwen3.7-Plus) and 7 open-source (VLMs: Qwen3-VL-8B/32B, InternVL3.5-8B/38B, Cosmos-Reason1-7B, RoboBrain2.0-7B/32B).
- **Closed-loop Evaluation:** Models are deployed in a unified agentic harness. At each step, they receive current observation (symbolic graph or visual images), task goal, action skills, and recent action history. They select an action, and the harness updates the latent state and provides feedback.
- **Observation Interfaces:** The primary evaluation uses symbolic scene graphs for semantic closed-loop interaction, replacing visual input. An extension evaluates models using visual (RGB camera) observations in simulation backends (RoboTwin 2.0 for tabletop, RoboCasa for indoor).
- **Metrics:**
 - **Overall Performance:** Task Success Rate (SR, %) and Success weighted by Symbolic Action Length (SSAL).
 - **State Management:** Action Executability Rate (AER, %), Redundant Action Rate (RAR, %), State Exploration Gain (SEG, %), Action-Distance Reduction Rate (ADR, %), and Intervention Recovery Rate (IRR, %).
 - **Failure Recovery:** F1@Recovery Detection (F1@RD, %) and Acc@Recovery Grounding (Acc@RG, %).

### 4. Main findings and results

The evaluation of 15 agentic models across 588 episodes in RoboGraphBench yielded several key findings regarding agent performance and state-management capabilities.

**Overall Performance (RQ1):**
- **Model Hierarchy:** GPT-5.5 demonstrated the strongest overall performance, achieving a Task Success Rate (SR) of 78.4% on tabletop tasks and 82.5% on indoor tasks. It also had the highest SSAL, indicating efficient task completion relative to the oracle. Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.7 were the next highest performers among closed-source models.
- **Performance Gap:** There was a significant performance disparity between closed-source and open-source models. Open-source models, even the best among them (Qwen3-VL-32B at 9.3% SR on tabletop), showed substantially lower success rates, with some failing to complete any tasks.
- **Failure Modes:** Analysis of Normalized Goal Progress (NGP), termination distributions, and average step counts revealed different failure patterns. Models like Qwen3.6-Plus and Qwen3.7-Plus often achieved partial progress (NGP around 60-75%) but terminated prematurely, suggesting insufficient exploration. Other models, such as GPT-5.4 and open-source VLMs, frequently exhausted the step budget, indicating prolonged but unproductive exploration and poor state maintenance (e.g., InternVL3.5-8B averaging 96.0 steps).

**State-Management Capabilities (RQ1):**
- **State Maintenance:** GPT-5.5 exhibited superior state maintenance with the highest Action Executability Rate (AER) (97.4% tabletop, 99.4% indoor) and the lowest Redundant Action Rate (RAR) (12.2% tabletop, 10.6% indoor). Many models achieved high AER, but struggled with RAR, indicating they could often select executable actions but failed to leverage historical information to avoid redundant actions or accurately represent the current task state. For example, GPT-5.4 had a high AER (93.6%) but also a high RAR (47.7%), contributing to its lower SR.
- **State Exploration:** GPT-5.5 also performed best in state exploration, achieving 76.9% State Exploration Gain (SEG) and 16.7% Action-Distance Reduction Rate (ADR) on tabletop tasks, with Claude Opus 4.7 and Gemini 3.1 Pro also performing competitively. A general trend was lower SEG and ADR on indoor tasks compared to tabletop tasks, implying greater difficulty in resolving spatial and temporal dependencies in more complex indoor environments where agents must navigate rooms and locate objects.
- **State Updating and Recovery:** GPT-5.5 achieved a high Intervention Recovery Rate (IRR) of 78.6% on tabletop tasks, and GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.7 both reached 81.4% on indoor tasks. Recovery detection (F1@RD) was generally easier than grounding the correct recovery action (Acc@RG), particularly for actions like `grab` and `putin`.

**TSH Difficulty vs. Conventional Metrics (RQ2):**
- **Between-Scene Analysis:** When comparing task difficulty across different scenes (averaged over models), conventional metrics like oracle action-sequence length ($L_o$) and subgoal counts ($N_g$) showed only moderate correlation with SR. In contrast, AER was highly informative for tabletop tasks ($\rho = 0.69$), and RAR was highly informative for indoor tasks ($\rho = -0.71$). SEG and ADR showed weaker scene-level correlations, suggesting they might not be the primary indicators of inherent scene difficulty for all models.
- **Within-Scene Analysis:** When analyzing performance differences between models within the *same* scene, SEG and ADR exhibited strong positive correlations with SR (median $\rho$ of 0.72 and 0.74 for tabletop, 0.61 and 0.56 for indoor, respectively). This indicates that exploration metrics are effective at distinguishing models' capabilities in handling the state-management demands of a specific TSH-controlled scene.
- **Conclusion:** The study demonstrates that while action length and subgoal count offer a coarse measure of task difficulty, the proposed state-management metrics provide complementary and more nuanced insights into model capabilities under varying TSHs.

**Intervention Impact (RQ3):**
- **Occlusion as Most Disruptive:** The "added occlusion" intervention ($I_5$) resulted in the largest decline in Task Success Rate across both task families (10.4 percentage points on tabletop, 4.4 points indoors). This suggests that interventions introducing new spatial and temporal dependencies, particularly those requiring agents to re-explore occluded states, impose the most demanding state-updating requirements.
- **Recovery Latency:** Action failures ($I_1$) incurred the longest average recovery latency (approximately 36 steps), indicating that recovering from such failures requires substantial replanning and action to repair consequences, beyond mere detection.

**Extension to Visual Backends:**
- Evaluation on RoboTwin 2.0 (tabletop) using RGB camera observations showed positive correlations between metrics in the semantic and visual settings (e.g., Spearman's $\rho=0.37$ for NGP, $\rho=0.75$ for ADR).
- However, task success rates were substantially lower in the visual simulation backend, suggesting that visual grounding and state decoding introduce additional challenges not present in the symbolic setting. This indicates that symbolic evaluation primarily isolates planning and state-management, while visual evaluation additionally assesses perception and precise physical execution.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of embodied AI, with potential impacts on the development and evaluation of intelligent agents.

Firstly, the introduction and formalization of the **task-state horizon (TSH)** provide a novel and complementary perspective on long-horizon task difficulty. By explicitly identifying and quantifying the demands for state maintenance, exploration, and updating, the work addresses a critical gap in existing benchmarks that often conflate state-management challenges with action-sequence length or subgoal complexity. This new framework allows for a more granular understanding of why embodied agents succeed or fail, enabling researchers to attribute performance issues to specific state-management deficits (e.g., forgetting a location, failing to explore hidden areas, or not adapting to environmental changes).

Secondly, the development of **RoboGraph** is significant as a robust and flexible task compiler. Its ability to translate spatial and temporal state dependencies into executable symbolic graphs, and to dynamically introduce various types of interventions, offers a systematic method for generating diverse and controlled TSHs. This programmatic control over task complexity is crucial for targeted evaluation and training, ensuring that benchmark tasks are not merely long but also specifically challenging in their state-management requirements. The design also facilitates execution across different backends (symbolic, simulated, real-world), enhancing its versatility.

Thirdly, the creation of **RoboGraphBench** provides a valuable new benchmark dataset. With 588 TSH-controlled robotic tasks across various scenes and intervention conditions, it offers a comprehensive testbed for current and future agentic models. The benchmark, coupled with the suite of new state-management specific metrics (AER, RAR, SEG, ADR, IRR), enables a detailed assessment of model capabilities beyond traditional success rates, facilitating a deeper analysis of planning, memory, and adaptability.

The findings from evaluating 15 frontier agentic models highlight substantial remaining gaps in handling demanding TSHs, particularly concerning state maintenance (e.g., avoiding redundant actions), state exploration (especially in complex indoor environments), and robust state updating in the face of dynamic interventions like occlusion. Even top-performing models like GPT-5.5 exhibit imperfections, indicating areas for future improvement.

The work's impact could be multifaceted:
- **Improved Model Development:** By clearly identifying and measuring state-management deficiencies, this research can guide the development of more sophisticated agent architectures and learning algorithms that explicitly address long-term memory, active exploration, and dynamic world state tracking.
- **Standardized Evaluation:** TSH-controlled task compilation and the accompanying metrics could become a standard framework for evaluating embodied agents, complementing existing benchmarks and leading to more holistic assessments of agent intelligence.
- **Robust Agents:** Ultimately, by focusing on reliable state management in dynamic and evolving worlds, this research contributes to the development of more robust, autonomous, and general-purpose embodied agents capable of operating effectively in complex real-world scenarios.
