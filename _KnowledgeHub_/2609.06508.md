# VLA-Corrector: Stage-Aware Observable State Understanding for Prompt-Based Closed-Loop Recovery of Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> VLA-Corrector adds a non-invasive detect-and-correct loop around a frozen pi0.5 VLA policy. A GRU-based Learned Verifier reads dual-view images, proprioception, and executed actions to estimate execution risk plus a five-stage manipulation phase; on a gated trigger it appends a stage-conditioned recovery directive to the original instruction so the same policy emits corrective actions. Standard LIBERO success rises from 70.9% to 80.2% with no privileged state.

## Problem

- Prior VLA policies generate instruction-compliant actions but carry no mechanism to verify execution fidelity, so accumulated deviations derail otherwise valid trajectories, and final task success is the only supervision signal available, giving no diagnostic information about when or why action noise, object displacement, or goal shift caused failure.
- Existing recovery schemes need policy finetuning, extra action heads, trajectory editing, or privileged simulator state, none deployable around a frozen released policy.
- Action chunking introduces replanning latency during which stale actions persist despite environment changes, and long-horizon tasks leave no horizon for autonomous recovery.

## Method

- **Learned Verifier**: parameter-free spatial-statistics visual featurizer over two views plus projected state-action vector, fused by a 64-unit ==GRU== over an 8-step history, with risk and stage heads.
- Teacher supervision converts privileged simulator progress into operational labels only offline: stage classes plus a binary risk label within 10 steps of failure events, ambiguous 10-20 step windows excluded.
- **Prompt Recovery** behind an online ==trigger gate==: risk threshold 0.40, four-window consecutive confirmation, 10-step cooldown, queries every two steps, then preserve the original instruction, concatenate an 'Immediate recovery:' stage directive routed by predicted stage, with stage confidence below 0.9625 falling back to a generic prompt, let the frozen policy act, then restore the original instruction.

## Results

- Standard LIBERO success goes **70.9%** (no assistance) to **82.6%** with the privileged rule verifier and **80.2%** with the Learned Verifier, a **2.4 pp** privileged-to-observable gap; per-perturbation averages reach **81.14%**, **88.12%**, and **86.88%**, with combined perturbation gains of **+11.7 pp** (rule) and **+9.3 pp** (learned) over no assistance.
- Verifier detection hits **93.1%** accuracy, **94.9%** recall, **61.1%** precision, **74.4%** positive F1, AUROC **0.902**, with **0.457 ms** p50 latency and **79.2%** post-gate trigger precision.
- Ablations drop positive F1 by **15.0** (single frame), **15.8** (state-action only), **10.8** (agent view only), and **6.3** (vision only); real-robot success is **88.3%** versus **82.5%** baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Observable visual-proprioceptive-action history suffices to infer latent task stage and execution risk, so monitors need not depend on privileged simulator coordinates.
> - Language is a usable actuator for correction: appending a stage directive turns any frozen instruction-following policy into its own recovery controller, without retraining.
> - Temporal context matters more than visual capacity here, since a parameter-free featurizer plus GRU works while single-frame input costs 15 positive-F1 points.
> - Detector metrics and closed-loop success diverge, so a 61% precision monitor can still help once consecutive-window confirmation and cooldown gating suppress false triggers.
> - Caution: labels derive from a privileged simulator teacher, so the pipeline still needs a simulator with ground-truth state to bootstrap the deployable monitor.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies have become a major approach for general-purpose robot manipulation. Systems such as RT-1, RT-2, PaLM-E, OpenVLA, π0, Octo, and related models use visual observations and natural-language instructions to produce robot actions. These systems benefit from large-scale multimodal pretraining, action chunking, and receding-horizon execution, allowing a single policy to address many manipulation tasks.

However, most VLA systems are primarily action-generation systems. They produce actions conditioned on the current instruction and observations, but they generally lack an explicit mechanism for determining whether the executed actions have caused the robot to deviate from a valid task trajectory. This limitation is particularly relevant for long-horizon tasks, in which small errors can accumulate across approach, grasping, transport, and placement.

The paper addresses this gap between action generation and execution monitoring. It is related to several existing research directions: visuomotor progress estimation, temporal representation learning, failure detection, language-guided replanning, robot recovery controllers, and privileged-state supervision in simulation. Unlike approaches that retrain the policy, add a dedicated recovery action head, or directly modify trajectories, VLA-Corrector places a separate verifier around a frozen VLA policy. The verifier monitors observable execution history and uses a language prompt to request recovery from the same policy.

The work therefore contributes primarily at the system and control-interface level. Its central claim is that visual observations, proprioception, and executed-action history can provide sufficient information to estimate latent execution progress and risk, even without object coordinates or simulator success predicates.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has three closely related objectives.

First, it aims to define execution-state understanding as a distinct capability from action generation. A VLA policy may understand the task instruction and produce locally plausible actions while still entering an invalid state because of action noise, an imperfect grasp, object displacement, or a shifted goal region. The authors argue that final task success is insufficient for diagnosing such failures because it does not reveal when or why the trajectory became unsafe.

Second, the work seeks to develop a deployable failure verifier that does not depend on privileged environmental information. In simulation, it is possible to access object poses, target coordinates, end-effector geometry, gripper status, task predicates, and success flags. These signals can support a rule-based state machine, but they are usually unavailable on a physical robot. The proposed Learned Verifier is intended to operate only on data that can be observed during normal execution: two camera views, proprioceptive states, and actions that were actually executed.

Third, the authors aim to recover from detected failures without modifying the underlying VLA policy. When the verifier identifies abnormal execution, the system preserves the original instruction and appends a stage-specific recovery directive. The same frozen policy then generates corrective actions. This design is motivated by the practical cost of fine-tuning large VLA models and by the desire to use existing policies as reusable controllers with an external monitoring layer.

The manipulation process is represented through five semantic stages: approach, alignment, grasp, move or transport, and placement. These stages provide an interpretable description of progress and allow the recovery prompt to be conditioned on the likely location of the failure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. System Architecture

The overall system consists of a frozen VLA policy, an execution verifier, a trigger gate, and a prompt-based recovery mechanism. At each control cycle, the policy receives the original task instruction and recent observations, then produces an action chunk. The system executes only the first five actions in that chunk before querying the policy again. This receding-horizon arrangement limits the duration of open-loop execution.

The base controller is the pretrained OpenPI `pi05_libero` policy. Its parameters, visual encoder, and action-generation head remain unchanged. Images are rendered at 256 × 256 pixels and resized to 224 × 224 for policy inference. The action dimension is seven.

#### 3.2. Observable History

At time \(t\), the verifier receives an agent-view RGB image, a wrist-view RGB image, an eight-dimensional proprioceptive vector, and a seven-dimensional executed-action vector. It processes an eight-step history window sampled every two environment steps. Importantly, the history contains actions actually executed in the environment rather than only actions proposed by the policy. This distinction allows the verifier to detect discrepancies between intended and realized behavior.

#### 3.3. Learned Verifier

The visual processing branch is deliberately lightweight. Each image view is reduced using adaptive average pooling to a \(3 \times 4 \times 4\) RGB representation, and the image mean and standard deviation are appended. This produces 54 features per camera view. The combined visual representation is projected to 64 dimensions.

The proprioceptive and action vectors are concatenated into a 15-dimensional state-action input and projected to 32 dimensions. The visual and state-action features are then combined and processed by a GRU with a hidden dimension of 64. Two output heads are trained:

1. A binary execution-risk head estimates the probability that a teacher-defined failure event will occur within the next ten environment steps.
2. A stage-classification head predicts one of five nonterminal stages: approach, alignment, grasp, move, or place.

The stage prediction is used for routing recovery prompts rather than as a direct failure detector.

#### 3.4. Teacher Labels and Training

A privileged rule-based verifier is constructed using simulator state. It tracks the sequence approach → alignment → grasp → move → place → success and uses ground-truth object and region poses, end-effector kinematics, gripper status, motion predicates, timeouts, and task flags.

These privileged variables are not supplied to the learned model at deployment. Instead, they are converted into operational labels. A risk label is positive when a teacher-defined failure event occurs within ten steps of the current time and negative when the nearest failure event is more than twenty steps away. Intermediate windows are omitted from binary training to avoid ambiguous labels.

The verifier is trained on 120 episode-disjoint examples, divided into 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% test sets. Training lasts 30 epochs with a batch size of 32, a learning rate of \(10^{-3}\), and weight decay of \(10^{-4}\). The model runs on a CPU.

#### 3.5. Triggering and Prompt Recovery

The binary risk probability is converted into an intervention using a threshold of 0.40. The system requires four consecutive positive verifier windows and applies a ten-sample cooldown to suppress transient alerts and repeated interventions. Verifier queries occur every two environment steps.

When a trigger is accepted, the currently pending action chunk is discarded. The predicted stage determines a recovery prompt, such as an approach- or grasp-specific directive. If stage confidence is below 0.9625, the system uses a generic unknown-failure prompt. The recovery instruction is concatenated with the original task instruction, ensuring that the policy retains the overall task objective. After recovery actions are completed, the original instruction is restored and normal execution resumes.

The study compares three configurations: No Assistance, Rule + Prompt using the privileged verifier, and Learned Binary + Prompt using the deployable learned verifier.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Performance

On standard LIBERO, the reported aggregate success rates are 70.9% for No Assistance, 82.6% for Rule + Prompt, and 80.2% for Learned Binary + Prompt. Thus, both monitoring-based methods improve over the frozen baseline, while the learned verifier remains 2.4 percentage points below the privileged rule-based verifier.

For LIBERO Plus, the average success rates across clean and perturbed conditions are 73.7%, 79.7%, and 79.0% at difficulty 4 for the three configurations, respectively. At difficulty 5, the corresponding values are 66.7%, 73.6%, and 72.6%. These results indicate that prompt-based recovery continues to provide gains as task difficulty increases, although the learned method remains slightly below the privileged alternative.

The perturbation study on standard LIBERO reports average success rates of 81.14% for No Assistance, 88.12% for Rule + Prompt, and 86.88% for Learned Binary + Prompt. Under the combined perturbation condition, success is 70.7% without assistance, 82.6% with the rule-based verifier, and 80.2% with the learned verifier.

The largest improvements occur under target-object shifts, goal-region shifts, and their combination. This supports the paper’s focus on execution deviations rather than language misunderstanding. Action noise also reduces baseline performance, but the assisted systems retain higher success rates.

#### 4.2. Verifier Quality

The Learned Verifier achieves 93.1% accuracy, 61.1% precision, 94.9% recall, and a positive-class F1 score of 74.4%. Its AUROC is 0.902 and its AUPRC is 0.7123. These results show that the verifier detects most teacher-defined risk events, although its precision indicates that some alerts are false positives.

The trigger gate improves operational usefulness by requiring repeated risk predictions before intervention. Gate-level trigger precision is reported as 79.2%. Recovery succeeds in 77.0% of cases for Rule + Prompt and 75.0% for Learned Binary + Prompt. Verifier inference latency is 0.457 milliseconds at the median and 0.589 milliseconds at the 95th percentile.

#### 4.3. Ablation Results

The full temporal-fusion model performs better than masked alternatives. It obtains a positive F1 score of 74.35%, compared with 59.33% for a single-frame model, 63.56% for agent-view-only input, 68.03% for vision-only input, and 58.55% for state-action-only input.

The results indicate that temporal context is important for recognizing execution risk. Camera observations are also more informative than proprioceptive and action history alone, but the strongest performance comes from combining both modalities over time. The ablations support the claim that failure recognition depends on changes and interactions across observations, actions, and robot state rather than on a single image.

#### 4.4. Real-World Transfer

The physical-robot evaluation includes Pick and Open, Place, and Pour Water. Each task is tested over 40 trials, with the target object deliberately moved after execution begins. No physical-world data are used to retrain either the policy or verifier.

Across the three tasks, the proposed system achieves an average success rate of 88.3%, compared with 82.5% for the frozen `pi05_libero` baseline, an absolute improvement of 5.8 percentage points. The result suggests that the observable-history verifier and prompt interface can transfer beyond simulation, although the experiments cover only three task types and one perturbation style.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VLA-Corrector presents a modular strategy for improving reliability without changing a pretrained VLA policy. Its principal significance is the separation of control and execution monitoring: the VLA generates actions, while a lightweight temporal model estimates whether those actions are leading toward failure. This arrangement could reduce the need for policy retraining when new execution disturbances are encountered.

The use of stage-conditioned prompts also provides an interpretable interface between monitoring and action generation. Rather than issuing an undifferentiated reset, the system can communicate that the robot should re-approach an object, reattempt a grasp, or correct a placement.

Several limitations remain. The verifier is trained using privileged simulator-derived labels, so its deployability depends on whether those labels adequately represent real-world failures. The perturbations are controlled and limited in type, and the evaluation does not establish performance under arbitrary occlusions, hardware faults, novel object geometries, or severe sensor errors. Recovery also depends on the frozen VLA policy’s ability to interpret the appended prompts. Finally, the study emphasizes final success and operational trigger quality more than detailed analyses of intervention frequency, false recovery costs, or failure modes not captured by the five-stage representation.

Within these limits, the paper provides evidence that observable visual-proprioceptive-action histories can support execution-risk estimation and closed-loop recovery for existing VLA policies. Its approach is relevant to research on deployable robot reliability, policy monitoring, and modular safety mechanisms for long-horizon manipulation.
