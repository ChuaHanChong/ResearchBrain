# PredTac: Learning Contact-Rich Manipulation with Predicted Touch

> [!summary] Summary
> PredTac replaces measured tactile input with predicted touch: a predictor trained under tactile supervision infers current contact fields from causal wrist RGB and robot-state histories, is then frozen, and supplies tactile observations to policies during both training and execution. On three contact-rich real-robot tasks it averages 70.0% success versus 72.2% for measured touch and 21.1% for vision-only ACT.

## Problem

- Cameras leave contact-rich states partially observable: no contact, edge contact and partial seating can look identical in a wrist view, so visual policies miss decisive events.
- Physical tactile sensors resolve that ambiguity but add hardware, calibration, synchronization and maintenance burden at both policy-training and deployment time, complicating contact-rich policy learning.
- Prior touch-supervised methods bury contact knowledge in distilled weights or judge visual-to-touch predictors by reconstruction error alone, so whether generated contact content actually drives control stays untested.

## Method

- A causal ==tactile predictor== fuses three wrist-RGB frames and matched 14-D robot states through a shared CNN, state MLP, two eight-head temporal ==Transformer== layers and spatial ==cross-attention==.
- Structured contact heads (contact, support, phase, side activity, area, shape) gate a base-plus-bounded-residual decoder, trained by a loss combining full-field reconstruction, active-contact weighting, temporal consistency and geometry terms.
- Two-stage workflow freezes the predictor, then trains policies on its imperfect outputs: a **ContactWorld** world-model ==CEM== planner in simulation, and **ACT** with dual-finger taxel forces on hardware.

## Results

- Simulation goal-offset success with predicted touch reaches **27.0%**, **52.0%** and **44.7%** on USB, Barbed-spike and Valve, beating the visual controller by **8.0** to **13.7** percentage points.
- Real-robot predicted-touch ACT scores **70.0%**, **50.0%** and **90.0%** across 30 trials each, mean **70.0%**, against **72.2%** measured touch and **21.1%** vision.
- Fixed-policy interventions: zeroing current touch costs **15.0** points on USB; spatial-block reordering that preserves the value histogram costs **10.7** points on Valve.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Tactile supervision can be paid once at training time: a frozen visual-to-touch predictor keeps most of the benefit without sensors on the deployed robot.
> - Policies read spatial structure of contact, not just aggregate intensity, so reconstruction error is an incomplete proxy for downstream control value.
> - Predictor reuse across tasks is uneven: a shared predictor helped five of six training tasks yet hurt the sixth, so transfer needs per-task checking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Contact-rich manipulation includes tasks in which physical interaction with an object is central to success, such as insertion, extraction, grasping, and rotation. These tasks are difficult to solve using vision alone because visually similar configurations can correspond to different contact states. For example, a connector may appear aligned with a port while being only partially inserted, pressing against an edge, or fully seated. Tactile sensors can reveal contact location, pressure, support, and release events that are poorly observable from cameras.

Most existing approaches address this problem by adding physical tactile sensors to the robot’s policy input. This creates practical challenges, including sensor calibration, mechanical integration, synchronization with cameras and robot states, sensor wear, and maintenance. A related line of research predicts tactile observations from vision, depth, geometry, or robot state. Other work uses tactile data during pretraining or teacher-policy learning, then deploys a student policy without tactile input. PredTac is positioned between these approaches. Rather than using predicted touch only as an internal training signal or latent representation, it treats predicted touch as an explicit input channel for a downstream manipulation policy during both policy training and execution.

The paper therefore addresses two related questions. First, can a policy trained and deployed with predicted tactile observations perform better than a vision-only policy, without receiving measured touch at runtime? Second, which characteristics of the predicted tactile signal matter for control? The second question is important because low reconstruction error does not necessarily mean that a prediction is useful to a policy. A predicted field may have an inaccurate shape but still provide relevant contact timing, or it may reproduce overall intensity while losing spatial structure needed for manipulation.

PredTac is related to cross-modal visual–tactile prediction, tactile representation generation, visuotactile policy learning, tactile world models, and policy distillation. Its experimental emphasis distinguishes it from work that reports only tactile reconstruction quality or only final task success. The paper combines controller comparisons with fixed-policy interventions that replace or corrupt predicted tactile inputs. This design attempts to separate the benefit of using a tactile-like signal from the particular ways in which a trained policy depends on that signal.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to determine whether learned, current-touch estimates can substitute for measured tactile input in contact-rich manipulation. The proposed workflow has two stages:

1. A tactile predictor is trained using visual observations, robot-state histories, and measured tactile data as supervision.
2. The trained predictor is frozen, and a manipulation policy is trained and executed using the predictor’s outputs rather than measured touch.

This arrangement is intended to avoid a train–test mismatch. If a policy were trained with measured tactile data and then deployed with predicted touch, it could fail because predicted signals are noisier or have different statistics. In PredTac, the policy sees the same imperfect predictor outputs during policy learning and deployment.

The motivation is not to claim that predicted touch reproduces physical sensing exactly. Instead, the paper treats predicted touch as a control representation that may encode contact-relevant information in a form compatible with tactile-policy architectures. The predictor uses only causal data from the current and two preceding time steps. It does not use future observations, measured touch at deployment, demonstration actions, control outcomes, or future state information. The term “predicted touch” therefore refers to inference without runtime tactile input, rather than forecasting a future tactile state.

The paper also investigates whether policies use meaningful structure in predicted touch. It evaluates the effects of removing current predictions, substituting predictions from other episodes, replaying temporally displaced predictions, and spatially rearranging values while preserving their histogram. These tests are designed to examine dependence on contact presence, intensity, temporal alignment, and spatial arrangement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

At time \(t\), the predictor receives three wrist-RGB frames and three corresponding robot-state vectors:

\[
\hat{\tau}_t = g_{\phi}(v_{t-2:t}, p_{t-2:t}, u),
\]

where \(v\) denotes RGB observations, \(p \in \mathbb{R}^{14}\) is the robot-state vector, and \(u\) is an optional task or source context used by some predictor checkpoints. The output is a bilateral tactile field, although the exact physical meaning varies by experiment. In simulation, it is adapted to the ContactWorld tactile interface. For the real robot, the predictor estimates native dual-finger taxel forces.

#### 3.2. Tactile Predictor Architecture

Each RGB frame is processed by a shared convolutional encoder. The robot state is embedded through a multilayer perceptron with dimensions 14–128–256 and fused with visual tokens. Two eight-head temporal Transformer layers combine the observations from the three time steps at each spatial location. Subsequent spatial attention and encoding produce a 512-channel latent representation, which is processed by six residual blocks.

The decoder includes structured contact-conditioning branches. These estimate variables such as contact state, support, interaction phase, side activity, contact area, amplitude, and contact-shape descriptors. These variables control decoder gates and blending operations. The architecture also retains a direct appearance pathway, allowing visual spatial features to contribute directly to the predicted field.

The field decoder follows a base-plus-bounded-residual design. For each tactile side and time step, a stable side-specific base is combined with an appearance-derived residual. The residual is clipped to a fixed range and modulated by contact-, phase-, support-, and shape-dependent gates. This design is intended to prevent unconstrained visual artifacts and to impose a structured relationship between contact activity and output intensity. The native predictor generates six fields at \(128 \times 128\): left and right fields for each of the three input times. The simulator runtime selects the required side and resizes it for the downstream controller.

#### 3.3. Training Objective

The tactile predictor is supervised by measured tactile fields. Its objective combines several terms:

- Full-field reconstruction loss.
- Increased weighting for active-contact pixels.
- Temporal-difference consistency.
- Contact classification loss.
- Contact-area matching.
- Amplitude matching.
- Contact-centroid matching.

The weights are checkpoint-specific. The paper reports a source predictor trained on 1,022 real-operation episodes, corresponding to approximately 32,700 frames. Additional simulation predictors are trained either from scratch or by adapting this architecture to task-specific tactile targets.

For USB simulation training, the authors use 161 episodes, with 20 validation episodes, 1,000 updates, and a fixed seed of 42. A separate shared predictor is trained across six simulation tasks—Barbed-flat, Barbed-spike, Lidded-loose, Peg, Power, and USB—with equal task sampling and 3,000 updates. Valve tactile data are excluded from this shared predictor’s training to test cross-task reuse.

#### 3.4. Policy Learning

In simulation, PredTac supplies predicted touch to the ContactWorld controller together with visual and state observations. ContactWorld contains visual–tactile encoders, a latent dynamics model, and a cross-entropy-method planner. Its planner uses a horizon of six, 100 candidate action sequences, eight elites, and four iterations.

The primary conditions are:

- **T/T:** Simulator touch is used during policy training and execution.
- **P/P:** Predicted touch is used during both stages.
- **Vision:** A separately trained RGB-only controller is used.
- **P/0:** A predicted-touch policy is executed after predicted current and goal touch are removed.

Additional fixed-policy interventions replace the current predicted field with zeros, predictions from other episodes, temporally shifted predictions, or spatially rearranged fields.

#### 3.5. Real-Robot Evaluation

The physical platform consists of a six-axis RealMan RM65B arm, a WHEELTEC gripper, two PaXini M3025 tactile arrays, and two Orbbec Femto Bolt cameras. Each tactile array contains 77 taxels with three force components per taxel. ACT policies operate at 10 Hz, use \(128 \times 128\) wrist images, and predict eight-step action chunks.

Three policy conditions are compared:

- Visual ACT uses current RGB and robot state.
- Measured-touch ACT additionally receives measured tactile data.
- Predicted-touch ACT receives predicted tactile data during both training and execution, with measured tactile data excluded from policy input.

The tasks are USB insertion, Barbed extraction, and Valve rotation. Each condition is evaluated over 30 physical trials per task, for 270 trials in total.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Performance

Under the goal-offset simulation protocol, predicted-touch policies achieve:

- **USB:** 27.0% success.
- **Barbed-spike:** 52.0% success.
- **Valve:** 44.7% success.

The separately trained visual policies achieve 19.0%, 43.3%, and 31.0%, respectively. Thus, predicted touch improves over the visual baseline by 8.0 percentage points on USB, 8.7 points on Barbed-spike, and 13.7 points on Valve.

The simulator-touch policies achieve 27.0%, 52.3%, and 48.7%. Predicted-touch performance is therefore close to measured simulator touch on USB and Barbed-spike, but lower by four percentage points on Valve. These comparisons are descriptive and do not demonstrate statistical equivalence.

Removing the predicted touch input from the already trained predicted-touch policy reduces performance by 11.7, 1.7, and 8.3 points in the broader P/0 evaluation for USB, Barbed-spike, and Valve. This indicates that the policies are not simply ignoring the predicted tactile channel, although the magnitude of dependence varies across tasks.

#### 4.2. Sensitivity to Contact Content

In a separate fixed-policy intervention suite, zeroing current predicted touch causes success reductions of 15.0 points on USB, 5.3 points on Barbed-spike, and 7.0 points on Valve. Replacing current predictions with random donor episodes produces drops of 4.0, 9.0, and 3.7 points, respectively.

On Barbed-spike, matching the donor prediction’s contact pattern and intensity reduces the degradation relative to random replacement, suggesting that aggregate contact timing and magnitude are useful. On Valve, spatial-block rearrangement produces the largest observed reduction, lowering success by 10.7 points despite preserving the exact pixel-value histogram. This result suggests that spatial arrangement, rather than only total intensity or value distribution, can influence control. Temporal replacement generally causes smaller observed changes, although the authors do not interpret this as definitive evidence that temporal information is unimportant.

#### 4.3. Real-Robot Results

Predicted-touch ACT achieves:

- **USB insertion:** 70.0%, or 21 successes out of 30.
- **Barbed extraction:** 50.0%, or 15 out of 30.
- **Valve rotation:** 90.0%, or 27 out of 30.

Visual ACT achieves 3.3%, 23.3%, and 36.7%, while measured-touch ACT achieves 76.7%, 53.3%, and 86.7%. The equal-weight three-task means are 70.0% for predicted touch, 72.2% for measured touch, and 21.1% for vision only.

Predicted touch therefore substantially exceeds visual ACT on all three tasks and is close to measured-touch ACT in the reported trials. However, the physical comparisons use only 30 trials per condition, and the paper reports descriptive differences rather than confidence intervals or significance tests. In addition, the Barbed policies use different model capacities and training configurations across visual and tactile conditions, which limits direct attribution to the tactile modality alone.

#### 4.4. Prediction Quality and Reuse

For USB, the best separately evaluated predictor reports a field MAE of 0.0282 and contact-mask intersection-over-union of 0.8845. These metrics characterize reconstruction quality but are not directly sufficient to predict policy performance.

In a fixed-policy Valve study, target-supervised predictors improve success from 37.3% for the source predictor to as high as 53.3% when trained from scratch with 82 target episodes. Fine-tuning and training from scratch both improve performance, but fine-tuning does not consistently outperform training from scratch at matched data budgets.

The shared six-task predictor improves mean success from 41.7% to 43.7% across the six tasks when substituted into fixed policies. Five tasks improve, while Lidded-loose declines from 29.7% to 24.0%. When substituted into the Valve P/P policy without retraining and without Valve tactile supervision, it yields 44.0% success compared with 44.7% using the original predictor.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PredTac provides evidence that predicted tactile observations can function as an explicit control interface for contact-rich manipulation. Its main practical implication is that a robot may use tactile supervision during development while avoiding physical tactile sensors as policy inputs during deployment. This could reduce hardware complexity and simplify calibration and maintenance, provided that the available visual and state observations contain enough information to infer useful contact states.

The intervention results also contribute to understanding what makes predicted touch useful. They suggest that task policies may depend on different aspects of contact information: intensity and contact matching in one task, and spatial structure in another. This supports evaluating predicted tactile signals by downstream control utility rather than reconstruction error alone.

The study has important limitations. It does not isolate the contributions of temporal fusion, structured contact conditioning, state input, or bounded decoding. Simulation and physical experiments use different protocols, and the policy-training comparisons are based on limited seeds. Predictor reuse is inconsistent across tasks, indicating that cross-task transfer is not guaranteed. Finally, the predictor still requires tactilely supervised data during training, so the method removes runtime tactile input rather than eliminating the need for tactile data collection entirely. Further evaluation across objects, task distributions, sensors, and repeated training runs is needed to establish generalization and robustness.
