# Learning Humanoid Navigation from Human Data

> [!summary] Summary
> EgoNav enables zero-shot humanoid robot navigation in diverse, unseen environments by learning exclusively from human walking data. The system develops a scene-aware, multimodal distribution of plausible paths, allowing a Unitree G1 robot to navigate autonomously over 1,100 meters while effectively avoiding obstacles, including transparent glass walls.

## Problem

- The high cost and difficulty of acquiring robot-specific data for training navigation policies on humanoid platforms.
- Limitations in existing learning-based approaches, including narrow camera fields of view, difficulty with transparent/reflective surfaces, lack of semantic understanding, and unimodal trajectory predictions.
- Challenges in transferring human commonsense navigation behaviors to robots due to embodiment-specific interfaces and insufficient human data alone for robust generalization.

## Method

- Collected a large-scale human walking dataset using body-mounted cameras, including RGBD and 6-DoF localization, across diverse environments and conditions.
- Developed a comprehensive scene representation combining a 3600 egocentric ==Visual Memory== (RGBD + semantic channels) and appearance-level ==DINOv3 features== for robust scene understanding, including transparent surfaces.
- Employed a ==non-autoregressive UNet diffusion model== for multimodal 6-DoF trajectory prediction, utilizing a ==hybrid DDIM-DDPM sampling scheme== for real-time inference, and integrated it with a receding horizon controller for zero-shot deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid.

## Results

- EgoNav enabled the Unitree G1 humanoid to autonomously navigate over **1,137 meters** across diverse, unseen indoor and outdoor environments with **96-99%** autonomous time, exhibiting emergent behaviors like crowd navigation and obstacle avoidance.
- The system demonstrated superior offline collision avoidance (**91.4%**) and multimodal trajectory coverage (minADE-15 of **0.39**) compared to unimodal baselines.
- Real-world deployment confirmed the necessity of DINOv3 features for avoiding transparent glass walls, with intervention rates nearly tripling without them, and highlighted the role of semantic labels and the momentum penalty in robust navigation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning an embodiment-agnostic navigation prior exclusively from human walking data is feasible and enables zero-shot transfer to physical humanoids, eliminating the need for robot-specific data or finetuning.
> - A rich, multimodal scene representation, incorporating both a panoramic visual memory with semantic understanding and appearance features, is crucial for robust navigation, especially for challenging elements like transparent obstacles and dynamic environments.
> - Conditional diffusion models can effectively capture multimodal distributions of plausible human-like trajectories, and a hybrid sampling approach can enable their real-time deployment for closed-loop control on robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot navigation, particularly for humanoid platforms, presents challenges related to data acquisition, environmental generalization, and real-time performance. Traditional approaches often rely on explicit map building or extensive robot-specific data collection, which is costly and difficult to scale. Previous learning-based methods, such as cross-embodiment policies like NoMaD, demonstrate transfer across platforms but still necessitate in-domain training data, and large-scale humanoid navigation datasets are currently unavailable. Vision-language models (VLMs) like NaVILA offer an alternative by translating language instructions into locomotion commands, yet they typically require explicit human instructions, produce discrete unimodal commands, lack spatial grounding, and are often too slow for closed-loop control in dynamic environments.

In the domain of robotic manipulation, learning from human demonstrations has shown promise, with systems like UMI and DexCap achieving successful policy transfer to robots without robot-specific data. However, applying this paradigm to navigation has been challenging, with some attempts (e.g., HEAD) finding human data alone insufficient, requiring supplementary robot demonstrations for robust generalization.

This work positions itself as an advancement in this landscape by addressing several identified limitations:
- **Scene Coverage:** Many existing trajectory prediction methods assume ground truth scenes or predict solely from past trajectories. Egocentric perspectives, while practical, often provide limited field of view (∼90°), struggle with transparent/reflective surfaces, and lack semantic understanding.
- **Unimodal Predictions:** Human motion is inherently multi-modal, offering several plausible future trajectories. Most methods, however, produce single-trajectory estimates, which may provide incomplete information for path selection.
- **Interface Representation:** Some end-to-end methods couple terrain traversal and locomotion, hindering generalization, while others predict robot-specific hand and eye trajectories, which can be embodiment-dependent.

EgoNav aims to fill a perceived "missing middle layer" between high-level reasoning (e.g., VLMs for goals) and low-level locomotion, by providing a robust, generalizable navigation prior. This prior is defined as a scene-informed distribution of plausible paths. The proposed system distinguishes itself by learning this navigation prior *entirely from human walking data*, without requiring any robot-specific data or finetuning, and demonstrating zero-shot deployment on a physical humanoid robot. It also contributes a comprehensive scene representation via a 360° visual memory augmented with appearance-level features (DINOv3) and utilizes a conditional diffusion model for multimodal trajectory prediction with a hybrid sampling scheme for real-time inference. This approach seeks to provide a scalable and cost-effective method for humanoid navigation that captures human commonsense behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable a humanoid robot to navigate diverse and previously unseen environments autonomously by learning exclusively from human walking data. This core objective is motivated by several challenges and opportunities in current robot navigation research.

**Motivation:**

1. **Cost and Scalability of Robot Data:** Traditional methods of training robot navigation policies often involve extensive and costly data collection directly on robot platforms. This process is difficult to scale to a wide variety of environments and situations, limiting the generalization capabilities of learned policies.
2. **Richness of Human Data:** Human walking data is abundant, inexpensive to collect, and inherently encodes rich commonsense knowledge about navigation. This includes understanding physically plausible paths, effective obstacle avoidance strategies, and the existence of alternative routes in complex environments. Leveraging this data could significantly reduce the data collection burden on robots.
3. **Limitations of Existing Learning-Based Approaches:**
 - **Insufficient Scene Understanding:** Many contemporary navigation systems suffer from limited scene awareness. Camera fields of view are often narrow, and conventional depth sensors struggle with transparent or reflective surfaces (e.g., glass walls). Furthermore, a lack of semantic understanding prevents robots from differentiating between traversable elements (like open doors) and impassable obstacles (like solid walls).
 - **Unimodal Trajectory Predictions:** Human navigation often involves multiple plausible paths. However, many predictive models generate only a single, deterministic future trajectory. This unimodal output provides incomplete information, limiting the robot's ability to make informed decisions when faced with multiple valid options.
 - **Embodiment-Specific Interfaces:** Policies trained on robot data or designed with specific robot kinematics often result in embodiment-dependent interfaces between navigation and locomotion. This makes direct transfer of learned behaviors from humans to robots challenging without significant re-engineering or additional robot demonstrations.

**Key Objectives (addressed through EgoNav's design principles):**

1. **Human-Native Learning:** Develop a system capable of learning navigation policies solely from human walking demonstrations, thereby eliminating the need for robot-specific data, simulation, or task-specific engineering.
2. **Scene-Aware Navigation:** Ensure the robot derives comprehensive scene understanding from egocentric observations without relying on pre-built maps, prior environment models, or external localization systems. This involves capturing a broad field of view and extracting rich visual and semantic cues.
3. **Distributional Path Prediction:** Model the full distribution of plausible future paths, rather than a single trajectory, to provide the downstream controller with diverse options for informed selection and to better reflect the multi-modal nature of human navigation.
4. **Robot-Ready Deployment:** Design the learned prior to be directly deployable on physical humanoid robots in real-time. This requires addressing practical constraints such as inference speed, system latency compensation, maintaining closed-loop mode consistency, and ensuring robust obstacle avoidance in dynamic, unseen environments.
5. **Embodiment-Agnostic Interface:** Create an interface representation (6-DoF waypoints in world coordinates) that conveys "where to go" without dictating "how to go," thus facilitating zero-shot transfer from human data to diverse robot platforms, specifically humanoids.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to establish a scalable, efficient, and robust paradigm for humanoid navigation that significantly reduces the dependency on robot-specific data and enhances the robot's ability to navigate complex, real-world environments with human-like commonsense.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EgoNav's methodology is structured around learning an embodiment-agnostic navigation prior exclusively from human walking data and deploying it zero-shot on a humanoid robot. The system comprises a data collection pipeline, advanced scene representation, a conditional diffusion model for trajectory prediction, and a receding horizon controller for real-time execution.

**A. Data Collection**
The dataset was collected by a single person wearing body-mounted cameras. The setup included an Intel RealSense T265 for SLAM-based 6-DoF localization and a RealSense D455 stereo camera for aligned RGBD data, all recorded at 20 Hz. The complete dataset consists of 44 sequences, totaling 300 minutes and over 25 km of walking. The environments were diverse, covering indoor spaces (labs, offices, elevators, stairwells) and outdoor settings (crosswalks, off-road paths, gardens), under varied conditions of surface types, lighting (day/night), weather (sun/rain), and traffic density (empty to crowded).
Preprocessing involved semantically labeling color frames into 8 classes (ground, stair, door, wall, obstacle, movable, rough ground, unlabeled) using DINOv2 with a Mask2Former head. Depth frames were processed with a Canny edge filter to mitigate stereo artifacts. All past poses were transformed into an egocentric coordinate frame. Overlapping sub-trajectories were extracted for data augmentation, resulting in over 320,000 training samples.

**B. Scene Representation**
To overcome the limitations of a narrow camera field of view, EgoNav employs two complementary scene representations:

1. **Visual Memory (VM):** This component constructs a 360° egocentric panorama by accumulating a rolling buffer of RGBD frames. The VM has a resolution of 180x360 pixels and 5 channels (R, G, B, depth, semantic), effectively providing 1 pixel per degree. It is encoded by a pretrained spatial Variational Autoencoder (VAE), trained with InfoLoss, L1 loss on RGBD, and cross-entropy on semantic channels. The VAE compresses the VM into an 8x20x8 latent feature map, preserving geometric layout, which is then further reduced to a 64-dimensional embedding by a lightweight adapter using learned attention. The VAE remains frozen during the diffusion model training.
2. **Egocentric Video Features:** To address issues like transparency (glass walls), sensor inaccuracies, and dynamic obstacles, appearance-level cues are extracted. A frozen DINOv3 ViT-S/16 backbone processes the most recent egocentric RGB frame, yielding a 15x27 spatial grid of 384-dimensional patch tokens. A lightweight adapter then aggregates these tokens through learned attention (horizontal then vertical, with a 1D convolution) into a 64-dimensional embedding. This process is designed to run efficiently (approx. 6 ms per frame) for real-time deployment.

**C. Trajectory Diffusion Model**
The core of the prediction module is a non-autoregressive UNet diffusion model. This model is designed to simultaneously predict all 100 future steps (a 5-second horizon at 20 Hz) of a trajectory, ensuring temporal coherence.
The model's architecture incorporates multi-head self-attention (MHSA) layers between downsample and upsample blocks within the UNet, which helps relate conditioning embeddings to different parts of the predicted trajectory. The past trajectory is provided as input, represented by 100 steps of 6D poses (x, y, z position and ortho6d orientation, totaling 9 channels).
The VM encoding, egocentric video features, and the past trajectory are fused by an adapter module. These combined conditions are then injected into each UNet block using classifier-free guidance, where each condition is randomly dropped 10% of the time during training to enhance robustness.

**D. Deployment on Unitree G1**
The system is deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. The data collection cameras are mounted on the robot's chest. Computation is distributed: VM construction, VAE encoding, and the trajectory controller run on the onboard Jetson Orin NX. The more computationally intensive diffusion model and semantic segmentation are handled by a Jetson Thor, carried on the robot's back and connected via a low-latency local network. The complete prediction loop operates at approximately 1.7 Hz, which is deemed sufficient for closed-loop control.

1. **Hybrid Generation:** To achieve real-time inference speed for the diffusion model, a hybrid sampling scheme is introduced. This method begins with a few DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) steps to rapidly approximate the overall trajectory distribution. This is followed by a few DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models) refinement steps to recover finer details. The model is trained with a linear noise schedule. The optimal configuration identified is 5 DDIM steps followed by 5 DDPM steps, which achieves near-full-DDPM quality with significantly fewer total steps (100x faster than full DDPM). With a batch size of 64, this allows for the generation of 110 trajectories per second on the Jetson Thor.
2. **Receding Horizon Controller:** The navigation prior outputs 6-DoF waypoints in a local egocentric frame, serving as an embodiment-agnostic interface that specifies "where to go" rather than "how to move." At each prediction cycle, 64 candidate trajectories are generated over a 5-second horizon.
 - **Filtering:** Trajectories that collide with obstacles in the VM point cloud are filtered out using KD-tree queries (collision detected if >10 points within a 16 cm radius).
 - **Clustering:** The remaining valid trajectories are clustered using K-Means (k=3) based on their positions at a 2-second horizon, identifying distinct plausible modes.
 - **Scoring and Selection:** Each cluster is scored based on its popularity (fraction of trajectories within it) and a momentum penalty (distance from the cluster center to the previously selected intention point). This scoring mechanism favors large, consistent clusters and discourages erratic switching between modes. The medoid of the highest-scoring cluster is selected for execution.
 - **Execution:** The controller dynamically estimates system latency, discards the initial segment of the selected trajectory corresponding to this latency, and executes the plan with smooth blending against the previously executed trajectory until the next prediction batch is available.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

EgoNav's performance was evaluated through both offline metrics and real-world zero-shot deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid.

**A. Offline Evaluation**
The offline evaluation addressed the contribution of each design component and compared EgoNav's multi-modal approach against unimodal baselines.
Metrics used included:
- **Collision-free score (CFS):** Measures consecutive collision-free steps against static obstacles re-projected from the VM (excluding doors/movable objects).
- **Smoothness:** Reciprocal of the mean absolute error in speed and acceleration relative to ground truth.
- **Best of N (minADE-K):** Measures multi-modal coverage by selecting the closest of K predicted trajectories to ground truth.
- **Best of 1 (ADE):** Evaluates single-prediction accuracy for unimodal comparisons.

**Key Offline Results:**

- **Scene Understanding Components:**
 - Complete removal of visual input (VM and DINOv3 features) resulted in the worst collision score (82.5), confirming the critical role of scene context.
 - Removing the semantic channel caused a notable drop in collision avoidance (−5.1), indicating the model's inability to differentiate between traversable and non-traversable elements like doors and walls.
- **Model Architecture:** Adding multi-head self-attention (MHSA) layers between UNet blocks improved collision avoidance by +2.6, suggesting its utility in relating VM context to trajectory generation.
- **DINOv3 Video Features (Offline Impact):** Zeroing out DINOv3 features at inference led to a modest offline drop in collision (91.4 to 90.6). This small change was attributed to the depth-based collision metric not fully capturing the benefits of DINOv3 for transparent surfaces or dynamic obstacles, which are more relevant in real-world scenarios.
- **Data Scaling:** Training on the full 5-hour dataset significantly improved all metrics compared to the pilot subset (e.g., collision improved from 89.2 to 91.4, Best of 15 from 0.47 to 0.39), demonstrating the scalability of the navigation prior with more diverse data.
- **Hybrid Generation Scheme:** A systematic evaluation of DDIM/DDPM step combinations showed that 5 DDIM + 5 DDPM steps achieved near-full-DDPM quality with a 100x reduction in steps, enabling real-time inference. Pure DDIM with the same total steps performed worse in both collision and smoothness.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - EgoNav (pilot data) achieved a collision score of 89.2, outperforming VAE-LSTM (84.5) and CXA-Transformer (80.3).
 - EgoNav also demonstrated better multi-modal coverage (Best of 1: 0.87) compared to unimodal baselines (VAE-LSTM 1.01, CXA 1.25).
 - VAE-LSTM showed high smoothness (9.09) due to its auto-regressive, step-by-step nature but was slower and unimodal.
 - CXA-Transformer exhibited lower collision avoidance, likely due to its lack of a comprehensive panoramic scene representation.
 - Overall, EgoNav demonstrated superior collision avoidance and multi-modal trajectory coverage.

**B. Real-world Deployment**
EgoNav was deployed zero-shot on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot in previously unseen indoor and outdoor environments, without any robot-specific training data or finetuning. No baseline comparisons were made due to their lack of real-time systems or reliance on robot-collected data.

**Key Real-world Results:**

- **Autonomous Operation:** Over 37.5 minutes and 1,137 meters of autonomous navigation, the system achieved 96–99% autonomous time. Corridors showed 99% autonomy, while more challenging glass and dynamic scenes had slightly lower rates (96.2% and 96.0%, respectively).
- **Emergent Behaviors:** The learned navigation prior enabled the humanoid to exhibit several sophisticated, unprogrammed behaviors:
 - **Adaptation to Clutter:** Successfully navigated a kitchen even with cabinets pulled out, demonstrating generalization to rearranged objects.
 - **Multi-modal Decision Making:** At a T-junction, EgoNav predicted a bimodal distribution of paths, and the momentum penalty in the controller guided the robot to commit to a turn.
 - **Semantic Understanding:** The robot waited at a closed door until it opened, then proceeded when the path was clear, indicating an understanding of traversable vs. non-traversable states.
 - **Crowd Navigation:** Successfully navigated through narrow gaps between pedestrians in crowded scenes.
 - **Glass Obstacle Avoidance:** Critically, the system avoided glass walls that are invisible to depth sensors.
- **Role of DINOv3 Features:** The DINOv3 video features were essential for glass environments. Without them, the intervention rate in these environments nearly tripled (from 0.77 to 2.2 interventions per minute), confirming their real-world contribution beyond the modest offline impact.
- **Failure Mode Analysis:**
 - Lack of DINOv3 led to collisions with transparent glass walls.
 - Absence of semantic labels caused the model to predict paths through solid walls.
 - Disabling the momentum penalty in the controller resulted in oscillatory behavior, as the system would frequently switch between equally plausible trajectory modes at decision points.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of EgoNav represents a notable advancement in humanoid robot navigation, primarily through its demonstration of effective learning from solely human walking data and its zero-shot transferability to a physical robot in unseen environments.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Paradigm Shift in Data Acquisition:** The work establishes the feasibility of training complex robot navigation policies using only human demonstrations, circumventing the traditional need for costly, difficult-to-scale, and robot-specific data collection. This approach significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing and deploying autonomous humanoid robots.
2. **Generalizable and Commonsense Navigation Prior:** EgoNav successfully learns an embodiment-agnostic navigation prior that captures human commonsense behaviors. This prior, providing a scene-informed, multimodal distribution of plausible paths, acts as a crucial "missing middle layer" between high-level task planning (e.g., from VLMs) and low-level locomotion controllers. It informs the robot not just where it *can* go, but also what the environment *looks like* along those paths, enabling more intelligent and adaptive navigation.
3. **Enhanced Scene Understanding for Robustness:** The integration of a 360° panoramic visual memory, fusing color, depth, and semantic information, with appearance-level cues from DINOv3 video features, addresses critical limitations of previous systems. This comprehensive scene representation enables the robot to perceive and react to diverse environmental elements, including transparent surfaces (like glass walls) and dynamic obstacles, which are typically challenging for depth-only sensors.
4. **Real-Time Practicality:** The innovative hybrid DDIM-DDPM sampling scheme dramatically accelerates the diffusion model's inference process, making real-time, closed-loop control on a physical humanoid feasible. Combined with a receding-horizon controller that manages latency and maintains mode consistency, EgoNav demonstrates practical deployability in dynamic real-world settings.
5. **Emergent Sophisticated Behaviors:** The learned prior naturally gives rise to complex navigation behaviors—such as waiting for doors to open, skillfully navigating through crowds, and avoiding obstacles invisible to depth sensors—without requiring explicit programming or task-specific engineering. This highlights the effectiveness of learning from human demonstrations in encoding nuanced environmental understanding and decision-making.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robotics Development:** By significantly reducing the data requirements for training, EgoNav can accelerate the development and deployment of humanoid robots in various applications, from logistics and assistance to exploration and complex human-robot interaction scenarios.
- **Broader Accessibility of Humanoid Robotics:** The ability to leverage readily available human walking data democratizes the development of advanced navigation capabilities, potentially making humanoid robotics more accessible to researchers and developers without extensive hardware resources.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The concept of a generalizable navigation prior lays a strong foundation for future research. It can be integrated with goal-conditioned trajectory selection for directed navigation, adapted to monocular depth estimation to reduce sensor complexity, and scaled to an even wider array of environments and tasks. Its semantic awareness can also guide the selection of appropriate low-level locomotion skills for varied terrains.
- **Contribution to the Research Community:** The public release of the dataset and trained models will provide valuable resources, fostering further research and benchmarks in human-to-robot transfer and learning-based navigation.

In conclusion, EgoNav pushes the boundaries of autonomous humanoid navigation by demonstrating a viable and scalable approach to learning complex, commonsense behaviors directly from human experience, thus paving the way for more capable and adaptable robotic systems.
