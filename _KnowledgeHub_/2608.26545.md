# Memory Anchors for Continual Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Memory Anchors are the small subset of replayed past-task data lying where new-task observations collapse onto old-task representations yet require conflicting actions. A three-step extractor finds latent overlap, isolates action disagreement via noise-denoise policy queries, then retrieves the most similar old data. Removing the top 10% raises forgetting 4.5x; enriching buffers with them (AnchorER) cuts high-conflict forgetting 63% and lifts real-robot success 1.7x.

## Problem

- Experience replay buffers in robot continual learning are populated uniformly at random, treating every past transition as equally valuable for retaining prior task performance.
- Prior robotic continual-learning studies compared only coarse buffer statistics like size and sampling frequency, never how inter-task relationships determine which memories matter.
- Continual robot learning is evaluated almost entirely in simulation, lacking real-robot suites of observationally similar tasks that demand conflicting action strategies.

## Method

- **Memory Anchor** extraction, step one: embed new-task data in the current policy's ==observation latent space== and keep points falling inside the past-task data manifold.
- Step two isolates ==action disagreement== by adding Gaussian noise to ground-truth actions and denoising with the current policy, avoiding multimodality false positives when prediction error is large.
- Step three retrieves nearest old-task data to that conflict set as Memory Anchors; **AnchorER** reserves a fixed buffer fraction for them during new-task ==experience replay== training.

## Results

- Excluding the top **10%** of Memory Anchors from LIBERO sampling raised forgetting over **4.5x** at equal buffer size; even **1%** exclusion increased forgetting.
- AnchorER cut forgetting **63%** on the six most conflicting task pairs per suite and reduced LIBERO-Goal average NBT **37%**; real-robot success rose **1.7x**.
- AnchorER beat MIR (**0.14**), GSS (**0.17**), and the no-action-disagreement ablation (**0.13**) at **0.11** NBT, with anchor concentration **0.27** versus RandER's **0.10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Replay value is concentrated, not uniform: data at input-overlap plus output-conflict regions disproportionately anchors past performance, so curate buffers by inter-task relationships.
> - Pretrained VLAs are not immune; with buffers small relative to past data they forget and remain sensitive to anchor availability.
> - Requires access to policy latents and past training data, plus recomputing representations each task, so proprietary models and very long sequences remain open.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of continual learning (CL) addresses the challenge of enabling artificial intelligence models, particularly robot policies, to acquire new capabilities sequentially without degrading performance on previously learned tasks. This phenomenon, known as catastrophic forgetting, is a significant hurdle for deploying intelligent systems in dynamic, real-world environments where continuous adaptation is required. Existing approaches to combat catastrophic forgetting generally fall into three categories: regularization-based methods that penalize changes to parameters important for prior tasks, architectural methods that dedicate separate parameters or subnetworks to each task, and replay buffer methods that store and re-train on a subset of past experiences.

Experience Replay (ER) is a prominent replay buffer technique favored for its simplicity, general applicability across architectures, and effectiveness in mitigating forgetting. While ER has been widely adopted in robotic continual learning benchmarks, prior studies have often relied on uniform sampling strategies for populating these replay buffers.

This research contributes to the continual learning landscape by advancing the understanding and application of Experience Replay in robotics. It posits that not all past experiences are equally important for preventing forgetting; rather, a small, specific subset plays a disproportionately critical role. This work moves beyond uniform sampling by introducing a method to identify and prioritize these critical memories, termed "Memory Anchors." By focusing on the interplay between observation representation overlap and action disagreement between tasks, this research integrates concepts from data retrieval for policy learning into the ER framework. It distinguishes itself by providing a mechanism-driven explanation for *why* certain memories are vital and by demonstrating the practical benefits of targeted memory selection in real-world robotic tasks, which often involve visually similar objects requiring conflicting actions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of continual robot learning by addressing catastrophic forgetting more effectively than conventional Experience Replay (ER) methods. The motivation stems from several observations regarding the limitations of standard ER in robotic contexts:

Firstly, robot policies deployed in dynamic environments must continually learn new tasks without compromising existing behaviors. However, new tasks frequently share observational similarities with previously learned tasks while simultaneously demanding different or even conflicting actions. For instance, a robot learning to open a jar clockwise might forget how to open a similar jar counter-clockwise due to the visual resemblance and the opposing motor commands required. Balancing these commonalities and contradictions is crucial for successful continual learning.

Secondly, while ER is a common strategy to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by replaying past data, the authors observed that standard ER, which often samples randomly, exhibits sensitivity to the specific data points included in its buffer. Experiments revealed that different random samples from the ER buffer could lead to varying levels of forgetting, with some tasks degrading significantly more than others (Figure 2). This indicated that certain past experiences might be more critical than others in anchoring learned behaviors.

Thirdly, the research identifies that catastrophic forgetting is not uniformly distributed across all past tasks; instead, it is often concentrated in specific "conflict regions" or task-task interactions. These are situations where an old task shares significant observation representation overlap with a new task but requires distinct or conflicting actions (Figure 3 and 4). The current policy, when learning the new task, tends to collapse the representations of these conflicting tasks, leading to destructive overwriting of past knowledge. The authors hypothesize that a small set of memories from these conflict regions, termed "Memory Anchors," are vital for regularizing these conflicts and preventing severe performance drops.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives are:
- To formally define and identify "Memory Anchors" as specific experiences within the replay buffer that are critical for preserving past task performance, particularly for tasks with high observational similarity but conflicting actions.
- To develop a principled, three-step method for extracting these Memory Anchors from past task data given a new task and the current policy's latent space.
- To empirically demonstrate the disproportionate impact of Memory Anchors on catastrophic forgetting by both reducing and enriching their presence in the ER buffer.
- To propose and evaluate ANCHORER, an algorithm that systematically enriches ER buffers with identified Memory Anchors to improve task retention.
- To validate the effectiveness of ANCHORER in complex, real-world robotic continual learning scenarios designed to elicit the very task conflicts that Memory Anchors are hypothesized to address.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research addresses continual robot learning where a policy sequentially learns a series of tasks, aiming to acquire new capabilities without forgetting previous ones. The primary metric for forgetting is Negative Backward Transfer (NBT), which quantifies the drop in success rate of a task after subsequent tasks have been learned. The core of the methodology involves identifying and leveraging "Memory Anchors" within the Experience Replay (ER) buffer.

**Continual Learning Framework:**
The robot policy, typically a diffusion policy in simulation or a Vision Language Action (VLA) model, is trained through behavior cloning on task datasets. During training for a new task $T_k$, data is sampled from both the current task dataset $D_k$ and an ER buffer $D_{ER}$ containing past task data.

**Memory Anchor Extraction Process (Algorithm 1, Figure 5):**
The method for identifying Memory Anchors $D_{Anchor}$ from past task data $D_{<n} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1} D_i$ using the new dataset $D_n$ and the current policy $\pi^{n-1}_\theta$ (trained on past tasks) involves three steps:

1. **Find State Representation Overlap:**
 - The goal is to locate new task data points that resemble old task data in the policy's latent observation space $\phi^{n-1}_\theta$.
 - For each observation $s$ in the new task dataset $D_n$, its latent embedding $\phi^{n-1}_\theta(s)$ is compared to all latent embeddings of past task data $D_{<n}$.
 - A feature vector is constructed for each new data point, comprising percentile-level statistics (nearest neighbor, 1%, 5%, 10%) of these latent distances.
 - K-means clustering is applied to these feature vectors to group new data points into two clusters. The cluster with the smaller mean distance to old task data is selected as $D_n^{S_{ID}}$, representing new data with observational overlap. This adaptive clustering method is preferred over fixed thresholds to account for varying overlap levels across tasks.

2. **Isolate Action Disagreements through Generative Policy:**
 - From the observationally overlapping new data $D_n^{S_{ID}}$, the objective is to find a subset $D_n^{OD}$ that requires actions significantly different from what the old policy $\pi^{n-1}_\theta$ would predict for those states.
 - For each $(s, a) \in D_n^{S_{ID}}$, Gaussian noise is added to the ground truth action $a$ to create $\tilde{a}$. The old policy $\pi^{n-1}_\theta$ is then used to denoise this action, predicting $\hat{a}$. This noise-denoise approach is used to prevent false positives from action multimodality.
 - Action disagreement is measured as $d_\pi(s, a) = ||\hat{a} - a||_2^2$.
 - $D_n^{OD}$ is populated with samples where $d_\pi(s, a)$ exceeds a threshold, specifically $\mu + 2\sigma$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of action disagreement calculated on a held-out validation set of old-task data. This identifies samples that would cause significant policy change.

3. **Extract Memory Anchors from Old Data:**
 - The final step identifies actual Memory Anchors ($D_{Anchor}$) from the past task data $D_{<n}$. These are the old data points that are most similar in the policy's latent space to the highly conflicting new data $D_n^{OD}$.
 - Each sample $(s', a') \in D_{<n}$ is scored by its median k-nearest neighbor distance to the samples in $D_n^{OD}$.
 - The $M_A$ old samples with the smallest scores are selected as the Memory Anchors $D_{Anchor}$.

**ANCHORER Algorithm:**
ANCHORER is an Experience Replay strategy that explicitly incorporates Memory Anchors. Instead of purely random sampling, ANCHORER reserves a specified percentage (e.g., 10% or 20%) of the total ER buffer capacity for the extracted Memory Anchors $D_{Anchor}$. The remaining capacity of the buffer is filled with randomly sampled data from the rest of the past task experiences.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulation Experiments:**
 - **Benchmark Suites:** The LIBERO benchmark was used, encompassing four sequential task suites (Long, Goal, Object, Spatial), each with ten tasks.
 - **Policy Architecture:** A diffusion policy with a u-net backbone and ResNet-18 image encoder was trained via behavior cloning.
 - **ER Buffer Sizes:** Experiments varied buffer sizes. For analysis of Memory Anchor reduction, a 1000-memory buffer was used. For enrichment experiments (ANCHORER), a proportionally small buffer (1% of past task datasets) was used to expose more catastrophic forgetting.
 - **Metrics:** Negative Backward Transfer (NBT), Area Under Learning Curve (AUC), and Forward Transfer (FWT) were evaluated across three task permutations. A "NBT High Conflict" metric was also introduced to specifically measure forgetting on the six most conflicting task pairs.
 - **VLA Models:** The π0.5 VLA was tested on LIBERO-Goal with small ER buffers to assess its sensitivity to Memory Anchors.
- **Real Robot Experiments:**
 - **Robot Platform:** ARX robot arm with the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) for data collection and deployment.
 - **Task Suites:** Two challenging suites were designed:
 - **OpenJar:** Three tasks involving identically shaped jars requiring distinct opening strategies (counterclockwise, clockwise, direct lift) based on visual labels/textures.
 - **SweaterFold:** Three bimanual, language-conditioned garment folding behaviors (fold left, fold right, fold up) on the same sweater.
 - **Policy Architecture:** A u-net diffusion policy with a TIMM VIT encoder for RGB inputs (OpenJar) and an additional CLIP-style language embedding (SweaterFold).
 - **Buffer Size:** ER buffer sizes of 5000 transitions for OpenJar and 125 for SweaterFold were used.
- **Baselines and Ablations:** ANCHORER was compared against Maximal Interference Retrieval (MIR), Gradient-Based Sample Selection (GSS), and an ablation of ANCHORER that removed the action disagreement step (Ours-ActionDis). A "Memory Anchor Concentration" metric was used to quantify the proportion of top Memory Anchors in sampled buffers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the role and impact of Memory Anchors in continual robot learning:

**Sensitivity of Experience Replay to Data Selection:**
- A comparison between different random sampling strategies for ER buffers showed that a more diverse sampling approach (RandER, sampling at transition level and resampling D_ER after every task) resulted in 36% lower variance in Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) and 25% lower worst-case forgetting compared to FixedER (episode-level sampling without resampling).
- However, even with RandER, forgetting levels for a specific task could vary by up to 20% across different D_ER samples, indicating that continual learning performance is sensitive to the exact data contained in the buffer (Figure 2).

**Task Relationships and Catastrophic Forgetting:**
- Analysis of task-task interactions revealed that a small proportion of these interactions contribute disproportionately to catastrophic forgetting. For example, in LIBERO-Goal, the forgetting of only 2 out of 10 tasks accounted for over 50% of all negative backward transfer.
- The most significant forgetting occurred when new and old tasks shared observation representation overlap (e.g., visual similarity or semantic closeness) but required conflicting actions (Figure 3, Figure 4, Table 5). This combination was identified as a primary mechanism for catastrophic forgetting.

**Impact of Memory Anchor Availability:**
- **Reducing Access:** When the availability of identified Memory Anchors in the ER buffer was reduced, catastrophic forgetting increased significantly. Excluding only 10% of the best Memory Anchors from the sampling pool led to a greater than 4.5x increase in catastrophic forgetting across the LIBERO benchmark suites. Even excluding 1% of Memory Anchors increased forgetting in some cases (Figure 6 Left, Table 3). This supports the hypothesis that a small subset of experiences plays a critical role in maintaining past performance. Forward Transfer (FWT) remained largely unaffected, indicating that Memory Anchors primarily impact stability rather than plasticity.
- **Adding More Memory Anchors (ANCHORER):** Conversely, enriching the ER buffer with additional Memory Anchors through the ANCHORER algorithm demonstrated benefits. Reserving 10% of a small ER buffer for Memory Anchors reduced forgetting on high-conflict task pairings by 63% (Figure 6 Right). For homogeneous task suites like LIBERO-Goal, ANCHORER improved overall task retention, reducing the average NBT by 37% (Table 4). This effect was more pronounced with smaller buffer sizes, where random sampling is less likely to include sufficient Memory Anchors (Figure 12).

**VLA Sensitivity to Memory Anchors:**
- Experiments on the π0.5 Vision Language Action (VLA) model, using proportionally smaller ER buffers, showed that VLAs are also susceptible to catastrophic forgetting.
- Reducing access to Memory Anchors increased forgetting by 2.9x, and enriching the buffer with Memory Anchors reduced forgetting by 22% (Table 1), consistent with the findings for diffusion policies trained from scratch.

**Real Robot Validation:**
- **OpenJar and SweaterFold Suites:** On two real-robot task suites designed to feature homogeneous tasks with observational similarities and conflicting actions, ANCHORER significantly improved continual learning performance.
- For the OpenJar suite (jarlifting/rotating tasks), ANCHORER improved both backward transfer (preventing forgetting of previously learned jar-opening strategies) and forward transfer (enabling proper learning of new strategies), achieving a better balance than random ER (Figure 7).
- Similarly, for the SweaterFold suite (bimanual garment folding), ANCHORER led to a 1.7x higher final success rate compared to a randomly sampled ER buffer with the same budget (Figure 8).
- Qualitative analysis demonstrated that Memory Anchors extracted for these real-world tasks focused on critical decision points and contact regions where action disagreements were high (Figure 10).

**Baselines and Ablations:**
- ANCHORER outperformed Maximal Interference Retrieval (MIR), Gradient-Based Sample Selection (GSS), and an ablation of ANCHORER without the action disagreement step, in terms of average NBT on LIBERO-Goal (Table 2).
- A positive correlation was observed between the concentration of Memory Anchors within a method's buffer and its task retention ability, further supporting the central hypothesis. The ablation without action disagreement performed worse, indicating the importance of identifying action conflicts beyond mere state similarity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of continual robot learning by introducing the concept of "Memory Anchors" and providing empirical evidence for their critical role in mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The study offers a refined understanding of Experience Replay (ER) mechanisms, moving beyond uniform data sampling to a more targeted approach based on task relationships.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:

- **Improved Continual Learning Robustness**: By demonstrating that a small, identifiable subset of past experiences (Memory Anchors) disproportionately influences the retention of learned behaviors, the research provides a method to make continual learning systems more robust. This is particularly relevant for robotic systems that must operate in complex, real-world environments requiring continuous skill acquisition without sacrificing reliability on existing tasks.
- **Mechanism-Based Understanding of Forgetting**: The identification of Memory Anchors through observation representation overlap and action disagreement offers a principled explanation for *why* certain task interactions lead to catastrophic forgetting. This mechanism-driven approach can inform the development of future continual learning strategies beyond just buffer management.
- **Practical Applicability for Robot Deployment**: The ANCHORER algorithm provides a practical method to enhance ER buffers. Its validation on challenging real-robot tasks, particularly those involving visually similar objects requiring conflicting actions (e.g., OpenJar, SweaterFold), underscores its potential for real-world robot deployment. This could enable robots to learn new manipulation skills more efficiently and reliably, reducing the need for extensive re-training or manual intervention.
- **Efficiency for Small Buffers**: The findings indicate that ANCHORER becomes particularly advantageous when replay buffer sizes are constrained, a common scenario in long-sequence continual learning or resource-limited systems. This efficiency is critical for scalable robot learning.
- **Insights for Foundation Models in Robotics**: The demonstration that even pre-trained Vision Language Action (VLA) models benefit from Memory Anchors when buffer sizes are limited suggests that this concept has broader applicability across different policy architectures, including large foundation models.

**Potential Impact**:
The proposed method has the potential to accelerate the development of more adaptable and generalist robotic agents. By enabling robots to learn new tasks with less forgetting and greater stability, it could lead to broader adoption of intelligent robots in manufacturing, healthcare, and service industries where dynamic skill acquisition is essential. Furthermore, the emphasis on understanding the underlying mechanisms of forgetting could inspire new theoretical directions in continual learning research.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The research acknowledges several limitations. The Memory Anchor extraction process requires access to the current policy's latent space and past training data, which might not always be available for proprietary models. The computational intensity of computing representations for all past datasets at the start of every new task could be a bottleneck for very large datasets or long task sequences. Additionally, the current evaluations were conducted on relatively short task sequences (3-10 tasks), which may not fully represent the challenges of continuous, lifelong learning in complex real-world scenarios.

Future work could explore using alternative latent spaces or open-source data to extract Memory Anchors, reducing dependence on internal policy mechanisms. Research into more computationally efficient methods for anchor extraction would also be beneficial. Expanding evaluations to much longer task sequences and more diverse real-world deployments would further validate the scalability and robustness of the ANCHORER approach. Finally, deeper investigation into the distinction between homogeneous and heterogeneous task continual learning could yield further insights into optimal memory management strategies.
