# Breaking the Vision-Action Shortcut: Latent Interface Training for Generalizable Robotics Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Latent Interface Training (LIT) is a model-agnostic two-stage recipe for robot foundation models. Stage 1 pretrains the action expert without images, conditioned on language, robot state, and each demonstrated chunk's terminal SE(3) end-effector pose. Stage 2 routes all visual conditioning through learnable latent tokens supervised to reconstruct that pose. Across four VLA and WAM architectures it lifts LIBERO-Plus success by 3.87 to 10.70 points while preserving in-distribution success.

## Problem

- Action experts trained on rich visual representations latch onto task-irrelevant cues that correlate with demonstrated actions, and those correlations break under visual distribution shift.
- Representation-enhancement methods add spatial, motion, or depth structure to the inputs but never constrain how the action expert consumes that information, so shortcuts remain available.
- Existing image-free staged pretraining lacks an explicit per-chunk spatial goal, and the later visual stage reintroduces unconstrained visual conditioning, leaving the policy shortcut-prone.

## Method

- Stage 1 spatial-goal-conditioned action pretraining: a from-scratch action expert sees only frozen-backbone language and state tokens plus an 8-dimensional terminal pose encoded by a three-layer GELU MLP, trained with the native ==flow-matching loss==.
- Stage 2 ==latent interface==: 100 learnable tokens run ==self-attention==, then semantic and visual ==cross-attention== at each coupling layer, with attention parameters shared every m layers, forming the expert's only visual pathway.
- ==Pose-reconstruction supervision==: an MLP decoder recovers the same terminal goal from the final latent tokens, giving loss L_act plus 0.3 times L_pose; both pose encoder and decoder are dropped at inference.

## Results

- LIBERO-Plus overall success rises from **68.97** to **79.67** for pi-0.5, **63.62** to **71.92** for MolmoAct2, **51.44** to **60.63** for FAST-WAM, **83.02** to **86.89** for ImageWAM, improving **26 of 28** architecture-perturbation cells.
- Real-robot multi-task evaluation on MolmoAct2 raises aggregate success from **74.7** to **88.0** in distribution, **53.3** to **70.0** under lighting shift, **30.0** to **46.7** under camera shift, **50.0** to **63.3** with distractors.
- Ablations against **71.92** OOD: removing Stage 1 gives **68.23**, dropping pose supervision **68.86**, allowing direct visual access **67.74**; each component alone reaches only about **65.5** versus the **63.62** baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Restricting the visual pathway generalizes better than enriching it: granting direct visual access costs 4.18 OOD points while leaving in-distribution success unchanged.
> - The three components are synergistic rather than additive; each alone buys roughly 2 points, whereas their combination yields 8.3 points of OOD gain.
> - Caution: language-instruction perturbations regressed on two of four architectures, so a pose-shaped bottleneck fixes visual nuisance sensitivity but not semantic robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a central problem in robot foundation models: strong performance on training-like environments does not necessarily translate into robustness when visual conditions change. Recent vision–language–action (VLA) models and world–action models (WAMs) combine large pretrained visual, language, or video representations with action-generation modules. These systems can perform a wide range of manipulation tasks, but their policies may rely on visual features that are correlated with actions in the demonstrations without being causally relevant to the task.

For example, a model may associate a particular camera angle, background texture, lighting condition, or object appearance with a specific action. Such a correlation can produce high in-distribution accuracy but fail when the correlation changes. This issue is related to shortcut learning and causal confusion in imitation learning.

Existing approaches address generalization in several ways. Some enrich visual representations with spatial, temporal, depth, trajectory, or reasoning information. Others pretrain action experts without images so that the model learns reusable action priors before visual conditioning is introduced. A third group filters or regularizes visual features to reduce the use of nuisance information. The paper argues that these approaches do not fully solve the problem because they either add information without controlling how it is used, or introduce visual conditioning later without explicitly constraining the interface between vision and action generation.

Latent Interface Training (LIT) is positioned as a combination of these ideas with an explicit information-routing mechanism. It separates the learning of goal-directed action generation from the learning of visual conditioning. The approach is designed to be compatible with different VLA and WAM architectures rather than requiring a new backbone or action-generation formulation.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to improve robot-policy generalization under visual distribution shifts while preserving the spatial information required for adaptive manipulation.

The authors identify two requirements that can appear to conflict:

1. **The policy should become insensitive to task-irrelevant visual variation.** Changes in viewpoint, lighting, sensor noise, background, or distractor objects should not cause large changes in the action trajectory when the underlying task remains unchanged.
2. **The policy must remain sensitive to task-relevant spatial information.** It should respond appropriately when object layouts, robot initial states, or instructed goals change, because these changes may require different actions.

The paper’s motivation is that simply providing richer visual features does not ensure that the action expert will use them appropriately. A model can still exploit shortcuts within a rich representation. Likewise, image-free action pretraining may establish an action prior, but once visual inputs are added, the action expert may again rely on scene-specific correlations.

LIT therefore uses the terminal end-effector pose of each demonstrated action chunk as a shared spatial target. During the first stage, this pose explicitly conditions action generation. During the second stage, the visual interface is trained to reconstruct the same pose. The intended effect is to make the visual pathway preserve spatial information relevant to the action goal while restricting direct access to visual representations that may contain nuisance correlations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

At time \(t\), the policy receives an image observation, a language instruction, and robot state information. It predicts an action chunk of horizon \(H\), rather than a single action. For every demonstrated chunk, the terminal state is used as a goal:

- Three values describe end-effector position.
- Three values describe orientation using axis-angle coordinates.
- Two values describe gripper joint positions.

The resulting goal vector has eight dimensions. It is used during training only and is not required during inference.

#### 3.2. Stage 1: Spatial-Goal-Conditioned Action Pretraining

In the first stage, the action expert is trained without image inputs. A frozen pretrained backbone processes the language instruction and robot state. A trainable multilayer perceptron encodes the terminal goal pose into goal tokens. These goal tokens are concatenated with the language–state representations at each coupling layer and supplied to the action expert through the architecture’s native conditioning mechanism.

The action expert is trained to generate the demonstrated action chunk using the model’s native objective. In the reported experiments, this is flow matching. A clean action chunk is interpolated with Gaussian noise, and the action expert learns to predict the corresponding velocity field.

Only the action expert and the goal-pose encoder are updated during this stage. The visual-language or world-model backbone remains frozen. This stage is intended to teach the action expert a goal-directed action prior that does not depend on visual cues.

#### 3.3. Stage 2: Vision–Action Interface Learning

The second stage initializes the action expert with the weights learned in Stage 1 and introduces visual conditioning through a latent interface.

The interface consists of 100 learnable latent tokens. At each coupling layer, the tokens undergo:

1. Self-attention among the latent tokens.
2. Cross-attention to language and robot-state representations.
3. Cross-attention to visual representations.

The latent tokens serve as queries, while the backbone representations provide keys and values. The resulting tokens condition the corresponding action-expert layers. Crucially, the action expert is not given direct access to the backbone’s visual representations; all visual information must pass through the latent interface.

The final latent representation is passed through an MLP decoder that reconstructs the terminal goal pose used in Stage 1. The Stage 2 loss combines the action-generation loss and the pose-reconstruction loss:

\[
L_{\text{stage2}} = L_{\text{action}} + \lambda_{\text{pose}}L_{\text{pose}},
\]

with \(\lambda_{\text{pose}}=0.3\). The pose objective is intended to encourage the latent interface to preserve goal-relevant spatial information.

Unlike Stage 1, Stage 2 jointly trains the backbone, modality encoders, action expert, latent tokens, interface modules, and pose decoder. At inference, the pose encoder and pose decoder are removed. The policy uses only images, language, and robot state, with the latent interface remaining active.

#### 3.4. Architectures and Evaluation

The method is integrated into four architectures:

- **π⁰.⁵**, a VLA using a mixture-of-transformers design and shared self-attention between visual-language and action branches.
- **MolmoAct2**, a VLA whose action expert uses layer-wise cross-attention to backbone key–value representations.
- **FAST-WAM**, a world–action model using future-video prediction during training but action-only inference.
- **ImageWAM**, which retains image-editing-based conditioning at inference.

The experiments use LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus. LIBERO contains 40 tasks across four suites, with 50 rollouts per task for in-distribution evaluation. LIBERO-Plus evaluates 10,030 perturbed instances across seven shift types: camera viewpoint, sensor noise, lighting, background texture, robot initial state, object layout, and language instruction. Models are trained only on the original demonstrations and evaluated without adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. In-Distribution Performance

LIT preserved or improved average LIBERO success for all four architectures:

- **π⁰.⁵:** 87.75% to 91.80%.
- **MolmoAct2:** 93.50% to 94.10%.
- **FAST-WAM:** 97.60% to 98.10%.
- **ImageWAM:** 98.10% to 98.40%.

Some individual task-suite scores decreased slightly, particularly for MolmoAct2 on the Object suite and ImageWAM on the Object suite, but no model showed a decrease in average performance.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Generalization

LIT improved overall LIBERO-Plus success for every architecture:

- **π⁰.⁵:** 68.97% to 79.67%, a gain of 10.70 percentage points.
- **MolmoAct2:** 63.62% to 71.92%, a gain of 8.30 points.
- **FAST-WAM:** 51.44% to 60.63%, a gain of 9.19 points.
- **ImageWAM:** 83.02% to 86.89%, a gain of 3.87 points.

The improvements occurred in 26 of 28 architecture–perturbation comparisons. The largest gains included:

- π⁰.⁵ under changed camera viewpoints: **+22.01 points**.
- π⁰.⁵ under altered language instructions: **+19.35 points**.
- MolmoAct2 under sensor noise: **+21.74 points**.
- MolmoAct2 under object-layout changes: **+15.71 points**.
- FAST-WAM under camera changes: **+27.43 points**.
- ImageWAM under changed robot initial states: **+12.38 points**.

There were small decreases for MolmoAct2 under language perturbations and ImageWAM under language perturbations, at −2.11 and −0.92 points respectively. The results suggest that LIT can improve robustness across different visual-action coupling mechanisms and across both VLA and WAM families.

#### 4.3. Real-World Evaluation

The authors evaluated a MolmoAct2-based policy on three tasks: detaching LEGO blocks and placing them in a box, wiping table debris with a dustpan, and transferring an egg from a pan to a bowl. The policy was trained using 300 demonstrations, with 100 per task.

Aggregated across tasks, LIT improved success from:

- **74.7% to 88.0%** under in-distribution conditions.
- **53.3% to 70.0%** under lighting changes.
- **30.0% to 46.7%** under camera changes.
- **50.0% to 63.3%** with added distractor objects.

The largest task-level changes occurred for egg transfer. Under lighting changes, success increased from 30% to 90%; under distractors, it increased from 20% to 90%. For wiping trash, camera-shift performance increased from 10% to 80%.

#### 4.4. Behavioral and Ablation Evidence

Attention visualizations indicated that baseline action-to-image attention shifted more under perturbations, whereas LIT’s effective attention remained more concentrated on robot–object regions. Counterfactual tests showed that LIT trajectories changed less when images were blurred or distractors were added, while remaining responsive when the instructed goal changed.

Ablation results on MolmoAct2 showed that each major component contributed to performance:

- Removing Stage 1 reduced OOD success from 71.92% to 68.23%.
- Removing pose supervision reduced it to 68.86%.
- Allowing direct visual access reduced it to 67.74%.
- Removing both Stage 1 and pose supervision produced 65.70%.
- A staged-training alternative inspired by LA4VLA reached 65.46%.
- Adding pose supervision to the baseline reached 65.45%.

These comparisons indicate that the gains depend on the combination of spatial action pretraining, pose supervision, and restricted visual access.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The paper presents an architectural-training intervention for a recognized weakness of robot foundation models. Its main significance lies in treating the visual–action interface itself as an object of supervision. Rather than only improving visual representations or adding auxiliary reasoning signals, LIT constrains the route by which visual information reaches the action expert.

If supported at larger scales, the approach could reduce the need for exhaustive visual-domain randomization and improve deployment across camera configurations, illumination conditions, sensor characteristics, and cluttered environments. Because it preserves the underlying backbone and action expert designs, it may be applicable to multiple model families and action-generation methods.

The evidence is consistent with reduced shortcut reliance, but the causal interpretation remains partly indirect. Attention maps and counterfactual trajectories provide behavioral support, while the benchmarks remain limited in task and scale. The real-world study uses three tasks and a relatively small demonstration set. The authors also note that evaluation at larger real-world scales and on more diverse tasks remains open. Further work should examine computational overhead, sensitivity to the choice and quality of terminal poses, performance with long-horizon tasks, and whether the method remains effective when demonstrations contain substantial ambiguity or inaccurate state measurements.
