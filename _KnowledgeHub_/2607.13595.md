# Active Trust Management for Successful Human-Robot Teaming: Moving from a Trust Repair to a Trust Satisficing Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> Webb and Hunt propose an Active Trust Management Framework for human-robot teams, transitioning from reactive trust repair to a proactive "trust satisficing" approach in dynamic, high-risk environments. The framework integrates multi-modal online trust measurement with variable autonomy mechanisms, aiming to continuously maintain trust at a "good enough" level while ensuring human value alignment.

## Problem

- Existing human-robot teaming (HRT) trust research often focuses reactively on "trust repair" after a trust breach, rather than continuous, proactive management.
- Ensuring appropriate trust levels in HRTs is critical for mission success in dynamic, high-risk environments, where both under-trust and over-trust can lead to failures.
- Multi-robot and ad hoc teams in emergency scenarios require rapid trust formation and mechanisms to align robot actions with human values and moral judgments.

## Method

- Develop an **Active Trust Management Framework** structured into pre-mission, on-mission, and post-mission phases for continuous ==trust monitoring== and intervention.
- Adopt a ==trust satisficing== perspective to maintain trust at a "good enough" level, which involves building, dampening (to prevent over-reliance), or adjusting trust requirements.
- Utilize ==variable autonomy== as a key mechanism to dynamically adjust robot control levels based on assessed trust, task demands, and the need for human ethical oversight.

## Results

- Articulated a **three-phase** Active Trust Management Framework (pre-mission, on-mission, post-mission) centered on the "trust satisficing" concept for human-robot teams.
- Integrated diverse **multi-modal proxy metrics** for online trust assessment, highlighting the potential of behavioral cues (e.g., inter-agent movement synchronization) as unobtrusive indicators.
- Proposed variable autonomy as a central adaptive strategy within the framework to dynamically adjust robot autonomy based on trust levels and the need for human intervention in ethically sensitive situations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Trust in human-robot teams is a dynamic, context-dependent, and multi-faceted concept that necessitates continuous, proactive management rather than just reactive repair.
> - Online assessment of trust can be achieved through a combination of unobtrusive multi-modal proxy metrics, including behavioral cues like inter-agent movement synchronization, psychophysical measures, and social network analysis.
> - Variable autonomy serves as a crucial control mechanism, allowing humans to reassert control and responsibility for value alignment and moral judgments, thereby managing trust requirements during evolving missions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of mobile robots, endowed with increasing levels of artificial intelligence (AI), into human teams is a developing area of research with significant implications for hazardous environments, such as search and rescue operations. This field, broadly known as Human-Agent Teaming (HAT) or specifically Human-Robot Teaming (HRT) for embodied agents, recognizes that the establishment and maintenance of trust between human and robot team members is critical for achieving high team performance.

Existing research on trust in HRT often focuses on reactive strategies, such as **trust repair**—addressing situations where trust has been damaged due to robot errors or unexpected behaviors (e.g., Baker et al., 2018; Kox et al., 2021). While acknowledging the importance of trust repair, this paper argues for a more proactive and continuous approach. The current work positions itself within a landscape that recognizes trust as a multi-dimensional concept, encompassing performance and moral aspects (Malle & Ullman, 2021), and dynamic, varying with situational context (Huang et al., 2021; Wildman et al., 2025). The Lewis and Marsh (2022) model, which defines trust in terms of capability, predictability, and integrity, is adopted here.

Furthermore, a significant portion of HRT trust research has traditionally focused on dyadic (one human, one robot) interactions. This paper addresses the increasing complexity of multi-robot teams and the particular challenges of **ad hoc teams** formed in emergency scenarios where team members, human and robot, may not have prior experience working together, thus necessitating "swift trust" (Meyerson et al., 1996; Milivojevic et al., 2024).

The paper distinguishes itself by moving from a reactive "trust repair" paradigm to a more comprehensive **"trust satisficing" perspective**. This perspective acknowledges that trust is inherently fluctuating and context-dependent, requiring active management not just when trust is broken, but continuously to ensure it remains at a "good enough" level for the evolving mission demands. It also integrates the crucial aspect of human responsibility in moral judgments, proposing variable autonomy as a mechanism to manage trust requirements and align robot actions with human values. This approach offers a framework for understanding and managing trust that is intended to be more robust and adaptable for complex, high-risk HRT deployments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this work stems from the anticipated increased deployment of intelligent mobile robots in human teams, particularly in challenging and high-risk environments like emergency response. While these autonomous capabilities promise enhanced effectiveness, they also introduce potential strains on inter-agent trust. Robots, due to their autonomy, may make mistakes, or their perceived priorities might conflict with the team's best interest, leading to fluctuations in human trust. If trust falls below sufficient levels, the success of cooperative tasks and ultimately the mission can be jeopardized. The authors observe that much existing research on HRT trust predominantly focuses on reactive "trust repair" after a breach has occurred.

The primary objectives of this paper are:

- To present a **conceptual framework for active trust management** in human-robot teams (HRTs), particularly those operating in dynamic, high-risk environments. This framework aims to provide a structured approach for continuous trust monitoring and intervention throughout a mission's lifecycle.
- To advocate for a **"trust satisficing" perspective**, which broadens the scope beyond mere trust repair. This perspective acknowledges the multi-faceted, dynamic, and context-dependent nature of trust and its requirements, suggesting that trust needs to be maintained at a "good enough" level—which might involve increasing trust, prudently dampening it (to prevent over-reliance), or reducing trust requirements through operational adjustments.
- To detail the specific components and mechanisms within this framework, including:
 - **Online measurement of proxy metrics for trust**: Identifying and categorizing behavioral, psychophysical, and social network analysis-based indicators that can be monitored continuously during a mission.
 - **Closed-loop adaptation of robot behavior**: Describing how trust estimates can inform adaptive changes in robot actions and communication.
 - The strategic application of **variable autonomy**: Emphasizing its role in allowing human team members to intervene and assume greater control, especially in situations demanding value judgments or when robot capabilities are a poor match for evolving task demands, thereby managing trust requirements.
- To integrate the concept of **"swift trust"** for ad hoc HRTs, recognizing that in emergency scenarios, pre-existing trust relationships may be limited, necessitating rapid trust formation.
- To highlight the importance of **human responsibility for value alignment**, asserting that humans must retain oversight to ensure robot goals and tasks align with individual, team, and broader societal values in morally sensitive contexts.
- To underscore the foundational role of a well-defined **Concept of Operations (CONOPS) for variable autonomy** in establishing shared mental models, managing expectations, and clarifying roles and responsibilities pre-mission.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need for a more comprehensive, proactive, and adaptable approach to trust in HRTs to ensure their successful and ethical deployment in demanding real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper primarily employs a **conceptual framework development approach**, synthesizing existing literature, theoretical models, and insights from ongoing experimental research (including that of the authors) to propose a novel model for active trust management in Human-Robot Teams. It does not present new empirical data but rather constructs a theoretical and practical blueprint.

The methodology can be broken down into several key elements:

- **Phased Framework Structure:** The core of the approach is a three-phase framework:
 - **Pre-mission:** Focuses on establishing foundational trust and preparing the HRT. This involves trust surveys for baseline assessment (e.g., using Schaefer's 2016 scale), trust priming through transparency and training (e.g., Pataranutaporn et al., 2023), and developing a comprehensive Concept of Operations (CONOPS) for variable autonomy. The CONOPS is designed to define roles, responsibilities, autonomy levels, and triggers for change in advance, aiding in shared mental model formation.
 - **On-mission:** Represents the continuous loop of trust monitoring, modeling, and satisficing. This is the most detailed phase, focusing on real-time adaptive management.
 - **Post-mission:** Emphasizes learning and system improvement through trust model recalibration and counterfactual analysis (CFA).

- **Trust Satisficing Perspective:** The central conceptual shift is from "trust repair" to "trust satisficing." This involves identifying when trust is "good enough" for the current task, and then either building/repairing it if insufficient, dampening it if excessively high (to prevent over-reliance), or adjusting trust requirements by altering autonomy levels.

- **Multi-Modal Trust Measurement:** The framework proposes incorporating diverse proxy metrics for online trust assessment:
 - **Behavioral Metrics:** Observable indicators such as team performance, decision-making speed, reliance on robots, and frequency of human interventions (Kohn et al., 2021). Notably, the authors reference their own experimental work (Webb et al., 2024; Milivojevic et al., 2024) exploring non-verbal cues specific to embodied agents, such as inter-agent movement synchronization, proximity, and gaze patterns, as potential early-warning signals for trust changes.
 - **Psychophysical Metrics:** Physiological measures like Electroencephalograms (EEG), Electrocardiograms (ECG), and Electrodermal Activity (EDA), as well as eye-tracking data (Ajenaghughrure et al., 2020; Hulle et al., 2024). The paper acknowledges their potential invasiveness makes them less suitable for ad hoc, high-risk field deployments.
 - **Social Network Analysis (SNA):** To map and estimate trust relationships across a broader network including human team members, commanders, and other stakeholders, allowing for system-level trust assessment and "recommended trust" calculations (Gong et al., 2020).

- **Dual Trust Modeling with Bayesian Belief Networks (BBNs):** A key proposed mechanism is for each autonomous agent (robot) to maintain two distinct BBN models: a 'trust-out' model (its trust in others) and a 'trust-in' model (its inference of others' trust in itself). These BBNs would infer latent trust variables from the various measured proxy metrics, allowing for probabilistic updates and reasoning under uncertainty, similar to distributed situation awareness modeling (Yusuf & Baber, 2022).

- **Variable Autonomy as a Trust Satisficing Action:** The framework posits variable autonomy as a crucial control input. When trust levels are insufficient, or mission requirements become morally sensitive, the system or human can proactively adjust the robot's level of autonomy (e.g., from full autonomy to supervised autonomy or teleoperation). This mechanism is intended to align robot actions with human values and allow humans to assume responsibility for complex ethical dilemmas (Verhagen et al., 2024).

- **Emphasis on Situation Awareness and Value Alignment:** The framework stresses the continuous monitoring of the mission environment to maintain situation awareness (Endsley, 2023; Ezenyilimba et al., 2023), informing dynamic trust requirements. It also highlights the ongoing human responsibility to ensure that robot goals and tasks align with individual, team, and social values, especially when reprioritization is needed.

- **Integration of "Swift Trust":** The authors integrate findings and considerations related to "swift trust" in ad hoc teams (Milivojevic et al., 2024; Haring et al., 2021), acknowledging that traditional long-term trust-building may not be feasible in emergency scenarios.

By combining these theoretical concepts with practical considerations for measurement and intervention, the paper builds a comprehensive and actionable framework for managing trust in complex human-robot collaborations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a conceptual paper, this work does not present new experimental data but rather synthesizes existing knowledge and proposes a novel, detailed framework for active trust management in human-robot teams. The main "findings" are therefore the articulated components and relationships within this proposed framework, supported by references to relevant literature and the authors' related empirical investigations.

The primary outcome is the proposed **Active Trust Management Framework**, structured across pre-mission, on-mission, and post-mission phases, and underpinned by a "trust satisficing" perspective.

**Key results and components of the framework include:**

- **Shift to Trust Satisficing:** The paper successfully argues for and outlines a shift from a reactive "trust repair" approach to a proactive "trust satisficing" approach. This involves continuously monitoring trust levels to ensure they are "good enough" for the task at hand, which may necessitate boosting trust, dampening over-trust, or reducing trust requirements. This re-framing acknowledges the fluctuating, multi-faceted, and context-dependent nature of trust in HRTs.

- **Pre-Mission Preparations for Trust:** The framework identifies critical pre-deployment activities:
 - **Trust Surveys:** Establishing baseline self-reported trust levels in human team members, particularly for routine missions.
 - **Trust Priming:** Deliberate actions to shape users' mental models of robots through transparency, training on capabilities and limitations, and insights into decision-making processes to promote appropriate initial trust calibration and mitigate over- or under-trust.
 - **Concept of Operations (CONOPS) for Variable Autonomy:** The explicit development of a CONOPS is presented as a crucial step for pre-mission trust building. It defines clear responsibilities, acceptable autonomy levels, and specific triggers for autonomy changes, fostering shared mental models, managing expectations, and providing a basis for accountability.

- **On-Mission Dynamic Trust Management Loop:** The core of the framework is an iterative loop during the mission:
 - **Multi-Modal Trust Measurement:** The framework details a range of online, unobtrusive trust proxy metrics.
 - **Behavioral Metrics:** Includes team performance, robot reliance, and, significantly, nonverbal communication cues specific to embodied agents. The authors reference their own work (Webb et al., 2024) suggesting that **inter-agent movement synchronization** between a human and robot can serve as an online indicator of trust development or impairment, particularly after communication disruptions. Proximity and kinesics are also highlighted as important.
 - **Psychophysical Metrics:** Physiological measures like EEG, ECG, EDA, and eye-tracking are acknowledged for their ability to provide physiological perspectives on trust, though their practical applicability in ad hoc field missions is noted as limited due to invasiveness.
 - **Social Network Analysis (SNA):** Proposed for estimating system-level trust networks among all stakeholders (humans, robots, commanders), allowing for calculation of "recommended trust" even between indirectly interacting agents.
 - **Dual Trust Modeling:** The proposal for each robot to maintain both a 'trust-out' (its trust in others) and 'trust-in' (others' trust in it) model, potentially implemented via **Bayesian Belief Networks (BBNs)**, allows for dynamic inference of latent trust states from diverse, potentially noisy, proxy measures.
 - **Dynamic Trust Requirements and Situation Awareness:** The framework emphasizes that trust requirements evolve with changing task demands and environmental contexts, necessitating continuous situation awareness.
 - **Trust Satisficing Actions:** A range of strategies are outlined, including:
 - **Verbal and Non-verbal Trust Repair/Build actions:** Apologies, explanations, promises, as well as non-verbal behaviors like proximity, synchronized movement, and successful task completion.
 - **Trust Dampening:** Proactive warnings about robot limitations to prevent over-reliance.
 - **Variable Autonomy:** Identified as a central mechanism. It allows for the dynamic adjustment of robot autonomy levels (human intervention, supervised autonomy, full autonomy) based on detected trust levels, task demands, and critically, the need for human judgment in morally sensitive situations, ensuring **value-driven goal and task reprioritization**.

- **Post-Mission Learning and Improvement:** The framework includes post-mission activities for continuous improvement:
 - **Trust Model Recalibration:** Refining the BBN models and weighting of different trust metrics based on mission data.
 - **Counterfactual Analysis (CFA):** Simulating alternative actions to evaluate the effectiveness of trust satisficing strategies employed.
 - **CONOPS Evaluation:** Using the CONOPS as a baseline to diagnose failures and refine future operational procedures.

- **Relevance of "Swift Trust":** The paper explicitly addresses the need for "swift trust" in ad hoc HRTs encountered in emergency scenarios, integrating this concept into the framework's considerations, particularly for the pre-mission and on-mission phases where long-term trust building is not feasible. Insights from their related experimental work (Milivojevic et al., 2024) on this topic are referenced.

In summary, the paper's main "findings" are the comprehensive, theoretically grounded, and practically oriented framework for active trust management, along with the detailed exposition of its constituent parts, informed by relevant research and early empirical insights from the authors' related studies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The active trust management framework proposed in this paper has several significant implications and potential impacts across theoretical, practical, and ethical dimensions of human-robot teaming.

**Theoretical Significance:**

- **Conceptual Shift:** The introduction and detailed articulation of the **"trust satisficing" perspective** represents a notable conceptual contribution. By moving beyond a singular focus on reactive "trust repair" to a proactive approach that encompasses building, maintaining, dampening, and dynamically adjusting trust requirements, the paper offers a more nuanced and ecologically valid model for understanding trust in complex, dynamic HRT environments. This reframing could influence future theoretical developments in human-robot interaction (HRI) and human factors.
- **Integrated Framework:** The paper provides a comprehensive, multi-phased framework that systematically integrates diverse aspects of trust management—from pre-mission preparation to online measurement, modeling, adaptive control, and post-mission analysis. This holistic approach can serve as a robust blueprint for future research, guiding investigations into the interdependencies of various trust-related factors.
- **Emphasis on Embodied Interaction:** By highlighting non-verbal communication, such as movement synchronization and proxemics, as online trust indicators, the work underscores the unique aspects of HRT with embodied agents. This opens new theoretical avenues for understanding how implicit, physical interactions contribute to trust dynamics, distinct from human-AI teaming with non-embodied agents.
- **Dual Trust Modeling:** The proposal for 'trust-out' and 'trust-in' models using Bayesian Belief Networks offers a formal and computationally tractable approach to represent and infer distributed dynamic trust states within a team, moving towards mutually intelligible trust models for both humans and robots.

**Practical Impact:**

- **Enhanced HRT Performance and Safety:** By enabling continuous, real-time monitoring and adaptive management of trust, the framework aims to significantly improve the effectiveness, resilience, and safety of human-robot teams, particularly in high-stakes applications like search and rescue, disaster response, and defense. Proactive trust management can mitigate risks associated with both under-trust (leading to underutilization of robots) and over-trust (leading to over-reliance and potential accidents).
- **Improved Robot and AI System Design:** The framework's detailed requirements for measurable trust proxies, transparency, and variable autonomy can directly inform the design and development of future autonomous systems. Robots designed with "trust-awareness" capabilities, that can sense, model, and adapt to human trust, would be more robust and user-centric.
- **Guidance for Training and Operations:** The pre-mission phase elements, including trust surveys, priming, and the development of a Concept of Operations (CONOPS) for variable autonomy, provide actionable strategies for organizations deploying HRTs. These steps can facilitate appropriate trust calibration in human operators, prepare them for dynamic interactions, and ensure clear allocation of responsibilities and decision-making authority.
- **Addressing "Swift Trust" in Ad Hoc Teams:** The framework's consideration of "swift trust" is particularly relevant for emergency services where ad hoc teams are common. It offers strategies to rapidly establish provisional trust, which is crucial for immediate and effective cooperation in critical situations.

**Ethical and Societal Impact:**

- **Responsible AI and Meaningful Human Control:** The framework explicitly integrates the need for humans to retain responsibility for value judgments and to align robot behavior with individual, team, and societal values. The mechanism of variable autonomy, triggered by trust levels and moral sensitivity, supports the concept of "meaningful human control" over autonomous systems, addressing growing ethical concerns regarding AI decision-making in morally fraught situations.
- **Facilitating Adoption:** By providing robust mechanisms for managing trust, the framework can contribute to building greater confidence among human operators and the public in autonomous technologies. This could accelerate the responsible adoption of robots in critical sectors by demonstrating a clear commitment to safety, reliability, and human oversight.
- **Foundation for Policy and Regulation:** The structured approach to defining responsibilities within a CONOPS, especially concerning variable autonomy, could inform the development of policies and regulations for HRT deployment, clarifying legal and ethical accountability for robot actions.

In conclusion, this paper's active trust management framework represents a forward-looking approach that addresses a fundamental challenge in HRT. Its potential impact lies in fostering more effective, safer, and ethically aligned human-robot collaborations, thereby contributing to the successful integration of advanced robotics for widespread societal benefit.
