# Erase to Improve: Erasable Reinforcement Learning for Search-Augmented LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Erasable Reinforcement Learning (ERL) enhances search-augmented Large Language Models (LLMs) by enabling self-correction, improving their reliability in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks. The method, termed ESearch, achieved average gains of +8.48% Exact Match and +11.56% F1 score for the Qwen2.5-3B model across four multi-hop QA benchmarks, setting new state-of-the-art results.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) are prone to hallucination and factual errors, particularly in knowledge-intensive and multi-step reasoning, due to static pretraining corpora.
- Existing Reinforcement Learning (RL) based search-augmented LLMs are structurally fragile; minor errors in decomposition, retrieval, or reasoning can compromise an entire multi-hop trajectory.
- Current RL-based agents suffer from three critical failure modes: decomposition errors (incorrect sub-queries), retrieval missing (failure to obtain relevant documents), and reasoning errors (logical mistakes in integrating information).

## Method

- Introduces Erasable Reinforcement Learning (ERL), a framework that integrates ==error detection==, erasure, and regeneration within a structured, multi-round reasoning process for search-augmented LLMs.
- Models reasoning as a sequence of rounds, where the agent performs atomic actions like observation, sub-answer generation, and search, with the ability to revert flawed steps.
- Employs a dense, stepwise reward system (search reward for novelty/relevance, sub-answer reward for intermediate accuracy) in addition to a sparse terminal reward, providing richer supervision during training.
- Implements an erasure operator that discards flawed segments of a reasoning trajectory (e.g., sub-answer, subsequent search, or initial plan) based on reward thresholds, allowing the agent to regenerate from a corrected state.

## Results

- ESearch consistently surpassed baselines and previous state-of-the-art methods across four multi-hop QA datasets (HotpotQA, MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA, Bamboogle), achieving average gains of +**8.48%** EM and +**11.56%** F1 for Qwen2.5-**3B** and +**5.38%** EM and +**7.22%** F1 for Qwen2.5-**7B**.
- In online evaluation with Google Search API, ESearch continued to demonstrate superior performance and adaptability, with the Qwen2.5-**3B** model showing gains of +**10.90%** EM and +**13.18%** F1.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the full ERL framework is optimal, with erasure mechanisms correcting **2.01%** of decomposition errors, **6.53%** of retrieval failures, and **9.6%** of reasoning errors, demonstrating varied effectiveness across error types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Enabling fine-grained self-correction, akin to human error recovery, significantly boosts the robustness and reliability of search-augmented LLMs in complex multi-hop reasoning.
> - The combination of dense stepwise rewards and targeted erasure mechanisms effectively addresses specific failure modes, transforming error propagation into error recovery.
> - Different erasure mechanisms contribute distinctly: sub-answer erasure is most impactful for reasoning errors, while search erasure benefits retrieval-intensive tasks, and plan erasure aids in initial strategic correctness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advancements across various Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, including question answering and reasoning. However, a limitation of these models, particularly in knowledge-intensive domains and multi-step reasoning, is their susceptibility to hallucination and factual errors, often due to constraints of their static pretraining corpora. Even advanced models designed for rigorous reasoning encounter difficulties with complex multi-hop problems that require precise decomposition, reliable information retrieval, and consistent logical progression.

To address these limitations, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a prominent paradigm. RAG enhances LLMs by integrating external knowledge sources, enabling them to access and synthesize information beyond their internal parameters. This paradigm has evolved to include sophisticated research agents that iteratively combine search and reasoning within autonomous loops.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a significant mechanism for optimizing these search-augmented agents. Existing RL-based approaches utilize reward signals to improve various sub-processes, such as sub-query generation, evidence retrieval, and the construction of reasoning chains. While these methods have shown gains on benchmarks like HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and 2WikiMultiHopQA, they generally exhibit brittleness. Minor errors in decomposition, retrieval, or reasoning can compromise an entire multi-hop trajectory, leading to catastrophic failure in the final answer. This contrasts with human reasoning, where self-correction mechanisms allow for the identification and rectification of flaws in intermediate steps.

The current work positions itself within this landscape by identifying the structural fragility of existing RL-based search agents, which often treat the entire search and reasoning trajectory as a monolithic Markov Decision Process (MDP) optimized solely via sparse terminal rewards. This monolithic design exacerbates the impact of single-step errors, especially as reasoning chains extend. The paper aims to mitigate this brittleness by introducing a fine-grained self-correction mechanism, drawing inspiration from human error recovery processes. It addresses three critical failure modes observed in existing RL-based search agents: decomposition errors, retrieval missing, and reasoning errors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the reliability of search-augmented Large Language Models (LLMs) in complex multi-hop reasoning tasks by introducing a robust self-correction mechanism. The work is motivated by the observation that despite significant advancements, current search-augmented LLMs exhibit "catastrophic fragility" in their reasoning processes. A single error at any intermediate stage can propagate, leading to an incorrect final answer and undermining the entire reasoning trajectory.

The motivation is specifically detailed through three critical failure modes identified in existing RL-based search agents:

1. **Decomposition Errors:** These occur when the model incorrectly breaks down a complex question into sub-queries. A flawed sub-query can lead to the retrieval of irrelevant or insufficient evidence, preventing subsequent reasoning steps from accessing necessary information and thus derailing the entire process.
2. **Retrieval Missing:** Even with appropriate sub-queries, retrieval can fail to provide relevant documents. This can be due to noise in the search results, ambiguity in the query, or incomplete coverage of the external knowledge source. Such failures mean that crucial evidence is not obtained, causing subsequent reasoning to be based on incomplete or incorrect information.
3. **Reasoning Errors:** LLMs may make logical mistakes when integrating retrieved information to form intermediate conclusions. These errors can accumulate across multiple steps, systematically compromising the reliability and accuracy of the final answer.

These issues are exacerbated by the typical design of current reinforcement learning agents, which often optimize the entire search and reasoning trajectory as a single Markov Decision Process (MDP) using only sparse terminal rewards. This monolithic optimization strategy renders the system vulnerable, as a single misstep can invalidate the entire sequence. As reasoning chains lengthen, this fragility intensifies, leading to a significant degradation in performance.

The central motivation for this work is to overcome this structural brittleness by enabling agents to emulate human-like self-correction. Humans identify flaws, correct them, and proceed from the rectified point, rather than discarding the entire reasoning effort. The research aims to equip search-augmented LLMs with analogous mechanisms: detecting errors, surgically removing the flawed segments of a reasoning trajectory, and regenerating the process from the most recently corrected state. This fine-grained corrective approach is intended to transform inherently fragile reasoning into a more resilient and robust process, allowing agents to recover from mistakes gracefully.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces Erasable Reinforcement Learning (ERL), a framework designed to imbue search-augmented LLMs with self-correction capabilities. ERL extends traditional reinforcement learning by integrating error detection, erasure, and regeneration within a structured, multi-round reasoning process.

#### 3.1. Reinforcement Learning with a Search Engine

The foundation of ERL is a reinforcement learning (RL) approach that explicitly incorporates search engines into the policy optimization process. The objective is to maximize an expected reward `r_phi(x, y)` while regularizing the policy `π_θ` against a reference model `π_ref` using KL-divergence. This enables `π_θ` to learn the integration of retrieval and reasoning. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is employed for training, optimizing a clipped objective function that considers the advantage `A_t` derived from Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). The inputs `x` to the policy include both natural language components and retrieved results.

#### 3.2. Round-Based Reasoning

Reasoning is modeled as a sequence of structured rounds, denoted `T`. Each round `t` generates an interaction pair `⟨a_t, e_t⟩`, where `a_t` is the agent's action and `e_t` is the retrieved evidence. The agent's action space is structured into atomic operations that govern reasoning, searching, and answering.
- If `a_t` is an intermediate step `⟨(o_t, r_t), q_t⟩`, the agent performs a sequence of unit actions:
 - `<observation>` `o_t` `</observation>`: Reasoning an observation based on `e_t` and previous state `s_t-1`.
 - `<sub_answer>` `r_t` `</sub_answer>`: Yielding an intermediate phased conclusion.
 - `<search>` `q_t` `</search>`: Producing a query `q_t` that is submitted to an external search tool to obtain evidence `e_t`.
- If `a_t` is `⟨A_final⟩`, the process terminates with the final answer.
The state `s_t+1` is formed by appending the new action sequence and environment to the history `H_t`. Actions are sampled from the LLM's conditional distribution given the current state `s_t`.

#### 3.3. Reward Design

ERL employs dense stepwise rewards in addition to the sparse terminal reward to provide more informative supervision during training:
- **Search reward (`R_search_t`):** This reward encourages the retrieval of novel and relevant evidence while penalizing redundancy. It is calculated based on:
 - Coverage gain (`G_t`): Measures the maximum TF-IDF cosine similarity between newly retrieved documents `D^(t)` and gold evidence `D*`. A coverage vector `m_t` tracks previously covered gold evidence to reward only new coverage.
 - Redundancy penalty (`P_t`): Penalizes retrieved documents that were already present in the history of retrieved documents `H_t-1`.
 - The final search reward is `G_t - P_t`. This reward is attributed to the `</search>` token.
- **Sub-answer reward (`R_sub_answer_t`):** This reward encourages accurate intermediate reasoning. It is calculated based on the F1 overlap between the generated sub-answer `r_t` and gold sub-answers `A*`. Similar to `R_search_t`, it tracks improvements in F1 score against a running maximum `u_t` to reward only genuine progress. This reward is attributed to `</observation>` and `</sub_answer>` tokens.
- **Final reward (`R_answer`):** This is the terminal reward, computed as the average of the Exact Match (EM) and F1 scores between the `A_final` and the `A_gold`. This reward is attributed to the `</answer>` token.

#### 3.4. Erasable Reinforcement Learning (ERL)

The core innovation of ERL is its ability to identify and correct errors within the reasoning trajectory through erasure and regeneration. This is achieved via an erasure operator `E` that modifies the action sequence based on predefined conditions and thresholds (`α` for local errors, `β` for plan-level errors).
- **Sub-Answer Erasure:** If the `R_sub_answer_t` falls below a threshold `α`, indicating an incorrect intermediate conclusion, the current round's actions (observation, sub-answer, and subsequent search) are discarded. The state reverts to `s_t`, effectively allowing the agent to retry the current reasoning step.
- **Subsequent Search Erasure:** If `R_search_t` is below `α` for `t > 1`, indicating a faulty search query or irrelevant results in subsequent rounds, only the search behavior `<search>` is erased. The correct observation `o_t` and sub-answer `r_t` from that round are retained, and the agent re-attempts the search from that point.
- **Initial Search/Plan Erasure:** If `R_search_1` (the reward for the first search) is below a threshold `β` in the initial action round (`t = 0`), the entire trajectory is reset to the initial state `s_0`. This mechanism addresses fundamental errors in the initial planning or first search attempt.

This framework transforms error propagation into error recovery, allowing the model to adaptively correct its reasoning path.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of ERL, termed ESearch, was conducted on four multi-hop Question Answering (QA) benchmarks: HotpotQA, MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and Bamboogle. Performance was measured using Exact Match (EM) and F1 scores. Two model scales were used: Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B, with both base and instruct variants. The study compared ESearch against several strong baselines and state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods, using both offline retrieval (Wikipedia 2018 dump with E5 retriever) and online retrieval (Google Search API).

#### 4.1. Overall Performance (Offline and Online Evaluation)

- **Offline Evaluation:** ESearch consistently surpassed baselines and previous SOTA methods across all four datasets.
 - The Qwen2.5-3B model trained with ERL achieved average gains of +8.48% EM and +11.56% F1 compared to previous SOTA.
 - The Qwen2.5-7B model trained with ERL achieved average gains of +5.38% EM and +7.22% F1 compared to previous SOTA.
- **Online Evaluation:** When using the Google Search API for online retrieval, which provides fresher and more comprehensive information, ESearch continued to demonstrate superior performance and adaptability.
 - The Qwen2.5-3B model showed gains of +10.90% EM and +13.18% F1.
 - The Qwen2.5-7B model showed gains of +5.98% EM and +7.88% F1.
 ESearch consistently delivered the largest relative gains across different scales and environments, indicating its robustness in dynamic settings.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Classical Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

A direct comparison with classical RL algorithms, PPO and GRPO (which rely solely on task-level success for rewards), demonstrated ERL's effectiveness.
- ERL consistently and substantially outperformed both PPO and GRPO on both the 3B and 7B models.
- GRPO exhibited faster initial learning but suffered from instability and potential performance collapse in later training stages for search agent tasks.
- ERL achieved higher learning efficiency than PPO while maintaining training stability.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

A systematic ablation study quantified the contributions of each erasure mechanism.
- The full ERL framework achieved the best performance, indicating that the three erasure mechanisms are complementary.
- **Plan-triggered erasure** proved important for highly structured datasets, with disabling it resulting in a -2.05% F1 on 2WikiMultiHopQA. Its effectiveness in addressing missing retrieval was limited.
- **Search-triggered erasure** showed advantages for retrieval-intensive tasks, with disabling it leading to a -2.40% F1 on Bamboogle, but it did not remedy global reasoning errors.
- **Sub-answer-triggered erasure** benefited reasoning-intensive tasks, with disabling it yielding a -1.80% F1 on Musique. While it alleviated error propagation, it did not prevent erroneous reasoning from emerging.
- The mechanisms exhibited a clear hierarchy of effectiveness: sub-answer erasure > search erasure > plan erasure, suggesting that correcting errors during the reasoning process has a greater impact than preventing initial planning or search mistakes.
- ERL corrected 2.01% of decomposition errors, 6.53% of retrieval failures, and 9.6% of reasoning errors, demonstrating varied effectiveness across error types.

#### 4.4. Sensitivity Analysis of Erasure Trigger Thresholds

The study investigated the sensitivity of ERL's performance to the trigger thresholds (`α` and `β`) for its three erasure mechanisms.
- **Search module:** Exhibited the highest sensitivity. Lower thresholds (e.g., `α` ∈ {0.05, 0.10}) facilitated rapid convergence and optimal performance, while a higher threshold (`α` = 0.2) caused a sharp performance decline, suggesting it acts as an information bottleneck.
- **Planning module:** Demonstrated strong robustness. Varying `β` within {0.1, 0.2, 0.3} yielded nearly identical learning trajectories, indicating that downstream processes can compensate for high-level planning imperfections.
- **Sub-answer module:** Showed moderate and regular sensitivity. Increasing `α` led to a stable convergence trend but a stepwise decrease in final Answer F1 scores, suggesting that excessive erasure impedes logical information accumulation.

#### 4.5. Cost-Effectiveness and Dynamics of Erasure Events (Appendix findings)

- **Retry Budget:** While increasing erasure-retry budgets led to longer per-step wall-clock times, applying erasure (particularly to "Sub-Answer" stages) resulted in a higher rate of improvement in training metrics per unit of computation time compared to PPO. GRPO was more efficient per unit time but had a lower performance ceiling.
- **Erasure Dynamics:** The average frequency of sub-answer erasures became more pronounced as reasoning depth increased, suggesting that later stages of multi-hop reasoning are more prone to incorrect sub-answers and require more correction. Similar trends were observed for search erasures in later rounds, while plan erasure remained stable as it only applies to the first round.

#### 4.6. Number of Retrieved Documents (Appendix finding)

Varying the number of top-K retrieved documents showed that three documents (`k=3`) generally yielded the most reliable improvements and strongest overall results. A single document (`k=1`) was insufficient, while five documents (`k=5`) could sometimes introduce irrelevant information, leading to degraded performance in some datasets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Erasable Reinforcement Learning (ERL) as a method to address the brittleness of search-augmented Large Language Models (LLMs) in complex multi-hop reasoning. By explicitly identifying and correcting errors in decomposition, retrieval, and reasoning, ERL offers a mechanism for transforming fragile reasoning trajectories into robust ones.

The significance of this work lies in its contribution to improving the reliability and robustness of LLM-based agents in knowledge-intensive tasks. The consistent performance improvements achieved by ESearch across multiple multi-hop QA benchmarks, including HotpotQA, MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and Bamboogle, demonstrate the effectiveness and generalizability of the ERL framework. The establishment of new state-of-the-art results on these benchmarks provides empirical validation of the approach.

Potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Reliability in Complex AI Systems:** By enabling LLMs to self-correct during multi-step reasoning, ERL contributes to the development of more trustworthy and dependable AI agents, particularly in applications requiring accurate, multi-hop information processing.
- **Improved User Experience:** In question-answering systems and research tools, the ability to gracefully recover from intermediate errors could lead to more accurate final answers and a reduced incidence of misleading or incorrect information.
- **Advancement of Agentic AI:** The work moves towards equipping LLMs with more human-like reasoning capabilities, where errors are not catastrophic but opportunities for iterative refinement. This could inform the design of more sophisticated and resilient autonomous agents.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The ERL framework and its structured approach to error detection, erasure, and regeneration provide a foundation for further exploration. Future work may extend this mechanism to a broader range of generative tasks beyond question answering, or integrate it with online learning to enhance adaptive error-correction capabilities in dynamic environments.

While the current sequential design of ERL might incur increased computational overhead and face challenges with multiple simultaneous heterogeneous errors, the insights gained regarding error types and correction mechanisms are valuable. Addressing these limitations by developing strategies for coordinated error mitigation and a more global understanding of reasoning trajectories represents a significant direction for future research, aiming to further enhance the robustness and efficiency of search-augmented language models.
