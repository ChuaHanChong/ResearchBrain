# DemoSpeedup: Accelerating Visuomotor Policies via Entropy-Guided Demonstration Acceleration

> [!summary] Summary
> DemoSpeedup, a self-supervised method developed by researchers at Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute and Tsinghua University, preprocesses human robot manipulation demonstrations by intelligently accelerating less precise segments. This approach allows visuomotor policies trained on these accelerated demonstrations to execute tasks up to 3 times faster while maintaining or improving task success rates across various simulated and real-world tasks.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art visuomotor policies, despite high task completion rates, execute tasks unsatisfactorily slowly due to the inherent tardiness of human-collected demonstrations.
- Human teleoperation for robot control is constrained by factors like limited perception, lack of sensory feedback, and morphological differences, resulting in slow, cautious movements.
- Existing test-time acceleration methods for robot policies often lead to significant performance degradation due to distributional shifts.

## Method

- A self-supervised, multi-stage pipeline called **DemoSpeedup** preprocesses human demonstrations to accelerate them before policy training.
- A ==proxy policy== is trained on original demonstrations to estimate per-frame ==action entropy==, which identifies 'precision-required' (low entropy) and 'casual' (high entropy) segments.
- An entropy-guided acceleration strategy uses ==HDBSCAN clustering== to segment trajectories and applies piecewise varying-speed downsampling with a ==Replicate-Before-Downsample (RBD)== method to preserve state diversity.

## Results

- Achieved a **1.7x** to **3x** speedup in task completion time across 11 simulated and 5 real-world manipulation tasks using both ACT and Diffusion Policy.
- Maintained or improved task success rates compared to policies trained on original-speed data; for instance, ACT's success rate on 'Transfer Cube' increased from **72%** to **81%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the Replicate-Before-Downsample strategy and maintaining geometrical consistency in chunk length are essential for preserving high success rates during acceleration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action entropy can serve as a self-supervised proxy to distinguish between high-precision and casual segments within human demonstrations, without requiring explicit human labels.
> - Intelligently accelerating demonstrations *before* policy training, rather than at test-time, is crucial for achieving speedup while maintaining or improving task success rates.
> - Accelerating demonstrations can reduce the decision-making horizon and compounding errors, potentially leading to increased policy robustness and higher success rates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has witnessed remarkable progress, largely fueled by imitation learning (IL). Generative policies like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) and Diffusion Policy (DP) have achieved high task completion rates by learning directly from human demonstrations. However, a pervasive challenge remains: the learned policies often execute tasks unsatisfactorily slowly. This slowness is primarily attributed to the inherent tardiness of human-collected demonstrations, which are often slower than optimal for robotic execution due to the complexities of teleoperation (e.g., lack of full 3D view, absence of tactile feedback, morphological heterogeneity between human and robot, and equipment latency).

This paper addresses a crucial, yet often overlooked, aspect of robot policy deployment: **time efficiency**. While much research has focused on improving success rates and generalization, the speed of execution directly impacts productivity in real-world scenarios such as manufacturing assembly lines, caregiving for the elderly, or time-sensitive tasks.

Current attempts to address policy speed are limited:
- **Test-time acceleration techniques** involve naively down-sampling action chunks during deployment. While these can speed up execution, they often lead to significant performance drops due to distributional shifts.
- **Data curation for manipulation** has traditionally focused on improving success rates by filtering or down-sampling demonstrations based on metrics like geometric features or human labels. However, these methods either do not prioritize speed, or rely on closed-loop control that can make manipulation time longer, or require privileged information (e.g., contact oracle, human labels) that is not easily available. Some, like AWE, focus on minimizing trajectory length but might not ensure smooth actions suitable for acceleration.

"DemoSpeedup" distinguishes itself by proposing a novel, self-supervised approach to **accelerate the demonstrations themselves *before* policy training**. This pre-processing aims to teach the policy to be fast from the outset, rather than attempting to speed up a slow policy at test time. The key novelty lies in its **entropy-guided mechanism**, which intelligently identifies "precision-required" (low entropy) and "casual" (high entropy) segments of a demonstration. By accelerating the "casual" parts more aggressively while preserving the critical "precision" parts, DemoSpeedup aims to achieve speedup without compromising task success. Furthermore, it avoids the need for external annotations or task-specific heuristics, relying solely on the inherent information within the original demonstrations and a self-supervised proxy policy. This positions DemoSpeedup as a significant step towards more practical and efficient robotic manipulation in real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a self-supervised method, named **DemoSpeedup**, to **accelerate the execution speed of visuomotor policies** trained via imitation learning, while simultaneously **maintaining or even improving their task completion performance**.

The core motivations behind this objective stem from critical observations and limitations in current robot manipulation paradigms:

- **Slow Policy Execution:** Despite high task completion rates achieved by state-of-the-art imitation learning policies (e.g., ACT, Diffusion Policy), their real-world execution speed is often disappointingly slow. This "tardiness" directly originates from the human demonstrations used for training.
- **Challenges of Human Teleoperation:** Human operators, even highly skilled ones, struggle to teleoperate robot arms as fluently and quickly as they use their own hands. This is due to several factors:
 - **Perceptual Limitations:** Lack of full-directional, non-occluded 3D scene views.
 - **Sensory Deficiency:** Absence of tactile and proprioceptive feedback.
 - **Morphological Heterogeneity:** Differences in kinematics and dynamics between human and robot bodies.
 - **Equipment Latency:** Delays inherent in teleoperation systems.
 These factors collectively lead to slower, more cautious demonstration speeds.
- **Limitations of Existing Acceleration Methods:** Naive test-time acceleration (uniform downsampling) introduces significant distributional shifts, leading to notable performance degradation, making it impractical for reliable deployment.
- **Practical Applicability in Time-Sensitive Scenarios:** Slow execution hinders the deployment of robots in real-world applications where time efficiency is paramount, such as manufacturing assembly lines, logistics, or assisting in time-critical personal care (e.g., caring for babies/elderly). Boosting speed is crucial for improving productivity and ensuring success in such contexts.

The authors hypothesize that by intelligently accelerating only the "casual" (less precise, higher action entropy) sections of demonstrations, while preserving "precision-required" (high precision, lower action entropy) sections, they can train policies that are inherently faster without sacrificing accuracy. An intriguing secondary objective is to explore if this demonstration acceleration can *improve* success rates, potentially by reducing compounding errors over shorter decision-making horizons.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DemoSpeedup is a self-supervised, multi-stage pipeline designed to preprocess human demonstrations for training faster visuomotor policies. Its core idea revolves around using action entropy as a proxy for the precision required in different segments of a task.

The methodology proceeds as follows:

1. **Proxy Policy Training:**
 - An arbitrary generative behavior learning policy (e.g., ACT or Diffusion Policy) is initially trained on the **original, non-accelerated source dataset** of human demonstrations.
 - **Crucially, this "proxy policy" is not the final policy for deployment.** Its sole purpose is to serve as a **per-frame action entropy estimator**. It distills the implicit action entropy information embedded in the original dataset.

2. **Action Entropy Estimation:**
 - For each timestep `t` and observation `o_t` in a demonstration, the proxy policy is used to sample `N` possible action chunks `A_t`.
 - **Gaussian Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)** is then applied to these `N` action samples to obtain an estimated probability density distribution `p(a_t|o_t)`.
 - The **conditional action entropy** `H(a_t|o_t)` is then calculated using this estimated probability distribution.
 - **Rationale:** The key insight is that in "casual" sections of a task (e.g., free-space movement), human operators tend to have more diverse yet acceptable actions, leading to higher action entropy. Conversely, in "high-precision" sections (e.g., grasping, insertion), the range of acceptable actions is very narrow, resulting in lower action entropy. This correlation allows entropy to implicitly indicate the required precision level without explicit human labels.

3. **Entropy-Guided Demonstration Acceleration:**
 - **Entropy Preprocessing:** The estimated per-frame entropy values `H(a_t|o_t)` for each trajectory are first processed. **Isolation Forest** is used to detect and substitute outlier entropy values, mitigating noise from teleoperation data. The entropy values are then concatenated with their time index and normalized, preserving temporal properties for subsequent clustering.
 - **Clustering for Precision Labeling:** A **density-based clustering method, Hierarchical DBSCAN (HDBSCAN)**, is applied to the normalized entropy points.
 - High-entropy points are typically labeled as outliers or assigned to "casualness" clusters (set `C`).
 - Low-entropy areas form distinct clusters, which are identified as the "precision set" (set `P`). Further filtering ensures only clusters with mean entropy lower than zero (normalized) are considered precision sets.
 - This step segments each original demonstration trajectory into alternating "precision" and "casualness" segments.
 - **Replicate-Before-Downsample (RBD) Strategy:** To avoid significant information loss and reduction in visited state diversity caused by naive downsampling, DemoSpeedup employs an RBD strategy.
 - Instead of just skipping intermediate frames, if a segment is to be accelerated by `N` times, the target action chunk (and associated observation) is effectively replicated `N` times.
 - Each copy is then down-sampled with a different starting offset (e.g., the `i`-th copy starts `i` frames later). This ensures that while the effective action frequency is reduced, all original observation frames are still "seen" by the training process, preserving diversity.
 - **Piecewise Varying-Speed Acceleration:** Based on the precision labels (`P` or `C`), segments are down-sampled at different rates:
 - **Casualness segments (high entropy):** Accelerated at a higher down-sampling rate (`r_high`).
 - **Precision segments (low entropy):** Accelerated at a lower down-sampling rate (`r_low`), or even maintained at original speed in highly critical moments.
 - **Geometrical Consistency:** The chunk length for the *accelerated* policy is adjusted to maintain the **same approximate geometrical distance traveled by an action chunk as in the original policy**. This means if a policy is accelerated 2x, its action chunk length is halved. This design choice helps the accelerated policy converge better and reduces compounding error, as it needs to fit fewer action labels for the same physical movement.
 - **Controller Requirements:** The authors acknowledge that high-precision robot controllers are essential for successful high-speed execution. They highlight cases where gripper gain had to be increased to track faster motions.

4. **Accelerated Policy Training:**
 - Finally, a new behavior cloning policy (the "accelerated policy") is trained on the **newly generated, accelerated demonstration dataset**. This is the end product used for real-world deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DemoSpeedup's effectiveness was rigorously evaluated through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world environments, using two popular generative behavior cloning policies: ACT and Diffusion Policy (DP).

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments (11 tasks from Aloha and BiGym)

- **Speedup Achieved:** DemoSpeedup consistently achieved a significant speedup in task completion time, ranging from **1.7x to 3x** across different tasks and algorithms, compared to policies trained on original-speed demonstrations.
- **Success Rate Maintenance/Improvement:** Critically, this speedup was achieved while maintaining comparable task completion success rates. In many instances, policies trained with DemoSpeedup even showed **higher success rates** than those trained with non-accelerated data. For example, on "Transfer Cube" with ACT, success rate increased from 72% to 81%, and episode length decreased from 291 to 121.
- **Superiority over Test-Time Acceleration:** Direct comparison with naive test-time acceleration (e.g., ACT-2x, DP-2x) revealed DemoSpeedup's clear advantage. While test-time downsampling also shortened completion time, it typically resulted in an average performance drop of over 8%, validating DemoSpeedup's approach of training on accelerated data.
- **Algorithm Generalization:** DemoSpeedup proved effective with both ACT and Diffusion Policy, demonstrating its general applicability across different generative policy architectures.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments (5 tasks and a variation on Galaxea R1 bimanual humanoid platform)

- **Real-World Speedup:** The results mirrored simulation findings, with DemoSpeedup policies achieving the lowest cost time across tasks while maintaining performance. For tasks requiring high accuracy (e.g., "Bomb Deposal," "Kitchenware"), DemoSpeedup achieved at least **160% speedup**. For less precision-demanding tasks (e.g., "Sort"), speedup reached up to **278% (ACT) and 214% (DP)**.
- **Potential for Success Rate Improvement:** The trend of improved success rates observed in simulation also held in the real world. For instance, in "Pen in Cup," ACT+Ours achieved 24/30 successes with 8.28s cost, compared to ACT's 16/30 successes with 19.45s. The authors attribute this partly to the **reduced decision-making horizon** (which mitigates compounding errors in imitation learning) and the **increased marginal information per timestep** (which aids policy training convergence).
- **Impact of Inference Delay:** The authors noted that DemoSpeedup achieved higher speedup on ACT than DP in real-world scenarios, partly due to DP's inference delay which can cause jittery motions at higher speeds.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of key design choices:
 - **Replicate-Before-Downsample (RBD) Strategy:** Ablating RBD (i.e., using naive downsampling) led to a significant drop in success rates (e.g., ACT success rate fell from 56% to 29%), highlighting its role in preserving state diversity.
 - **Geometrical Consistency:** Removing this (using the same action chunk length as original) also resulted in a considerable performance drop (e.g., ACT success rate fell to 31%), emphasizing its benefit for convergence and error reduction.
 - **High-Precision Controller:** Lack of high-precision control for the robot gripper also negatively impacted success rates (e.g., ACT success rate fell to 53%), reinforcing the need for capable robot hardware.

#### 4.4. Visualization Analysis

- Visualizations of entropy curves against demonstration snapshots effectively illustrated that the estimated entropy successfully distinguished between "delicate skills" (e.g., picking objects, careful withdrawals, precise alignments – corresponding to low entropy) and "casual movements" (e.g., free-space approaches, broad movements – corresponding to high entropy). This qualitative analysis validated the core assumption of the entropy-guided approach.

#### 4.5. Comparison with Other Demonstration Speedup Methods

- **Contact Oracle:** A heuristic method using ground-truth contact information performed worse or comparably to DemoSpeedup, suggesting that contact alone doesn't capture all precision patterns and the constant time window around contact changes is insufficient.
- **AWE* (modified AWE):** An adapted dynamic programming method based on joint angles resulted in jittery motions and worse performance than even constant downsampling, highlighting the unique challenge of maintaining smoothness for accelerated policies.
- **Constant 2x/3x Downsampling:** While constant downsampling could achieve speedup, it often led to success rate degradation, particularly at higher rates (e.g., Constant 3x had significant drops), whereas DemoSpeedup maintained a better balance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"DemoSpeedup" presents a highly significant contribution to the field of robot manipulation, particularly in bridging the gap between high-performance imitation learning policies and their practical, real-world deployment.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Addresses a Critical Practical Bottleneck:** The primary impact is its effective solution to the long-standing problem of slow policy execution in imitation learning. By enabling policies to operate up to 3 times faster without performance degradation (and often with improvements), DemoSpeedup makes robot manipulation viable for time-sensitive industrial and domestic applications.
- **Self-Supervised and Annotation-Free:** A major strength is its self-supervised nature. It does not require additional human annotations (e.g., marking precision points) or privileged information (e.g., contact sensors), which are typically costly, time-consuming, and difficult to obtain at scale. This significantly lowers the barrier to adopting acceleration techniques.
- **Intelligent Demonstration Acceleration:** Unlike crude uniform downsampling, DemoSpeedup intelligently segments demonstrations based on action entropy, preserving critical high-precision movements while accelerating casual ones. This adaptive approach is key to maintaining task success.
- **Improved Success Rates:** The observation that DemoSpeedup can sometimes *improve* success rates is a compelling additional benefit. This suggests that accelerating demonstrations effectively reduces the decision-making horizon and compounding errors inherent in imitation learning, and potentially increases the "signal-to-noise" ratio of information per timestep, leading to more robust policies.
- **Generality and Robustness:** Its demonstrated effectiveness across different generative policy architectures (ACT, Diffusion Policy) and diverse robot platforms (simulation and real-world bimanual systems) underscores its robustness and generalizability.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The work opens new avenues for research into data-centric approaches for improving robot policy performance, not just in terms of success, but also efficiency.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Industrial Automation:** Faster policies can significantly boost productivity on assembly lines, in logistics, and other manufacturing processes, leading to increased throughput and cost-efficiency.
- **Service Robotics:** In domains like elder care or domestic assistance, quicker and more fluid robot movements can enhance user experience, provide more timely assistance, and improve the overall perceived competence of the robot.
- **Time-Critical Operations:** Enables robots to perform tasks that are inherently time-sensitive, such as manipulating fast-moving objects on a conveyor belt, or disarming a "bomb toy" before a countdown ends, as demonstrated in the paper.
- **Data Efficiency and Quality:** The finding that accelerating slow demonstrations can improve training efficiency and even success rates suggests a deeper connection between data speed/density and policy learning effectiveness. It encourages further exploration of how data characteristics impact learned behaviors.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
- **Occasional Minor Performance Drops:** While generally improving or maintaining success rates, minor drops can occur, possibly due to dynamics mismatch between original and accelerated demonstrations.
- **Manual Tuning of Acceleration Rates:** The desired acceleration rates (`r_high`, `r_low`) still need to be manually determined for each task/dataset, as the inherent "potential for acceleration" varies depending on the original human demonstration speed. This indicates a need for more adaptive rate determination.
- **Diffusion Policy Inference Delay:** The method doesn't explicitly address the inference delay inherent in certain generative policies like Diffusion Policy, which can cause jitter at very high speeds. The authors suggest future work could involve distillation methods or flow-based policies to mitigate this.

Despite these limitations, DemoSpeedup represents a substantial step forward in making learned robot behaviors more practical and efficient, moving imitation learning beyond mere task completion to real-world performance metrics.
