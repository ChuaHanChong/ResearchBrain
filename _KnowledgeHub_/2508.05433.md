# Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search for Programmatic Control Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (MLES) framework enables the direct synthesis of high-performing, transparent programmatic control policies by integrating Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) with evolutionary computation and visual feedback-driven behavior analysis. This approach achieved performance comparable to DRL baselines like PPO on tasks such as Lunar Lander and Car Racing, while producing human-readable code and traceable policy evolution.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policies, often represented as opaque neural networks, lack transparency, making them difficult to understand, verify, debug, and deploy in safety-critical applications.
- Existing methods for interpretable policies either rely on post-hoc explanations (limited for proactive design) or direct methods constrained by static grammars and limited search spaces.
- Previous LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (LES) in RL primarily focused on indirectly shaping reward functions for opaque neural policies, rather than directly synthesizing transparent programmatic policies.

## Method

- The **MLES** framework combines Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) with ==Evolutionary Computation== (EC) in a closed-loop system for end-to-end programmatic policy discovery.
- Policies are represented as executable Python code paired with natural language 'thought' rationales and enhanced with quantitative metrics and multimodal behavioral evidence (BE), primarily visual feedback.
- Evolutionary operators (exploration and multimodal modification) leverage BE and MLLMs to generate new policies by identifying behavioral shortcomings and proposing targeted revisions or parameter adjustments.

## Results

- MLES achieved training performance comparable to PPO on Lunar Lander (1.090 vs. 1.032) and Car Racing (98.070 vs. 99.212), while outperforming an LES variant without multimodal analysis (EoH) across both tasks.
- MLES demonstrated superior search efficiency, discovering high-performing policies faster than DRL baselines on Lunar Lander and with lower variance on Car Racing, consuming approximately 5,000 environment resets for Lunar Lander.
- Ablation studies confirmed that multimodal modification operators, which utilize behavioral evidence, were critical for performance, with their removal causing the most pronounced degradation (e.g., **42.24%** drop on Lunar Lander).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating visual feedback-driven behavioral evidence (BE) transforms LLM-driven policy generation from stochastic trial-and-error into a more diagnostic and targeted refinement process, significantly enhancing search efficiency and robustness.
> - The natural language 'thought' accompanying policy code is crucial for MLLMs to interpret policies and facilitates more effective reasoning and modification.
> - The performance and efficiency of MLES are directly linked to and scale with the capabilities of the underlying MLLMs, indicating its long-term viability and potential for future improvements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) has seen significant advancements, particularly with deep reinforcement learning (DRL) achieving notable successes in various control tasks. However, a persistent challenge in DRL is the opacity of its policies, which are typically represented as complex neural networks. This lack of transparency impedes human understanding, verification, and debugging, thereby undermining trust and hindering deployment in safety-critical applications such as autonomous driving and healthcare. The learning process in DRL, relying on gradient-based optimization in high-dimensional parameter spaces, further complicates analysis, intervention, and knowledge reuse. This context has motivated research into developing control policies that are not only high-performing but also transparent, verifiable, and human-readable.

Existing approaches to create transparent policies broadly fall into two categories:
1. **Post-hoc methods:** These aim to explain or distill a pre-trained black-box policy into a more interpretable model, such as decision trees, mathematical formulas, or domain-specific language (DSL) programs. While these methods can achieve high performance by mimicking opaque policies, they generally offer limited insight into proactive policy construction or improvement and may struggle with generalization beyond the oracle's domain.
2. **Direct methods:** These involve discovering policies from scratch through environmental interaction, often generating logic rules or programs within a pre-defined DSL. However, these methods are frequently constrained by static grammars or limited search spaces, which can restrict their expressiveness and scalability.

The recent emergence of large language models (LLMs) has introduced new possibilities. LLMs exhibit strong capabilities in contextual understanding, reasoning, and code generation. Building on this, the LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (LES) paradigm integrates the generative and reasoning strengths of LLMs with the iterative optimization power of Evolutionary Computation (EC). Previous work within the LES paradigm in the RL domain has primarily focused on indirectly optimizing agents by shaping reward functions to improve the performance of downstream neural policies. However, the resulting policies in these approaches remain neural and opaque.

This research addresses a gap in the landscape by exploring whether LES can directly synthesize *programmatic policies*—executable programs with natural language rationales—that are both effective and transparent. It further investigates how this process can be enhanced for reliability and efficiency. The proposed Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (MLES) framework positions itself as a direct approach that expands upon LES by incorporating multimodal feedback, specifically visual feedback-driven behavior analysis, to guide policy generation. This aims to overcome the limitations of predefined DSLs and facilitate the discovery of human-aligned, transparent policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel approach for programmatic control policy discovery that addresses the dual challenge of achieving high performance while ensuring transparency and verifiability. This overarching goal is motivated by several limitations observed in current DRL and existing LES paradigms:

- **Addressing DRL Opacity:** Deep reinforcement learning, despite its performance, typically produces policies as opaque neural networks. This "black-box" nature fundamentally hinders understanding of decision-making processes, complicates verification, and undermines trust. Such limitations are particularly critical in safety-critical domains (e.g., autonomous driving, healthcare) where interpretability and explainability are paramount for real-world deployment. Furthermore, the gradient-based optimization intrinsic to DRL makes the learning process difficult to analyze, intervene in, or reuse. The research aims to overcome this by generating policies that are directly human-readable and semantically meaningful.

- **Extending LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (LES):** While LLMs have demonstrated strong capabilities in code generation and reasoning, and LES has shown success in automated design, previous applications in reinforcement learning primarily focused on using LLMs to *shape reward functions* for neural network-based policies. This indirect approach still results in opaque control policies. A key motivation for this work is to investigate if LES can be extended to *directly synthesize* high-performance programmatic policies, thereby yielding agents that are inherently transparent from the outset.

- **Enhancing Policy Reliability and Discovery Efficiency:** Beyond merely demonstrating the feasibility of direct programmatic policy synthesis, the research is motivated by the need to develop a process that is efficient and produces reliable policies. Traditional evolutionary search methods, when combined with LLMs, can suffer from stochastic trial-and-error without grounded feedback. The motivation is to introduce mechanisms that transform this into a more diagnostic and targeted refinement process.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth three specific contributions, which serve as its key objectives:
1. **Propose the MLES Framework:** To integrate Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) with Evolutionary Computation (EC) for the direct synthesis of programmatic control policies. This framework is designed to enable end-to-end policy discovery through environmental interaction, drawing parallels with DRL methods in its directness but diverging in its outcome of transparent policies.
2. **Evaluate a Prototypical MLES Instantiation:** To empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of MLES by applying it to standard RL benchmarks (Lunar Lander and Car Racing). This objective includes showing that MLES can produce effective policies with performance comparable to strong DRL baselines (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO) while simultaneously providing transparent control logic and traceable design processes.
3. **Conduct Comprehensive Analysis and Ablation Studies:** To thoroughly investigate MLES's effectiveness, search efficiency, and the influence of different forms of behavioral evidence and prompt designs on the policy evolution process. This aims to provide insights into the internal mechanisms and specific contributions of the framework's components.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Multimodal LLM-assisted Evolutionary Search (MLES) framework operates as a closed-loop evolutionary process designed to iteratively discover programmatic control policies. The policy discovery problem is framed as an MLLM-driven search within an open-ended semantic policy space to maximize policy performance.

#### 3.1. MLES Framework Overview

The MLES framework requires configurable inputs: a selection of MLLMs, a formal task description, an optional initial set of programmatic policies, an evaluator for environment interaction, and a summarizer for generating behavioral evidence. It comprises six core components:
- **Evolutionary Search Loop:** Manages iterations and orchestrates the evolutionary process, balancing exploration and exploitation.
- **Policy Pool:** A dynamic repository that stores the current population of policies, selecting parents and integrating newly generated offspring.
- **Evaluators:** Execute policies within the target environment, yielding quantitative performance metrics (e.g., episode rewards) and raw behavior traces. Policy evaluation is parallelized for efficiency.
- **Behavior Summarizer:** A key innovation, this component converts raw behavior traces into *behavioral evidence (BE)*. Unlike evaluators that quantify performance, the summarizer provides rationale-rich feedback explaining *why* a policy succeeded or failed, enabling targeted policy modification.
- **Prompt Sampler:** Constructs multimodal few-shot prompts by integrating selected parent policies and their corresponding BE. These prompts guide MLLMs in proposing meaningful policy refinements.
- **LLMs Ensemble:** A configurable set of MLLMs that reason over the multimodal prompts, leveraging their vision-language understanding, reasoning, and code generation capabilities to produce new candidate policies.

#### 3.2. Policy Representation

Each candidate policy within the evolutionary search is treated as an "individual," structured as a tuple with four elements:
- **Code:** The policy is an executable Python program, ensuring transparency, modularity, human-readability, debuggability, and reusability.
- **Thought:** A natural language summary of the policy’s underlying rationale or design intent, which aids MLLMs in interpreting the code and facilitates deeper reasoning.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Task-specific numerical evaluations (e.g., episode rewards, success rates) obtained from environment execution. These metrics serve as the objective function for selection and convergence.
- **Behavioral Evidence (BE):** Captures the policy’s overall behavior or highlights key events to identify areas for improvement. BE provides diagnostic signals for policy generation, enabling targeted modifications beyond just scalar performance. Its format is flexible (images, videos, text state sequences); in this work, images are primarily used.

#### 3.3. MLES-EoH Implementation

The paper instantiates MLES using the Evolution of Heuristics (EoH) as its evolutionary backbone, termed MLES-EoH. This specific implementation minimizes structural changes to search operators to isolate the impact of visual feedback-driven behavior analysis.
- **Evolutionary Backbone:** The algorithm iteratively selects parent policies, obtains their BEs, constructs multimodal few-shot prompts, generates offspring via MLLMs, evaluates offspring, creates their BEs, and updates the policy pool.
- **Parent Selection:** Exponential rank selection is used, prioritizing high-performing individuals for exploitation while retaining a non-zero probability for lower-ranked policies to maintain diversity. This high selection pressure supports sample efficiency given MLLM inference costs.
- **Policy Pool Management:** Offspring are filtered for redundancy, and the top N (N=16) individuals, ranked by quantitative metrics, are retained.
- **Evolutionary Operators:** MLES employs both exploration and multimodal modification operators:
 - **Exploration Operators (E1 and E2):** E1 generates novel policies with fundamentally different control strategies. E2 identifies shared patterns across multiple parents to construct generalized policies, akin to crossover.
 - **Multimodal Modification Operators (M1 M and M2 M):** These operators critically leverage BE. M1 M directs MLLMs to identify behavioral shortcomings by analyzing policy code and BE, then revise the control logic. M2 M prompts MLLMs to identify and adjust critical policy parameters based on observed evidence. The integration of BE provides grounded insights for reasoning, leading to more purposeful offspring generation.
- **Multimodal Few-shot Prompt Construction:** The prompt sampler dynamically combines a static task description, parent policy information (code and thought), relevant BEs, and operator-specific instructions.
- **Policy Generation & Individual Construction:** MLLMs generate candidate policies, which are then executed by evaluators to assess quantitative metrics. The raw execution data is processed by the behavior summarizer to create BE, which is stored with the new individual for future reuse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Setting

MLES was evaluated on two OpenAI Gym tasks: Lunar Lander (discrete actions, 8D state) and Car Racing (continuous actions, 96x96x3 image observations). Baselines included Deep Q-Network (DQN) for Lunar Lander, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for both, and EoH (an LES variant without multimodal behavioral analysis) for both. Experiments used GPT-4o-mini as the MLLM, with a budget of 2000 LLM requests and equivalent environment resets for baselines. The policy pool size was 16, and initial policies were shared with EoH. Each experiment was repeated five times.

#### 4.2. Comparative Evaluations of MLES

(Table 1 in paper)
MLES achieved the highest training performance on Lunar Lander and ranked second-best on Car Racing, performing comparably to the PPO baseline. For instance, on Lunar Lander, MLES had an average training score of 1.090 compared to PPO's 1.032. On Car Racing, MLES scored 98.070, close to PPO's 99.212. These results indicate that MLES can synthesize high-performing policies directly through environment interaction, matching the performance of established DRL methods.

Compared to EoH, MLES consistently and significantly outperformed it across both tasks, exhibiting superior convergence and stability (smaller Standard Error of the Mean, SEM). This performance gap was particularly pronounced in the more challenging Car Racing task (MLES 98.070 vs. EoH 89.808), demonstrating the substantial benefit of integrating behavior analysis into policy evolution. A consistent decline in testing performance relative to training was observed across all methods, indicating a general challenge in generalization.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Policy Discovery Efficiency

(Figs. 3 and 4 in paper)
MLES demonstrated superior search efficiency compared to baselines. On Lunar Lander, MLES discovered high-performing policies within approximately 5,000 environment resets, which was faster than both PPO and DQN. On Car Racing, MLES achieved a convergence speed comparable to PPO but with significantly lower variance across runs, indicating more stable learning dynamics. MLES also showed faster convergence and higher final performance than EoH across tasks. The ability of MLES and EoH to leverage prior knowledge as initial policies resulted in higher initial performance compared to DRL baselines, highlighting MLES's capacity for knowledge reuse and transfer.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Analysis of Transparency

(Fig. 5 in paper)
The evolutionary process for Car Racing policies generated by MLES was shown to be interpretable and traceable. Visualizations illustrated how individual scores changed over generations and how specific policies were refined. MLLMs leveraged Behavioral Evidence (BE) to identify failure patterns in parent policies and implement targeted improvements. The final policy's code and accompanying natural language "thought" were human-readable, and a review by graduate students confirmed their understandability. This indicates transparency at both the policy level (interpretable code) and the discovery process level (traceable rationale for evolution).

#### 4.5. Verification of Cold-Start Capability

(Table 2 in paper)
MLES was shown to be capable of autonomously discovering high-performing policies even when starting entirely from scratch (without an initial policy). With a stronger MLLM (GPT-5-mini), MLES achieved strong performance in cold-start scenarios, successfully solving tasks without human prior knowledge within the query budget. The study also confirmed that ingesting prior knowledge accelerates policy discovery, highlighting MLES's flexibility in leveraging existing information.

#### 4.6. Impact of Underlying MLLM Capabilities

(Table 2 in paper)
Using stronger MLLMs (e.g., GPT-5-mini) significantly boosted both the performance and search efficiency of MLES. For instance, on the Car Racing task, GPT-5-mini achieved the maximum score (100.0) using approximately 17% of the sample budget required by GPT-4o-mini. This suggests that MLES's capabilities are intrinsically linked to and will benefit from advancements in foundation models, demonstrating the framework’s longevity and scalability.

#### 4.7. Ablation Study on Evolutionary Operators

(Table 3 in paper, expanded in Appendix B.4)
The ablation study assessed the individual contributions of MLES's evolutionary operators. The removal of multimodal modification operators (M1 M and M2 M), which leverage behavioral evidence, resulted in the most pronounced performance degradation (e.g., a 42.24% drop on Lunar Lander for the combined removal). This highlights the critical role of targeted refinement based on execution feedback. The absence of exploration operators (E1 and E2) led to a notable performance decrease in the more challenging Car Racing task (e.g., 11.32% for E1 removal), indicating their importance in complex policy spaces. However, their effect on the simpler Lunar Lander was minimal, suggesting that aggressive exploration might introduce counterproductive variance in less complex environments. The full MLES framework consistently outperformed all ablated variants, confirming the synergistic effect of combining exploration (for diversity) and multimodal modification (for evidence-driven refinement). Removing the natural language "Thought" description also led to a substantial performance drop (4.75% on Lunar Lander, 10.04% on Car Racing), emphasizing the importance of semantic guidance for MLLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Multimodal Large Language Model-assisted Evolutionary Search (MLES) framework represents a notable advancement in the development of control policies that address the critical need for both high performance and transparency.

**Bridging Performance and Transparency:** MLES successfully demonstrates the capability to generate programmatic policies with performance comparable to strong DRL baselines like PPO, while simultaneously providing transparent control logic and traceable design processes. This directly addresses a long-standing challenge in DRL, where policy opacity hinders trust, verification, and deployment in sensitive or safety-critical domains.

**Novel Paradigm for Policy Discovery:** By unifying MLLMs, evolutionary search, and multimodal evaluation, MLES introduces a new paradigm for direct policy synthesis. Unlike prior LES applications that focused on reward shaping for opaque neural policies, MLES enables end-to-end discovery of human-readable, executable code.

**Overcoming Limitations of Existing Approaches:** MLES overcomes the expressive limitations of predefined domain-specific languages (DSLs) and the constrained search spaces typically associated with other direct policy learning methods. By leveraging the open-ended generative capabilities of MLLMs, it can explore a richer and more flexible policy space.

**Enhanced Efficiency and Robustness:** The integration of visual feedback-driven behavior analysis fundamentally transforms LLM-driven discovery from stochastic trial-and-error into a more grounded and diagnostic refinement process. This leads to significantly enhanced search efficiency, faster convergence, and improved robustness of the generated policies, as evidenced by narrower confidence intervals in experimental results.

**Facilitating Knowledge Transfer and Reuse:** MLES intrinsically supports knowledge transfer and reuse through its code-based policy representation. Unlike DRL, which often requires complex transfer learning mechanisms, MLES can leverage existing heuristics as initial policies, accelerating discovery. The modular nature of the generated code further simplifies adaptation and reuse in related control scenarios.

**Potential for Real-world Deployment and Human Collaboration:** The transparent, verifiable, and debuggable nature of programmatic policies makes them more suitable for real-world applications where accountability is crucial. The modular architecture of MLES-discovered policies also enables hybrid AI systems (e.g., integrating deep learning for perception with transparent MLES logic for control), enhancing safety. Furthermore, the framework's human-readable outputs and traceable evolution process facilitate human-in-the-loop collaboration, allowing experts to inspect, modify, and refine policies via natural language feedback or direct code editing.

**Scalability with MLLM Advancements:** The framework's performance and efficiency are shown to scale with the capabilities of underlying MLLMs. This indicates that MLES is a forward-looking paradigm poised to benefit from continued advancements in large language models, ensuring its longevity and increasing power over time.

#### 5.1. Identified Limitations and Future Directions

- **Task-Specific Behavioral Evidence Design:** A current limitation is the manual effort required to design effective behavioral evidence (BE) for new tasks. Future work aims to reduce this through reuse of existing BE pipelines (e.g., frame stacking, trajectory mapping), leveraging raw video as BE, or even automating the BE-generation pipeline design itself as a meta-learning problem.
- **Dependency on MLLM Capabilities:** The performance ceiling of MLES is directly tied to the MLLMs it employs. However, this also positions MLES to continuously improve as foundational models advance, suggesting a symbiotic relationship.

In summary, MLES offers a promising paradigm for developing transparent and verifiable control policies, reducing human effort, and fostering knowledge reuse. Its ability to generate effective, human-aligned, and interpretable policies, combined with its inherent scalability and potential for hybrid systems, positions it as a significant contribution to the field of trustworthy AI control systems.
