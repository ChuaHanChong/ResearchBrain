# SpatiaLQA: A Benchmark for Evaluating Spatial Logical Reasoning in Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Zhejiang University introduce SpatiaLQA, a benchmark demonstrating current Vision-Language Models struggle with spatial logical reasoning, particularly in complex multi-step planning and inferring action preconditions. Their proposed Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR) technique helps models better integrate visual-spatial information for improved performance.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) reportedly lack "spatial logical reasoning"—the ability to understand spatial relationships and infer logical dependencies for multi-step tasks in complex, real-world environments.
- Existing benchmarks (VQA, logical reasoning, embodied QA) do not adequately or systematically assess this integrated spatial and logical reasoning capability.
- State-of-the-art VLMs struggle with making sound decisions in scenarios requiring complex interdependencies in physical space, such as needing to remove obstructions before manipulating a target object.

## Method

- Developed ==SpatiaLQA==, a large-scale benchmark comprising **9,605** image–text QA pairs from 241 real indoor scenes, requiring multi-step, open-vocabulary answers with explicit precondition annotations.
- Implemented a three-stage data augmentation process (manual annotation, subgraph extraction, graph expansion) to create diverse and complex reasoning scenarios, enhancing dataset scale and difficulty.
- Proposed ==Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR)==, an iterative method that uses visual foundation models (Depth Anything V2, SAM) to generate task-relevant scene graphs, guiding VLMs in multi-step reasoning.

## Results

- Evaluated **41** VLMs on SpatiaLQA, revealing a substantial performance gap: the best VLM (GPT-5) achieved F1 scores of **76.0** (content) and **47.0** (preconditions), while humans achieved **97.6** (content) and **92.5** (preconditions).
- VLMs demonstrated a notable deficiency in causal reasoning, with precondition prediction (F_p) consistently much lower than step content prediction (F_c), highlighting struggles in inferring logical dependencies.
- The proposed RSGAR method, using GPT-4o, improved performance to F_c **69.8** and F_p **28.1**, outperforming baselines and showing particular benefits for complex tasks with more answer steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spatial logical reasoning is a distinct and crucial cognitive capability for VLMs, bridging spatial perception and multi-step logical inference, which is foundational for embodied AI tasks.
> - Automated evaluation of open-vocabulary, multi-step answers with preconditions can be reliably achieved using advanced VLMs like GPT-4o for scoring, validated against human judgments.
> - Explicitly representing object relationships through iterative scene graph construction (RSGAR) helps VLMs decompose complex visual scenes and significantly improves performance on intricate multi-step spatial logical tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated considerable advancements in tasks such as Visual Question Answering (VQA), task planning, image captioning, and scene understanding. Contemporary VLMs, including models like GPT-4o, exhibit capabilities in common VQA tasks and abstract logical reasoning. However, the paper identifies a gap in their ability to perform "spatial logical reasoning" within complex, real-world environments. This specific capability involves understanding the spatial relationships among objects in a scene and inferring the logical dependencies between steps required to complete multi-step tasks.

The authors delineate spatial logical reasoning from related research areas:
- **Visual Question Answering (VQA):** Traditional VQA benchmarks often focus on recognizing visual content and executing short-range, single-step reasoning, such as object identification, counting, or basic spatial relation queries. In contrast, spatial logical reasoning requires models to perform long-range, multi-step reasoning that infers a sequence of dependent operations based on spatial configurations, rather than providing a singular factual answer.
- **Logical Reasoning:** Benchmarks for logical reasoning, including mathematical problem-solving, typically assess a model's ability to establish causal or consistent relationships within abstract textual or symbolic domains. These tasks often decouple the reasoning process from real-world spatial information. Spatial logical reasoning, conversely, necessitates the joint comprehension of spatial relationships and causal logic within realistic visual scenes, presenting a more integrated and complex form of reasoning.
- **Embodied Question Answering (EQA):** EQA evaluates an agent's capacity to translate abstract language instructions into physically executable action sequences within interactive environments, often constrained by a predefined set of motor primitives. Spatial logical reasoning, as defined in this work, does not involve physical execution. Instead, it focuses on the model's ability to deduce a logically consistent and spatially coherent multi-step reasoning process at a visual-semantic level, with answers typically belonging to an open-vocabulary space. This emphasis on cognitive and linguistic abstraction positions spatial logical reasoning as a foundational cognitive capability that underpins embodied tasks, without requiring direct physical interaction.

By focusing on spatial logical reasoning, the work addresses an underexplored area that bridges spatial perception and multi-step logical inference. This area is considered crucial for enhancing the overall reasoning capacity of VLMs across diverse real-world applications and for improving performance in embodied AI tasks. The introduction of a dedicated benchmark aims to systematically evaluate this specific VLM capability, which existing benchmarks reportedly do not fully capture.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from an observed limitation in current Vision-Language Models (VLMs): despite their advanced capabilities in general understanding and reasoning, they reportedly exhibit deficiencies in making sound decisions within complex real-world environments that demand an integration of spatial and logical understanding. This specific deficiency is termed "spatial logical reasoning" by the authors.

The paper outlines the following key objectives and their underlying motivations:

1. **To define and highlight spatial logical reasoning as a critical VLM capability:** The authors define spatial logical reasoning as the ability to understand spatial relationships among objects in complex scenes and to discern the logical dependencies between sequential steps in multi-step tasks. The motivation is derived from observations where state-of-the-art VLMs, despite excelling in common VQA and logical reasoning, fail in tasks that require reasoning about interdependencies in a physical space, such as needing to remove obstructing objects before manipulating a target object. This capability is considered essential for models to perform effectively and safely in real-world scenarios and to serve as a cognitive foundation for embodied AI tasks.

2. **To introduce SpatiaLQA, a benchmark for evaluating spatial logical reasoning:** The authors contend that existing benchmarks do not systematically or accurately assess VLM performance in spatial logical reasoning. This creates a critical gap that constrains the safe and effective deployment of VLMs in practical applications. To bridge this gap, the objective is to create a large-scale, comprehensive benchmark dataset, SpatiaLQA, that specifically targets and quantifies this integrated reasoning ability. SpatiaLQA is designed to require models to provide multi-step, logically ordered, and dependency-aware answers within an open-vocabulary space, reflecting higher cognitive abstraction.

3. **To conduct a systematic evaluation of mainstream VLMs:** A key objective is to empirically assess the spatial logical reasoning capabilities of a broad range of representative VLMs (41 models) using the newly developed SpatiaLQA benchmark. This evaluation aims to identify the current performance levels of VLMs in this domain, highlight specific weaknesses, particularly in handling complex tasks and inferring causal dependencies, and establish a baseline for future research. The motivation is to provide concrete evidence of the performance gap between VLMs and human-level reasoning in spatial logical tasks.

4. **To propose a method to enhance VLM performance in spatial logical reasoning:** Recognizing the identified deficiencies, the research aims to develop and demonstrate a novel approach, Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR). This objective is motivated by the need for practical solutions to improve VLM performance in complex, multi-step spatial logical tasks by providing structured scene understanding. The method aims to leverage visual foundation models to decompose complex scenes into task-relevant scene graphs, thereby facilitating more accurate multi-step reasoning for VLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the creation of a new benchmark dataset, SpatiaLQA, and a proposed method, Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR), to address the identified challenges in spatial logical reasoning for VLMs.

#### 3.1. SpatiaLQA Benchmark Construction

**Definition of Spatial Logical Reasoning:** The authors define spatial logical reasoning as the ability of a model to solve complex problems by generating a sequence of logically coherent steps, integrating both spatial understanding and logical inference.

**Dataset Composition:** SpatiaLQA comprises 9,605 image–text question-answer (QA) pairs collected from 241 real-world indoor scenes, spanning 13 distinct scene categories. The QA pairs require multi-step answers, where each step includes `content` (the action to be performed) and `precondition` (steps that must be completed beforehand). The answers are open-vocabulary.

**Dataset Collection Process:** The data collection involved a three-stage augmentation process to enhance diversity and complexity:
1. **Manual Annotation:** Initially, 2,401 real indoor scene images were acquired. Trained annotators manually generated one QA pair per image, with answers containing 2 to 8 steps. Annotations focused on direct logical dependencies (preconditions). A two-round review process ensured data quality. Each annotation was conceptually represented as an undirected graph, with nodes for step content and edges for logical dependencies.
2. **Subgraph Extraction Augmentation:** This stage augmented the 2,401 manually annotated samples by deriving 2,251 new QA pairs. Subgraphs, each containing at least one edge, were extracted from the original answer graphs. A new question was formulated corresponding to the final step of each extracted subgraph.
3. **Graph Expansion Augmentation:** Applied to the combined 4,652 samples from the previous stages, this process generated 4,953 new QA pairs. It involved a heuristic method where original "Remove" or "Pick up" tasks were transformed into "Place B on A" tasks. This modification entailed changing existing steps (e.g., "Remove A" to "Pick up A") and adding new logically consistent steps (e.g., "Put down A", "Place B on A").

#### 3.2. Evaluation Metrics

To enable automated evaluation of open-vocabulary answers, the authors developed a three-step evaluation process:
1. **Step-Level Matching:** GPT-4o was employed to compare predicted steps with ground-truth steps. Given an image, GPT-4o determined whether the semantic content of a predicted step was consistent with a ground-truth step, generating a binary matching matrix (allowing one-to-many matches).
2. **Optimal One-to-One Matching:** The Hungarian algorithm was applied to the matching matrix to filter redundant matches and establish the maximum possible one-to-one correspondence between predicted and annotated steps.
3. **F1 Score Calculation:** Based on the filtered one-to-one matches across all samples, Recall (R) and Precision (P) were calculated for both content (R_c, P_c) and preconditions (R_p, P_p). The F1 scores (F_c, F_p) served as the primary evaluation metrics.

**Human Alignment of VLM-based Evaluation:** To validate the reliability of the GPT-4o-based scoring, an analysis compared its results with human judgments on 300 randomly sampled instances. Proprietary models (GPT-4o, Qwen-VL-Max) demonstrated higher Pearson correlation coefficients and lower mean absolute errors (around 3 percentage points) relative to human evaluators, compared to open-source scoring models (mean absolute errors exceeding 10 percentage points). Consequently, GPT-4o was selected as the scoring VLM for the main evaluation.

#### 3.3. Vision-Language Model Evaluation

Forty-one representative VLMs were systematically evaluated on SpatiaLQA. These models spanned various types, including those in "non-thinking" and "thinking" modes, and models supporting text-image input versus general multimodal inputs. A human participant was also engaged to establish a human-level performance baseline.

#### 3.4. Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR)

To improve VLM performance on complex spatial logical reasoning tasks, the authors proposed RSGAR, which consists of three steps:
1. **Perception Integration:** Depth Anything V2 and SAM were utilized to obtain depth and segmentation maps of the scene image.
2. **Recursive Scene Graph Generation:** The task-specified object was designated as the initial "source object." A VLM, using the original image, depth map, and segmentation map, identified "target objects" in direct contact or functional relation with the source object and their spatial relationships. This information formed an initial scene graph. In subsequent iterations (up to a maximum T), previous target objects were treated as new source objects, and the process repeated, expanding the scene graph.
3. **Final Answer Generation:** The comprehensively generated scene graph, combined with the task prompt, was fed into the VLM to produce the final answer. This iterative process aims to incrementally decompose complex visual scenes into task-relevant scene graphs, enabling the VLM to focus on relevant spatial contexts for multi-step reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Evaluation of Vision-Language Models on SpatiaLQA

The extensive evaluation of 41 mainstream VLMs on the SpatiaLQA benchmark yielded several findings:
- **Overall Performance Gap:** Even the most advanced VLMs demonstrated a significant performance gap compared to human participants. Humans achieved an F1 score of 97.6 for content (F_c) and 92.5 for preconditions (F_p). In contrast, the best-performing VLM, GPT-5, achieved F_c of 76.0 and F_p of 47.0. This indicates that current VLMs struggle with spatial logical reasoning.
- **Performance Trends:**
 - **Model Size and Version:** Generally, VLMs with larger parameter sizes, newer versions (e.g., Qwen3-VL-4B-Ins vs. Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Ins), and those operating in a "thinking mode" (e.g., Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking vs. Kimi-VL-A3B-Ins) tended to exhibit better performance.
 - **Proprietary vs. Open-Source:** Proprietary models consistently surpassed the performance of open-source models across the benchmark.
- **Deficiencies in Causal Reasoning:** The prediction of preconditions (F_p) was consistently lower than the prediction of step content (F_c) across nearly all evaluated VLMs. This suggests a notable deficiency in the models' ability to infer and represent logical dependencies between actions, even when they can identify the actions themselves.
- **Recall vs. Precision Imbalance:** Recall scores for both content and preconditions were generally lower than precision scores. This implies that VLMs tend to generate a smaller number of highly certain steps or preconditions, potentially omitting uncertain but necessary steps. For instance, GPT-5 produced answers with an average of 3.1 steps, while ground-truth answers averaged 4.2 steps.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Poor Performance

A dimension-wise analysis further explored the reasons for VLM performance limitations:
- **Impact of Task Complexity:** VLM performance generally declined as the number of annotated answer steps increased. This indicates that tasks requiring longer and more complex reasoning sequences pose a significant challenge for current models, demanding more stable and extended reasoning processes.
- **Annotation Source Influence:** Performance varied with the data augmentation source: models performed best on samples generated via subgraph extraction augmentation (which typically contained fewer, simpler steps), followed by manually annotated samples, and worst on graph expansion augmentation samples (which introduced more complex, multi-step problems). This finding reinforces the observation that VLMs struggle disproportionately with more intricate reasoning tasks.
- **Consistency Across Scene Categories:** VLM performance showed relative consistency across the 13 different indoor scene categories, suggesting that the difficulty primarily lies in the logical and multi-step nature of the tasks rather than specific scene types.

#### 4.3. Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR) Results

The proposed RSGAR method, using GPT-4o as the VLM, was evaluated against several baselines:
- **Effectiveness:** RSGAR achieved the highest performance among all methods, with F_c of 69.8 and F_p of 28.1. Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting, which enables more stable reasoning, showed the second-best performance (F_c=67.6, F_p=27.0).
- **Comparison with Baselines:** Simply providing additional inputs such as depth maps, segmentation maps, or both, or employing PhysAgent, did not consistently improve performance over vanilla reasoning, and in some cases, led to degraded performance. This suggests that raw visual cues alone are insufficient for enhancing spatial logical reasoning without a structured processing approach.
- **Performance on Complex Tasks:** RSGAR demonstrated a significant improvement in VLM performance on samples requiring a higher number of answer steps, while showing a minor decrease on samples with fewer steps. This indicates that RSGAR effectively aids VLMs in tackling more complex, multi-step tasks by explicitly representing object relationships.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Iteration Number (T):** Increasing the number of iterations (T) in RSGAR led to improved performance (e.g., F_c rose from 68.5 at T=1 to 70.6 at T=7). More iterations allowed for a more comprehensive scene graph, providing richer information to the VLM.
 - **Perception Maps:** Omitting either the depth map or the segmentation map resulted in a decline in RSGAR's performance. This finding highlights that both types of visual information are distinct and crucial for generating an accurate and comprehensive scene graph for effective reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces SpatiaLQA, a benchmark that addresses an identified gap in evaluating the spatial logical reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). The work has several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Defines and Quantifies a Critical VLM Capability:** By formally defining "spatial logical reasoning" and creating a benchmark specifically for it, the paper highlights a crucial, yet previously underexplored, aspect of VLM intelligence. This capability is essential for VLMs to operate effectively and safely in real-world environments that require understanding both spatial configurations and logical task sequences.
- **Provides a Standardized Evaluation Tool:** SpatiaLQA offers a large-scale, structured dataset with 9,605 QA pairs from real indoor scenes, featuring multi-step, dependency-aware answers. This benchmark provides a standardized platform for researchers to systematically assess and compare the performance of VLMs, fostering consistent progress in this domain. The open-vocabulary nature of the answers and the explicit precondition annotations push beyond the limitations of existing VQA, logical reasoning, and embodied AI benchmarks.
- **Reveals Significant VLM Limitations:** The extensive evaluation of 41 diverse VLMs, including leading proprietary models, empirically demonstrates that current models struggle considerably with spatial logical reasoning, particularly in complex multi-step tasks and in accurately inferring preconditions. The substantial performance gap between VLMs and human-level performance, especially concerning causal reasoning (lower F1 for preconditions), provides concrete evidence of areas requiring focused research.
- **Proposes an Effective Mitigation Strategy:** The Recursive Scene Graph Assisted Reasoning (RSGAR) method offers a constructive approach to enhance VLM performance on complex spatial logical tasks. By leveraging specialized visual foundation models (Depth Anything V2, SAM) to incrementally construct task-relevant scene graphs, RSGAR demonstrates an ability to guide VLMs towards more accurate multi-step reasoning. This method provides a potential pathway for improving the practical applicability of VLMs in scenarios demanding intricate visual-semantic understanding.
- **Informs Future Research and Development:** The findings from the VLM evaluations and ablation studies offer insights for guiding future research directions. The identified weaknesses in causal reasoning and handling longer reasoning chains suggest the need for architectural improvements, novel training strategies, and better integration of structured knowledge representation within VLMs. The success of RSGAR points towards the utility of explicit scene graph representations for grounding VLM reasoning.
- **Enhances Real-World VLM Deployment:** Improving spatial logical reasoning is not only foundational for advancing embodied AI (providing a cognitive basis for EQA) but also critical for the reliable and safe deployment of VLMs in diverse real-world applications, ranging from robotic manipulation to intelligent assistance systems. The benchmark and proposed method contribute to developing more robust and capable AI agents.
- **Promotes Open Science:** By making the code and dataset publicly available, the authors encourage community engagement, replication of results, and further exploration and innovation in the field of spatial logical reasoning for VLMs.
