# Self-Adaptive Learning and Model Predictive Control for Tracking Unknown Dynamics with No Regret

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework from the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, integrates self-adaptive online learning and model predictive control to track targets exhibiting unknown and rapidly switching dynamics. It continuously learns multiple prediction models and adaptively selects the most suitable one, achieving near-optimal tracking performance with theoretical "no regret" guarantees in diverse simulations and hardware experiments.

## Problem

- Mobile robots face challenges in accurately tracking objects with unknown, non-stationary, or adversarial dynamics in complex environments.
- Traditional robust control is often conservative, while offline learning lacks real-time adaptability to novel or out-of-distribution dynamics.
- Existing online learning methods typically require scenario-specific tuning and struggle to self-adapt to rapidly switching target behaviors.

## Method

- A three-module architecture integrates ==Isolated (self-supervised) Online Learning (ISO)==, ==Adaptive Selection (AS)==, and ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)==.
- The **ISO** module continuously trains an ensemble of multiple target predictors from scratch using ==Random Fourier Features== and ==online gradient descent== for multi-step prediction.
- The **AS** module uses a ==bandit learning algorithm (Hedge)== to adaptively select the best-performing predictor based on real-time prediction accuracy, which is then fed to the MPC for anticipatory control.

## Results

- The method achieved an expected dynamic regret of **O(T^3/4)**, providing finite-time near-optimality guarantees against an optimal clairvoyant controller.
- Physics-based simulations demonstrated consistently lower tracking error and higher prediction accuracy compared to baseline algorithms across various non-switching target trajectories.
- Hardware experiments with Crazyflie quadrotors successfully validated the framework's real-time adaptability and robustness in tracking targets with online-switching and complex hybrid dynamics, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining self-supervised online learning of multiple predictors with an adaptive selection strategy allows robots to track diverse and switching target dynamics without prior training.
> - The use of Random Fourier Features for online identification provides computational efficiency and uniform approximation error guarantees, enabling one-shot learning from observed data.
> - Integrating a dynamically selected predictor into a Model Predictive Control framework facilitates anticipatory control, crucial for robust tracking of unknown future target behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Mobile robots operate in increasingly complex and dynamic environments, necessitating the ability to predict and track objects whose dynamics are often unknown, non-stationary, or even adversarial. This paper addresses a core challenge in robotics: achieving both accuracy and efficiency in such scenarios.

Existing approaches to prediction and tracking of unknown dynamics typically fall into three categories:
- **Robust and Adaptive Control:** These methods, while providing stability, can be conservative as they often account for worst-case scenarios rather than anticipating specific target behaviors. Adaptive control reacts to observed motion but typically does not build predictive models for receding horizon planning.
- **Offline Learning:** Machine learning, particularly deep learning, has shown success in prediction and control. However, these methods usually require extensive offline data collection and training, making them computationally expensive and potentially lacking adaptability to out-of-distribution dynamics encountered in real-time, novel environments.
- **Online Learning for Control:** This category, closer to the current work, quantifies performance via regret against an optimal clairvoyant controller. While advancements have been made in online convex optimization and recursive least squares for system identification, state-of-the-art online learning methods often require careful tuning tied to fixed target behaviors and struggle with self-adaptation to rapidly switching dynamics. For instance, prior work by Zhou and Tzoumas (2024) introduced RFF-based online least-squares for system identification but lacked mechanisms for adapting to switching dynamics. Online deep learning, while capable, often demands large datasets and may lack guarantees.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a method that simultaneously learns multiple predictors from scratch (one-shot, self-supervised) and adaptively selects the most suitable one online. This addresses the limitations of fixed-parameter online learning and the data-intensive nature of offline and online deep learning. By integrating model predictive control with a mixture-of-experts approach rooted in bandit learning, the paper aims to provide robust adaptation to non-stationary dynamics while offering theoretical performance guarantees in terms of dynamic regret. Unlike meta-learning and multi-task learning, which often require offline pre-training, this method focuses on rapid online adaptation from deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of mobile robots in dynamic environments for complex tasks such as target tracking, traffic control, and dynamic environment mapping. These tasks inherently involve interacting with objects, humans, or other robots whose dynamics are *a priori* unknown and may exhibit varied behaviors—structured, random, adversarial, or combinations thereof. Current control and learning paradigms face significant limitations in these scenarios: robust control is often too conservative, offline learning lacks real-time adaptability to novel dynamics, and existing online learning methods typically require scenario-specific tuning or fail to self-adapt to non-stationary (switching) target behaviors.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Self-Adaptive Online Learning for Control Method:** The primary objective is to design an algorithm that enables a robot to track unknown target dynamics that can exhibit switching behaviors (e.g., from structured to random to adversarial motion) without requiring prior training data or extensive manual tuning.
2. **Enable Simultaneous Multi-Predictor Learning and Adaptive Selection:** The method should learn multiple prediction models concurrently, leveraging self-supervised, one-shot, and computationally efficient online learning. Concurrently, it must adaptively select the most appropriate predictor in real-time based on observed target behavior. This aims to overcome the "one-size-fits-all" limitation of single-model learning approaches.
3. **Integrate Learning with Model Predictive Control (MPC):** The learned predictive models of target dynamics must be seamlessly integrated into an MPC framework to allow for control planning over a look-ahead horizon, enabling anticipatory rather than purely reactive control.
4. **Provide Finite-Time Near-Optimality Guarantees (No-Regret):** A crucial objective is to mathematically prove that the proposed method achieves finite-time near-optimality in expectation. This performance metric, characterized as "no regret," implies that the method's cumulative cost asymptotically approaches that of an optimal non-causal controller that possesses *a priori* knowledge of the target dynamics.
5. **Characterize Performance Degradation:** The theoretical framework should also quantify how performance degrades gracefully in the presence of learning errors in target dynamics and/or when the target dynamics frequently switch. This aims to provide insight into the method's robustness under realistic conditions.
6. **Empirical Validation:** The algorithm's efficacy must be demonstrated through rigorous empirical validation in both physics-based simulations and hardware experiments (using Crazyflie quadrotors) across a wide range of target trajectories, including deterministic, random, and adversarial, as well as scenarios with online-switching dynamics. Performance is to be benchmarked against diverse state-of-the-art online learning algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named "Self-Adaptive Learning and Model Predictive Control," is structured around three interconnected modules: Isolated (self-supervised) Online Learning of target dynamics (ISO), Adaptive Selection of predictors (AS), and Model Predictive Control (MPC).

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

At each time step:
1. **ISO Module:** Continuously trains an ensemble of multiple independent target predictors from scratch, each configured with potentially different motion models and learning parameters.
2. **AS Module:** Evaluates the real-time performance of all trained predictors and adaptively updates a probabilistic policy to select the most suitable predictor for the current target behavior.
3. **MPC Module:** Utilizes the predictor selected by the AS module to forecast the unknown target dynamics over a future horizon and computes the optimal control input for the robot.

#### 3.2. Isolated (Self-Supervised) Online Learning (ISO)

The ISO module is responsible for the online identification of the unknown target dynamics.
- **Multiple Motion Models for Multi-step Prediction:** Instead of using a single model recursively, which can lead to error accumulation, ISO maintains *N* distinct learners for each predictor *p* (denoted as $\hat{h}_j^p$), where *N* is the MPC horizon and *j* represents the prediction step ahead. Each $\hat{h}_j^p$ directly predicts the target state at *t+j+1* based on a history of observed target states up to *y_t*.
- **Random Fourier Features (RFF) Parametrization:** Each learner $\hat{h}_j^p$ approximates the unknown target dynamics using a finite number of RFFs. RFFs provide a computationally efficient way to approximate functions in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) and offer uniform approximation error guarantees. The function $\hat{h}(\cdot; \alpha)$ is represented as a linear combination of RFFs weighted by parameters $\alpha_i$.
- **Online Learning of RFF Parameters:** The parameters ($\alpha_j$) for each learner $\hat{h}_j^p$ are updated online using an efficient online gradient descent (OGD) subroutine, adapted from prior work (Zhou and Tzoumas 2024). This update minimizes the approximation error between observed target states and the model's predictions. The learning is self-supervised, leveraging a sliding window of past observed target states (e.g., $y_{t-\tau:t}$) to formulate the feature vector for prediction. Different predictors within the ISO ensemble may have varying hyperparameters such as learning rates ($\eta$), standard deviations of the Gaussian kernel ($\sigma$), and memory horizons ($\tau$).

#### 3.3. Adaptive Selection (AS)

The AS module orchestrates the selection of the best-performing predictor from the ISO ensemble.
- **Predictor Performance Evaluation:** At each time step, the performance of each predictor *p* is evaluated by comparing its multi-step predictions against observed ground-truth target states over a sliding memory window. The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of these predictions is used as a performance metric (or its inverse as a reward).
- **Adaptive Selection Policy (Hedge Algorithm):** The AS module employs the Hedge algorithm, a bandit learning algorithm, to maintain a probabilistic selection policy ($\Delta_t$) over the *P* predictors. This policy is updated based on the discounted cumulative prediction accuracy ($S_t^p = \gamma S_{t-1}^p + r_t^p$, where $\gamma$ is a forgetting factor). The forgetting factor allows the algorithm to prioritize recent performance, enabling adaptation to changing target behaviors. The selection policy for the next time step is then computed using a softmax function ($\Delta_t^p = \exp(\lambda S_t^p) / \sum_p \exp(\lambda S_t^p)$), where $\lambda$ is a learning rate. A predictor is then sampled from this distribution for use by the MPC.

#### 3.4. Model Predictive Control (MPC)

The MPC module utilizes the dynamically selected predictor to plan robot actions.
- **Prediction Horizon:** The MPC optimizes control inputs over a look-ahead horizon *N*.
- **Cost Function:** The objective is to minimize a cost function that typically includes tracking error (distance between robot and target) and control effort.
- **State Propagation:** The robot's known dynamics are used to propagate its state, while the selected $\hat{h}_j$ functions (from ISO) are used to predict the target's future states. This allows the MPC to compute control inputs that anticipate target motion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical guarantees and empirical validation through simulations and hardware experiments.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Guarantees

- **No-Regret (Theorem 1):** Under assumptions of bounded robot and target states, Lipschitz continuity of the value function, a perturbation bound on the MPC policy, and negligible approximation error in the best predictor with non-switching dynamics, the proposed method achieves an expected dynamic regret of $O(T^{3/4})$. This implies a vanishing average regret ($O(T^{-1/4})$) over time, meaning the method asymptotically matches the performance of an optimal non-causal controller.
- **Regret with Approximation Errors (Corollary 1):** In the presence of non-zero but non-switching approximation errors ($\epsilon_t$), the regret gracefully degrades. The expected regret is bounded by $O(T^{3/4} + \sqrt{T \sum_{t=1}^T \|\epsilon_t\|^2})$, indicating that performance remains close to optimal when learning errors are small.
- **Regret under Switching Dynamics (Corollary 2):** When target dynamics switch ($\kappa$ times), the expected regret scales with the number of switches as $O(\kappa^{1/4} T^{3/4} + \sqrt{T \sum_{t=1}^T \|\epsilon_t\|^2}) + \tilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa T})$. This demonstrates robustness, as the average regret scales sublinearly with the number of switches, even in challenging non-stationary or adversarial scenarios.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

The method was validated in physics-based Gazebo simulations across five non-switching target scenarios: lemniscate, sinusoidal, star, random walk, and randomized adversarial trajectories.
- **Superior or Comparable Tracking:** The proposed method consistently achieved lower tracking error and prediction accuracy compared to baseline algorithms (PLOT, RFF-MPC, MODL) across the diverse scenarios.
- **Robustness to Diverse Dynamics:** Unlike baselines that required re-tuning of hyperparameters (e.g., forgetting factor $\gamma$ for PLOT, kernel standard deviation $\sigma$ for RFF-MPC) to perform optimally on specific trajectory types, the proposed method performed well with a fixed set of hyperparameters, demonstrating its adaptability.
- **Prediction Accuracy:** The method exhibited consistently lower prediction error over the MPC horizon compared to baselines, which directly correlated with better tracking performance. RFF-MPC, while sometimes achieving comparable tracking, displayed higher jitter in predictions.

#### 4.3. Hardware Experiments

Experiments using Crazyflie quadrotors demonstrated the algorithm's performance in real-world scenarios involving online-switching target dynamics:
- **Hybrid Trajectories:** Tested on targets switching between (i) lemniscate, randomized adversarial, and back to lemniscate; (ii) star-shaped and Lissajous curve; and (iii) alphabetical letters "UM."
- **Real-World Adaptability:** The method maintained the lowest average tracking error throughout these complex, hybrid maneuvers. Baselines struggled significantly when target behavior switched, often performing well only during specific phases for which they were implicitly "tuned" by parameter choices.
- **Environmental Factors:** The method also coped with practical challenges such as the downwash effect from the pursuer drone on the target.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Individual Modules (Ablation Studies)

- **ISO Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** An ablation study on the ISO module showed that the "optimal" hyperparameters (e.g., kernel $\sigma$, learning rate $\eta$, memory horizon $\tau$) of a single predictor varied significantly between different target types (e.g., lemniscate vs. randomized adversarial). This underscored the necessity of the AS module for adaptive selection.
- **AS Convergence and Performance:** The AS module demonstrated its ability to adaptively assign higher probabilities to the best-performing experts as the target dynamics evolved. For some adversarial scenarios, the combined performance of the AS module (using a mixture of experts) even surpassed that of any single, individually optimized predictor.

#### 4.5. Empirical Regret Trends

- Empirical results on average regret and prediction error generally aligned with theoretical predictions: decreasing average regret for smooth, predictable trajectories (sublinear regret) and bounded or mildly increasing average regret for switching or random trajectories (graceful degradation).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of online learning for control, particularly for autonomous systems operating in uncertain and dynamic environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Autonomy and Robustness:** By enabling self-adaptive tracking of unknown, non-stationary, and even adversarial target dynamics without prior training or extensive tuning, the method substantially enhances the autonomy and robustness of mobile robots. This reduces the need for human intervention and pre-programming for varied scenarios.
- **Theoretical Foundations for Online Adaptation:** The provision of finite-time near-optimality ("no regret") guarantees, coupled with a characterization of graceful performance degradation in the presence of learning errors and switching dynamics, provides a strong theoretical basis for the method. This contributes to the trustworthiness and predictability of learning-enabled control systems, which is crucial for safety-critical applications.
- **Bridging Gaps in Control and Learning:** The work successfully integrates the strengths of online learning (adaptability, data efficiency) with the predictive capabilities of Model Predictive Control (anticipatory planning) and the ensemble approach of expert algorithms (robustness to uncertainty). This addresses known limitations of purely robust control, offline learning, and fixed-parameter online learning.
- **Real-World Feasibility and Scalability:** Validation through both high-fidelity simulations and physical hardware experiments on Crazyflie drones demonstrates its practical feasibility and computational efficiency for real-time robotic applications. The linear scaling of runtime with the number of predictors also suggests potential for scalability with parallel computation.
- **Reduced Development and Deployment Costs:** The "one-shot" and "self-supervised" nature of the learning process eliminates the costly and time-consuming data collection and training phases typical of deep learning methods, thereby lowering the barrier to deployment in novel environments.
- **Broad Applicability:** The method's ability to handle diverse and rapidly changing target behaviors has broad implications for applications such as:
 - **Dynamic Mapping:** Tracking moving landmarks in unknown environments.
 - **Traffic Control:** Predicting and adapting to the motion of other vehicles or pedestrians.
 - **Pursuit-Evasion:** Tracking agile or evasive targets.
 - **Human-Robot Interaction:** Adapting to unpredictable human movements.
 - **Logistics and Exploration:** Operating in dynamic warehouses or unknown terrains.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays the groundwork for several promising future directions, including self-adaptation of the predictor library, risk-aware predictor selection, and decentralized control for multi-robot systems, which could further extend its capabilities and impact.
