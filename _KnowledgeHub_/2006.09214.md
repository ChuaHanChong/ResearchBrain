# FCOS: A simple and strong anchor-free object detector

> [!summary] Summary
> FCOS, an anchor-free object detector developed at The University of Adelaide, redefines object detection as a per-pixel prediction task, eliminating the need for pre-defined anchor boxes. This approach simplifies the detection pipeline and achieves competitive to superior performance on benchmarks like COCO, reaching up to 47.9% AP, while showing robust results in crowded environments.

## Problem

- Traditional object detectors rely on anchor boxes, requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning for various scales and aspect ratios, which impacts performance and complicates design.
- Anchor-based methods generate a vast number of negative samples, exacerbating foreground-background class imbalance and incurring significant computational overhead for IoU calculations.
- Earlier anchor-free, Fully Convolutional Network (FCN)-based approaches struggled with handling objects of varying scales and resolving ambiguity from highly overlapping bounding boxes, limiting their use in generic object detection.

## Method

- **FCOS** reformulates object detection as a fully convolutional, ==per-pixel prediction== problem, directly predicting classification scores and bounding box distances for each spatial location on a feature map.
- It incorporates ==Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs)== for multi-level prediction, assigning objects to specific FPN levels based on their bounding box regression range to handle scale variations and mitigate ambiguity.
- A novel ==center-ness== branch is introduced to predict a score that down-weights low-quality bounding box predictions made by locations far from an object's center, improving detection quality and reducing false positives during ==Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)==.

## Results

- FCOS (ResNet-101-FPN) achieved **43.2% AP** on COCO test-dev, outperforming anchor-based RetinaNet (**39.1% AP**) and surpassing anchor-free methods like CornerNet (**40.5% AP**) while being simpler.
- With advanced backbones and techniques, FCOS reached **47.9% AP** (single model) and **50.4% AP** (test-time augmentation), demonstrating its scalability to state-of-the-art performance.
- On the CrowdHuman dataset, FCOS significantly improved the Miss Rate (MR -2) from **59.04%** to **51.34%** with the center-ness branch, confirming its effectiveness in handling highly crowded scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-performance object detection is achievable without pre-defined anchor boxes, challenging the long-standing paradigm that anchors are indispensable for robust detection.
> - Multi-level FPNs combined with a center-sampling strategy effectively resolve the ambiguity issues inherent in per-pixel object detection, making it viable for generic object recognition tasks.
> - A simple, learned 'center-ness' score provides an effective mechanism to filter out low-quality bounding box predictions from non-central regions, particularly beneficial in dense object scenes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Object detection is a foundational task in computer vision, underpinning various instance-level recognition tasks and numerous downstream applications. Historically, methods such as the sliding-window approach were employed, which exhaustively classified every possible location. The advent of deep learning, particularly with the introduction of Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) and the Faster R-CNN framework, significantly advanced the field.

Most contemporary state-of-the-art object detectors, including prominent one-stage methods like SSD, YOLOv2/v3, and RetinaNet, as well as two-stage methods like Faster R-CNN, rely on a mechanism of pre-defined "anchor boxes." These anchor boxes are a set of bounding box proposals of various scales and aspect ratios placed densely across an image. They are then classified as positive or negative samples and refined to predict the final object locations. The widespread adoption of anchor boxes in modern detectors suggests a prevailing belief in their necessity for achieving high detection performance.

However, anchor-based detectors present several limitations. Their performance is often sensitive to hyperparameters such as the sizes, aspect ratios, and number of anchor boxes, which require careful tuning and can impact AP by several percentage points on benchmarks like COCO. Fixed anchor shapes can struggle with objects exhibiting significant shape variations, particularly small objects, and hinder generalization to new datasets requiring redesigned anchors. Furthermore, the dense placement of anchors generates a large number of negative samples, exacerbating the foreground-background class imbalance during training (e.g., over 180K anchors in FPN for an 800-pixel shorter side image). Anchor-based methods also involve computationally complex operations, such as calculating Intersection Over Union (IoU) scores between anchors and ground-truth boxes for sample assignment.

In parallel, FCNs have achieved substantial success in other dense prediction tasks, including semantic segmentation, depth estimation, and keypoint detection, where predictions are made on a per-pixel basis. Object detection, due to its reliance on anchor boxes, has largely remained distinct from this per-pixel prediction paradigm. Some earlier attempts, like DenseBox, explored FCN-based object detection but often faced challenges with handling objects of varying sizes without image pyramids and particularly struggled with the ambiguity arising from highly overlapping bounding boxes, leading to their predominant use in specialized domains like face or scene text detection rather than generic object detection.

This work positions itself within this landscape by challenging the necessity of anchor boxes in generic object detection. It aims to unify object detection with other dense prediction tasks by reformulating it as a per-pixel prediction problem using FCNs, building upon recent developments in multi-level feature processing (FPNs) and novel strategies to mitigate the inherent challenges of anchor-free, per-pixel approaches. It directly compares its performance against established anchor-based detectors and other contemporary anchor-free methods (e.g., YOLOv1, CornerNet, FSAF, CenterNet) to demonstrate its efficacy and simplicity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a fully convolutional one-stage object detector (FCOS) that operates in a per-pixel prediction fashion, thereby eliminating the need for pre-defined anchor boxes. This approach aims to unify object detection with other dense prediction tasks traditionally solved by FCNs, such as semantic segmentation.

The motivation for pursuing an anchor-free design stems from several identified drawbacks of prevalent anchor-based object detectors:
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Complexity:** Anchor-based detectors require careful tuning of numerous hyperparameters, including the sizes, aspect ratios, and quantity of anchor boxes, as well as IoU thresholds for assigning positive and negative samples. These hyperparameters are sensitive to detection performance and task-specific, increasing the complexity of detector design and training. FCOS aims to remove these parameters entirely.
- **Limited Generalization and Shape Variation Handling:** Fixed anchor box shapes can struggle to accurately represent objects with diverse scales and aspect ratios, particularly small objects or those with unusual geometries. This also limits the generalization ability of detectors, often necessitating re-design for new detection tasks. An anchor-free approach, by directly regressing bounding boxes, is expected to be more flexible.
- **Foreground-Background Imbalance:** The dense placement of anchor boxes leads to an excessive number of negative samples during training, which exacerbates the class imbalance problem and can impede learning. Removing anchors is intended to alleviate this issue.
- **Computational Overhead:** Anchor boxes introduce computational overhead, such as the extensive Intersection over Union (IoU) computations required for matching anchor boxes with ground-truth boxes during training. FCOS seeks to simplify this process.
- **Addressing Ambiguity in Per-Pixel Prediction:** Prior FCN-based, anchor-free methods for object detection often encountered difficulties in generic object detection, particularly when handling highly overlapping bounding boxes. For pixels in overlapped regions, it becomes ambiguous which ground-truth box a location should regress (as depicted in Figure 1). A key motivation for FCOS is to demonstrate that this ambiguity can be effectively resolved, enabling robust generic object detection without anchors.
- **Mitigating Low-Quality Detections:** In per-pixel prediction, locations far from the center of an object may still produce bounding box predictions, which are often of lower quality. FCOS aims to introduce a mechanism to identify and suppress these low-quality detections.

By addressing these motivations, the authors intend to propose a simpler, more flexible, and equally or more accurate alternative to anchor-based object detection frameworks, encouraging a re-evaluation of the necessity of anchor boxes in the field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FCOS reformulates object detection as a per-pixel prediction problem, analogous to semantic segmentation, directly predicting bounding box coordinates and a class label for each location on a feature map.

**Core Per-Pixel Prediction:**
For each spatial location $(x, y)$ on a feature map $F_i$, FCOS directly predicts:
- An 80-dimensional vector $\mathbf{p}_{x,y}$ for classification (80 classes for COCO, trained with C binary classifiers using focal loss).
- A 4-dimensional vector $\mathbf{t}_{x,y} = (l, t, r, b)$ encoding the distances from the location $(x, y)$ to the left, top, right, and bottom sides of the bounding box. These regression targets are normalized by the stride $s$ of the feature map to prevent gradient explosion. ReLU is applied to the regression branch output to ensure positive distances.

**Positive Sample Definition:**
A location $(x, y)$ is considered a positive training sample if it falls within the *center area* of any ground-truth bounding box. The center area is defined as a sub-box within the ground-truth box, centered on the ground-truth box's center, with a size determined by a radius $r$ (set to 1.5 in experiments) relative to the feature map stride $s$. This approach, termed "center sampling," is an improvement over the preliminary version where any location within a ground-truth box was considered positive. If a location falls into the center areas of multiple ground-truth boxes (ambiguous sample), the ground-truth box with the minimal area is chosen as its regression target. For the CrowdHuman dataset, the target with minimal distance to the location is chosen instead.

**Multi-level Prediction with FPN:**
To handle objects of varying scales and mitigate the ambiguity of overlapping bounding boxes, FCOS incorporates Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) as in RetinaNet.
- **Feature Levels:** Five levels of feature maps ($P_3, P_4, P_5, P_6, P_7$) are used, with strides 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128, respectively. $P_3, P_4, P_5$ are derived from backbone CNN features ($C_3, C_4, C_5$) via top-down connections, while $P_6, P_7$ are generated by applying convolutional layers with stride 2 on $P_5, P_6$ respectively.
- **Scale Assignment:** Unlike anchor-based FPNs that assign anchors of different scales to different levels, FCOS directly assigns objects to FPN levels by constraining the range of bounding box regression. Specifically, a location $(x, y)$ at feature level $i$ is only responsible for regressing a bounding box if $\max(l^*, t^*, r^*, b^*) \le m_i$ and $\max(l^*, t^*, r^*, b^*) \le m_{i-1}$, where $m_i$ are predefined maximum distances for each level (e.g., $m_3=64, m_4=128, \dots$). This ensures that complete objects fall within the receptive field of the assigned FPN level.
- **Shared Heads:** The classification, regression, and center-ness prediction heads are shared across different FPN levels, contributing to parameter efficiency and performance. Learnable scalars are maintained for compatibility.

**Center-ness Branch:**
A dedicated single-layer branch, parallel to the regression branch, is introduced to predict a "center-ness" score for each location.
- **Definition:** The center-ness target is calculated as $\sqrt{\frac{\min(l^*, r^*)}{\max(l^*, r^*)} \times \frac{\min(t^*, b^*)}{\max(t^*, b^*)}}$. This score ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating the exact center of the bounding box and decaying towards 0 as the location moves away.
- **Purpose:** To down-weight low-quality bounding box predictions made by locations far from the object's center.
- **Inference:** During inference, the predicted center-ness score $o_{x,y}$ is multiplied with the classification score $p_{x,y}$ (specifically, $\sqrt{p_{x,y} \times o_{x,y}}$) to form the final confidence score used for Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). This allows NMS to prioritize detections from central locations.

**Loss Function:**
The total training loss combines three components:
- **Classification Loss ($L_{cls}$):** Focal Loss, similar to RetinaNet.
- **Regression Loss ($L_{reg}$):** Generalized IoU (GIoU) Loss.
- **Center-ness Loss:** Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) Loss.

The overall loss is: $L = \frac{1}{N_{pos}}\sum_{x,y} L_{cls}(\mathbf{p}_{x,y}, c^*_{x,y}) + \frac{\lambda}{N_{pos}}\sum_{x,y} \mathbf{1}_{\{c^*_{x,y}>0\}} L_{reg}(\mathbf{t}_{x,y}, \mathbf{t}^*_{x,y}) + \frac{1}{N_{pos}}\sum_{x,y} \mathbf{1}_{\{c^*_{x,y}>0\}} L_{center}(\text{centerness}_{x,y}, \text{centerness}^*_{x,y})$.

**Network Architecture and Training:**
FCOS uses standard backbone networks (e.g., ResNet-50, ResNet-101, ResNeXt) pre-trained on ImageNet. Training details generally follow RetinaNet, including SGD optimizer, learning rate schedules, weight decay, momentum, and input image resizing strategies (e.g., shorter side 800, longer side $\le 1333$ for single-scale training). Multi-scale training is employed for state-of-the-art comparisons. Group Normalization (GN) is used in the classification and regression heads.

**Inference:**
Inference is a single forward pass to obtain classification scores, bounding box regressions, and center-ness scores. Predicted bounding boxes are derived from the regression outputs, and the final confidence scores are computed by combining classification and center-ness scores. Standard NMS (with a threshold of 0.6) is then applied to filter overlapping boxes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that support the efficacy of the FCOS framework:

**Ablation Studies on COCO val2017:**
- **Comparison with Anchor-based RetinaNet:** FCOS, even without the center-ness branch, achieved 38.0% Average Precision (AP), outperforming an improved anchor-based RetinaNet (which includes universal improvements like Group Normalization, GIoU loss, and NMS threshold 0.6) by 0.4% AP (38.0% vs. 37.6% AP). With the center-ness branch, FCOS further improved to 38.9% AP.
- **FCOS vs. Single-Anchor RetinaNet:** FCOS outperformed a RetinaNet configured with a single anchor per location (38.0% vs. 35.2% AP). This indicates that FCOS is not merely a single-anchor detector but represents a distinct paradigm shift by eliminating anchor-based sample assignment (IoU between anchors and ground-truth).
- **Best Possible Recall (BPR):** FCOS with FPN achieved a BPR of 98.95%, which is comparable to anchor-based RetinaNet's BPR of 99.32%. This addresses concerns that anchor-free detectors might suffer from lower recall due to large strides or lack of anchor coverage.
- **Ambiguous Samples:** Multi-level prediction with FPN and center sampling significantly reduced the ratio of ambiguous samples (locations assigned to multiple ground-truth boxes) from 23.40% (without FPN or center sampling) to 2.66% (with both). This suggests that the problem of ambiguity in per-pixel prediction is largely mitigated.
- **Effect of Center-ness:** The center-ness branch consistently improved detection performance. It boosted AP from 38.0% to 38.9% and was observed to reduce low-quality detections far from object centers. Computing center-ness from the predicted regression vector (without a dedicated branch) was found to degrade performance, confirming the necessity of a separate, learned center-ness branch.
- **Other Design Choices:**
 - Removing Group Normalization (GN) from the heads led to a 1% AP drop.
 - Replacing GIoU loss with the original IoU loss resulted in a 0.3% AP drop.
 - Using C5 features instead of P5 for FPN construction slightly degraded performance.
 - The optimal radius `r` for defining positive sample regions was found to be 1.5.
 - The strategy of assigning objects to FPN levels based on $\max(l^*, t^*, r^*, b^*)$ achieved the best performance, outperforming other FPN assignment strategies (e.g., those based on object area or original FPN ROI assignment).

**Comparison with State-of-the-art Detectors on COCO test-dev:**
- **Outperformance of Anchor-based Detectors:** With a ResNet-101-FPN backbone, FCOS achieved 43.2% AP, significantly outperforming the original RetinaNet (39.1% AP), SSD513 (31.2% AP), YOLOv3 (33.0% AP), and even the two-stage Faster R-CNN (36.2% AP).
- **Competitive with Anchor-free Detectors:** FCOS surpassed previous anchor-free detectors like CornerNet (40.5% AP) and CenterNet (44.9% AP) while being described as simpler, as it avoids complex post-processing steps like grouping corners with embedding vectors.
- **Advanced Configurations:** Integrating advanced backbones (ResNeXt), deformable convolutions, and BiFPN further boosted FCOS performance up to 47.9% AP (single model). Test-time augmentation pushed the best performance to 50.4% AP.

**Real-time FCOS (FCOS-RT):**
- FCOS-RT models demonstrated favorable speed-accuracy trade-offs. A ResNet-50-based FCOS-RT achieved 40.2% AP at 38 FPS on a 1080Ti GPU.
- Using a DLA-34 backbone with BiFPN, FCOS-RT reached 42.1% AP at 43 FPS.
- Compared to CenterNet (DLA-34, 37.4% AP at 52 FPS), an FCOS-RT model (DLA-34 with shared towers) achieved 39.1% AP at 52 FPS, indicating superior accuracy at comparable speeds.

**Performance on CrowdHuman Dataset (Crowded Scenarios):**
- Despite a higher ratio of ambiguous samples than COCO, FCOS without center-ness achieved 83.16% AP, comparing favorably with an improved RetinaNet (81.60% AP) in terms of AP and Jaccard Index (JI).
- The center-ness branch was particularly effective in crowded scenarios, significantly reducing the Miss Rate (MR$^{-2}$) from 59.04% to 51.34% and improving AP to 85.0% and JI to 74.97%. This indicates its role in suppressing numerous false positives in dense environments.
- Further incorporating Multi-Instance Prediction (MIP) and Set NMS, FCOS achieved 87.28% AP, 51.21% MR$^{-2}$, and 77.34% JI, demonstrating its adaptability to highly crowded scenes and showing that anchor boxes are not a prerequisite for high performance in such conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FCOS has several implications for the field of object detection and broader computer vision:

- **Re-evaluation of Anchor Box Necessity:** FCOS challenges the long-standing paradigm that pre-defined anchor boxes are indispensable for high-performance object detection. By demonstrating superior or comparable performance without anchors, it prompts the research community to reconsider the fundamental design principles of object detectors.
- **Simplification of Detector Design and Training:** The anchor-free nature of FCOS significantly reduces the complexity associated with hyperparameter tuning related to anchor scales, aspect ratios, and IoU thresholds. This simplification can make object detection models easier to design, train, and deploy, potentially accelerating research and development in the field.
- **Unification with Dense Prediction Tasks:** By framing object detection as a per-pixel prediction problem, FCOS aligns it more closely with other successful dense prediction tasks like semantic segmentation. This unification can facilitate the transfer of ideas, methodologies, and architectural advancements across different vision tasks, potentially leading to more generalized and efficient frameworks.
- **Foundation for Future Instance-level Tasks:** The simple and flexible FCOS framework has already served as a foundational architecture for various other instance-level recognition tasks, including instance segmentation (e.g., PolarMask, Mask Encoding, CenterMask, BlendMask), keypoint detection (DirectPose), text spotting (ABC-Net), and object tracking (SiamCAR). This extensibility highlights its versatility and potential to inspire further research in related domains.
- **Improved Generalization and Efficiency:** Eliminating fixed anchor shapes inherently allows FCOS to generalize more effectively to objects with diverse and unseen aspect ratios or scales without requiring extensive re-tuning. The reduction in anchor-related computation also contributes to faster training and inference times, as evidenced by the FCOS-RT models.
- **Strong Performance in Challenging Scenarios:** FCOS demonstrates competitive to superior performance across standard benchmarks like COCO, and importantly, shows robust performance in challenging, highly crowded scenarios (e.g., CrowdHuman dataset). The "center-ness" branch is particularly impactful in these dense environments by effectively suppressing low-quality predictions.
- **Catalyst for Anchor-free Research:** Following its initial publication, FCOS has inspired numerous follow-up works focusing on enhancing its features, loss functions, and sample assignment strategies (e.g., BorderDet, Generalized Focal Loss, AutoAssign, ATSS). This indicates its significant influence as a benchmark for subsequent anchor-free object detection research.
