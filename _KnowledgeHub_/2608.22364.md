# WAM-OPD: On-Policy Distillation for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> WAM-OPD is a post-training recipe that applies on-policy distillation to a video-first world action model. The accelerated student rolls out in the environment, a frozen slower teacher labels those student histories with coherent video and action targets, and the student action branch trains under its own stop-gradient video plan. Rank-8 JointLoRA adapters plus pseudo-Huber and flow-matching losses lift held-out success from 0.0% to 58.3% on one task.

## Problem

- Few-step distillation of world action models compresses inference but can silently drop task capabilities the slower teacher retained, leaving gaps offline distillation data never resolved.
- Offline imitation and supervised fine-tuning optimize a fixed distribution, while deployment histories come from the student's own closed-loop decisions, producing classical covariate shift.
- Online RL restores student-controlled interaction but relies on sparse binary success rewards, making credit assignment over long robot trajectories expensive.

## Method

- **WAM-OPD**'s ==deployment-consistent distillation==: the released student collects environment histories and, during training, generates its own video plan first, then predicts actions from a ==stop-gradient== copy of that plan.
- Joint objective combining ==pseudo-Huber endpoint regression== on video and action targets from a frozen teacher, plus an action ==flow-matching== regularizer at the high-noise boundary, weighted 1/1/0.2.
- Parameter-efficient update via rank-8 ==JointLoRA== adapters inserted across all 30 shared Transformer blocks, freezing released weights so both modalities can reshape the shared representation.

## Results

- On RoboTwin 2.0 HANDOVER MIC, exact-paired held-out success rises from **0.0%** to **58.3%**, a **+58.3** percentage-point gain over the released accelerated checkpoint.
- On PUT OBJECT CABINET, success improves from **16.7%** to **33.3%** (**+16.7 pp**), showing the same recipe helps twice but with markedly task-dependent effect size.
- Gains come from a small budget: **eight** student trajectories, **160** teacher-labeled contexts, **120** optimizer steps, **three** epochs, evaluated on **six** scene seeds under **two** fixed noise banks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Where supervision is evaluated matters more than what reward is used: dense teacher labels on learner-visited states can substitute for sparse-reward RL in post-training.
> - When a policy conditions actions on its own generated plan, training must reproduce that conditional path, or the deployed interface silently mismatches the training interface.
> - Paired-seed evaluation on two tasks with divergent effect sizes proves a mechanism, not generalization; unmeasured cross-modality gradient interference remains a real risk.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the evolving field of robot learning, specifically focusing on the post-training refinement of complex robot policies known as World Action Models (WAMs). The broader landscape can be characterized by several interconnected trends:

**Generalist Robot Policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models:** Contemporary robot policies are increasingly designed as general-purpose models, moving beyond task-specific controllers. Seminal works like RT-1 and RT-2 demonstrated the efficacy of Transformer policies trained on large-scale real-robot experience and integrated with internet-scale vision-language pretraining. Initiatives such as Open X-Embodiment, Octo, and OpenVLA have further advanced cross-embodiment learning and provided open-source generalist policy initializations. Alongside token-based VLAs, generative action decoders, including Diffusion Policy and $\pi_0$ (which use diffusion or flow matching), offer alternatives to direct regression for modeling multimodal action distributions. These developments have established VLAs as a foundational substrate for various downstream robot adaptation tasks.

**Predictive Robot Models and World Action Models (WAMs):** While many VLAs implicitly handle future physical evolution, a distinct and growing family of predictive robot models, termed World Action Models (WAMs) in this paper, makes this structure explicit. WAMs couple visual future prediction with robot action generation. Examples include RoboDreamer, which uses generated video for compositional planning, Prediction with Action, which learns visual prediction and control jointly, and more recent unified video-action models. LingBot-VA is highlighted as a specific autoregressive video-action world model that predicts future video latents to condition action generation. This causal, video-first factorization provides an inductive bias where visual prediction signifies intended state change, and inverse dynamics grounds this prediction in control. The current work specifically builds upon the LingBot-VA/Flash-WAM pipeline, which utilizes this explicit video-first prediction.

**Deployment Bottleneck and Acceleration of Generative Models:** Generative models, particularly those based on diffusion or flow matching, typically require multiple network evaluations for inference, creating a deployment bottleneck. Methods like progressive distillation, consistency models, and latent consistency models aim to reduce this computational cost by learning few-step or one-step maps that preserve the performance of a more complex, multi-step generative process. Flash-WAM adapts consistency distillation specifically for LingBot-VA, accelerating its inference to few-step operations without altering the base model architecture. This offline, denoising-step distillation focuses on preserving capabilities on the original training data.

**Post-Training Adaptation and Covariate Shift:** A critical challenge arises when an accelerated student model is deployed. It may not uniformly preserve all capabilities of its slower teacher, and downstream tasks can expose performance gaps not resolved by offline distillation. Furthermore, deployed policies generate their own closed-loop histories, which can deviate from the fixed data distribution used during offline training—a phenomenon known as covariate shift. This necessitates online or interactive post-training methods.

**Online Reinforcement Learning (RL) vs. On-Policy Distillation (OPD):** Online RL (e.g., SimpleVLA-RL, WAM-RL) offers a direct approach to improve task success by optimizing policies within the environment. However, robot task success often provides sparse rewards, making credit assignment over long trajectories computationally costly. On-policy distillation (OPD) presents a complementary post-training interface. Instead of sparse rewards, OPD allows the student policy to determine the state occupancy distribution, and a stronger, frozen teacher provides dense supervision on the states the student actually visits. Prior work on OPD includes Generalized Knowledge Distillation for autoregressive models, VLA-OPD for robot action tokens, and DiffusionOPD/Flow-OPD for continuous generative processes.

**WAM-Specific Challenges for OPD:** The current work identifies specific technical differences when applying OPD to an accelerated WAM compared to action-only policies. A video-first WAM policy factorizes as `p(z_t, a_t | h_t) = p(z_t | h_t) p(a_t | h_t, z_t)`, where `z_t` is the generated video plan and `a_t` is the action chunk. This introduces two coupled distribution shifts:
1. **Environment-level:** Supervision must cover student-induced histories (`h_t ~ d_π_S`).
2. **Model-level:** The deployed action branch is conditioned on the student's *own* video plan (`z_S`), not the teacher's plan (`z_T`). Training actions solely under `z_T` would create a conditional-interface mismatch.
Furthermore, video and action paths in WAMs often share Transformer blocks, meaning updates to one modality can impact the other's representation. WAM-OPD addresses these unique challenges by designing a deployment-consistent distillation objective.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical limitations encountered when deploying accelerated World Action Models (WAMs). While offline distillation methods (like Flash-WAM) can significantly reduce inference costs, they do not inherently guarantee the preservation of all task capabilities or address performance degradation when the student policy encounters states outside its original training distribution.

The **key objectives** of this work are:

1. **To investigate the feasibility of On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for repairing accelerated WAM students:** The central question is whether dense teacher supervision on student-induced histories can effectively restore task capabilities without requiring the complexities and costs associated with sparse-reward reinforcement learning.
2. **To develop a deployment-consistent post-training recipe for video-first WAMs:** The proposed method, WAM-OPD, aims to align the distillation process with the operational logic of the deployed student WAM. This involves ensuring that the student's action branch is trained under its *own* generated video plan, mirroring how it functions during real-world deployment.
3. **To explicitly address the conditional-interface mismatch inherent in video-first WAM OPD:** Recognize that a WAM's actions are conditioned on its internal video plan. If the teacher's action target is based on the teacher's video plan, and the student's action branch operates on its own plan, a discrepancy exists. The objective is to design a loss function and training procedure that mitigates this mismatch.
4. **To design a joint video-action loss and a parameter-efficient update mechanism:** Given that video and action modalities often share computational blocks within WAM architectures, the objective is to create a combined objective that supervises both modalities and an update strategy (e.g., LoRA) that allows for efficient, targeted modifications to the shared backbone.
5. **To provide an initial capability proof on specific robotic tasks:** The research aims to demonstrate, through preliminary experimental studies on a limited set of tasks, that the WAM-OPD framework can effectively improve held-out task success for an accelerated WAM student, thereby establishing its potential.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between efficient offline model acceleration and robust online performance for WAMs, leveraging the strengths of dense teacher supervision within an on-policy framework tailored to the unique architectural and deployment characteristics of video-first predictive robot models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The WAM-OPD method introduces a deployment-consistent post-training recipe specifically for video-first World Action Models (WAMs), addressing the challenges of covariate shift and conditional interface mismatch during distillation.

**Core Principle of WAM-OPD:**
The fundamental idea is to allow the accelerated student WAM to interact with the environment, thereby generating its own history distribution. A frozen, stronger teacher model then provides dense, coherent supervision (both video plans and action targets) for these student-induced histories. This setup ensures that the student receives learning signals on the states it is likely to encounter during actual deployment.

**Deployment-Consistent Student Training:**
The WAM-OPD approach explicitly models the student's inference graph during training. For each history `h_S` collected from the released Flash-WAM student:
1. The trainable student first generates its own future video-plan latent `z_S` using its video prediction function `f_vid_θ(h_S)`.
2. Subsequently, the student predicts an action chunk `a_S` by conditioning its action generation function `f_act_θ` on both the history `h_S` and its *own* generated video plan `z_S`. A crucial aspect here is the application of a stop-gradient (`sg`) operation to `z_S` before it conditions `a_S`: `a_S = f_act_θ(h_S, sg(z_S))`. This prevents gradients from the action loss from directly flowing back and altering the video generation process itself, maintaining the deployment-time causal dependency. However, it does not prevent both video and action losses from updating shared backbone parameters.
3. A frozen, slower LingBot-VA teacher is queried with the same student-generated history `h_S` to produce a coherent video target `z_T` and action target `a_T`. In the current implementation, the teacher's action target `a_T` is generated using its *own* video plan `z_T`. This implies that the video loss plays a dual role: it directly supervises the student's visual prediction and implicitly helps reduce the discrepancy between the teacher's plan (`z_T`) used for generating `a_T` and the student's plan (`z_S`) used for `a_S`, thereby mitigating the conditional gap.

**Joint Objective Function:**
The training objective combines three loss components to supervise both video and action modalities robustly:
`L(θ) = λ_z ρ_δ(z_S − z_T) + λ_a ρ_δ(a_S − a_T) + λ_FM ||v_act_θ(x_a_σ=1, 1, c) − (ϵ_a − a_T)||^2_2`
- **Video and Action Endpoint Regression:** The first two terms utilize the pseudo-Huber penalty `ρ_δ(e) = δ^2(√(1 + (e/δ)^2) − 1)` for robust regression of the student's generated video (`z_S`) towards the teacher's video target (`z_T`), and similarly for the student's action (`a_S`) towards the teacher's action target (`a_T`). This focuses on aligning the final outputs.
- **Action Flow-Matching Auxiliary Loss:** The third term is an L2 loss for the action flow-matching vector field `v_act_θ` at the high-noise boundary (`σ=1`). This term helps retain a continuous flow learning signal for the action generation process, which is critical for flow-based generative models.
- **Loss Weights:** The pilot study sets the weights `(λ_z, λ_a, λ_FM)` to `(1, 1, 0.2)`, indicating balanced importance for direct video and action alignment, with a smaller contribution from the flow-matching auxiliary.

**Parameter-Efficient Update Mechanism:**
To enable efficient adaptation without retraining the entire large WAM, the method employs rank-8 JointLoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) adapters. These adapters are strategically inserted across all 30 shared Transformer blocks (blocks 0–29) within the WAM's architecture. The original, released weights of the Flash-WAM model are frozen, and only the lightweight LoRA parameters are updated during WAM-OPD training. This allows both video and action modalities to collaboratively modify the shared feature representations within the backbone, aligning the trainable scope with the shared computation, while keeping the number of trainable parameters minimal.

**Training Protocol (Pilot Study):**
The current proof-of-concept implementation uses a fixed package of trajectories for training:
- **Data Collection:** Eight student trajectories are collected using the released Flash-WAM student, from which 160 teacher-labeled macro-step contexts are extracted. Four disjoint calibration trajectories are also used.
- **Optimization:** The AdamW optimizer is used with a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁵. Training is conducted with a batch size of 4 for three epochs, totaling 120 optimizer steps.
- **Checkpoint Selection:** Checkpoints are screened on a disjoint split of data and selected based on a pre-declared hierarchy of metrics: task success, semantic progress, and calibration loss. For both tasks, epoch 3 was selected.
- **Limitation:** It is acknowledged that using a fixed package of trajectories means the "on-policy" property progressively degrades as the student model updates. A fully iterative version of WAM-OPD would involve alternating fresh student trajectory collection, teacher labeling, and bounded optimization steps.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Initialization:** All experiments start from the released Flash-WAM one-video/one-action-step checkpoint.
- **Tasks:** Evaluation is conducted on two tasks from the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark in a "clean" setting: HANDOVER MIC and PUT OBJECT CABINET.
- **Evaluation:** Exact-paired held-out evaluation is performed. For each task, six held-out scene seeds are combined with two fixed noise banks, yielding 12 exact-paired evaluation units. Within each pair, the 'Released' (baseline) and 'WAM-OPD' (proposed method) runs share the instruction, initial simulator state, scene seed, and noise bank. The primary metric is RoboTwin's latched `eval_success`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of WAM-OPD was conducted as a preliminary capability proof on two specific tasks within the RoboTwin 2.0 simulation environment. The results are based on exact-paired held-out evaluations, comparing the performance of the released Flash-WAM student (baseline) against the same student after WAM-OPD post-training.

**Summary of Task Success Improvements:**

The following table details the percentage success rates for the two tasks:

| Task | Released Flash-WAM | WAM-OPD | Improvement (percentage points) |
| :------------------- | :----------------- | :------ | :------------------------------ |
| HANDOVER MIC | 0.0% | 58.3% | +58.3 |
| PUT OBJECT CABINET | 16.7% | 33.3% | +16.7 |

**Detailed Observations and Interpretations:**

1. **Consistent Improvement Across Tasks:** The WAM-OPD recipe resulted in improved held-out success rates for both tasks tested. This consistency suggests that the core mechanism proposed by WAM-OPD, involving deployment-consistent on-policy distillation with joint video-action supervision, can be beneficial for refining accelerated WAMs.

2. **Significant Gain on HANDOVER MIC:** On the HANDOVER MIC task, the baseline Flash-WAM achieved 0.0% success, indicating a complete failure to perform the task. After WAM-OPD, the success rate increased to 58.3%, representing a substantial improvement of 58.3 percentage points. This specific task involves coordinating two robot hands to transfer a microphone, a process that relies heavily on precise visual planning and action synchronization. The inability of the original Flash-WAM to complete this task and the subsequent recovery with WAM-OPD suggests that the distillation process effectively addressed critical visual-plan and action coordination errors.

3. **Moderate Gain on PUT OBJECT CABINET:** For the PUT OBJECT CABINET task, the baseline Flash-WAM achieved an initial success rate of 16.7%. WAM-OPD improved this to 33.3%, yielding an increase of 16.7 percentage points. While positive, this gain is less dramatic than that observed for HANDOVER MIC. This task requires placing and releasing an object into a cabinet drawer. The smaller magnitude of improvement implies that the benefits of WAM-OPD may vary depending on the specific task characteristics and the initial performance level of the accelerated student.

4. **Nature of the Results (Capability Proof):** The authors emphasize that these task-specific results serve as an "initial capability proof" rather than definitive evidence of broad or uniform generalization. The evaluation setup involved a limited number of tasks (two) and a small held-out set (six scene seeds under two noise banks per task). While the exact-paired evaluation design supports the repeatability of the gains beyond a single task, the small sample size and specific task selection limit the scope for general claims about performance across diverse environments or tasks.

In summary, the main findings indicate that WAM-OPD can effectively leverage dense teacher supervision on student-induced histories to enhance the task success of accelerated video-first WAMs, particularly demonstrating a significant recovery of capability on a task where the baseline failed completely. The varying magnitudes of improvement highlight the task-dependent nature of the gains and underscore the need for further, more extensive evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The WAM-OPD framework represents a targeted effort to address specific challenges in the deployment and refinement of accelerated World Action Models (WAMs). Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

**1. Addressing Post-Distillation Performance Gaps:** The research demonstrates that even after acceleration via offline distillation (like Flash-WAM), robot policies can exhibit significant task-specific deficiencies, particularly in closed-loop settings. WAM-OPD offers a mechanism to recover these lost capabilities, providing a crucial bridge between highly efficient offline models and robust online performance. This is particularly relevant as robot models become increasingly complex and are adapted for various downstream tasks.

**2. Novel Application of On-Policy Distillation to WAMs:** WAM-OPD extends the concept of on-policy distillation (OPD) to the unique architecture of video-first WAMs. It specifically identifies and addresses the architectural challenges, such as the action branch being conditioned on the student’s own video plan and the shared backbone for video and action processing. By proposing a deployment-consistent training graph and a joint video-action loss, the work contributes to the methodological development of OPD for multimodal generative models in robotics.

**3. Alternative to Sparse-Reward Reinforcement Learning (RL):** For many complex robot manipulation tasks, environmental rewards are sparse, making traditional online RL challenging and computationally expensive. WAM-OPD provides a complementary, dense supervision signal derived from a frozen, expert teacher. This approach leverages existing strong models to guide student learning efficiently, potentially reducing the need for extensive trial-and-error reinforcement learning in scenarios where a competent teacher policy is available.

**4. Enhanced Robustness to Covariate Shift:** By allowing the student to generate its own on-policy histories, WAM-OPD inherently addresses the problem of covariate shift. The student learns from states it actually encounters, which is crucial for building policies that are robust to variations and uncertainties in real-world environments that may not be fully represented in offline datasets.

**5. Parameter-Efficient Adaptation:** The use of JointLoRA adapters enables parameter-efficient fine-tuning. This is impactful for large-scale WAMs, as it allows for targeted updates to shared computational blocks without the prohibitive cost of retraining the entire model. This makes post-training adaptation more practical and scalable for deployed systems.

**6. Informing Future Research and Development:** The explicit identification of limitations and planned future work within the paper (e.g., broader task suites, randomized settings, ablations on joint supervision, iterative on-policy recollection) highlights WAM-OPD as a foundational contribution. It opens new avenues for research into:
 - **Generalization and Robustness:** Testing across a wider array of tasks and in domain-randomized environments will be critical for assessing its broader applicability.
 - **Mechanism Understanding:** Ablation studies on the different loss components (video supervision, action supervision, flow matching) and the impact of shared adapter updates will deepen the understanding of why WAM-OPD works.
 - **Online Learning Paradigms:** Comparing fixed-package OPD with refreshed on-policy data collection will inform strategies for maintaining the "on-policy" property over extended training periods.

While the current results are a "capability proof" based on a limited scope, they suggest that dense teacher supervision on student-induced histories, coupled with a deployment-consistent objective, is a promising direction for post-training adaptation of video-first WAMs. This could ultimately lead to more reliable and adaptable robot policies in real-world applications by refining their performance on specific tasks after initial training and acceleration.
