# UniDex-ViTac: Learning Unified Visuo-Tactile Dexterous Manipulation Policy from Human Video Data

> [!summary] Summary
> UniDex-ViTac turns annotated human hand-object videos into robot visuo-tactile demonstrations: object-specific residual RL specialists in Isaac Lab adapt DexYCB references to an arm-hand robot, and their verified rollouts, paired with simulated binary fingertip contacts, train one ACT-based generalist on point clouds, proprioception and four contact bits. It reaches 68.3% simulated success versus 55.5% point-cloud-only, and 66.4% on real seen and unseen objects without real-robot data.

## Problem

- Geometric human-object references from video guide motion but neither give robot-executable actions nor ensure a physical robot can grasp and lift under its own kinematics and contact dynamics.
- Passive video recordings carry no tactile or contact measurements, so prior video-to-robot pipelines such as ViViDex train vision-only policies without contact-conditioned observations.
- Existing visuo-tactile policies need tactile data recorded during human or robot demonstrations, while dexterous teleoperation to collect such data requires complex interfaces that bridge embodiment gaps.

## Method

- ==Bounded residual RL specialists==: one PPO policy per object in Isaac Lab adds EMA-smoothed, clipped task-space corrections (±0.1 m wrist, ±40°, ±0.05 m fingertips) to mapped DexYCB MANO references using privileged state.
- ==Verified action-contact dataset==: specialist rollouts are kept only if five perturbed re-runs all succeed, yielding 10,000 trajectories pairing final corrected wrist-and-fingertip targets with simulated binary fingertip contacts from 50 human demos.
- ==Dual-path four-bit contact interface== in an ==ACT== generalist: contact bits label four forward-kinematics fingertip points in a PointNet++ point cloud and also form a separate MLP token, conditioning 30-step action chunks.

## Results

- In simulation, PCD+Contact reaches **68.3%** macro success versus **55.5%** point-cloud-only and a **56.0%** Multi-Task RL endpoint; privileged specialists hit **94.3%**, a **26.0-point** distillation gap.
- Fusion ablation is non-additive: one-hot labels alone give **56.8%** and contact token alone **56.4%**, only **+1.3** and **+0.9** points, while both paths together give the full **+12.8**.
- On a real Franka with 16-DoF hand, without real demos or fine-tuning, contact lifts success from **60/110** to **73/110** (**66.4%**): **70.0%** vs **55.0%** seen, **62.0%** vs **54.0%** unseen.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulation can manufacture missing sensory modalities: video supplies kinematic intent, and physics rollouts synthesize the tactile channel that passive human recordings never captured.
> - Even sparse touch, four binary fingertip bits, helps grasping and survives sim-to-real when contact thresholds are matched (1.0 N in simulation, per-sensor calibration on hardware).
> - Contact fusion is non-additive: spatial fingertip anchors plus a global token jointly deliver the gain either path alone misses, so ablate fusion combinations rather than single components.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

UniDex-ViTac addresses a central problem in dexterous robot learning: how to obtain useful training data for multi-fingered manipulation without relying on extensive robot teleoperation. Human videos contain examples of grasping and object interaction at a scale that is difficult to reproduce with physical robot demonstrations. However, they do not directly provide robot-executable actions, and ordinary videos do not contain tactile information about when and where contact occurs.

Existing research has explored several partial solutions. Human-video-based methods such as DexMV, VideoDex, and ViViDex extract hand–object geometry or interaction trajectories from videos and use them to guide robot learning. These approaches reduce the need for direct robot demonstrations but still face embodiment differences between the human hand and the robotic hand. A human grasp trajectory may be geometrically plausible but infeasible for a robot because of differences in joint limits, hand morphology, contact mechanics, or arm positioning.

A second relevant research direction is specialist-to-generalist learning. In this paradigm, object-specific controllers or reinforcement-learning policies solve individual tasks, and their successful behavior is pooled to train a shared policy. This separates the difficult process of adapting demonstrations to a robot from the deployment problem of learning one policy that works across multiple objects.

A third direction involves visuo-tactile manipulation. Vision provides object shape and spatial context, whereas tactile sensing provides local information about physical contact, which can be important for stable grasping. Prior work has used detailed tactile images, force measurements, cross-modal networks, or tactile human demonstrations. UniDex-ViTac differs by using only four binary fingertip contact signals and by generating those signals in simulation during human-reference-guided robot interactions. Its contribution is therefore the integration of three components: human-video-derived interaction references, simulated robot adaptation, and a unified policy conditioned on visual, proprioceptive, and tactile observations.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Primary Objective

The paper seeks to learn one deployable robot policy for grasp-and-lift manipulation across multiple objects using human video demonstrations as the initial source of behavioral information. At deployment, the policy should operate without human motion references, object identity labels, ground-truth object poses, or privileged simulator states.

The target policy is represented as:

\[
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid P_t, p_t, c_t),
\]

where \(P_t\) is a scene point cloud, \(p_t\) is robot proprioception, and \(c_t\) is a four-element binary fingertip-contact vector.

#### 2.2. Motivation

The work is motivated by two limitations of video-only imitation learning. First, human motions cannot simply be copied by a robot because of the embodiment gap. Second, video does not directly reveal the tactile conditions required for a successful grasp. The authors therefore use human video as a behavioral reference rather than as a direct action demonstration.

The proposed strategy is to let object-specific reinforcement-learning specialists correct the mapped human trajectory in simulation. These specialists use privileged simulator information and human references during training. Their successful robot rollouts then provide action targets and robot-side contact labels. The resulting dataset is used to train a generalist policy that relies only on inputs available on the physical robot.

The motivation for including tactile information is that point clouds may describe object geometry but cannot reliably indicate whether a fingertip has established contact, whether the object is being held, or whether a grasp is failing because a finger is not engaged. The paper investigates whether even a highly compressed tactile representation—one binary bit per fingertip—can improve generalization and sim-to-real performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Human–Object Interaction References

The authors use the DexYCB dataset, which provides annotated human hand and object motion. The annotations include MANO hand parameters and object poses. From the human data, the method extracts the human wrist pose, wrist-relative fingertip positions, and object trajectory.

The human hand reference is mapped to corresponding targets for the robotic arm–hand system. Specifically, the method generates a robot wrist target and four fingertip targets expressed in the local wrist frame. The object trajectory is retained as part of the reference used by the reinforcement-learning specialists.

From the 20 objects available in DexYCB, the authors select ten objects and five demonstrations per object, resulting in 50 human demonstrations. The selected objects include cans, boxes, bottles, a mug, a power drill, and a wood block. Demonstrations with unsuitable initial placements, such as unstable or unsupported tabletop configurations, are excluded.

#### 3.2. Object-Specific Residual Reinforcement Learning

One specialist policy is trained for each object using PPO in Isaac Lab. The specialists do not replace the mapped human trajectory. Instead, they predict bounded residual corrections in task space. This allows the robot to follow the general structure of the human interaction while adapting the motion to its own kinematics and contact dynamics.

The specialist receives privileged observations, including joint torques and velocities, object velocities and pose, fingertip velocities, contact states, contact-force features, physical properties, and the human–object reference. The actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with hidden dimensions of 1024, 1024, 512, and 256 units.

At 20 Hz, the specialist predicts a residual command. An exponential moving average smooths the command, which is then accumulated over time. The accumulated correction is clipped to fixed bounds: wrist translation is limited to ±0.1 meters, wrist orientation to ±40 degrees, and wrist-relative fingertip position to ±0.05 meters. The corrected reference becomes the robot action target.

The reward combines object tracking, fingertip tracking, wrist tracking, contact activation, and motion penalties. Contact reward counts active fingertips. Penalties discourage collisions, excessive impacts, and high joint velocities. Domain randomization varies object position, vertical-axis rotation, physical parameters, and scale to broaden the simulation distribution.

Each specialist is trained with 8,192 parallel environments for 1,000 policy updates. Training all ten specialists and collecting rollouts requires approximately two days on one NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, excluding generalist training.

#### 3.3. Construction of the Simulated Demonstration Dataset

A grasp-and-lift trial is considered successful when the robot grasps the object, lifts it at least 20 centimeters, and holds it for at least three seconds. Candidate trajectories undergo five additional verification rollouts with varied initial perturbations. A trajectory is retained only if all five verification rollouts succeed.

This filtering process produces 10,000 simulated trajectories, with 1,000 trajectories per object. Each trajectory contains sensory observations and the final corrected task-space action targets generated by the specialist. Importantly, these targets no longer require the human reference during generalist execution. The simulated contact measurements are retained as part of the observation data.

#### 3.4. Unified Visuo-Tactile Generalist

The generalist is based on Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT). The visual input consists of 508 camera-derived point-cloud samples selected by farthest-point sampling, plus four forward-kinematics fingertip points. The resulting point cloud has 512 points, with each point containing three-dimensional coordinates and a three-dimensional one-hot feature indicating whether it is a camera point, a non-contacting fingertip, or a contacting fingertip.

The contact vector is also supplied separately as a four-bit token. Thus, fingertip contact is represented through two paths: spatially labeled fingertip points and a global contact token. The proprioceptive vector has 42 elements, including hand and arm joint positions, wrist pose, and world-frame fingertip positions.

The action has 21 components. It specifies the desired wrist position and rotation, using a continuous six-dimensional rotation representation, together with four wrist-relative fingertip positions. A damped least-squares inverse-kinematics solver controls the arm, while an analytical solver controls the hand.

The policy predicts 30-step action chunks, corresponding to a 1.5-second horizon at the nominal 20 Hz command rate. During training, an ACT conditional variational autoencoder infers a latent style variable from proprioception and the demonstrated action sequence. At deployment, the latent is fixed to the prior mean, and the CVAE encoder is discarded. Temporal ensembling combines overlapping action-chunk predictions.

#### 3.5. Hardware Tactile Interface and Evaluation

The physical system consists of a Franka Emika Panda arm, a 16-degree-of-freedom hand, fingertip pressure sensors, and an Intel RealSense L515 depth camera. Each finger has a 15-element tactile array. A contact bit is activated when at least one sensor exceeds a calibrated threshold based on a nominal 1-newton force.

The principal comparison is between a point-cloud-only policy and the full point-cloud-plus-contact policy. Additional comparisons include state-based ACT, Diffusion Policy, BC-Transformer, and several contact-fusion configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Specialists Versus the Unified Generalist

The object-specific specialists achieve a macro-average simulated success rate of **94.3%**. The unified generalist achieves **68.3%**. The 26.0 percentage-point difference reflects the cost of moving from object-specific policies with privileged information and human references to one policy using deployable sensory inputs.

Generalist performance varies considerably by object. It reaches 94.0% on bleach cleanser and power drill and 88.0% on mustard bottle, but only 48.0% on pudding box and mug. A multitask reinforcement-learning baseline trained directly across all ten objects reaches 56.0%, below the offline-trained generalist’s 68.3%.

#### 4.2. Effect of Contact Information

The full PCD+Contact configuration reaches **68.3%** simulated success, compared with **55.5%** for the point-cloud-only baseline. This is an improvement of 12.8 percentage points. Adding contact to a state-based ACT policy improves performance from 53.4% to 58.4%, a smaller increase of 5.0 points.

The fusion ablation indicates that both contact pathways are useful together. The spatial fingertip-label-only configuration reaches 56.8%, and the separate-token-only configuration reaches 56.4%. These are only slightly above the 55.5% point-cloud-only result. In contrast, the combined configuration reaches 68.3%. This suggests that spatial information about fingertip location and a global representation of the contact pattern provide complementary conditioning. The advantage is not uniform across all objects, however, and some individual object rankings differ between configurations.

#### 4.3. Physical-Robot Transfer

On the physical robot, the contact-augmented policy succeeds in **73 of 110 trials**, or **66.4%**, while the point-cloud-only policy succeeds in **60 of 110 trials**, or **54.5%**. The difference is 11.8 percentage points.

For six seen objects, PCD+Contact achieves 42/60 successes, or 70.0%, compared with 33/60, or 55.0%, for PCD Only. The largest gains occur for pudding box, potted meat can, and tomato soup can. For five unseen objects, the contact policy achieves 31/50 successes, or 62.0%, compared with 27/50, or 54.0%, for the baseline. Performance therefore transfers to objects not included in the training set, although the improvements are object-dependent. The contact policy performs worse than the baseline on bleach cleanser and plastic wine cup.

The physical results were obtained without real-robot demonstrations and without policy fine-tuning after training. This supports the paper’s claim that simulated contact observations can contribute to a deployable policy, but the trial counts remain limited and do not establish uniform reliability across object categories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

UniDex-ViTac provides a framework for converting human video data into robot action–contact demonstrations without requiring direct human-to-robot teleoperation. Its main methodological significance is the separation between reference-guided data generation and reference-free deployment. Human references and privileged state are used by specialists only during simulation, while the final generalist relies on point clouds, proprioception, and tactile signals that can be measured on the robot.

The results indicate that even sparse binary contact sensing can improve both simulated and real-world grasp-and-lift performance when combined with spatial fingertip labels. The approach could reduce the cost of collecting dexterous manipulation data and support policies that cover multiple objects with a single controller.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation. The tactile interface discards force magnitude and contact location. The simulated and physical sensing regions are not identical, creating a sim-to-real discrepancy. The evaluation covers one grasp-and-lift skill, ten simulated objects, and eleven physical objects. In addition, specialist data generation remains object-specific and depends on successful simulation and reference annotations. Future extensions would need to address richer tactile representations, improved sensor simulation, longer-horizon manipulation, and more complex contact-rich tasks.
