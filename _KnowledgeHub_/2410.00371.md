# AHA: A Vision-Language-Model for Detecting and Reasoning Over Failures in Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NVIDIA, the University of Washington, MIT, and other institutions developed AHA, a Vision-Language Model that detects robotic manipulation failures and provides natural language explanations for why they occurred. The model, trained on a large-scale procedurally generated dataset of failures, demonstrated superior generalization to novel tasks and real-world scenarios, improving the performance of various downstream robotic applications.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and robotic systems often lack the ability to autonomously detect and provide detailed, natural language explanations for their own failures in manipulation tasks.
- Robots operating in real-world environments frequently encounter stochasticity and unexpected events, necessitating constant human intervention when errors occur due to the absence of self-correction capabilities.
- There is a scarcity of large-scale, diverse, and well-annotated datasets specifically for robotic failure modes, making it challenging to train robust failure reasoning models.

## Method

- Developed **AHA**, an ==instruction-tuned Vision-Language Model (VLM)== designed to predict task success and generate ==free-form natural language explanations== for robotic manipulation failures.
- Created ==FailGen==, a novel, automated, and scalable ==procedural data generation pipeline== that systematically perturbs successful robot trajectories in simulation to induce and label diverse failure modes.
- Instruction-tuned **AHA** using a ==multi-modal data mix== consisting of 49K+ procedurally generated failure examples from the **AHA** dataset and 765K examples from general visual question-answering datasets to ensure both specialized and general visual understanding.

## Results

- AHA-13B consistently outperformed six state-of-the-art VLMs (including GPT-4o) across simulated and real-world test datasets, showing superior performance in both binary success detection and natural language explanation generation (e.g., **0.446** ROUGE-L on AHA dataset (Test), **0.280** ROUGE-L on RoboFail real-world dataset).
- The model successfully generalized from simulated training data to real-world robot failures and different robotic embodiments, maintaining its lead over other VLMs.
- Integrating AHA's failure feedback improved downstream robotic applications: RL reward synthesis achieved **22.34%** higher task success, Task and Motion Planning saw **36.7%** higher task success, and zero-shot robot data generation increased success by **5%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Framing failure detection as a free-form reasoning task rather than a binary classification allows VLMs to provide detailed, actionable natural language explanations for robotic errors.
> - Procedurally generating synthetic failure data (via FailGen) is effective for training VLMs for failure reasoning, with the model demonstrating generalization from simulation to real-world robotic tasks and different robot embodiments.
> - Instruction-tuning a VLM on domain-specific failure data, when combined with general VQA data, can specialize its capabilities for robotics while retaining common sense knowledge and general visual understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research positions itself at the intersection of robotic manipulation, vision-language models (VLMs), and the critical challenge of autonomous learning from failure. It builds upon and addresses limitations in several key areas of the current AI and robotics landscape:

- **Foundation Models in Robotics:** Recent years have seen a significant surge in the application of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) to robotics. These "foundation models" have demonstrated impressive capabilities in interpreting human instructions, spatial reasoning, object recognition, and multimodal problem-solving, enabling robots to tackle more open-world tasks. This work acknowledges and leverages these advancements, integrating VLMs and LLMs into various robotic applications like reward generation for reinforcement learning, task planning, and zero-shot trajectory generation.
- **Limitations of Current Foundation Models:** Despite their successes, current foundation models often suffer from "hallucinations" – generating incorrect or untrue responses. More critically for robotics, they frequently lack the ability to autonomously detect and reason about their own mistakes. This contrasts sharply with human intelligence, which fundamentally relies on learning from errors. The paper directly addresses this gap, recognizing that for robots to operate reliably in dynamic, unpredictable real-world environments, they must possess similar self-correction capabilities.
- **Failure Detection in Robotics:** Prior work in robotics has explored failure detection, particularly within human-robot interaction (HRI) and Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) communities. With the advent of LLMs/VLMs, some approaches use these models for binary success detection. However, a significant limitation of these methods is their inability to provide detailed, natural language explanations for *why* a failure occurred. AHA differentiates itself by framing failure detection not as a binary classification but as a "free-form reasoning task" that generates explanatory language. This moves beyond simple error flagging to deep causal reasoning, which is crucial for autonomous learning and remediation.
- **Data Generation for Robotics:** A perennial challenge in robotics is the scarcity of large-scale, diverse, and well-annotated datasets, especially for complex phenomena like failures. The paper tackles this by introducing "FailGen," a novel procedural data generation pipeline. This approach aligns with broader trends in synthetic data generation (e.g., MimicGen, RoboPoint) that leverage simulation to create large datasets for training robotic policies and VLMs. FailGen specifically focuses on systematically creating failure trajectories, addressing a gap in existing datasets that primarily focus on successful demonstrations.
- **Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reflection:** The concept of "learning from failure" is central to human intelligence and has been effectively applied in training foundation models through techniques like Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF). This work extends this concept towards *autonomous* failure reasoning. By integrating AHA's natural language failure feedback into downstream robotic applications, the paper contributes to closing the loop for autonomous self-improvement and reflection in robotic systems, akin to how Eureka uses LLMs for reward design through reflection.

In summary, AHA pushes the boundaries of VLM capabilities in robotics by enabling sophisticated, language-based failure reasoning, moving beyond simple detection to explain *why* errors occur. It simultaneously addresses the data bottleneck through scalable synthetic data generation and demonstrates the practical utility of this capability by significantly improving the performance of various VLM/LLM-assisted robotic systems. This work represents a crucial step towards more robust, adaptive, and truly autonomous robotic agents capable of self-diagnosis and learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations behind the AHA research can be summarized as follows:

- **Objective 1: Enable Autonomous Failure Detection and Reasoning in Robotic Manipulation:** The primary goal of AHA is to equip robotic systems with the ability to autonomously detect when a manipulation task has failed and, crucially, to understand *why* that failure occurred. This goes beyond simple binary success/failure classification, aiming for detailed, free-form natural language explanations.
- **Motivation 1: Bridging the Gap between Human and Robotic Intelligence:** Humans intuitively learn from their mistakes. Current foundation models, despite their advancements in open-world tasks, often lack this fundamental ability to recognize and correct their own errors. This limitation severely hinders their applicability in real-world robotic scenarios, where stochasticity and unpredictability are common. The research is motivated by the need to imbue robots with a similar capacity for self-reflection and learning from failure.
- **Objective 2: Develop a Generalizable Vision-Language Model for Failure Reasoning:** The aim is to create an open-source VLM (AHA) that is adaptable across various robots, camera viewpoints, tasks, and environments, in both simulated and real-world settings. This emphasizes the need for a robust and flexible solution, not one tied to specific hardware or scenarios.
- **Motivation 2: Enhancing Real-World Applicability of Robotic Systems:** Without robust failure detection and reasoning, robots cannot recover from errors, necessitating constant human intervention. This limits their practical deployment in dynamic and complex environments. By enabling robots to understand their failures, the work seeks to make them more autonomous, reliable, and applicable in real-world settings.
- **Objective 3: Create a Scalable Data Generation Pipeline for Robotic Failures:** To effectively train a VLM for such a nuanced task, a large and diverse dataset of failure trajectories is essential. The paper aims to address this data scarcity by developing "FailGen," a novel, automated, and scalable framework for procedurally generating robotic failure demonstrations from successful simulations.
- **Motivation 3: Overcoming Data Scarcity in Robotics:** Collecting real-world robotic failure data at scale is incredibly challenging, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous. Simulation offers a safe and efficient alternative, but generating diverse and realistic failure modes procedurally has been an underexplored area. FailGen directly addresses this data bottleneck, enabling the creation of the first large-scale robotic failure dataset (AHA dataset).
- **Objective 4: Demonstrate the Utility of Failure Reasoning in Downstream Robotic Applications:** Beyond just detecting failures, the research aims to show that AHA's natural language failure feedback can directly improve the performance of existing LLM/VLM-assisted robotic systems.
- **Motivation 4: Improving Robot Learning and Planning:** Many modern robotic systems rely on LLMs/VLMs for reward function generation, task planning, or sub-task verification. Providing these systems with precise, natural language explanations of failures can significantly accelerate learning, optimize planning, and enhance overall task success rates, making these applications more efficient and effective.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to advance autonomous robotics by enabling machines to engage in a fundamental aspect of intelligence: learning from their mistakes. AHA provides a crucial capability for robots to not just perform tasks but to understand their performance, diagnose errors, and contribute to their own improvement, paving the way for more intelligent and resilient robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers around the development of **AHA**, an instruction-tuned Vision-Language Model (VLM), supported by a novel data generation pipeline called **FailGen**.

#### 3.1. Failure Reasoning Formulation

Unlike previous approaches that typically treat failure detection as a binary classification (success/failure), AHA frames it as a **free-form reasoning task**. The VLM is prompted to:
1. Predict a binary success condition ("Yes" or "No") for a given sub-task based on a natural language specification and an input image.
2. If the answer is "No" (failure), generate a concise, free-form natural language explanation detailing *why* the sub-task failed.

This formulation enables deeper insights into failure modes and provides actionable feedback. Robotic manipulation tasks are defined as a series of sub-tasks, each representing primitive actions between keyframes.

**Input Formulation for VLMs:**
To provide sufficient context, the input image to the VLM is carefully constructed:
- It includes a single frame representing the robot's trajectory up to the current sub-task.
- This frame is concatenated with images from other camera viewpoints to mitigate occlusions and provide comprehensive spatial information.
- Temporal relationships are captured by concatenating keyframes up to the current sub-task in temporal order (left to right), with unused keyframe slots filled with white patches.
- A detailed task description is included in the natural language query prompt.

This structured image and language input ensures consistency across different tasks and viewpoints during both fine-tuning and evaluation.

#### 3.2. The AHA Dataset (Data Generation with FailGen)

To enable the instruction-tuning of AHA, the researchers developed **FailGen**, a scalable and automated data generation pipeline for robotic manipulation failures.

- **Failure Taxonomy:** The first step was to systematically categorize common failure modes observed in robotic manipulation. Through analysis of existing datasets (DROID, Open-X Embodiment) and policy rollouts, seven distinct failure modes were formalized:
 - **Incomplete Grasp (No_Grasp):** Gripper reaches grasp pose but fails to close.
 - **Inadequate Grip Retention (Slip):** Object grasped but slips during movement.
 - **Misaligned Keyframe (Translation):** Gripper offset in X, Y, or Z axis.
 - **Incorrect Rotation (Rotation):** Gripper orientation offset (roll, yaw, pitch).
 - **Missing Rotation (No_Rotation):** Gripper fails to achieve necessary rotation.
 - **Wrong Action Sequence (Wrong_action):** Actions performed out of order.
 - **Wrong Target Object (Wrong_object):** Robot interacts with an incorrect object.
- **Procedural Generation in Simulation:** FailGen operates as an environment wrapper around robotic simulators, specifically demonstrated with RLBench and ManiSkill. It leverages the keyframe-based nature of RLBench to dynamically induce failures:
 - It systematically perturbs successful robot trajectories by modifying keyframes, substituting objects, or reordering action sequences.
 - YAML-based configuration files, generated by sweeping through potential failure modes and parameters, dictate how FailGen perturbs keyframes to induce specific failures.
 - Simultaneously, language templates are used to generate corresponding question-answer pairs for each task and induced failure mode. For instance, `No_Grasp` is implemented by omitting gripper commands, `Slip` by timed release, `Translation` and `Rotation` by perturbing position/orientation, etc.
- **The AHA Dataset:** Using FailGen, the researchers curated the "AHA dataset (Train)," a large-scale collection of 49K+ image-query pairs across 79 diverse simulated tasks from RLBench. This dataset serves as the primary source for instruction-tuning AHA for failure reasoning.

#### 3.3. Synthetic Data for Instruction-tuning (Data Mix)

To ensure AHA retains general visual understanding while specializing in failure reasoning, a multi-modal data mix was used for co-fine-tuning:
- **The AHA dataset (Train):** 49K failure-specific image-text pairs.
- **VQA dataset:** 665K general visual question-answering conversation pairs, sourced from internet data. This helps the model retain pre-trained knowledge and general visual reasoning abilities.
- **LVIS dataset:** 100K instances with predicted bounding box centers and dimensions, contributing to object detection and spatial understanding capabilities.

This diverse data mix ensures that AHA can reason about failures across different domains, embodiments, and tasks, while also functioning as a general-purpose VLM.

#### 3.4. Instruction Fine-tuning (Model Architecture)

The instruction fine-tuning pipeline follows established practices, similar to LLaVA-v1.5:
- **Model Architecture:** AHA utilizes an architecture comprising an image encoder (for visual input), a linear projector, a language tokenizer, and a transformer-based language model (LLaMA-2-13B or LLaMA-2-7B).
- **Training Strategy:** All components are initialized with pre-trained weights. During fine-tuning, only the linear projector (a 2-layer linear projection from image tokens to the language model's embedding space) and the transformer weights of the language model are updated. The vision encoder and language tokenizer remain frozen.
- **Autoregressive Generation:** The model operates autoregressively, predicting response tokens and a special token indicating the boundary between the instruction and response.

This methodology combines a novel problem formulation for failure reasoning, a scalable data generation approach to overcome data scarcity, and a robust instruction-tuning strategy to create a powerful VLM capable of providing detailed, natural language explanations for robotic manipulation failures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate AHA's superior performance in failure detection and reasoning, its generalization capabilities, and its significant positive impact on downstream robotic applications.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Evaluation on Failure Detection and Reasoning

AHA (specifically AHA-13B) was benchmarked against six state-of-the-art VLMs, including open-source models (LLaVA-v1.5-13B, LLaVA-NeXT-34B, Qwen-VL) and proprietary models (Gemini-1.5 Flash, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-ICL with 5-shot in-context learning). Evaluation was conducted across three distinct datasets, none of which were included in AHA's fine-tuning, to rigorously test generalization:
- **AHA dataset (Test):** 11K image-question pairs from 10 *novel* RLBench tasks, assessing generalization to out-of-domain tasks within the same simulator.
- **ManiSkill-Fail:** 130 image-question pairs across 4 tasks in the ManiSkill simulator, evaluating performance in a *different simulator* and under changing viewpoints.
- **RoboFail:** A dataset featuring real-world robot failures (7 UR5 robot tasks), assessing generalization to *real-world trajectories* and *different embodiments*.

Four metrics were used to evaluate semantic similarity and detection accuracy: ROUGE-L, Cosine Similarity, LLM Fuzzy Match (using Claude-3-Sonnet for semantic evaluation), and Binary Success rate.

**Key Performance Highlights:**
- **Overall Superiority:** AHA-13B consistently outperformed all other VLMs across nearly every evaluation metric and dataset.
- **AHA dataset (Test):** AHA-13B achieved 0.446 ROUGE-L, 0.583 Cosine Similarity, 0.702 Binary Success, and 0.768 LLM Fuzzy Match. It significantly outstripped GPT-4o ICL (0.226 ROUGE-L, 0.380 Cosine Similarity, 0.611 Binary Success, 0.776 LLM Fuzzy Match), demonstrating a clear advantage in generalizing reasoning to novel tasks.
- **ManiSkill-Fail:** AHA-13B scored 0.600 ROUGE-L, 0.681 Cosine Similarity, 1.000 Binary Success, and 0.633 LLM Fuzzy Match. It again outperformed GPT-4o-ICL (0.341 ROUGE-L, 0.429 Cosine Similarity, 0.971 Binary Success, 0.630 LLM Fuzzy Match) by a substantial margin, highlighting its robustness to different simulation domains.
- **RoboFail (Real-World Generalization):** Even on real-world robot failures, AHA-13B maintained its lead (0.280 ROUGE-L, 0.471 Cosine Similarity, 0.643 Binary Success, 0.465 LLM Fuzzy Match), surpassing GPT-4o-ICL (0.236 ROUGE-L, 0.429 Cosine Similarity, 0.571 Binary Success, 0.418 LLM Fuzzy Match). This is a critical finding, demonstrating generalization from simulated training data to the complexities of the real world and different robot embodiments.
- **Comparison to Base Model:** AHA-13B (derived from LLaVA-v1.5-13B) showed a massive improvement of over 43.0% compared to its base model, underscoring the effectiveness of the fine-tuning approach and the AHA dataset.

#### 4.2. Retention of Common Sense Knowledge

Despite being fine-tuned on domain-specific robotic failure data, AHA-13B successfully retained general world knowledge. Evaluations on standard VQA benchmarks (MMBench, ScienceQA, TextVQA, POPE, VizWiz) showed that AHA-13B performed comparably to LLaVA-v1.5-13B (the base VLM fine-tuned on general VQA data), with only a minor performance drop (e.g., 65.20% vs. 67.70% on MMBench). This indicates AHA can function as a general-purpose VLM in addition to its specialized failure reasoning capabilities.

#### 4.3. Scaling Law with Data Size

An ablation study on the AHA dataset size (from 3k to 60k image-text pairs) revealed a clear scaling effect, where increasing the amount of fine-tuning data led to improved model performance. An average quadratic fit gradient of 0.0022 across metrics suggests that further scaling of the procedurally generated data could yield even better results.

#### 4.4. Downstream Robotics Tasks (Practical Impact)

The research demonstrated AHA's practical utility by integrating its failure reasoning feedback into three distinct robotic applications:
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Reward Synthesis (Eureka adaptation):** By incorporating AHA-13B's failure explanations into Eureka's LLM reflection mechanism for automated reward editing, task success rates improved significantly. AHA-13B-aided policies outperformed GPT-4o by an average of **22.34%** in task success rate across five ManiSkill RL tasks, demonstrating more intuitive and effective reward function refinement.
- **Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) (PRoC3S adaptation):** Integrating AHA-13B into PRoC3S to provide failure explanations for LLM replanning and final plan verification led to substantial improvements. AHA-13B outperformed GPT-4o by **36.7%** in task success rate across three planning tasks, showcasing its ability to refine task plans effectively.
- **Zero-Shot Robot Data Generation (Manipulate-Anything adaptation):** Replacing GPT-4V with AHA-13B in the sub-task verification module of the Manipulate-Anything framework resulted in improved reasoning accuracy and an average **5%** increase in overall task success rate across four RLBench tasks. This highlights AHA's utility in ensuring correctness during autonomous data generation.

Overall, the main findings strongly support the paper's claims: AHA is a highly effective VLM for natural language-based robotic failure detection and reasoning, exhibiting strong generalization across various domains, and providing tangible benefits to a wide range of downstream robotic applications by enabling better error recovery and policy improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of AHA represents a significant advancement in the field of robotics and artificial intelligence, with far-reaching implications and potential impacts:

- **Enabling Truly Autonomous and Resilient Robots:** The most profound significance of AHA lies in its ability to empower robots with a crucial aspect of intelligence: the capacity to detect and reason about their own failures. Unlike previous systems that might simply halt or require human intervention upon error, AHA allows robots to understand *why* they failed. This moves robotic autonomy beyond mere task execution to genuine self-diagnosis and, critically, self-correction. This capability is paramount for deploying robots in complex, unstructured, and unpredictable real-world environments where human oversight is not always feasible or desirable.
- **Accelerating Learning and Iteration in Robotics:** By providing detailed, natural language explanations of failures, AHA offers a powerful feedback mechanism. This feedback can be directly integrated into various learning and planning paradigms (as demonstrated with RL, TAMP, and zero-shot generation), enabling faster policy iteration, more effective reward shaping, and more efficient plan generation. This significantly reduces the time and effort required to develop robust robotic behaviors, making the robotic development cycle more agile and scalable.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Failure Understanding:** The research successfully demonstrates that AHA, trained solely on procedurally generated simulated data via FailGen, generalizes effectively to real-world failure datasets and different robotic embodiments. This is a critical breakthrough, as it suggests that complex failure modes, which are difficult and costly to capture in the real world, can be effectively learned in simulation. This capability can dramatically reduce the need for extensive real-world data collection, lowering barriers to entry for robotics research and development.
- **Scalable Data Generation for Complex Phenomena:** The FailGen pipeline itself is a significant contribution. It provides a systematic, automated, and scalable method for generating diverse robotic failure trajectories. This addresses a major bottleneck in robotic learning: the lack of large, high-quality datasets for training on complex, less common events like failures. FailGen’s flexibility across different simulators further amplifies its potential to create foundational datasets for various robotic tasks and failure scenarios.
- **Advancing Vision-Language Models for Embodied AI:** AHA extends the capabilities of VLMs beyond general image understanding and question answering to specialized, embodied reasoning tasks. By framing failure detection as a free-form reasoning problem and effectively fine-tuning VLMs on a tailored dataset, the work showcases the potential of VLMs to become truly intelligent agents capable of nuanced understanding of physical interactions and their outcomes.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The decision to release AHA as an "open-source" model, along with the FailGen framework and the AHA dataset, is a substantial contribution to the research community. This will enable other researchers to build upon this work, replicate results, and accelerate further innovation in failure detection, robust robotics, and general embodied AI.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** While not explicitly the primary focus, a robot that can articulate its failures in natural language can foster more intuitive and effective human-robot collaboration. Humans could receive clear explanations of problems, facilitating faster diagnosis and joint problem-solving, improving trust and efficiency in shared workspaces.

In conclusion, AHA is a landmark achievement that tackles a fundamental challenge in robotic autonomy. By enabling robots to detect, understand, and articulate their mistakes, this research paves the way for a new generation of intelligent, self-correcting, and highly resilient robotic systems that can operate effectively and adaptively in the open world. Its impact will be felt across various facets of robotics, from research and development to practical deployment and human-robot interaction.
