# What Probing Reveals about Autonomous Driving: Linking Internal Prediction Errors to Ego Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Gwangju Institute of Science and Technology and New York University researchers investigated how autonomous driving models form and use internal predictions of surrounding vehicles for ego-planning. They demonstrated that while models improve with data, their predictions are often imprecise and activated too late in critical situations, yet confirmed that correcting these internal predictions causally leads to more adaptive and safer ego-vehicle trajectories.

## Problem

- Autonomous driving policies show strong performance in nominal scenarios, but aggregate metrics can mask fundamental flaws, unreliable heuristics, or a lack of true understanding of dynamic environments.
- Current models may rely on simplistic cues or ignore critical information about other vehicles, raising questions about their generalizable prediction and planning capabilities for safety-critical situations.
- A lack of understanding regarding the causal link between internal predictions of surrounding vehicles and the subsequent adaptive planning of the ego autonomous vehicle (AV).

## Method

- Trained ==Behavior Cloning (BC)== and ==Reinforcement Learning (RL)== policies using the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, varying training data scales to observe performance and internal representation changes.
- Employed ==linear probing== to quantitatively assess the information encoded in models' internal representations about surrounding vehicles' future positions and their relevance to ego-other vehicle distance.
- Conducted ==causal interventions== by directly modifying internal surrounding-vehicle predictions to investigate their influence on ego-vehicle planning, assessing adaptiveness and recovery behaviors.

## Results

- Both BC and RL models improved closed-loop performance with increased data, but linear probing revealed limited absolute predictive accuracy (F1 Macro scores below **10%**) for surrounding vehicles' future positions.
- Removing surrounding vehicles in perturbed simulations consistently led to degraded performance (increased off-road rates, reduced goal progress), highlighting models' reliance on non-interactive vehicle presence.
- Causal interventions demonstrated that manipulating internal surrounding-vehicle predictions reliably adjusted ego planning, with ego-planning probes changing in **36/59** (BC), **37/58** (RL), and **40/64** (IL) cases, affirming a causal relationship.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Driving policies, despite scaling with data, exhibit spurious dependencies on the mere presence of surrounding vehicles, with performance degrading when irrelevant vehicles are removed.
> - Models' internal predictions of surrounding vehicles are often imprecise and activated late, with critical signals only emerging in the final 10 steps before a near-collision, indicating insufficient early anticipation.
> - A causal link exists where improved or corrected internal predictions of surrounding vehicles lead to more adaptive and safer ego-vehicle planning decisions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous driving research has experienced significant advancements, largely driven by the availability of large-scale datasets such as Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD), nuScenes, and Argoverse, alongside the development of GPU-accelerated simulation environments like GPUDrive and Waymax. These resources have facilitated the improvement of driving policies, particularly those based on Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), which are widely used for both motion prediction of surrounding agents and ego-vehicle trajectory generation.

However, the paper identifies a critical challenge within this landscape: while current driving policies may demonstrate strong performance in nominal scenarios and achieve high scores in closed-loop simulations, these aggregate metrics often obscure underlying deficiencies. Prior work has indicated that training datasets are heavily biased towards routine, low-interaction driving situations, leading models to potentially rely on simplistic heuristics or even ignore information about other vehicles. For instance, some studies suggest that models can perform well on standard benchmarks by primarily attending to the ego vehicle's state, or by forming incorrect causal models of interactions. This raises questions about whether these models truly develop generalizable prediction and planning capabilities essential for robust and safe autonomous driving, especially in safety-critical, non-nominal situations.

This research addresses this gap by moving beyond summary performance scores to investigate the internal reasoning mechanisms of driving policies. It integrates concepts from data scaling laws and interpretability in deep learning, particularly linear probing, to analyze how specific competencies—prediction of surrounding vehicle movements and adaptive planning of the ego vehicle—emerge and evolve with increasing training data. By focusing on these internal capabilities, the work aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of model quality, complementing existing evaluation methods that often fall short in revealing fundamental flaws or brittle heuristics. It extends interpretability methods to a multi-agent driving context, specifically targeting the interaction between an autonomous vehicle's internal predictions and its subsequent planning decisions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to gain a deeper understanding of the internal mechanisms and limitations of autonomous driving policies, particularly concerning their capabilities in perceiving and reasoning about surrounding vehicles and subsequently generating safe driving plans. The authors argue that strong performance in conventional closed-loop simulations can conceal flawed reasoning, unreliable heuristics, and a lack of true understanding of dynamic environments.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several core indicators that, if absent, would suggest a failure to internalize key driving skills:
- **Responsiveness to Perturbations:** An effective driving policy should exhibit sensible reactions to changes in the environment. For example, the removal of a lead vehicle should enable the ego vehicle to accelerate, while the introduction of an obstacle should prompt deceleration. Robust policies should ideally improve performance (e.g., increased driving progress, fewer collisions) when environmental interactions become simpler.
- **Reasoning about Surrounding Vehicles:** Safe driving necessitates an understanding of surrounding traffic. A competent policy should attend to relevant vehicles while discounting irrelevant ones (e.g., distant cars that do not impact the ego's trajectory). It should respond appropriately to critical events, such as a slowing lead vehicle.
- **Contingency Planning:** Robust safety requires the ability to plan for unexpected scenarios and maintain internal predictions of safety-critical trajectories. This includes adapting to abrupt cut-ins or other sudden changes in the environment.

Based on these requirements, the study narrows its focus to two concrete, probeable competencies:
1. **Prediction:** Defined as the ability to infer the future positions of surrounding vehicles.
2. **Planning:** Defined as the adaptiveness of the ego autonomous vehicle (AV)'s planning in response to changes in these internal predictions during decision-making.

The research is further motivated by the question of how these capabilities evolve with scale. By training models across various dataset sizes, the authors aim to determine whether performance gains observed from larger datasets and extended simulation training genuinely reflect stronger prediction and planning abilities, or merely indicate an improvement in behavioral heuristics that succeed in common, nominal scenarios. Ultimately, the paper seeks to establish a causal link: to ascertain if better internal predictions translate into more effective and safer ego planning, and if correcting erroneous internal predictions can lead to improved planning outcomes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology to investigate the internal workings of autonomous driving models. This involves training various models at different data scales and subjecting them to a series of analytical techniques, including linear probing, perturbed closed-loop simulations, near-collision analysis, and causal interventions.

**Models:**
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) Policy:** A transformer-based model, similar to existing architectures, trained to imitate expert demonstrations.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Policy:** A PPO-based policy, trained in a simulated environment to learn optimal driving behaviors.
- **SMART (IL):** A multi-agent trajectory prediction model, representing a state-of-the-art Imitation Learning approach, used to demonstrate the generality of the probing framework.

**Dataset:**
All models are trained using the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD), which contains over 400,000 driving scenes. Scenes with traffic lights and overpasses are filtered out, resulting in approximately 90,000 scenes. Each observation is partially observable and provides ego vehicle, up to 127 surrounding vehicle, and map information in an ego-centric view. Action labels for BC and RL are derived through inverse kinematics using either a delta dynamics model or a kinematic bicycle model. Trajectories are classified into 'Straight', 'Turn', 'Reverse', and 'Uncategorized', revealing a data imbalance heavily skewed towards 'Uncategorized' and 'Straight' scenarios.

**Experimental Setup and Analysis Techniques:**

1. **Data Scaling Laws:**
 - Models (BC and RL) are trained with increasing dataset sizes, ranging from 100 to 80,000 scenes for BC and 100 to 10,000 scenes for RL. Three different random seeds are used for each scale.
 - Evaluation is performed in the GPUDrive simulator using Waymo Open Sim Agents Challenge (WOSAC) metrics (Realism Meta, Kinematic, Interactive, Map-based, minADE) and standard closed-loop metrics (Vehicle Collision (Veh-Coll), Off-Road Rate, Goal Progress Ratio). This initial step serves to confirm expected performance improvements with scale.

2. **Linear Probing (LP) for Surrounding Vehicles Prediction:**
 - **Objective:** To quantitatively assess what information about surrounding vehicles' future positions is encoded in the internal representations of the trained models.
 - **Methodology:** A linear classifier is trained to predict future vehicle positions (1, 2, 3, and 4 seconds ahead, corresponding to 10, 20, 30, and 40 timesteps) from the intermediate activations (early and late attention layers) of the driving models. A baseline probe is also trained directly on raw input features. Future positions are discretized into an 8x8 grid within the ego vehicle's field of view.
 - **Metrics:** F1 Macro score and per-trajectory-type accuracy. The "relative probing gain" (LP score - Raw score) is used for cross-model comparison.
 - **Analysis:** Correlation between probing performance and future ego-other vehicle distance is examined to determine if the models prioritize safety-relevant agents.

3. **Randomly Removing Surrounding Vehicles (Perturbed Closed-Loop Evaluation):**
 - **Objective:** To evaluate whether policies spuriously depend on the mere presence of surrounding vehicles, even when their removal should simplify the driving task.
 - **Methodology:** A fraction of active surrounding vehicles are randomly removed from 10,000 validation scenes.
 - **Metrics:** Changes in Off-Road Rate, Vehicle Collision Rate (adjusted for remaining vehicles), and Goal Progress Ratio are observed.

4. **Near-Collision Analysis:**
 - **Objective:** To explicitly examine the timeliness and focus of surrounding-vehicle prediction in safety-critical situations.
 - **Methodology:** A "pre-collision window" (10 timesteps before a collision) is defined. The normalized difference between the predicted probability from the surrounding-vehicle linear probe within this window and the average probing signal over the full episode is computed for each spatial grid cell.
 - **Visualization:** Heatmaps are generated to visualize spatial regions where the probing signal becomes more or less pronounced closer to a collision event.

5. **Causal Intervention for Adaptiveness of Ego AV Planning:**
 - **Objective:** To causally investigate how changes in internal surrounding-vehicle predictions influence ego planning.
 - **Methodology:** A selected surrounding vehicle's earlier layer representation is directly modified by adding a "label direction" derived from the linear probe's weights for a specific future position. The effect of this perturbation on the ego vehicle's later layer planning representation is then observed.
 - **Intervention Settings:**
 - **Adaptiveness:** Perturb a surrounding vehicle's prediction to create a potential conflict with the ego's path, then assess if the ego's plan adapts to avoid this induced conflict.
 - **Recovery:** Correct an identified incorrect surrounding-vehicle prediction by substituting it with the ground-truth label, then assess if the ego's plan recovers towards a safer or more accurate trajectory.
 - **Metrics:** Qualitative analysis of ego plan changes (e.g., route changes, speed adjustments).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings regarding the internal prediction and planning capabilities of autonomous driving models:

**Data Scaling Laws:**
Both Behavior Cloning (BC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) models exhibited expected improvements in closed-loop performance metrics (e.g., Goal Progress Ratio, reduced Off-Road and Vehicle Collision rates) as the training dataset size increased. These improvements generally followed a curved power law, with performance plateauing after approximately 20,000 scenes for BC and 10,000 scenes for RL. WOSAC metrics also showed strong correlation with data scale, saturating around 10,000 scenes. This confirmed that the models successfully learned to improve driving performance on standard metrics with more data, establishing a foundation for analyzing their internal representations.

**Linear Probing for Surrounding Vehicles Prediction:**
- **Overall Predictive Power:** While models did learn to encode more information about surrounding vehicles beyond raw inputs (indicated by positive LP – Raw score differences), the absolute predictive accuracy for surrounding vehicles remained limited (F1 Macro scores generally below 10%). This suggests that while some information is learned, it may not be sufficiently precise or comprehensive.
- **Scaling Trends:** For BC, the F1 Macro score difference (LP – Raw) increased until around 10,000 scenes before plateauing or slightly decreasing. RL initially showed a higher F1 score difference than BC, but this advantage diminished at larger scales. SMART (IL) demonstrated an advantage, particularly at longer prediction horizons (e.g., 40 steps), suggesting better preservation of long-term surrounding vehicle information.
- **Prediction Horizon and Trajectory Types:** BC showed decreased F1 scores for longer prediction horizons, indicating a focus on imminent events. RL, while generally stronger, also showed weaker gains or degradation at longer horizons as dataset size increased, possibly due to its single-step input nature. For specific trajectory types, BC improved on simpler cases (Uncategorized, Straight) with scale, but struggled with complex "Turn" and "Reverse" scenarios. RL maintained a largely similar pattern across scales.
- **Relevance to Distance:** At smaller dataset sizes (100 scenes), the linear probe's advantage over the raw input showed only a weak correlation with the future ego–other vehicle distance. However, with larger datasets (10,000 and 80,000 scenes), this correlation became more pronounced and negative. This indicates that models learned to filter out clearly irrelevant, distant vehicles. However, among nearby vehicles, models still attended to both approaching and receding ones, suggesting an incomplete ability to specifically identify the *most safety-critical* agents (i.e., those moving closer to the ego vehicle).

**Randomly Removing Surrounding Vehicles:**
Perturbed simulations revealed that removing surrounding vehicles, even when this should logically simplify the driving task (single-AV setting), led to a degradation in performance for both BC and RL models, characterized by increased off-road rates and reduced goal progress. This finding suggests that the policies partially rely on the *presence* of other vehicles in a spurious manner, rather than strictly using them as interaction partners. The performance drop decreased with larger datasets, indicating that increased data partially mitigates this issue, but the dependency persisted.

**Near-Collision Analysis:**
During near-collision events, both BC and RL models demonstrated a critical limitation in timely prediction. Probing signals, reflecting the model's attention to surrounding vehicles, remained weak and diffuse at earlier horizons (40, 30, 20 steps before collision). A clear concentration of the signal near the ego vehicle emerged only in the final 10 steps before impact. While this indicated that models eventually registered the colliding vehicle, this late emphasis was insufficient for effective avoidance, suggesting a failure to anticipate dangerous interactions early enough.

**Intervention Test for Adaptiveness of Ego AV Planning:**
Causal intervention experiments showed that providing models with corrected or manipulated surrounding-vehicle predictions reliably influenced ego planning.
- **Adaptiveness:** When surrounding-vehicle predictions were perturbed to create potential conflicts, the ego's planning typically adjusted to avoid the induced collision (e.g., changing lanes, slowing down).
- **Recovery:** Correcting mistaken surrounding-vehicle predictions often led to the restoration of safer and more appropriate ego plans, steering trajectories back towards the ground truth or a collision-free path.
- **Model-Specific Tendencies:** BC models were more effective at interventions requiring route changes or slowdowns but struggled with increasing speed. RL models demonstrated a more balanced response across various intervention types. SMART (IL) also showed good adaptability, especially in adjusting speed.
- **Causal Link:** The ego-planning probe changed in a significant proportion of cases across all models (36/59 BC, 37/58 RL, 40/64 IL), indicating a causal relationship between surrounding-vehicle predictions and ego planning. However, in some instances, ego plans remained aligned with the ground truth even with imperfect surrounding-vehicle predictions, suggesting that highly precise predictions are not always a prerequisite for acceptable planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the understanding and development of autonomous driving systems by probing the internal decision-making processes of driving policies, rather than solely relying on external performance metrics. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Interpretability of Driving Models:** The study introduces and validates a framework using linear probing and targeted causal interventions, providing concrete tools to analyze what information driving models learn and how they utilize it. This moves beyond opaque "black-box" evaluations and offers a deeper, mechanistic understanding of model behavior, which is crucial for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving.
- **Revealing Hidden Weaknesses in Current Policies:** The findings highlight critical limitations of current data-driven driving policies that are masked by strong performance in nominal scenarios. Specifically, the observed spurious reliance on the mere presence of surrounding vehicles and the failure to make timely predictions of safety-critical interactions, especially during near-collision events, indicate that existing models may lack robust, generalizable understanding. This implies that current policies might be exploiting statistical correlations in biased datasets rather than learning true causal relationships.
- **Establishing a Causal Link between Prediction and Planning:** By demonstrating that correcting erroneous internal predictions of surrounding vehicles causally leads to improved ego planning and safer trajectories, the research reinforces the fundamental importance of accurate multi-agent prediction for effective autonomous navigation. This provides empirical evidence for a core tenet of autonomous driving system design.
- **Guiding Future Model Development and Training:** The results offer concrete directions for improving autonomous driving models. Future research can focus on developing architectures and training methodologies that:
 - **Prioritize Safety-Critical Agents:** Models should be incentivized to more reliably and *timely* identify and predict the behavior of truly critical surrounding vehicles, rather than broadly suppressing irrelevant agents.
 - **Promote Robustness to Perturbations:** Training data and objectives should be designed to reduce spurious dependencies on environmental context, ensuring policies can perform robustly even when conditions deviate from common training scenarios.
 - **Leverage Continuous Representations:** The identified coarseness of discretized probing suggests a need for more fine-grained, continuous-space representations for both predictions and internal states, potentially unlocking more nuanced and precise planning.
- **Advancing the Science of Autonomous Driving:** By revealing the interplay between internal prediction signals and ego planning, this work contributes to the scientific understanding of how complex behaviors emerge in deep learning models applied to dynamic, multi-agent environments. It provides a foundation for designing more intelligent and trustworthy AI agents in real-world applications.

The study's insights pave the way for developing more robust, transparent, and ultimately safer autonomous driving systems by offering diagnostic tools and clear directions for addressing the root causes of reasoning failures. Future work could build upon these findings by exploring more sophisticated model architectures, incorporating auxiliary tasks, or integrating language-based inputs to foster a richer and more context-aware understanding of surrounding vehicle behavior.
