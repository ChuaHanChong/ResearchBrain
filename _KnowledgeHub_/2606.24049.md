# SPACE: Enabling Learning from Cross-Robot Data Toward Generalist Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called SPACE (State Prediction and Adaptive Command Execution) addresses action inconsistencies in robot learning by predicting universal end-effector displacements and adaptively translating them into robot-specific commands. The method improves success rates by an average of 30% for cross-embodiment tasks and demonstrates robustness with up to 84% better success rates under hardware variations and dynamic shifts like payload changes.

## Problem

- Robot actions, defined as control commands, are intrinsically tied to specific robot dynamics and controllers, leading to inconsistent motions across different embodiments or even units of the same model.
- This action discrepancy hinders the sharing of diverse data, limiting the ability to train generalizable policies and making policies brittle when deployed on different robots.
- Many valuable demonstration datasets, such as those from kinesthetic teaching, lack explicit robot-specific control commands, making them difficult to utilize with traditional behavior cloning methods.

## Method

- Introduces the **SPACE** framework, which trains a policy to predict controller-independent Cartesian state deltas (end-effector displacements) rather than robot-specific control commands.
- Develops an ==Action Adapter==, a lightweight linear model, that translates the predicted Cartesian state delta into robot-specific control commands during deployment.
- The Action Adapter is initialized with a brief offline calibration on the target robot and continuously updated online using the Least Mean Squares (LMS) algorithm to adapt to dynamic changes.

## Results

- Achieved an average **30%** success rate improvement for cross-embodiment co-training and enabled **92%** zero-shot transfer from UR5 to FR3 robots for policies trained solely on UR5 data.
- Demonstrated robustness across different hardware units of the same robot, reducing position tracking error from **32.6** mm to **14.6** mm and maintaining **84%** success on unseen units (compared to **18%** for baseline).
- Maintained high success rates (e.g., **92%** with **5x** payload increase, 95-**100%** with varying controller gains) under significant dynamic shifts at deployment time, outperforming baselines which often failed completely.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the policy's action prediction from robot-specific dynamics through a universal end-effector motion representation enables effective data sharing and the development of generalist policies across diverse robotic systems.
> - A practical, adaptive execution mechanism that translates universal motion predictions into robot-specific control commands is crucial for maintaining robust performance under varied hardware and dynamic operational conditions.
> - Combining a small initial calibration with continuous online adaptation allows policies to successfully deploy on unseen robot hardware and adapt to real-time changes like varying payloads or controller gains without extensive fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly adopting paradigms from large-scale machine learning, particularly the use of diverse, extensive datasets to train generalizable policies. This approach is inspired by the success of foundation models in domains like vision and language, which leverage vast amounts of heterogeneous data to achieve robust performance across a wide range of tasks. In robotics, this translates to training policies on datasets collected from various environments, different robot embodiments, or multiple hardware units of the same robot model. These policies are commonly learned through behavior cloning, where a model imitates actions recorded in pre-collected demonstrations.

A significant challenge in this landscape is the inherent inconsistency of robot actions across different data sources. Actions are typically defined as control commands sent to a robot's underlying controller. However, the implementation of these controllers, and thus the resulting motion for a given command, varies substantially. This variation occurs not only across different robot embodiments (e.g., a Franka vs. a UR5) but also among individual hardware units of the same embodiment due to factors like manufacturing variability, wear-and-tear, or differences in low-level controller gains. Consequently, a control command recorded on one robot may produce a different, or even invalid, motion on another, hindering the effective sharing of data, training of generalist policies, and reliable deployment on diverse target robots. Furthermore, some demonstration datasets, such as those from kinesthetic teaching or human hand-held grippers, may not contain explicit control commands, providing only recorded trajectories.

Prior research has attempted to address aspects of cross-embodiment policy learning through methods like embodiment-specific action heads, conditioning policies on embodiment information, or learning policies in latent action spaces. However, these often require fine-tuning for target robots or assume the availability of simulations for domain randomization. Other approaches focus on learning from observations by inferring commands using inverse dynamics models or predicting future states, but these may necessitate high-stiffness gains or path optimization algorithms for execution. Work on Universal Manipulation Interfaces (UMI) and FastUMI has explored predicting Cartesian state deltas for human-to-robot transfer, but relies on specific engineering techniques and focuses primarily on human-to-robot transfer. The present work positions itself by providing a unified framework that directly addresses the fundamental issue of control command inconsistency by proposing a universal, controller-independent action representation and an adaptive execution mechanism, aiming to enhance data sharing and policy robustness across a broad spectrum of robot variations without requiring extensive fine-tuning or specialized hardware configurations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable the effective learning and deployment of generalizable robot policies by overcoming the challenge of action inconsistency across diverse robot embodiments and operating conditions. The motivation stems from several critical limitations in current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Inconsistency of Control Commands:** The fundamental problem identified is that robot actions, conventionally defined as control commands, are intrinsically tied to the specific dynamics and underlying controller of the data collection robot. This means that identical control commands can produce different physical motions on different robots.
 - **Across Different Embodiments:** A control command effective for a UR5 robot may not produce the same motion on a Franka Research 3 (FR3) robot.
 - **Across Hardware Units of the Same Embodiment:** Even within the same robot model, individual units can exhibit dynamics discrepancies due to manufacturing variability, accumulated wear, or minor differences in their low-level controllers or gain settings.
 - **Within a Single Robot During Operation:** Dynamics can shift during operation due to varying payloads, changes in control frequency, or adjustments to controller gains.

2. **Hindrance to Generalization and Data Sharing:** This action discrepancy poses a significant barrier to scaling robot learning. When a policy is trained on data from multiple robots, the control commands in one robot's trajectory become a noisy or even invalid supervision signal for others. This severely limits the ability to leverage large, heterogeneous datasets for learning generalizable policies and makes policies brittle when deployed on robots different from those used during training.

3. **Limitations with Diverse Data Sources:** Many valuable demonstration datasets, such as those generated through kinesthetic teaching, leader-follower teleoperation where commands are in joint space, or human hand-held grippers, lack explicit robot-specific control commands. Current behavior cloning methods, which typically predict these commands, cannot directly utilize such data, thereby limiting the scope of learnable behaviors.

To address these motivations, the SPACE framework sets out with the following key objectives:
- **Establish a Universal Action Representation:** Develop an action representation that is controller-independent and agnostic to robot-specific dynamics, allowing for seamless data sharing and policy training across diverse robots. This involves moving from predicting control commands to predicting the actual end-effector displacement.
- **Develop an Adaptive Execution Mechanism:** Create a method to translate the universal action representation into robot-specific control commands during deployment, dynamically adapting to the target robot's unique dynamics and operational conditions.
- **Improve Cross-Embodiment Learning Performance:** Demonstrate that the proposed framework can substantially improve success rates when policies are trained on data from multiple distinct robot embodiments.
- **Enhance Cross-Hardware Learning Robustness:** Show that the framework can effectively learn from and deploy policies across different hardware units of the same robot model, mitigating issues arising from unit-to-unit dynamics variations.
- **Ensure Robustness to Dynamics Shifts:** Validate the framework's ability to maintain performance under dynamic variations encountered during deployment, such as changes in control frequency, object payload, and controller gains.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a robust and practical solution for scaling robot learning and enabling the development of more generalist robot policies that can operate reliably across a wide range of physical systems and environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, State Prediction and Adaptive Command Execution (SPACE), is designed to address the inconsistencies of control commands across diverse robots by decoupling the policy's action prediction from robot-specific dynamics. SPACE comprises two main components: a Cartesian state delta policy and an Action Adapter.

#### 3.1. Cartesian State Delta Policy

Instead of predicting robot-specific control commands, the SPACE framework trains a policy to predict the **Cartesian state delta** (`∆p_t`), which represents the end-effector displacement between the current pose (`p_t`) and the next pose (`p_{t+1}`). Specifically, `∆p_t = p_{t+1} - p_t`. For rotational components, current and next orientations (`r_t`, `r_{t+1}`) are converted to rotation matrices (`R_t`, `R_{t+1}`), the relative rotation `∆R_t = R_{t+1}R_t^T` is computed, and then converted back to Euler angles for `∆r_t`.

This choice of action space is motivated by several factors:
- **Dynamics Agnostic:** Cartesian state delta expresses only the end-effector motion, making it independent of the specific robot's internal dynamics or the underlying controller implementation.
- **Universal Representation:** It can be derived from any robot that provides end-effector Cartesian poses, regardless of how the original demonstrations were collected (e.g., teleoperation with joint commands, kinesthetic teaching, or human hand-held grippers where control commands are absent).
- **Invariance:** It is invariant to robot kinematics and base-frame translation, further enhancing its universality for sharing data across different embodiments and hardware units.

The policy (`π_θ`) is trained via behavior cloning to maximize the likelihood of the demonstrated Cartesian state deltas, given an observation `o_t = {p_t, I_t, l_t}` (end-effector pose, camera image, and language instruction). The objective is `max_θ E_{τ~D, (o_t,∆p_t)~τ} [log π_θ(∆p_t | o_t)]`. The base policy model adopted is `π_0.5`, a vision-language-action model, fine-tuned on the relevant datasets.

#### 3.2. Action Adapter

While the Cartesian state delta policy predicts a universal motion, directly commanding this predicted displacement to a target robot is often insufficient due to imperfect command tracking inherent in real-world robot controllers. The Action Adapter component bridges this gap by translating the predicted Cartesian state delta into a robot-specific control command (`u`) that will actually realize the desired motion on the target robot.

The Action Adapter is parameterized as a lightweight linear model: `û = W_t ∆p + b_t`. Its parameters (`W_t` and `b_t`) are determined through a two-stage process:

1. **Offline Initialization (Calibration):**
 - Before deployment, a small set of calibration trajectories (`D_cal`) is collected on the target robot. This involves rolling out a random scripted policy (`π_rand`) for a short duration (e.g., 10 trajectories of 50 steps each on an FR3 robot, taking approximately 1 minute).
 - For each step in these trajectories, both the achieved Cartesian state delta (`∆p`) and the executed control command (`u`) are recorded.
 - The initial parameters (`W_0` and `b_0`) of the Action Adapter are then fitted by minimizing a least-squares objective: `min_{W_0,b_0} Σ_{(∆p,u)∈D_cal} ||W_0 ∆p + b_0 - u||^2_2`. This linear regression provides an initial mapping between desired motion and control command.

2. **Online Adaptation (During Deployment):**
 - The parameters `W_t` and `b_t` are continuously updated during policy rollout using the Least Mean Squares (LMS) algorithm. This online adaptation mechanism is crucial for handling dynamic changes within a single robot during operation (e.g., changes in robot configuration, payload variations).
 - At each rollout timestep `t`:
 - The Cartesian state delta policy outputs `∆p_target_t`.
 - The Action Adapter calculates the control command: `û_t = W_t ∆p_target_t + b_t`.
 - This `û_t` is executed on the robot.
 - The robot's actual observed motion `∆p_obs_t = p_{t+1} - p_t` is measured.
 - A prediction error `e_t = W_t ∆p_obs_t + b_t - û_t` is computed. Importantly, the error uses the observed motion `∆p_obs_t` rather than the policy's target `∆p_target_t`, as no ground-truth control command exists for the latter.
 - The adapter parameters are updated: `W_{t+1} = W_t - μ e_t (∆p_obs_t)^T` and `b_{t+1} = b_t - μ e_t`, where `μ` is the learning rate. This is equivalent to an online gradient descent step on the squared error.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted on a real Franka Research 3 (FR3) robot utilizing the DROID platform. The control command modality for the DROID controller is Cartesian delta, which is the target output of the Action Adapter. The success rate for evaluation was averaged over 50 rollouts, with the Action Adapter being reset to its initial `W_0` and `b_0` before each rollout to ensure independence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates SPACE across three primary settings, consistently comparing its performance against a baseline "control command policy" that directly predicts robot-specific control commands.

#### 4.1. Cross-Embodiment Learning

- **Transfer from UR5 to FR3 (Co-training):** In tasks like Bottle, Cup, and Doll, where policies were co-trained with 250 UR5 demonstrations and 20 FR3 demonstrations, SPACE achieved an average success rate improvement of **30%** over the control command policy. It also outperformed policies trained exclusively on FR3 data.
- **Zero-shot Transfer from UR5 to FR3 (Cloth task):** When trained solely on UR5 data, SPACE enabled zero-shot transfer to the FR3 robot with a **92% success rate**. In contrast, the control command policy resulted in a 0% success rate due to significant under-reaching. Supplementary replay tests showed that SPACE, by using Cartesian state deltas with the Action Adapter, reduced position tracking errors on the FR3 from 116-169 mm (control command replay) to 15-19 mm, and rotational errors from 7-33 degrees to 1-8 degrees. Direct execution of the Cartesian state delta policy without the Action Adapter resulted in 0% success, highlighting the adapter's necessity.
- **Transfer from Human Hand-held Gripper (UMI) to FR3 (PnP Marker task):** Co-training with 550 UMI trajectories (lacking control commands) and 10 FR3 robot trajectories, SPACE **outperformed the control command policy by 50%**. This indicates that using a Cartesian state delta action space for robot data facilitated better transfer with observation-only UMI data.

#### 4.2. Cross-Hardware Learning

- **Hardware-induced Dynamics Variation:** An experiment replaying a trajectory from one FR3 robot on a different FR3 hardware unit (Robot 2) demonstrated that control commands recorded on Robot 1 produced a position tracking error of 32.6 mm on Robot 2. When the same trajectory's Cartesian state deltas were replayed on Robot 2 using Action Adapter, the error was substantially reduced to 14.6 mm, showing SPACE's ability to adapt to new hardware dynamics.
- **Policy Transfer Across Hardware Units (PnP Box task):** A policy trained on 50 demonstrations from FR3 Robot 1 was evaluated on both Robot 1 (seen) and Robot 2 (unseen). The control command policy's success rate dropped from 98% on Robot 1 to 18% on Robot 2. SPACE, however, maintained robustness, achieving an **84% success rate** on the unseen hardware unit (compared to 96% on Robot 1).
- **Learning from Multi-Hardware Data (DROID dataset):** When training on a large-scale dataset (DROID) collected from multiple FR3 hardware units, SPACE significantly outperformed the control command policy in tasks like "Marker to Cup" and "Remove Marker from Cup," demonstrating improvements of **80% and 84%** respectively. It also surpassed policies using other control command modalities like joint velocity and joint position.

#### 4.3. Robustness Under Dynamics Shift from Training Time

- **Control Frequency:** When the PnP Box task policy (trained at 15Hz) was executed at 30Hz, the control command policy experienced a 48% drop in success rate. SPACE maintained task performance while achieving a **reduction in task completion time from 12.4s to 8.1s**, showcasing its ability to adapt to higher execution frequencies without specific tuning.
- **Payload (Object Weight):** For the PnP Box task, increasing the box weight from 90g to 530g caused the control command policy's success rate to drop to 0%. SPACE, by adapting its control commands online, achieved a **92% success rate**. Analysis of the Action Adapter's bias component (`b_t`) revealed an increase in its z-axis value after grasping the heavy box, indicating active online compensation for the added weight.
- **Controller Gains:** Varying the proportional gains (`K_p`) of the DROID controller by factors of 0.5x and 1.5x from training time significantly degraded the control command policy's success rate (from 98% to 25% and 0%, respectively). SPACE maintained robust performance, achieving **95-100% success rates** under these altered gain settings, demonstrating its capability for dynamics adaptation.

**Ablation Studies (Appendix C.2, C.3):**
Ablations of the Action Adapter showed that both the offline initialization from calibration trajectories and the continuous online updates are crucial for performance. Removing either component led to a significant drop in success rate. Additionally, using diverse random trajectories for initial calibration yielded better tracking performance compared to structured (e.g., circle or square) calibration trajectories. Action Adapter also outperformed alternative approaches like gain tuning and delta accumulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPACE framework contributes to the field of robot learning by addressing a fundamental challenge in scaling generalizable robot policies: the inherent inconsistency of robot control commands across diverse embodiments and dynamic operational conditions. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Data Sharing for Generalist Policies:** By proposing Cartesian state delta as a universal, robot-agnostic action representation, SPACE facilitates the aggregation and utilization of heterogeneous datasets. This is critical for the development of "foundation models" in robotics, allowing policies to learn from a broader range of experiences from different robot types and hardware units, leading to more generalizable and capable robot behaviors.
- **Robustness to Hardware and Environmental Variations:** The framework's ability to handle dynamics variations—whether across different robot models, between individual hardware units of the same model, or within a single robot due to changing payloads, control frequencies, or controller gains—significantly enhances the reliability and deployment feasibility of learned policies. This reduces the need for extensive manual tuning or re-training for each new robot or operational scenario.
- **Practical Recipe for Deployment:** SPACE offers a practical and computationally efficient method (via the lightweight Action Adapter) to adapt policies to target robots. The rapid offline calibration and continuous online adaptation mechanisms allow for immediate deployment and sustained performance, even in environments with unforeseen dynamic shifts. This has direct implications for industrial applications where robots operate in dynamic settings or need to be swapped out.
- **Leveraging Diverse Data Sources:** The use of Cartesian state delta naturally allows policies to be trained on datasets that lack explicit control commands, such as those generated through kinesthetic teaching or with human hand-held grippers (e.g., UMI data). This expands the scope of available training data and potentially accelerates the learning of complex manipulation skills.
- **Advancement Beyond Command Prediction:** The research demonstrates that predicting desired end-effector motion (Cartesian state delta) rather than specific control commands provides a more robust and transferable foundation for robot learning. This distinction is a key conceptual shift from many prior behavior cloning approaches.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The paper acknowledges certain limitations and outlines directions for future research:
- **Force-aware Manipulation:** The current Cartesian state delta representation may not fully capture the nuances of force application, as different forces can achieve the same displacement. Future work could explore incorporating desired force prediction alongside Cartesian state delta to enable more sophisticated force-aware manipulation tasks.
- **Control Modality Conversion:** The current Action Adapter primarily converts Cartesian state delta to Cartesian delta control commands. Further research is needed to investigate how to effectively convert Cartesian state deltas into other control modalities (e.g., joint-space commands) for robots that do not support Cartesian delta control commands directly. This would further expand the applicability of SPACE to an even wider range of robotic platforms.

Overall, SPACE represents a step toward more scalable and robust robot learning, providing a framework that enables policies to learn from and operate effectively across the diverse and dynamic realities of robotic hardware.
