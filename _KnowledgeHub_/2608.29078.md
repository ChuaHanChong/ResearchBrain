# DREAM: Deployment-Time Demonstration Generation via Real-to-Sim for Scalable Policy Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> DREAM turns a short workspace video plus a language instruction into fine-tuning data for a pretrained VLA, with no task demonstration. It builds a metric Gaussian-splat digital twin aligned to the robot, has an LLM emit symbolic goals and staged success rubrics, plans feasible trajectories with GPU-parallel TAMP, augments them MimicGen-style, and renders image-action pairs. Fine-tuned pi-0.5 reaches 93.3% real success on block pick-and-place.

## Problem

- Pretrained VLA policies underperform in new workspaces whose object poses, clutter, support surfaces, and reachability differ from pretraining conditions, requiring workspace-specific action-labeled supervision.
- Teleoperation and autonomous real-robot collection cost operator time, hardware access, scene resets, and safety monitoring for every new workspace, arrangement, or task.
- Prior generators still need task-specific seed demonstrations, real-robot interaction, reward design, or hand-authored simulation scenes, and planning-based ones ignore the visual sim-to-real gap.

## Method

- **Digital-twin reconstruction**: ==COLMAP== plus ==Gaussian splatting==, auto-aligned to the robot base by segmenting the robot from the scan and fitting a scaled SIM(3) via ==FPFH-RANSAC== and scaled ICP.
- **LLM task specification**: GPT-4o-mini only selects and instantiates pre-implemented typed predicates into goal atoms and a staged success rubric, so malformed specifications fail closed instead of mislabeling actions.
- **TAMP synthesis plus augmentation**: ==PDDL== skeleton search with 1000-particle GPU constraint optimization, ==cuRobo== motion planning, plan-derived subtask boundaries for ==MimicGen==-style retargeting, and splat replay rendering into LeRobot datasets.

## Results

- Sim-real agreement across three architectures gave **MMRV 0.000** with Pearson r of **0.98** and **0.99**; simulation was pessimistic by **7-14 percentage points** but preserved policy ranking.
- With **1000** generated demos, pi-0.5 hit **93.3%** versus **86.7%** for 100 teleoperated demos on BlockIntoBowl and **86.7%** versus **80.0%** on FruitPacking; SmolVLA stalled at **6.7%**.
- Fixed costs of **36** and **95 minutes** make generation cheaper in wall-clock past **73** and **168** demonstrations; planning success fell **100%** to **62%** on the longer task, augmentation acceptance about **33%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Constrain the language model to selecting from pre-implemented predicates: specification errors then surface as missing demonstrations rather than as silently wrong action labels.
> - Separating visual realism (splat rendering) from physical feasibility (a compact collision world) yields deployable data without watertight scene meshes.
> - Synthetic demonstrations are individually worth less than teleoperated ones but cost machine time, so value comes from scaling far past the human dataset size.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively developing Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models for language-conditioned robot manipulation. These models, trained on extensive datasets and leveraging vision-language priors, are capable of interpreting natural language instructions and executing corresponding robot actions across a variety of tasks. However, a persistent challenge in deploying these pretrained policies is their performance degradation when introduced to new or unfamiliar workspaces. Factors such as novel object instances, varying object poses, clutter, different support surfaces, and robot reachability can lead to insufficient performance. Addressing this requires additional action-labeled data specific to the target environment.

Current methods for obtaining such supervision include human teleoperation, which yields high-quality data but is costly due to the time commitment, need for physical access to the robot, repeated scene resets, and safety monitoring. Autonomous real-robot data collection can increase throughput but still requires dedicated hardware and repeated interactions with the physical environment. These approaches are often deemed unsustainable for broad robot deployment scenarios where users or developers might need to adapt policies to numerous new workspaces.

Prior research has explored various strategies to reduce the reliance on manual data collection. Some methods focus on synthesizing imitation learning datasets from a limited number of existing demonstrations, while others generate robot training data from real-world captures or use reconstructed environments for policy improvement and evaluation. Examples include techniques that adapt object-centric motion segments (MimicGen), segment demonstrations into reusable skills (SkillMimicGen), or generate demonstrations for bimanual dexterous manipulation (DexMimicGen). However, many of these approaches still necessitate task-specific seed demonstrations, direct real-robot interaction, or intricate reward design. Similarly, planning-based data generation methods often operate within manually authored simulation environments, and existing real-to-sim pipelines primarily generate behavior by replaying or retargeting seed demonstrations or through task-specific scripted primitives.

DREAM is positioned within this landscape by aiming to provide a scalable solution that generates environment-specific fine-tuning data for VLA models without requiring any task-specific human demonstrations or extensive real-robot interaction for data collection. It integrates real-world scene reconstruction with task-and-motion planning (TAMP) to automatically generate long-horizon image-action demonstrations from language-specified goals within a digital twin of the deployment workspace. This distinguishes it from methods that rely on seed demonstrations or operate solely in generic simulated environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable and efficient framework, named DREAM, for generating action-labeled training data for Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models. This framework is designed to enable VLA policies to adapt to new, previously unseen workspaces without the need for task-specific human demonstrations.

The motivation behind DREAM stems from several limitations in current robot policy deployment:

- **Costly Data Collection for Workspace Adaptation:** While VLA models demonstrate progress in language-conditioned manipulation, their performance often degrades when deployed in new physical environments that differ from their training data. Improving performance in such scenarios typically requires additional action-labeled data specific to the target workspace, object arrangements, or tasks.
- **Inefficiencies of Human Teleoperation:** Human teleoperation is a common method for collecting high-quality demonstrations. However, it is labor-intensive, demanding significant operator time, physical access to the robot, repetitive scene resets, and continuous safety monitoring. This process is not scalable for scenarios where robots need to be adapted to a multitude of diverse workspaces.
- **Limitations of Autonomous Real-Robot Data Collection:** While autonomous data collection can increase throughput compared to teleoperation, it still ties up hardware resources and involves repeated physical interactions with the deployment environment. This also presents scalability challenges for widespread deployment.
- **Gap in Existing Data Generation Methods:** Prior efforts to reduce manual data collection, such as synthesizing demonstrations from a few examples or using reconstructed environments, often still rely on task-specific seed demonstrations, direct real-robot interaction, or the explicit design of reward functions. These methods do not fully address the requirement for a system that can generate fine-tuning data from a simple workspace capture and a language instruction without prior task demonstrations.

To address these issues, DREAM aims to:
1. **Eliminate Task-Specific Human Demonstrations:** By leveraging a reconstructed digital twin and automated planning, the framework intends to generate fine-tuning data solely from a short video of the workspace and a language instruction, thereby removing the bottleneck of human demonstrations for each new task or environment.
2. **Ensure Visual and Physical Grounding:** The system aims to generate supervision that is visually grounded in the deployment workspace (reflecting its layout, appearance, and camera configurations) and physically feasible for the target robot (considering grasps, motions, and placement targets).
3. **Provide Scalable Data Generation:** The goal is to create a system where the human effort remains minimal (a single capture and instruction) while the generation of a large number of diverse image-action examples can be largely automated, consuming machine time rather than operator time.
4. **Validate the Reconstructed Environment:** A key objective is to assess the reliability of the reconstructed digital twin as a source for data generation and policy evaluation by examining the correlation between simulated and real-robot policy performance.

By achieving these objectives, DREAM seeks to offer a more scalable and accessible approach to adapting VLA models for diverse real-world robotic manipulation tasks, contributing to broader robot deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DREAM operates as a four-stage pipeline designed to generate deployment-workspace training data from a short video of the target workspace, a language instruction, and the robot model with its camera calibration. The core principle involves separating visual realism (for rendering) from physical feasibility (for physics and planning), using a compact geometric world for the latter while maintaining photorealism for observations.

#### 3.1. A. Digital-Twin Reconstruction

1. **Capture and Splat Training:** A short RGB video of the workspace, captured by a handheld camera, is processed. Frames are uniformly extracted, and camera poses and a sparse point cloud are estimated using Structure-from-Motion (COLMAP). A 3D Gaussian Splatting representation is then optimized using `gsplat`, preserving calibrated intrinsics and disabling world-space normalization to enable metric expression in the robot frame.
2. **Alignment to the Robot Base Frame:** The monocular reconstruction, initially in an arbitrary coordinate frame, is registered to the robot. A reference point cloud of the robot is synthesized from its model at the capture-time joint configuration. Robot points in the reconstruction are identified using text-prompted segmentation (SAM3) with multi-view voting. These two point clouds are coarsely registered using FPFH features, RANSAC, and a scale-aware estimator, followed by refinement via a scaled Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm. The resulting similarity transform is incorporated into the splat, ensuring all subsequent stages operate in the metric robot frame.
3. **Asset Decomposition:** The aligned Gaussian splat is decomposed into independently posable assets. Gaussians near the posed robot surface are removed to create a robot-free static background. Per-link robot splats are extracted and transformed into link-local frames, creating a re-posable robot splat rig. Each manipulable object is represented by an appearance splat generated from photographs using an image-to-3D generative model (TRELLIS), calibrated to its physical size, and paired with a collision mesh for physics and planning.
4. **Environment Instantiation:** These assets are assembled into a scene description that includes the movable objects, their initial poses, a randomization region, the language instruction, and camera calibration. This metadata instantiates an `IsaacLab` environment. Only a ground plane, the robot articulation, and movable objects are simulated; the reconstructed surroundings serve exclusively as the Gaussian-splat background for rendering. Fixed and wrist cameras are instantiated with the same calibration used at deployment.

#### 3.2. B. Demonstration Synthesis Using TAMP

1. **Task Specification:** A large language model (GPT-4o-mini) translates the language instruction into symbolic goal atoms and staged success criteria. These are instantiated from a predefined vocabulary of predicates (e.g., `On(x, y)`), each with an associated evaluation function. The LLM selects and parameterizes these implemented predicates, ensuring valid output through grammatical parsing and argument resolution to real scene objects. Success criteria are defined as an ordered sequence of stages (e.g., reach, grasp, transport, place), each using one of thirteen predefined checkers.
2. **Planning:** Planning occurs in two stages. First, plan skeletons (operator sequences like `MoveFree`, `Pick`, `Place`) are enumerated using breadth-first search over a PDDL domain. Second, the continuous parameters of these skeletons (grasps, placements, robot configurations) are resolved using GPU-parallel optimization (cuTAMP). Particles are initialized from sampling streams (e.g., diffusion-based grasp generator GraspGen, heuristic placement sampler) and optimized against a weighted constraint cost for collision, reachability, and kinematic tolerances. The optimal particle is refined into an executable trajectory using segment-wise, collision-aware joint-space motion planning (cuRobo).
3. **Execution and Recording:** Each planned trajectory is executed open-loop in the simulator. Episodes are aborted if, for example, the gripper closes without acquiring an object. Successful episodes, as verified by the rubric, are recorded as time-indexed trajectories of robot states, gripper commands, and object states. Each frame is annotated with the symbolic operator it corresponds to. Object placements and planner samples are randomized per episode to increase diversity. The robot starts each episode from a fixed home configuration.

#### 3.3. C. Demonstration Augmentation

To scale the relatively expensive TAMP demonstrations, DREAM employs MimicGen-style augmentation. Source trajectories, which carry their symbolic plan, are segmented at subtask boundaries (e.g., after post-grasp lift, trimming terminal motions). For each new augmented trial, object poses are re-randomized. A source demonstration segment is selected by nearest neighbor in object pose and rigidly transformed into the new object frame. Consecutive segments are connected by interpolated bridge motions. A trial is accepted only if its terminal rubric criterion is met, and it passes an offline physical plausibility filter that checks for static object disturbance, pre-grasp bumping, dragging, and joint overspeed.

#### 3.4. D. Rendering and Dataset Construction

The augmented trajectories, lacking visual observations, are replayed through the Gaussian-splat scene. For each timestep, the robot-free background, re-posed per-link robot splats, and re-posed object splats are concatenated into a single Gaussian set. RGB observations are then rasterized for the policy’s cameras (fixed third-person and wrist-mounted) using their calibrated extrinsics and hand-eye calibration. Mutual occlusion is implicitly handled by depth-sorted alpha blending within the single Gaussian set. The final dataset, containing language instructions, rendered RGB observations, robot states, and action labels, is stored in the LeRobot format for policy training.

#### 3.5. E. Policy Fine-tuning

The synthesized demonstrations are used to train robot policies via imitation learning, fine-tuning pretrained VLA models. During deployment in the real world, these policies receive real camera observations and robot states in the same format used during training, along with the language instruction. TAMP is exclusively used during data generation and is not part of the deployed policy's inference process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate DREAM as a scalable data-collection system for unseen deployment workspaces, addressing two key research questions.

#### 4.1. A. Experiment 1: Sim-to-Real Correlation (RQ1)

This experiment assessed whether the reconstructed digital twin could reliably predict real-world policy performance.
- **Methodology:** Policy checkpoints of SmolVLA, π0.5, and Diffusion Policy (trained from scratch) were evaluated on the `BlockIntoBowl` and `FruitPacking` tasks using 100 teleoperated demonstrations. Each checkpoint was tested in both the reconstructed simulation and the real robot environment under matched object configurations and identical success criteria.
- **Results:**
 - For both tasks, the Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV) was 0.000, indicating that the reconstruction accurately preserved the relative ranking of the policies compared to their real-world performance.
 - Pearson's r correlation coefficient was 0.98 for `BlockIntoBowl` and 0.99 for `FruitPacking`, demonstrating a strong positive correlation between simulated and real-world success rates.
 - Simulated success rates were consistently 7 to 14 percentage points lower than real success rates, with SmolVLA on `FruitPacking` being a notable exception where they coincided at the lower end. This suggests the reconstruction provided a pessimistic but rank-preserving estimate of absolute policy performance.
- **Conclusion:** The reconstructed simulation was found to be a trustworthy environment for comparing policies, as it faithfully reproduced the real-world performance rankings, despite a slight underestimation of absolute success rates.

#### 4.2. B. Experiment 2: Scaling with Data Collection Time (RQ2)

This experiment investigated the cost-effectiveness and performance scaling of DREAM's automatically generated demonstrations compared to human teleoperation.
- **Methodology:** Policies (SmolVLA and π0.5) were fine-tuned using datasets from two sources:
 - **Human Teleoperation:** 10, 50, and 100 demonstrations per task. Costs were measured as operator time per demonstration.
 - **DREAM:** 100, 500, and 1000 demonstrations per task. Costs included a fixed initial setup for capture, reconstruction, and 50 TAMP source demonstrations, followed by incremental costs for augmentation and rendering per generated demonstration.
 - Policies were evaluated on the physical robot under held-out object configurations, with success rate as the primary metric. Generation metrics included valid trajectory ratio, planning success rate, augmentation acceptance rate, and average planning time.
- **Results:**
 - **Performance:** With a sufficient quantity of generated data, policies fine-tuned on DREAM data achieved higher success rates than those trained on teleoperated data for both tasks.
 - On `BlockIntoBowl`: π0.5 reached 93.3% with 1000 DREAM demonstrations versus 86.7% with 100 teleoperated. SmolVLA reached 80.0% with 1000 DREAM demonstrations versus 73.3% with 100 teleoperated.
 - On `FruitPacking`: π0.5 reached 86.7% with 1000 DREAM demonstrations versus 80.0% with 100 teleoperated. SmolVLA, however, stalled at 6.7% with DREAM data beyond 500 demonstrations, significantly below its 26.7% with 100 teleoperated demonstrations.
 - **Cost Efficiency:**
 - Human teleoperation costs scaled linearly with the number of demonstrations, directly proportional to operator time.
 - DREAM incurred a significant fixed initial cost (36 minutes for `BlockIntoBowl`, 95 minutes for `FruitPacking`) for workspace capture, reconstruction, and TAMP source demonstration generation. This fixed cost was largely dominated by TAMP, which increased with task horizon (e.g., 25.8 minutes for 50 source demonstrations on `BlockIntoBowl` vs. 84.6 minutes on `FruitPacking` due to longer episodes and lower planner success rate).
 - After this fixed cost, each additional DREAM demonstration incurred a substantially lower, machine-time cost (a few seconds per demonstration). DREAM became cheaper in terms of wall-clock time compared to teleoperation after approximately 73 demonstrations for `BlockIntoBowl` and 168 demonstrations for `FruitPacking`.
 - Augmentation acceptance rates were 33.0% for `BlockIntoBowl` and 32.6% for `FruitPacking`, indicating that a significant portion of augmented trials were rejected by the rubric and plausibility filters.
 - **Data Quality:** Generated demonstrations were noted to contain frequent wrist rotations and slightly redundant motions, implying they might be less "efficient" than human demonstrations. This suggests that a larger volume of generated data was required to achieve performance comparable to or exceeding a smaller set of high-quality teleoperated demonstrations.
- **Conclusion:** DREAM offers a scalable data-collection system where the human contribution is a one-time effort (scan, calibration, instruction), while dataset generation consumes machine time, which scales more favorably than human operator time. This enables the generation of large datasets that can improve policy performance, potentially surpassing teleoperation-trained policies, by leveraging quantity to compensate for variations in individual demonstration quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DREAM presents a framework that addresses a notable challenge in robotic manipulation: the costly and labor-intensive process of adapting Vision–Language–Action (VLA) policies to new deployment environments. The significance of this work lies in its potential to increase the scalability and accessibility of VLA model deployment.

The proposed system offers the following potential impacts:

- **Reduced Human Effort and Cost:** By enabling the automatic generation of workspace-specific fine-tuning data from a short video and a language instruction, DREAM substantially reduces the need for task-specific human teleoperation. This shift from operator-time-intensive data collection to machine-time-intensive generation could significantly lower the operational costs and human resource requirements for deploying robots in varied settings.
- **Accelerated VLA Policy Adaptation:** The ability to rapidly generate large datasets tailored to a specific environment could accelerate the fine-tuning process for pretrained VLA models. This could enable faster iteration and deployment cycles for robotic applications in new scenarios, contributing to broader adoption of VLA-powered robots.
- **Enhanced Generalization to New Workspaces:** DREAM’s methodology, which reconstructs a digital twin of the deployment environment, ensures that the generated training data is visually and geometrically grounded in the target workspace. This environment-specific data is designed to improve a policy's ability to generalize and perform effectively within the actual physical conditions of deployment, mitigating the sim-to-real gap.
- **Foundation for Future Automation:** The integration of scene reconstruction, large language models for task interpretation, and task-and-motion planning provides a modular framework. This could serve as a foundation for developing more autonomous robot deployment systems, where adaptation to new tasks and environments requires minimal human intervention.
- **Improved Understanding of Sim-to-Real Reliability:** The empirical validation of the reconstructed simulation's reliability (RQ1), demonstrating strong correlation with real-world performance despite a pessimistic absolute success rate, contributes to confidence in using synthetic environments for policy evaluation and data generation. This finding is relevant for the broader robotics community investigating sim-to-real transfer.

While the current framework targets approximately static tabletop scenes with rigid objects, its successful demonstration suggests a promising route for scalable VLA deployment. Future work involves extending DREAM to more complex scenarios, such as manipulating deformable and articulated objects, and handling more contact-rich tasks. The approach of using reconstructed deployment scenes as a source of action-labeled supervision has the potential to make advanced robot manipulation capabilities more readily adaptable and deployable in diverse real-world applications.
