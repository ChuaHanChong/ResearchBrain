# Compliant Non-Prehensile Pushing Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a control framework enabling impedance-controlled robots to perform compliant non-prehensile pushing while ensuring safety during human interaction. The framework combines an extended Model Predictive Control (MPC) with an energy tank passivity filter, achieving sub-centimeter positional accuracy (mean 1.65 x 10^-2 m) and preventing unsafe force escalation during external disturbances.

## Problem

- Existing non-prehensile pushing strategies are largely designed for stiff, position-controlled robots, limiting their safe deployment in environments shared with humans.
- Impedance-controlled robots, while offering compliance, struggle to maintain manipulation accuracy during pushing tasks and can exhibit uncontrolled energy buildup (leading to unsafe forces) when external interactions impede desired motion.
- Integrating advanced manipulation precision with robust safety guarantees during physical human-robot interaction in compliant pushing scenarios presents a significant control challenge.

## Method

- An extended ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework is developed to enable compliant non-prehensile pushing, optimally modulating the robot's position/velocity set-point to control contact force and desired sliding velocity.
- The planar outputs from the MPC are transformed into 3D Cartesian space for execution by an impedance-controlled manipulator.
- An energy tank passivity filter is integrated to monitor system energy and dynamically adjust the robot's velocity set-point, preventing passivity violations and uncontrolled force increases during external interactions such as human interference.

## Results

- Simulations and real-world experiments confirmed that the energy tank passivity filter prevents unbounded pushing force escalation during external interactions, maintaining task execution safety and limiting positional errors to below **1.5** x 10^-2 m in simulations.
- On an ABB YuMi robot, the system achieved high-precision tracking during human obstruction (positional error < 2 x 10^-3 m), with the passivity filter activating to safely manage interaction forces and resume the task upon release.
- A quantitative analysis on a KUKA LBR iiwa across 216 tests yielded a mean positional error of **1.65** x 10^-2 m and a mean rotational error of **0.10** rad, demonstrating robustness to object mass and friction variations, though performance degraded with higher velocities and curvilinear paths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A virtual spring model effectively links an impedance-controlled robot's displacement from a set-point to contact force, allowing force control through optimal set-point modulation.
> - The inherent underactuation in compliant pushing can be addressed by deriving a velocity set-point that mediates between controlling the contact force and achieving desired sliding velocity.
> - An energy tank passivity filter can actively detect and prevent unbounded energy accumulation and force escalation in compliant systems during external disturbances, ensuring safe human-robot interaction by dynamically limiting control output.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Service robots are increasingly deployed in environments shared with humans, necessitating capabilities for both object manipulation and safe interaction during physical contact. Non-prehensile pushing is a manipulation primitive suited for tasks where grasping is challenging, such as handling large, fragile, or numerous items in confined spaces. This approach involves controlling an object's motion by applying forces without fully enclosing or grasping it.

Existing research in non-prehensile pushing has explored various aspects, including quasi-static models that relate pushing forces to object velocities via friction limit surfaces, often employing ellipsoidal approximations for computational efficiency. These models form the basis for planning and control strategies. A notable advancement is the use of Model Predictive Control (MPC) frameworks that integrate complementarity constraints to manage transitions between sticking and sliding contact modes in real time, enhancing manipulation efficiency and responsiveness.

However, a majority of these pushing methodologies have been developed for position-controlled robots. While precise, position-controlled robots are inherently stiff, making them unsuitable for direct physical interaction with humans due to safety concerns. In human-populated environments, robots require inherent compliance and passive behavior to ensure safety during intentional or accidental contact. This necessitates extending manipulation strategies to impedance-controlled robots, which can react compliantly to external forces. The challenge lies in maintaining manipulation accuracy and preventing uncontrolled energy buildup when compliance leads to tracking errors or external interactions hinder desired motion.

Passivity-based control, particularly the use of energy tanks, has emerged as a method to ensure stability and safety in dynamic human-robot interactions by monitoring and regulating energy exchange. This approach prevents unbounded internal energy generation that could lead to excessive interaction forces. This research seeks to bridge the gap between advanced non-prehensile pushing control and safe, compliant human-robot interaction by integrating these concepts within a unified framework suitable for impedance-controlled robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a control framework that enables service robots to perform non-prehensile pushing tasks in human-populated environments while simultaneously guaranteeing safe, compliant, and passive behavior during external physical interactions. This objective is motivated by the growing deployment of robots in collaborative and assistive settings where direct contact with humans is inevitable.

Specifically, the work aims to address two core requirements:
1. **Achieve manipulation precision:** The robot must accurately steer an object along a desired trajectory, even when operating in a compliant mode. Existing advanced pushing strategies, like the MPC framework in, are typically designed for stiff, position-controlled robots. Adapting these to impedance-controlled robots, which inherently allow for position deviations, poses a challenge to maintaining task accuracy.
2. **Guarantee passive and compliant response:** The robot system must exhibit compliant behavior to external physical interactions (e.g., from a human operator) and ensure passivity. Compliance alone is insufficient, as external disturbances that impede the robot's motion can cause naive error compensation by the controller, potentially leading to an uncontrolled increase in pushing force and an unbounded accumulation of internal energy within the system. This uncontrolled energy exchange could result in unsafe interaction forces, posing a risk to humans or equipment.

To address these challenges, the research proposes to:
- Extend a state-of-the-art non-prehensile pushing MPC framework to be compatible with impedance-controlled robots. This involves developing a mechanism to optimally modulate the robot’s position/velocity set-point to achieve the desired pushing force and contact point adaptation.
- Integrate an energy tank passivity filter into the control framework. This filter is designed to monitor system energy and dynamically adjust the robot's velocity set-point when there is a risk of passivity violation, thereby preventing uncontrolled energy buildup and ensuring safe interaction forces.

The motivation stems from the need for robots to operate dependably and safely in close proximity to humans, exemplified by scenarios like hospital logistics where a robot might push a rack of test tubes while interacting with healthcare personnel.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed control framework integrates an extended non-prehensile pushing MPC with a passivity filter to enable compliant pushing by impedance-controlled robots. The architecture consists of three main components: a Compliant Pushing MPC, a Planar to Spatial Interaction block, and an Energy Tank Passivity Filter.

#### 3.1. Planar Pushing Model

The framework utilizes a quasi-static planar pusher-slider model, where a point pusher applies a contact force to a rectangular object sliding on a surface. The object's motion (position $x_b, y_b$ and orientation $\theta_b$) is controlled by this force. The relationship between the body wrench $f_b^b$ and the object twist $\omega_b^b$ is established via the ellipsoidal approximation of the limit surface, assuming maximal dissipation: $\omega_b^b = Lf_b^b$, where $L$ is a positive-definite matrix. The contact force $f_c^b$ at the contact point $x_c$ is transformed into the body wrench $f_b^b$ through the transpose of the contact Jacobian. The contact point coordinates are parameterized by an angle $\phi_b$ relative to the object's body frame.

The model accounts for three hybrid contact modes: sticking ($\dot{\phi}_b = 0$, force within friction cone) and two sliding modes ($\dot{\phi}_b > 0$ or $\dot{\phi}_b < 0$, force on friction cone boundary). These modes are incorporated into the MPC as complementarity constraints using non-negative auxiliary variables and a slack variable to ensure real-time mode transitions.

#### 3.2. Compliant Pushing Model

To enable impedance-controlled robots, a compliant pushing model is introduced. Unlike position-controlled robots that directly track the contact point, impedance-controlled robots require a displacement between a controlled set-point $x_d$ and the actual contact point $x_c$ to generate force. The force $f_c^b$ is modeled as a virtual spring: $f_c^b = K(x_d^b - x_c^b(\phi_b))$, where $K$ is the stiffness matrix.

A key challenge is that the robot's controllable set-point $x_d$ has two degrees of freedom, while the desired output (two force components and one sliding velocity $\dot{x}_c$) involves three. The framework addresses this underactuation by deriving a velocity set-point $\dot{x}_d^b$ that mediates between the objective of controlling the contact force and the desired sliding velocity. This $\dot{x}_d^b$ is a sum of contributions from the force dynamics (proportional to $\dot{f}_c^b$) and the sliding velocity (proportional to $\dot{x}_c^b(\phi_b, \dot{\phi}_b)$).

#### 3.3. Compliant Pushing MPC Formulation

The MPC problem is formulated to optimize the control inputs over a prediction horizon. The state vector includes the object state, the position set-point $x_d^b$, and the desired force $f_c^b$. The control inputs consist of the auxiliary variables for contact mode switching ($\dot{\phi}^+, \dot{\phi}^-$), the derivative of the pushing force ($\dot{f}_c^b$), and a slack variable for complementarity constraints. The objective function minimizes a combination of terminal state cost and a running cost, penalizing deviations from desired object position/orientation and non-zero values for other state and control variables. The MPC is discretized and solved at a specified frequency.

#### 3.4. From Planar to Spatial Interaction

The planar velocity set-points $\dot{x}_d$ and contact forces $f_c$ generated by the MPC are transformed into 3D Cartesian space for execution by a robot manipulator. The planar set-point is augmented with a constant reference height along the vertical z-axis and a fixed orientation (pushing tool pointing downward) to form a spatial twist set-point $\dot{x}_e^*$ for the robot flange. Dually, the planar contact force $f_c$ is mapped to an equivalent spatial wrench $f_p$ at the flange using transformation matrices.

#### 3.5. Manipulator Impedance Control

The robot manipulator is controlled using Cartesian impedance control. This involves commanding joint torques to achieve a desired dynamic relationship between the robot's Cartesian position and the external forces it exerts. The controller makes the robot behave like a virtual mass-spring-damper system. The main contribution to the external wrench $f_e$ in quasi-static conditions is from the stiffness term $K_d \tilde{x}_e$, where $K_d$ is the desired impedance stiffness and $\tilde{x}_e$ is the flange position/orientation error. The impedance stiffness is matched to the compliant pushing model's stiffness along the planar components.

#### 3.6. Passivity Filter

To prevent uncontrolled energy buildup during external interactions (e.g., human interference), an energy tank passivity filter is incorporated. The system's passivity is analyzed with respect to the input-output pair of human interaction wrench $f_h$ and twist error $\dot{\tilde{x}}_e$. Without the filter, the energy exchanged through the pushing wrench port can have an undefined sign, leading to passivity violation.

The energy tank, represented by a storage element $z$, monitors energy flow. Its dynamics recharge with dissipated energy and deplete with energy exchanged due to physical interaction. When the tank's energy drops below a lower threshold and internal energy is increasing (indicating a passivity violation risk), an activation parameter $\alpha$ switches to 0. This modifies the robot's twist set-point $\dot{x}_{e,p}^*$ to track the actual twist $\dot{x}_e$ rather than the MPC-generated (potentially non-passive) set-point. This effectively prevents the controller from continuously increasing pushing force in response to interaction-induced errors. The filter requires knowledge of the pushing wrench $f_p$, which is approximated by the MPC-predicted contact force. The planner is also informed of the tank activation to freeze the object reference set-point, preventing indefinite growth of tracking error.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed framework was evaluated through simulations and real-world experiments on two distinct robotic platforms.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

Simulations were conducted in RAISIM, a physics engine, using a 7-DOF KUKA LBR iiwa 7 robot pushing a steel box along an eight-shaped trajectory. Two scenarios were compared: with and without the passivity filter, during which an obstacle temporarily blocked the object's path.

- **Passive Case (Passivity Filter Active):** When the obstacle appeared, the object's tracking error and the pushing force increased. As the interaction continued, the energy tank's energy decreased, reaching its lower bound. At this point, the passivity filter activated, setting the activation parameter $\alpha$ to 0, which prevented further increases in the manipulator's velocity set-point. This action effectively bounded the pushing force and prevented its indefinite growth. After the obstacle was removed, the tank recharged, and the robot resumed normal operation. Positional tracking errors remained below 1.5 x 10$^{-2}$ m and orientation error below 10$^{-2}$ rad, demonstrating precision despite compliant operation and external interaction.
- **Non-Passive Case (Passivity Filter Removed):** Under identical conditions but without the passivity filter, the tracking error increased when the obstacle was encountered, and the pushing force rose rapidly and unboundedly until the obstacle was removed. This illustrated the unsafe behavior that the passivity filter is designed to prevent, as the controller continuously attempted to reduce the growing tracking error without energetic safeguards.

#### 4.2. Experimental Evaluation

Experiments were conducted on two robotic platforms to validate different aspects of the framework.

##### 4.2.1. ABB Mobile YuMi Experiments (Passive Interaction)

These experiments focused on validating safe passive interaction with humans. The ABB Mobile YuMi, a 7-DOF manipulator, pushed a rack of test tubes. Object pose was estimated using an Azure Kinect RGB-D camera.

- **Linear Trajectory:** A human operator intentionally hindered the object's motion. The system maintained high-precision tracking (positional error < 2 x 10$^{-3}$ m, orientation error < 6 x 10$^{-2}$ rad). During human interaction, the energy tank energy rapidly decreased, and the control action was disabled by the passivity filter once the lower bound was reached, preventing force escalation. Upon release, the robot resumed the task, and the tank recharged.
- **Curvilinear Trajectory:** Similar results were observed for a curvilinear path. Positional error magnitude remained below 3.5 x 10$^{-3}$ m, and orientation error below 6 x 10$^{-2}$ rad. The passivity filter similarly activated during human obstruction, preventing uncontrolled force generation, and the task was successfully completed after the interaction ceased.

These experiments demonstrated the framework's ability to ensure passive behavior during human-robot interaction in a compliant pushing task.

##### 4.2.2. KUKA LBR iiwa Experiments (Tracking Performance and Robustness)

A quantitative experimental analysis was performed on a KUKA LBR iiwa 7, a 7-DOF collaborative robot, to assess tracking performance and robustness under varying conditions. An Intel RealSense camera tracked the object's pose. The MPC was solved at 25 Hz.

An experimental campaign involved 216 tests, systematically varying:
- **Object-table friction coefficient ($\mu$):** Three levels (aluminum, nylon, steel tables).
- **Object mass ($m$):** Three levels (empty, half-full, full rack of test tubes).
- **Desired path ($s$):** Two levels (linear, curvilinear).
- **Desired trajectory mean velocity ($v$):** Four increasing levels.

Performance was evaluated using root mean square positional error ($\bar{E}_p$) and rotational error ($\bar{E}_\theta$). Statistical analysis (Mann-Whitney U test) revealed:

- **Friction:** $\bar{E}_p$ was largely unaffected by changes in friction. $\bar{E}_\theta$ showed statistically significant increases for the steel table (higher rotational friction), but remained within acceptable application requirements (below 0.2 rad).
- **Mass:** The method demonstrated robustness to mass variations. A statistically significant but minor improvement in $\bar{E}_p$ was observed at higher masses (m2 vs m3, p < 0.05).
- **Desired Path:** Curvilinear paths consistently resulted in statistically significant higher errors for both $\bar{E}_p$ and $\bar{E}_\theta$ (p < 0.001), indicating path choice significantly impacts tracking accuracy.
- **Mean Velocity:** Generally, higher desired velocities led to degraded tracking performance, affecting both $\bar{E}_p$ and $\bar{E}_\theta$ significantly in most comparisons.

Overall, the method achieved a mean positional error of 1.65 x 10$^{-2}$ m and a mean rotational error of 0.10 rad across all perturbed cases. These results are considered comparable to other reported pushing approaches, with the slightly higher positional error attributed to the inherent sensitivity of impedance control to disturbances like static and non-homogeneous friction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing a critical gap in the safe deployment of service robots in human-populated environments. By enabling compliant non-prehensile pushing with impedance-controlled robots, the framework broadens the applicability of robotic systems in sensitive contexts.

The primary significance lies in the development and validation of a control framework that jointly achieves precise manipulation and safe, passive physical human-robot interaction. The integration of an extended MPC formulation with an energy tank passivity filter provides a robust mechanism to prevent unsafe force escalation during unexpected external interactions. This feature is crucial for collaborative scenarios where human workers or individuals receiving assistance might come into physical contact with the robot.

The rigorous testing, encompassing both physics-based simulations and real-world experiments on two distinct robotic platforms (ABB Mobile YuMi and KUKA LBR iiwa 7), demonstrates the framework's effectiveness, generalizability, and robustness to variations in object parameters, surfaces, and trajectories. The quantitative analysis of tracking performance under various conditions provides empirical evidence of its capabilities and limitations, such as the increased error observed with higher velocities and curvilinear paths.

The potential impact of this work extends to various application domains, including:
- **Collaborative Industrial Workstations:** Robots can safely work alongside human operators, jointly handling objects without posing a hazard.
- **Assistive Robotics:** Robots can assist individuals with reduced mobility by repositioning objects, providing support, or performing other tasks in close proximity.
- **Logistics and Healthcare:** Robots can perform tasks like delivering medical supplies or meals in dynamic, human-dense environments like hospitals, as demonstrated in the conceptual scenario of pushing a rack of test tubes.

The open-source release of the control framework further contributes to the research community, enabling reproducibility and fostering future developments in compliant and safe robotic manipulation. Future work, exploring dynamic interaction models, integrating real-time force feedback, and quantifying robustness to diverse object shapes, could further enhance the capabilities and impact of this research.
