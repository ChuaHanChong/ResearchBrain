# Knapsack RL: Unlocking Exploration of LLMs via Optimizing Budget Allocation

> [!summary] Summary
> Knapsack RL introduces an optimization framework for dynamically allocating exploration budgets in Large Language Model (LLM) reinforcement learning, improving training efficiency and performance by focusing computational resources on tasks that yield the most informative gradients. This leads to consistent performance gains on reasoning benchmarks and achieves comparable results with half the computational budget of uniform allocation.

## Problem

- Existing Large Language Model (LLM) reinforcement learning (RL) methods use a uniform exploration budget per task, leading to inefficient computation.
- Uniform allocation causes "zero-gradient" scenarios for very easy or very hard tasks, where all rollouts succeed or fail, halting learning for those tasks.
- The high computational cost of generating exploratory trajectories (rollouts) for LLMs necessitates more efficient resource management.

## Method

- The method reframes exploration budget allocation as a ==knapsack optimization problem==, where tasks are "items" with a computational "cost" and a "value" representing learning potential.
- A novel ==value function== is introduced, combining the probability of yielding a non-zero gradient (for algorithms like GRPO) with an estimate of information gain based on task success rates.
- An efficient ==dynamic programming solver== adaptively distributes the total computational budget among tasks, employing a ==fallback strategy== to ensure even extremely hard tasks receive exploration.

## Results

- Knapsack-GRPO consistently improved LLM performance on reasoning benchmarks, with average gains of **2-4** points and peak gains of **9** points on specific tasks.
- The "effective gradient ratio" increased by **20-40%** compared to uniform allocation, demonstrating more efficient use of computational resources throughout training.
- The approach achieved performance equivalent to standard GRPO while using roughly **half** the computational budget, indicating substantial resource savings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A significant portion (over 40%) of rollouts in uniform exploration yield "zero gradients," hindering learning and wasting computational resources.
> - Dynamically allocating exploration budgets based on a task's current success rate (its "learnability") is critical to maximizing the effectiveness of each sampled trajectory.
> - Reconceptualizing exploration as a resource optimization problem allows for a "computational free lunch," enhancing LLM RL performance without increasing total compute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper operates within the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM) self-improvement through Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically focusing on RL with verifiable rewards. This paradigm, where LLMs iteratively refine their policies based on binary (correct/incorrect) feedback, has gained prominence for enhancing reasoning abilities in domains like mathematics, coding, and agentic tasks.

The broader landscape of LLM-RL faces several critical challenges:
1. **Computational Expense:** Generating trajectories (rollouts) for exploration is computationally very costly due to the autoregressive nature of LLMs and the need for many samples.
2. **Gradient Signal Sparsity/Inefficiency:** Many RL algorithms, including the widely used Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), rely on diverse outcomes (both successes and failures) within a batch to generate meaningful policy gradients. Uniformly small exploration budgets often lead to "zero-gradient" scenarios where all rollouts for a task either succeed or fail, halting learning for those tasks.
3. **Data Heterogeneity:** Training datasets for LLMs often contain tasks of varying difficulty, and this difficulty can change as the model learns. A uniform approach to exploration fails to adapt to this dynamic heterogeneity.

Prior research has attempted to address these issues through various angles:
- **Policy Optimization Improvements:** Works like REINFORCE, GRPO, RLOO, and ReMax focus on improving the quality and stability of gradient estimation (the "exploitation" phase) given the collected samples, often through refined baselines and variance reduction techniques. This paper builds on GRPO but tackles a different aspect.
- **Increasing Exploration Budget Uniformly:** A straightforward but computationally prohibitive approach is simply to increase the number of rollouts (N) for every task. As the paper demonstrates, setting a very large N (e.g., 100) across all tasks is impractical.
- **Prompt Selection and Curriculum Learning:** Methods in this category (e.g., dynamic sampling in DAPO, curriculum design based on advantage estimates or perplexity) aim to improve data efficiency by selecting *which* prompts to train on. While effective, these typically assume a fixed or homogeneous exploration budget *per selected prompt*. This paper explicitly differentiates itself by focusing on *how much* exploration to allocate *to each prompt*, rather than *which* prompts to choose.
- **Resource Allocation (General):** While resource allocation is a well-studied problem in operations research, its application to dynamic, per-task exploration budgeting in multi-task LLM RL is relatively novel. Previous theoretical work on constrained episodic RL often considers single-task settings or different forms of constraints.

This work specifically carves out a niche by addressing the *exploration budget allocation problem* as a first-class citizen. Instead of optimizing *how* gradients are computed (like ReMax) or *which* data points are used (like dynamic sampling), "Knapsack RL" optimizes *how many* exploratory samples are generated *per task* within a fixed overall computational budget. It posits that the problem of inefficient gradient signals stems fundamentally from a mismatch in exploration resources for tasks of varying difficulty, which the uniform allocation fails to resolve. By drawing a direct analogy to the classical knapsack problem, the authors introduce a principled, adaptive framework to improve the *quality of the exploration phase itself*, thereby yielding more effective gradients and accelerating learning without increasing total compute. This positions it as a "computational free lunch" that complements existing policy optimization techniques and data selection strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for "Knapsack RL" arises from a critical bottleneck observed in current Reinforcement Learning (RL) pipelines for Large Language Models (LLMs): **inefficient and uniform exploration budget allocation**.
The authors identify that while RL enables LLMs to self-improve, the exploration process—generating diverse trajectories to discover better solutions—is prohibitively expensive. This often forces existing methods to assign a **limited, uniform exploration budget (e.g., 8 rollouts) to each task**.

This uniform allocation leads to several problematic outcomes, particularly for the widely used **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** algorithm:
1. **Zero Gradients for Edge Cases:**
 - **Easy tasks:** When a task becomes too easy, all `N` rollouts may result in correct solutions (all positive rewards). This leads to a standard deviation of zero, yielding zero gradients, thus no learning signal.
 - **Difficult tasks:** Conversely, for very hard tasks, all `N` rollouts may result in incorrect solutions (all negative rewards). This also results in zero gradients, meaning the model cannot learn to overcome these challenges.
 - **Wasted Computation:** In both scenarios, significant computational resources are expended on generating rollouts that ultimately contribute nothing to model updates. The paper quantifies this with the "effective-gradient-ratio" metric, showing that over 40% of sampled data often yields zero gradients.

2. **Stalled Learning:** The lack of gradients for easy and hard tasks means that the model's learning stagnates on these critical parts of the data distribution. Easy tasks don't further refine the model, and hard tasks remain unsolved, limiting overall performance. The paper empirically illustrates these "dynamic training phases," where the effective gradient ratio decreases as training progresses due to a rise in both all-positive and all-negative reward outcomes.

3. **Mismatched Exploration Needs:** The fundamental problem, as argued by the authors, is a mismatch between a task's inherent difficulty (and its current learning status) and the fixed exploration budget it receives. Hard tasks require extensive exploration to find *any* successful trajectories, while easy tasks waste compute by being over-sampled.

Given these challenges, the **key objectives** of this work are:
- To **reconceptualize exploration budget allocation** for LLM RL as a resource optimization problem.
- To **derive an optimal assignment rule** that adaptively distributes computational resources based on the model’s current learning status (i.e., task success rates).
- To **increase the "effective gradient ratio"** during training, ensuring that more sampled data contributes meaningfully to policy updates.
- To **enable significantly larger exploration budgets for challenging problems** without increasing the total computational cost, thereby unlocking deeper exploration.
- To ultimately achieve **meaningful performance gains** on LLM reasoning benchmarks and demonstrate that this adaptive allocation can lead to **comparable performance with significantly fewer computational resources** than traditional uniform allocation.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to provide a "computational free lunch" – a method that enhances the efficiency and effectiveness of RL for LLMs by intelligently reallocating existing resources.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, "Knapsack RL," tackles the exploration–computation dilemma by reframing exploration budget allocation as a **classical knapsack optimization problem**.

#### 3.1. Core Formulation: The Knapsack Analogy

The central idea is to treat each potential exploration allocation for a task as an "item" in a knapsack scenario:
- **Item:** A specific task (prompt `x_i`) assigned a particular number of rollout trajectories (`N_i`).
- **Cost/Weight:** The computational effort required for `N_i` rollouts for task `x_i`. In this formulation, this is simply `N_i` itself, as rollouts are typically of similar cost.
- **Value:** The "learning potential" or benefit derived from allocating `N_i` rollouts to task `x_i`, denoted `Value(N_i, p_i)`, where `p_i` is the current success rate of task `x_i`.
- **Knapsack Capacity:** The total available computational budget, represented as `N_total` (e.g., `M` prompts * `N_uniform` rollouts).

The optimization problem is formally defined as:
`maximize Σ_{i=1 to M} Value(N_i, p_i)`
`subject to Σ_{i=1 to M} N_i = N_total`, and `N_low ≤ N_i ≤ N_up`, where `N_i` are positive integers.
`N_low` and `N_up` are bounds to ensure minimum coverage and prevent excessively large allocations, respectively.

#### 3.2. Value Function Definition

The core of the Knapsack RL method lies in its definition of `Value(N_i, p_i)`. For GRPO, this value is constructed to quantify the likelihood and informativeness of obtaining a non-zero gradient:

`Value(N_i, p_i) = ProbNonZeroGradient(N_i, p_i) × InfoGain(p_i)`

- **`ProbNonZeroGradient(N_i, p_i)`:** This term represents the probability of observing at least one successful and one unsuccessful rollout for task `x_i` given `N_i` rollouts and a success rate `p_i`. For GRPO (which requires mixed outcomes for gradients), this is derived as `1 - p_i^N_i - (1 - p_i)^N_i`. This probability is maximized when `p_i` is close to 0.5 and `N_i` is sufficiently large.
- **`InfoGain(p_i)`:** This term quantifies the informativeness of a gradient *if one occurs*. The authors approximate this expected increase in success probability after an update using a first-order Taylor expansion, yielding `InfoGain(p_i) ≈ p_i * (1 - p_i)^2`.
 - This approximation highlights that information gain is maximized when `p_i ≈ 1/3` (i.e., tasks that are somewhat challenging but not extremely hard or easy), and approaches zero for extremely hard (`p_i → 0`) or extremely easy (`p_i → 1`) tasks. This asymmetry emphasizes the value of converting failures into successes.

The interplay between `N_i`, `p_i`, and `Value` is crucial: tasks with success rates far from 0.5 (e.g., very hard or very easy) require larger `N_i` to yield a non-zero gradient probability, but their `InfoGain` might be lower. The knapsack solver balances these factors.

#### 3.3. Algorithmic Implementation Details

1. **Success Rate Estimation:** Since `p_i` (the true success rate) is unknown, it is estimated empirically using the observed success rates from the previous training epoch. This introduces some lag and noise but proves effective. The first epoch typically uses homogeneous allocation.
2. **Knapsack Solver:** The discrete constrained optimization problem is solved using standard dynamic programming techniques, which the authors report can run quickly (1-2 seconds with Numba acceleration).
3. **Handling Extreme Cases:**
 - **`p_i = 1.0` (Extremely Easy):** To prevent zero allocation (since `InfoGain` approaches zero), a minimum budget (`N_low`, e.g., 2) is always assigned. This ensures minor re-evaluation and accounts for potential estimation inaccuracies.
 - **`p_i = 0.0` (Extremely Hard):** The system uses a "fallback strategy." First, the budget is optimally allocated for tasks with `p_i ∈ (0, 1]`. Any remaining budget is then distributed among the extremely hard tasks (`p_i = 0.0`), giving them a chance to generate successful trajectories and learn. This is especially important in later training stages when many tasks become easy, freeing up resources.
4. **Rollout Balancing:** To address potential workload imbalances from heterogeneous budget allocation (some tasks might get 93 rollouts, others 2), the system dispatches individual rollouts as separate execution jobs to parallel workers (e.g., using vLLMs). This simple strategy avoids GPU idles and efficiently utilizes resources. For very long prompts, more sophisticated bin-packing algorithms could be used.
5. **Integration and Overhead:** Knapsack RL integrates seamlessly into existing large-scale RL training pipelines (e.g., Verl framework, FSDP, Megatron). It adds negligible computational overhead and introduces no new hyperparameters, making it a drop-in improvement.

#### 3.4. Extensions to Other RL Algorithms

The modularity of the framework allows for adaptation to other RL algorithms by simply redefining `ProbNonZeroGradient(N_i, p_i)` according to their gradient conditions, while `InfoGain(p_i)` remains the same. Examples include RLOO (similar to GRPO), ReMax (where a non-zero gradient occurs if a sampled trajectory differs from the greedy response), and vanilla REINFORCE (where a non-zero gradient only requires at least one positive reward).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors, primarily using Qwen series models (1.5B to 7B parameters) on the DAPO-Math-17K dataset and evaluated on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME, AMC, MATH, MINERVA, OLYMPIAD) and GPQA (scientific reasoning), yielded several compelling findings:

1. **Consistent Performance Gains:** Knapsack-GRPO consistently outperformed standard GRPO across all tested models and benchmarks.
 - Average performance improvements ranged from 2-4 points, with peak gains on specific tasks reaching 9 points (e.g., AIME for DPSK-R1-Distill-1.5B, AMC for Qwen3-4B-Base, GPQA for Qwen3-4B-Instruct, AMC for Qwen2.5-Math-7B). This demonstrates the method's robustness and effectiveness.

2. **Significant Increase in Effective Gradient Ratio:**
 - Knapsack-GRPO improved the "effective gradient ratio" (proportion of samples contributing non-zero gradients) by approximately 20-40% compared to GRPO.
 - Crucially, unlike uniform allocation, the knapsack method avoided the clear decreasing trend in effective gradient ratio during late-stage training, suggesting a more stable and continuous learning process. This directly validates the core hypothesis that intelligent allocation reduces wasted computation.

3. **Dynamic and Larger Exploration Budgets:**
 - The method dynamically allocated up to 93 rollouts for certain tasks, a level of deep exploration that is computationally prohibitive under conventional uniform allocation strategies. This highlights its ability to focus resources where they are most needed.
 - The distribution of allocated budgets showed a wide range, confirming the heterogeneous nature of the strategy.

4. **Improved Task Transition Dynamics:** Analyzing prompt status transitions (e.g., "extremely-hard" to "extremely-easy"):
 - Knapsack-GRPO showed a lower "self-absorption frequency" for "extremely-hard" tasks (43.4% vs. GRPO's 47.1%), indicating it was more effective at moving these difficult tasks out of a stalled learning state.
 - It also demonstrated a higher transition rate of tasks towards the "extremely-easy" category, meaning more tasks were effectively mastered.
 - Post-training, Knapsack-GRPO resulted in fewer "extremely-hard" tasks (3,596 vs. 3,793 for GRPO) and more "extremely-easy" tasks (9,274 vs. 8,676 for GRPO). This shift in distribution confirms a more efficient and thorough learning process.

5. **Computational Efficiency (2x Resource Savings):**
 - Experiments with varying total exploration budgets showed that Knapsack-GRPO achieved the same performance as standard GRPO with roughly *2x fewer computational resources*. For example, at a total budget of 1024, Knapsack-GRPO's performance (45.5) surpassed GRPO's performance at a total budget of 2048 (45.2). This is a significant practical advantage.

6. **Ablation Studies Validate Design Choices:**
 - **Fallback Strategy:** Removing the fallback strategy (for `p_i = 0.0` tasks) led to unstable training and degraded performance, underscoring its importance for addressing extremely challenging prompts.
 - **Bounds (`N_low`, `N_up`):** The chosen `N_low=2` and `N_up=128` were empirically supported as robust settings, preventing degenerate allocations without hindering performance.

7. **Complementarity with Dynamic Sampling:** When compared to "dynamic sampling" (which selects prompts with mixed outcomes), Knapsack-GRPO tackles a different aspect. While dynamic sampling showed performance boosts, combining it with Knapsack-GRPO did not always yield superior results over pure Knapsack-GRPO in terms of total exploration budget, partly because Knapsack-GRPO already ensures a high effective gradient ratio. However, when measured by *gradient update iterations*, both methods demonstrated that effective gradients lead to greater gains, validating their shared underlying motivation.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that Knapsack-GRPO offers a principled, efficient, and robust solution to the exploration budget allocation problem, leading to tangible performance improvements and significant computational savings in LLM RL training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Knapsack RL" framework presents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) Reinforcement Learning (RL), with wide-ranging significance and potential impact:

1. **"Computational Free Lunch" for LLM RL:** The most notable significance is its ability to improve LLM performance and training efficiency *without requiring additional computational resources*. By intelligently reallocating an existing, fixed budget, it effectively offers a "free lunch," a highly desirable property in an era where LLM training costs are astronomical. This makes high-quality RL fine-tuning more accessible and sustainable.

2. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency:** By ensuring that a significantly higher proportion of generated rollouts contribute to meaningful gradient updates (20-40% increase in effective gradient ratio), the method dramatically improves the sample efficiency of LLM RL. This means less data is wasted, and models learn faster from the same amount of interaction with the environment.

3. **Unlocking Deeper Exploration for Challenging Tasks:** Traditional uniform allocation often starves difficult tasks of the necessary exploration. Knapsack RL, by dynamically assigning much larger budgets (e.g., 93 rollouts) to these tasks, enables deeper and more effective exploration. This is crucial for LLMs to tackle complex reasoning problems (like advanced mathematics) that require many attempts to converge on a solution, pushing the boundaries of their capabilities.

4. **Principled Approach with Generalizability:** Framing the allocation problem as a classical knapsack optimization provides a theoretically sound and interpretable framework. The modular design of the value function (separating `ProbNonZeroGradient` and `InfoGain`) allows it to be adapted to various RL algorithms (e.g., RLOO, ReMax, REINFORCE) and different reward structures, making it broadly applicable beyond GRPO.

5. **Seamless Integration and Low Overhead:** The method integrates effortlessly into existing LLM RL training pipelines without adding new hyperparameters or significant computational overhead. This makes it highly practical for adoption by researchers and practitioners, requiring minimal changes to current infrastructure.

6. **Impact on LLM Alignment and Reasoning:** Better exploration and more effective learning from feedback are fundamental to LLM alignment goals (e.g., making models helpful, harmless, and honest) and enhancing complex reasoning abilities. By improving the RL training process, Knapsack RL contributes directly to the development of more capable, reliable, and robust LLMs.

7. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The paper identifies several promising future directions:
 - **Richer Cost and Value Functions:** Expanding "cost" beyond just rollout count to include token length, interaction turns, or GPU memory usage, and designing more sophisticated "value functions" that better capture learning potential, could further optimize resource allocation.
 - **Advanced Exploration Strategies:** Integrating Knapsack RL with more advanced exploration techniques like Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) or Tree-RL, which are effective in complex environments, could lead to even greater breakthroughs in solving extremely challenging tasks.
 - **Dynamic Adaptation of `p_i` Estimation:** More sophisticated online estimation techniques for success rates could improve the adaptiveness of the allocation.

In essence, "Knapsack RL" offers a foundational improvement to the RL training loop for LLMs. By addressing the often-overlooked problem of exploration budget allocation in a principled and practical manner, it paves the way for more efficient, effective, and ultimately, more powerful language models capable of tackling increasingly complex real-world problems.
