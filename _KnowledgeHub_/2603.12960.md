# Efficient Real-World Autonomous Racing via Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization (α-RPO) extends Residual Policy Learning by gradually phasing out a base policy's influence during training to create a standalone neural controller. This approach enabled a 1:10-scaled autonomous racing robot to achieve over 12% faster lap times on a real-world track and robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with a 3.5 ms inference latency on embedded systems.

## Problem

- Traditional Residual Policy Learning (RPL) methods require the continuous execution of a base policy during deployment, increasing computational load, system complexity, and inference latency on resource-constrained hardware.
- Static mixing factors in existing RPL approaches can prevent the learned agent from exploring optimal control strategies that significantly diverge from the base policy's behavior.
- Deploying Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in real-world autonomous systems, like racing, faces challenges of robust sim-to-real transfer and the need for efficient, standalone controllers.

## Method

- Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization (α-RPO) introduces a linear, iteration-dependent attenuation factor to dynamically blend a classical base policy with a learned residual policy during training.
- The base policy's influence is progressively reduced over training iterations, ultimately yielding a purely neural, standalone policy for efficient real-world deployment.
- A "synchronization trick" is integrated into the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) framework to maintain training stability despite the non-stationary environment introduced by the dynamic attenuation factor.

## Results

- α-RPO agents achieved the lowest average total race times (**46.24** s in training, **47.11** s on unseen test tracks) in simulation, consistently outperforming standard RPL and DRL baselines.
- The method demonstrated robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, successfully deploying on a physical Roboracer car with a low inference latency of **3.5** ms on embedded hardware.
- Real-world experiments on a SLAM-based map of the Munich track showed α-RPO improved lap times by over **12%** compared to a classical Stanley controller, showcasing optimized trajectories and effective obstacle avoidance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradually phasing out the base policy allows the DRL agent to both leverage strong inductive bias for initial learning stability and subsequently discover more optimal, aggressive control behaviors.
> - The synchronization trick is crucial for stabilizing training against the non-stationarity introduced by the continuously changing base policy contribution.
> - Architectural choices like LayerNorm within the FusionBlock and AdaptiveSpatialSoftmax1d for LiDAR feature reduction are critical for robust performance and effective generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses challenges in deploying Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in real-world autonomous systems, particularly within the context of autonomous racing. DRL has demonstrated success across various domains, yet its real-world application is often complicated by the sim-to-real gap and the complexities of thorough system testing.

A prominent paradigm facilitating DRL in such applications is Residual Policy Learning (RPL). RPL involves training a DRL policy to refine the output of a fixed base policy, typically a classical controller. This base policy provides a strong inductive bias during the learning process. RPL has been successfully applied to diverse robotic tasks, including manipulation, legged locomotion, drone control, and notably, autonomous racing.

Autonomous racing, specifically using platforms like the 1:10-scaled Roboracer series, serves as a challenging benchmark for DRL methods. It demands real-time decision-making, robustness to uncertain conditions, and offers well-defined objectives. Existing methods in autonomous racing include classical approaches based on offline racing line optimization and path tracking (e.g., Stanley controller) and reactive mapless methods (e.g., Follow-the-Gap, FTG controller). RPL has demonstrated effectiveness in both offline and online racing scenarios, including real-world deployments.

However, existing RPL approaches present certain limitations. A primary challenge is the static nature of the mixing parameter between the base and residual policies. While a strong base policy offers stability during early training, it can subsequently restrict the learned agent's potential to explore categorically different, potentially more optimal, decisions. Furthermore, standard RPL methods require the base policy to operate during deployment, inheriting its computational requirements and dependencies on specific input modalities (e.g., localization), which increases system complexity and inference latency. While some adaptive aggregation techniques for multiple base policies exist, the targeted objective of fully attenuating the base policy to yield a standalone neural policy for efficient online deployment has not been extensively explored.

This work, by introducing Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization (α-RPO), aims to address these identified limitations by enabling the gradual removal of the base policy, leading to a standalone neural controller for deployment. This positions α-RPO as an advancement in real-world DRL deployment, aiming to simplify system integration and reduce computational overhead while leveraging the benefits of an inductive bias during training. It stands in contrast to methods that prioritize real-world fine-tuning of a base policy or teacher-student approaches that can suffer from catastrophic forgetting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and evaluate Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization (α-RPO), an extension of Residual Policy Learning (RPL), designed to facilitate efficient and robust real-world autonomous racing.

The motivation for developing α-RPO stems from several practical challenges and limitations observed in conventional RPL when applied to real-world robotic systems:

1. **System Complexity and Inference Latency in RPL Deployment:** Standard RPL-based controllers necessitate the continuous execution of the base policy during real-world deployment. This often means inheriting the base policy's specific input modality requirements, such as precise localization for classical path-tracking controllers like Stanley. Such dependencies increase system complexity, computational load, and inference latency, which can be prohibitive for deployment on resource-constrained embedded systems commonly found in autonomous robots. The authors aim to reduce this complexity by producing a standalone neural policy.

2. **Limitations of Static Mixing Factors in RPL:** Traditional RPL approaches frequently employ a static mixing parameter to combine the base and residual policies. While this provides a strong inductive bias that aids in bootstrapping learning and stabilizing early training, it can become a constraint later in the training process. A static weighting may prevent the DRL agent from fully overriding the base policy's decisions or exploring optimal control strategies that diverge significantly from the base behavior, potentially hindering the achievement of peak performance. The motivation is to allow the residual policy to eventually make "categorically different decisions."

3. **Desire for Standalone, Efficient Neural Policies:** The goal is to develop a DRL method that, upon completion of training, yields a purely neural policy. Such a standalone policy offers significant advantages in terms of deployment efficiency, enabling fast GPU-based inference on dedicated hardware (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson platforms) and simplifying system integration by removing the need for a classical control stack at inference time. This efficiency is sought without compromising the robustness to unseen states that active interaction learning (as opposed to behavior cloning) provides.

4. **Leveraging Privileged Information During Training:** By progressively attenuating the base policy, α-RPO intrinsically enables a form of privileged learning. The base policy can leverage sensor modalities or information (e.g., global localization) that are readily available in simulation or during the training phase, but are computationally expensive or impractical to acquire for the final standalone deployment of the neural policy.

5. **Addressing Non-Stationarity in Policy Optimization:** Continuously altering the influence of the base policy through an attenuation schedule introduces a non-stationary environment from the perspective of the residual policy. The motivation includes integrating this mechanism seamlessly within a robust DRL algorithm like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to dynamically compensate for this non-stationarity and maintain training stability.

The authors motivate the choice of 1:10-scaled autonomous racing as their testbed due to its nature as a challenging, real-world DRL benchmark that demands both real-time performance and robustness in dynamic, uncertain conditions. The objective is to demonstrate that α-RPO can achieve competitive racing behavior through zero-shot real-world transfer, simplifying deployment on actual Roboracer cars.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization (α-RPO) as an extension of Residual Policy Learning (RPL), integrating it within the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) framework. The core principle of α-RPO is the progressive attenuation of a base policy's influence during training, aiming to produce a standalone neural policy for deployment.

**Policy Fusion Mechanism:**
α-RPO extends the standard RPL policy fusion by introducing an iteration-dependent attenuation factor, `α ∈`. The fused policy's distribution parameters `(μ, σ)` are defined as:
- `μ:= μ_θ(s) = (1 − α) · μ_B(s) + max(α, α_init) · f_R,θ(s)`
- `σ:= σ_θ(s) = exp(f'_R,θ(s))`
Here, `μ_B(s)` is the action from the static base policy, `f_R,θ(s)` is the output of the learned residual network, `α_init ∈` is a constant initial scaling for the residual network, and `f'_R,θ(s)` is a separate head for the standard deviation. The policy distribution `D` is set as a Truncated-Gaussian `N[-1,1](μ, σ^2)`. The attenuation factor `α` is updated using a linear schedule: `α(k) = min(1, k / K_end)`, where `k` is the current iteration and `K_end` is the iteration at which the base policy is fully phased out, typically set to 25% of total training steps. This ensures that the agent initially relies heavily on the base policy (when `α ≈ 0`) and transitions to a purely residual policy (when `α = 1`).

**Synchronization Trick:**
A key component of α-RPO is the "synchronization trick," designed to mitigate the non-stationarity induced by continuously altering `α`. In PPO, rollouts are typically collected using the current behavior policy, and updates are performed on this data. Naively updating `α` before data collection would mean the behavior policy `~π_θ_k` uses `α_{k+1}`, diverging from the policy `π_θ_k` that resulted from the previous parameter update. The synchronization trick addresses this by:
1. Collecting rollouts and calculating advantages using the behavior policy `~π_θ_k` with the *previous* attenuation factor `α_k`.
2. Updating `α` to `α_{k+1}` *after* data collection but *before* the PPO optimization loop.
This approach leverages PPO's importance sampling-based objective `ρ_t` to implicitly absorb the shift in the base policy's influence, ensuring the policy update remains unbiased.

**Autonomous Racing Framework:**
The authors developed a framework for 1:10-scaled autonomous racing on the Roboracer platform to evaluate α-RPO.
- **Real-World Platform:** Roboracer cars equipped with a 2D LiDAR (1081 readings, 270° FOV, 40 Hz) and an IMU (yaw rate). Longitudinal speed is estimated from motor position. Control actions are steering angle and target speed.
- **Simulation Environment:** A custom Roboracer gym simulator, enhanced with a dynamic single-track model and a nonlinear Pacejka tire model to capture realistic driving effects. Sensor noise is simulated for LiDAR, speed, and yaw rate.
- **α-RPO Agent Design:**
 - **Base Module:** The default base policy is the Stanley controller, a map-based path-tracking algorithm. This controller requires localization information during training, which is provided, but is completely phased out by `K_end`. An ablation also tests the FTG (Follow-the-Gap) controller as a reactive, mapless alternative.
 - **Residual Module:** A deep neural network (DNN) `f_R,θ(s)` that parametrizes the mean `μ_θ(s)` and standard deviation `σ_θ(s)` of the Truncated-Gaussian policy distribution.
 - **State and Action Space:** Observations `o_t` include a preprocessed LiDAR scan `L'_t` (cropped, normalized, downsampled to 512 readings), previous action `a_{t-1}`, current longitudinal speed `v_{long,t}`, and yaw rate `˙ψ_t`. Four consecutive observations are stacked to form the state `s_t`. Actions `a_t` are sampled from `π_θ(·|s)` (bounded to `[-1,1]`) and then rescaled to the vehicle's physical limits.
 - **Reward Formulation:** The reward function incentivizes progress along the track centerline (`0.1 · d_{cl,t+1}`), penalizes large steering changes (`-0.001 · |δ_{t+1} − δ_t|`), and heavily penalizes collisions (`-5 · 1_c`).
 - **Neural Network Architecture:** A DNN with 344,357 parameters, comprising a shared encoder for LiDAR and vehicle state, and separate heads for the residual policy and critic network. The LiDAR encoder uses five 1D convolutional layers, followed by an AdaptiveSpatialSoftmax1d layer (with `K=4` keypoints) and a linear projection. State information is processed by two linear layers. A FusionBlock, incorporating LayerNorm, concatenates and processes these embeddings. The final embedding feeds into independent fully connected layers for the PolicyHead, LogStdHead, and CriticHead.

**Training and Evaluation:**
Training is conducted for 2.5 million timesteps using 15 parallel environments, with trajectories of 1024 timesteps. Training utilizes 15 synthetically generated racetracks and evaluates generalization on 6 additional tracks, including a SLAM-based map of a real-world track (Munich). Evaluation consists of 5-lap races, with a 5-second penalty for collisions and car relocation. Results are averaged over 10 starting positions and 5 independent training runs. Baselines include standard RPL, DRL, DRL with behavior cloning (BC) pre-training, DRL with self-supervised learning (SSL), and classical FTG and Stanley controllers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of α-RPO involved extensive simulation benchmarks, ablation studies, qualitative trajectory analysis, and real-world experiments.

**Simulation Performance (Benchmark):**
1. **Training Curves (Figure 2):**
 - α-RPO and standard RPL agents initially exhibit similar high progress returns, demonstrating the effectiveness of the base policy as an inductive bias for bootstrapping learning.
 - After approximately 0.8 million steps, α-RPO consistently surpasses RPL in average progress, eventually achieving the highest value of 0.99. This supports the hypothesis that while the base policy is beneficial early on, its continuous presence in standard RPL may hinder optimal final performance by requiring the agent to continuously override its behavior.
 - DRL-based methods with BC-pretraining or SSL also show improved performance over vanilla DRL and similar final performance to RPL, but α-RPO generally demonstrates superior results.
 - In terms of collisions during training (Figure 2b), both RPL and α-RPO significantly reduce crash counts compared to DRL-only methods, confirming the safety benefit of the base policy. α-RPO with an FTG base policy caused the fewest collisions (fewer than 500).

2. **Lap Results (Table I):**
 - α-RPO consistently achieved the lowest average total race times across both training (46.24 s) and unseen test racetracks (47.11 s).
 - It demonstrated superior generalization capabilities compared to standard RPL, which showed a significant performance degradation on unseen tracks (average 58.03 s with 0.22 collisions per lap), whereas α-RPO maintained robust performance with zero collisions on test tracks.
 - α-RPO agents operated closer to the physical limits, achieving the highest average maximum speed of 5.41 m/s.
 - On the Munich racetrack (a real-world SLAM-based map), α-RPO improved lap times by over 12% compared to the Stanley controller, indicating strong potential for sim-to-real transfer.

**Ablation Studies (Figure 3):**
1. **Synchronization Trick (Figure 3a):** The synchronization trick was shown to be essential for stabilizing training. Without it, particularly when the residual network's initial scaling (`α_init`) was also modulated (set to 0), the training exhibited substantial instability, confirming its role in mitigating the non-stationarity introduced by the dynamic attenuation factor.
2. **Attenuation Schedule (Figure 3b):** Shorter attenuation schedules (e.g., `K_end` at 25% of total steps) were found to be more advantageous for performance. Longer schedules resulted in performance closer to standard RPL, suggesting that phasing out the base policy earlier allows the residual network more time to learn optimal behavior.
3. **Neural Network Design (Figure 3c):** The LayerNorm within the FusionBlock for feature normalization was identified as a critical architectural component. The AdaptiveSpatialSoftmax1d layer for LiDAR feature reduction was found to perform better than AveragePooling and Flatten, indicating its effectiveness in retaining topological structure while reducing feature space.

**Qualitative Analysis of Trajectories (Figure 4):**
- **α-RPO vs. Stanley (Figure 4a):** α-RPO's learned trajectories were observed to be shorter and closer to the inner walls in certain sections, enabling higher maximum speeds (exceeding 5 m/s) compared to Stanley's minimum-curvature lines.
- **α-RPO vs. DRL (Figures 4b, 4c):** On unseen test tracks like Toronto and Munich, α-RPO demonstrated more aggressive corner-cutting and optimized speed profiles (accelerating to higher speeds and braking harder) than the DRL baseline, contributing to significant lap-time reductions.

**Fine-Tuning on Single Racetracks (Table II):**
- Continuing training for 0.5 million steps on a single new racetrack after initial generalization training led to further performance improvements. Fine-tuned α-RPO agents (`α-RPO*`) achieved even lower lap times compared to their non-fine-tuned counterparts, highlighting the method's adaptability to specific environments.

**Real-World Experiments (Figure 5, Table III):**
1. **Deployment Efficiency:** α-RPO's design, yielding a standalone DNN, enabled low inference latencies of 3.5 ms on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super, including LiDAR preprocessing and ROS2 communication. This is considerably faster than reported localization-based stacks (e.g., 7.5 ms). The method eliminates the need for complex system integration with a base policy during deployment.
2. **Zero-Shot Real-World Transfer:** An agent trained entirely in simulation successfully generalized and drove robustly on the physical Munich racetrack (Figure 5a), which was not included in training. The agent consistently followed optimized trajectories.
3. **Real-World Performance:** Real-world lap times for α-RPO were marginally longer than in simulation (approx. 0.4 s increase), with maximum speeds around 5.0 m/s, indicating a minor simulation mismatch. Fine-tuned α-RPO* further improved real-world performance, achieving a finishing time of 28.1 s on Munich with 5.4 m/s max speed.
4. **Robustness:** The agent successfully adapted its trajectory to smoothly avoid static obstacles placed on the racetrack (Figure 5b), demonstrating consistent and repeatable collision avoidance behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces Attenuated Residual Policy Optimization (α-RPO), presenting it as a significant extension to the Residual Policy Learning (RPL) paradigm. The primary contribution of α-RPO is its ability to progressively attenuate the influence of a base policy during training, resulting in a standalone neural policy for deployment. This addresses critical limitations of traditional RPL, particularly the reliance on a base policy during inference and the constraints imposed by a static mixing factor.

The significance of α-RPO is primarily demonstrated through its empirical performance in the challenging domain of 1:10-scaled autonomous racing.
- **Improved Performance:** α-RPO consistently achieved superior driving performance, evidenced by faster lap times and higher maximum speeds in simulation compared to standard DRL and RPL baselines. The method effectively leverages the inductive bias of a base policy to bootstrap learning, while allowing the learned policy to eventually surpass the base policy's capabilities, leading to more optimal and aggressive racing lines.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The method exhibited strong generalization capabilities to unseen racetracks in simulation, maintaining high performance and avoiding collisions. This robustness was further validated through successful zero-shot real-world transfer to a physical Roboracer car, demonstrating consistent and repeatable behavior, including smooth obstacle avoidance.
- **Practical Deployment Efficiency:** A key impact of α-RPO is its practicality for real-world robotic deployment. By yielding a standalone neural policy, it eliminates the computational overhead and complex system integration associated with running a classical base controller at inference time. This results in significantly lower inference latencies (e.g., 3.5 ms on embedded hardware), making it suitable for resource-constrained autonomous systems and enabling faster decision-making in real-time applications. This advantage is crucial for competitive autonomous racing and other high-speed robotic tasks.
- **Stabilized Training:** The introduction of the "synchronization trick" within the PPO framework is a notable methodological contribution, ensuring stable training despite the non-stationarity induced by the changing base policy influence.

The potential impact of α-RPO extends beyond autonomous racing. The core concept of intelligently phasing out an inductive bias during training to achieve a high-performance, deployable neural policy could be broadly applicable to other domains of robotics and control where:
1. A strong classical controller or initial heuristic exists but needs to be surpassed.
2. Real-world deployment necessitates lightweight, low-latency inference.
3. Sim-to-real transfer is a primary challenge.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, α-RPO yields a non-verifiable neural policy, forfeiting the explicit safety guarantees inherent in classical control, which might be a concern for safety-critical applications. Secondly, the simulation parameters were empirically calibrated, not rigorously identified from real-world systems, suggesting that unmodeled dynamics could still limit performance at the extremes of vehicle handling. Future work could include rigorous system identification to improve sim-to-real fidelity. Thirdly, while fine-tuning on specific tracks proved effective, an online, real-world fine-tuning procedure is envisioned to further enhance adaptation. Lastly, the method's sensitivity to the quality of the base policy and the linearity of the attenuation schedule requires further investigation through additional ablations to understand their full relationship and explore alternative schedules. Extending α-RPO to other robotic control domains will be crucial for assessing its broader applicability.
