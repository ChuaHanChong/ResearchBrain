# Tactile Sim2Real without Tactile Simulation via Bottlenecked Latent Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> SBLR skips sensor-specific tactile simulation: policy trains on a simulator-native oracle sensor (point cloud plus fingertip forces), then rectified-flow networks align real sensor latents to oracle latents from unpaired random-play data, with a second RL stage bottlenecking reconstruction to close the information gap. Zero-shot hardware success reaches 85-97.5% across two tactile sensors, beating a physics-based simulation baseline by 7.5-15%.

## Problem

- Physics-based tactile simulation demands deep sensor-specific domain expertise, and computational approximations degrade the fidelity of the rendered signals used for training.
- Tactile sensor designs are highly diverse and evolve faster than engineers can build and validate matching physics-based simulators for each one.
- Domain randomization compensates for sim-to-real sensor mismatch but washes out fine-grained tactile detail, hurting high-precision contact-rich tasks.

## Method

- **Point Force Composition (PFC)**: a simulator-native oracle tactile representation combining fingertip and object point clouds with contact forces, needing no sensor-specific modeling.
- ==Rectified-Flow transformation networks== (bidirectional f_p2c and f_c2p) trained on nearest-neighbor pseudo-pairs from unpaired sim and real random-play data to align oracle and real sensor latents.
- **SBLR**'s two-stage RL with ==bottlenecked latent reconstruction==: Stage 1 trains on oracle PFC latents, Stage 2 adapts through round-trip oracle-to-real-to-oracle reconstruction to expose the policy to real information loss.

## Results

- Zero-shot hardware success of **92.5-97.5%** (GelSight Mini) and **85.0-92.5%** (DIGIT) across Peg Insertion and Gear Meshing, beating TacSL physics-based simulation baseline (77.5-82.5%) by **7.5-15%**.
- In simulated proxy-real evaluation (256 episodes), SBLR reaches **86.5-87.4%** average success versus 22.5-35.7% for Discrete Contact Position and 32.0-49.2% for a no-tactile baseline.
- Bottlenecked reconstruction stage consistently beats its own ablation (PFC+RF without Stage 2), with the largest gain on Nut Threading (**75.9%** vs 48.2% average), confirming the second RL stage matters most under high precision demands.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training on an information-richer oracle proxy plus post-hoc latent alignment can outrun careful physics-based sensor modeling, even with calibration effort invested in the baseline.
> - Exposing a policy to its own reconstruction-induced information loss during training, not just at deployment, is what lets it tolerate the gap between an idealized signal and a lossy real sensor.
> - Unpaired alignment via nearest-neighbor pseudo-pairing on shared proprioception sidesteps the need for synchronized sim-real data collection, a pattern portable to other hard-to-simulate modalities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a significant challenge in robot learning for contact-rich manipulation tasks: the accurate simulation of diverse and rapidly evolving tactile sensors for Sim2Real transfer. Traditional Sim2Real approaches often rely on high-fidelity sensor simulations to train policies in a virtual environment before deploying them in the real world. This reliance is particularly problematic for tactile sensors due to their varied designs, complex underlying physics, and the substantial domain expertise required to create and validate physics-based models for each sensor. Even with significant effort, simulated tactile readings rarely perfectly match real-world counterparts, necessitating extensive domain randomization which can obscure fine-grained sensory details crucial for precision tasks.

Previous research efforts in this domain include:
- **Unified Tactile Representation:** Some works focus on learning tactile representations transferable across different real sensors. While these approaches enable compatibility across hardware, they do not eliminate the need for sensor-specific simulation during policy training for Sim2Real transfer.
- **Physics-based Tactile Simulation:** Substantial progress has been made in generating realistic tactile simulations, with methods addressing aspects like image rendering, shadow processing, and modeling shear forces. However, these methods remain sensor-specific, demanding significant engineering and calibration efforts for each new sensor design, which can hinder the adoption of novel tactile sensors in simulation.
- **Cross-Domain Representation Alignment:** Research on aligning latent representations across different domains (e.g., sim and real, or human and robot) has explored various modalities. Some methods align tactile representations between simulation and reality, but often still necessitate physics-based sensor models or do not evaluate on robot manipulation tasks. Others focus on visual alignment for cameras or tactile gloves but do not directly address the challenge of tactile sensor simulation for policy training.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a method that explicitly *avoids* sensor-specific simulation altogether. Instead of simulating the real sensor, it leverages a generic "oracle sensor" available in simulation and then aligns real sensor data to this oracle's latent space. This approach positions itself as an alternative to the laborious and often imperfect process of physics-based tactile sensor modeling, aiming to reduce the barrier to integrating diverse tactile sensors into robot learning pipelines for Sim2Real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable effective Sim2Real transfer for contact-rich robot manipulation tasks, particularly those requiring tactile sensing, without the need for physics-based modeling and simulation of specific real-world tactile sensors. The authors propose to achieve this through a framework that trains policies on a readily available "oracle sensor" in simulation and subsequently adapts them for deployment with real tactile sensors.

The key motivations driving this objective are:

- **Diversity and Rapid Evolution of Tactile Sensors:** Tactile sensor designs are highly diverse and continuously evolving. Each new design typically requires a dedicated physics-based model, demanding substantial domain expertise in both computational simulation and sensor physics. This modeling process is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and often struggles to keep pace with sensor development.
- **Difficulty in Achieving High-Fidelity Tactile Simulation:** Despite advances in physics-based simulation, achieving perfect fidelity between simulated and real tactile sensor readings remains challenging. This discrepancy, known as the "sim-to-real gap" in the sensor modality, can degrade policy performance when transferred to the real world.
- **Limitations of Domain Randomization:** To bridge the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization is often employed, where simulation parameters are varied during training to make policies robust to real-world uncertainties. However, for tactile sensing, excessive domain randomization can "wash out" or obscure the fine-grained sensory details critical for high-precision manipulation tasks, potentially leading to suboptimal performance.
- **High Barrier to Adoption:** The necessity for sensor-specific simulation and calibration creates a high barrier for researchers and practitioners to adopt new tactile sensors or integrate them into complex manipulation systems. This limits the exploration and utilization of diverse tactile capabilities in robot learning.
- **Cost of Retraining for New Sensors:** Current methods often assume a specific sensor and require costly retraining of policies for each new sensor setup. The authors aim to develop a framework that allows a policy to be trained once in simulation and then deployed zero-shot or with minimal adaptation across different real sensors.
- **Addressing Information Asymmetry:** Real tactile sensors inherently convey different information content compared to an idealized simulator-native oracle. A policy trained solely on a rich oracle signal might struggle if the real sensor provides less information. The research aims to explicitly address this "information gap" during training.

In summary, the core motivation is to simplify and accelerate the process of developing tactile-enabled robotic manipulation by eliminating the most significant bottleneck: the engineering effort and fidelity challenges associated with tactile sensor simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Sim2Real via Bottlenecked Latent Reconstruction (SBLR), operates in four main steps, circumventing sensor-specific tactile simulation by aligning real sensor latent embeddings to those of a simulator-native oracle sensor.

#### 3.1. A. Alignment Data Construction

The framework begins by collecting unpaired random-play data in both simulation and the real world.
- **Process:** The robot is commanded to make automated random probing contacts with a fixed object in both environments. This ensures that the collected data represents the contact distribution expected during inference.
- **Pseudo-Pairing:** Since exact ground-truth pairings between simulation and real-world contacts are difficult to obtain, pseudo-pairs are formed using a nearest-neighbor search. This search is performed over proprioceptive transitions (robot joint positions at current and previous time steps) because a fixed object across domains means similar proprioceptive transitions indicate similar robot-object contacts. The similarity metric considers the L2 distance of current and previous proprioceptive states.

#### 3.2. B. PFC and Image Encoder Training

Raw point clouds and images are high-dimensional. To facilitate cross-modal transformation, they are embedded into lower-dimensional latent spaces.
- **PFC Encoder (f_pfc):** Point Force Composition (PFC) is used as the oracle sensor, encoding object full point clouds (X_o), fingertip point clouds (X_i), and contact forces (f_i).
 - For point cloud components (X_o, X_i), a Point Masked Autoencoder (Point-MAE) is trained to produce a global latent representation (z_x).
 - For the force component (f_i), a separate autoencoder is trained with a reconstruction loss to produce a latent representation (z_f).
 - The overall PFC latent (z_p) is the concatenation of z_x and z_f. This encoder is trained solely on the simulation dataset.
- **Tactile Encoder (f_c):** For visuotactile sensors, a Masked Autoencoder (MAE) encodes readings from each sensor (c_i) into a per-sensor latent (z_c_i). The multi-sensor tactile latent (z_c) is the concatenation of these per-sensor latents. This encoder is trained solely on the real-world dataset.
Both encoders are trained in a self-supervised manner and are frozen during subsequent policy training.

#### 3.3. C. Rectified-Flow-Based Transformation Networks

Two transformation networks, f_p→c (oracle latent to estimated real sensor latent) and f_c→p (real sensor latent to estimated oracle latent), are trained using Rectified Flow (RF).
- **Purpose:** RF is used because it can model multimodal distributions, which is beneficial when there is information asymmetry (e.g., a less informative real sensor latent might correspond to multiple oracle latents).
- **Training:** Given the pseudo-pairs from the alignment data, a velocity field is trained to map samples from a source Gaussian noise distribution to the paired target latent. This involves integrating the velocity field to generate the target latent.
- **Conditioning:** The transformation networks condition only on the source latent (z_p or z_c) and do not use robot proprioception, to avoid overfitting to off-task play data.

#### 3.4. D. RL Training with PFC (Stage 1)

In the first stage, a base RL policy (π_θ) is trained in simulation using the oracle PFC latents (f_pfc(X, f)).
- **Objective:** To acquire fundamental task proficiency using rich, simulator-native information. This stage is sensor-agnostic.
- **Randomization:** Light Gaussian noise is added to object pose and contact forces during PFC rendering. This is to make the policy robust to imperfect PFC reconstructions expected from real sensors and to artifacts from simplified physics models, without washing out fine details.
- **Deployment Goal:** At inference, real tactile readings will be mapped into this PFC latent space using f_c→p, enabling the same policy to be applied.

#### 3.5. E. RL Training with Bottlenecked Latent Reconstruction (Stage 2)

This second RL stage adapts the policy to the information gap between the ideal PFC and the real tactile readings.
- **Initialization:** The policy is initialized with the weights from the Stage 1 PFC policy.
- **Bottlenecking Process:** At each simulation time step:
 1. The simulated PFC latent (z_p) is mapped to an estimated real tactile latent: ˆz_c = f_p→c(z_p). This step simulates the information loss that would occur if a real sensor were used.
 2. The estimated real tactile latent (ˆz_c) is then mapped back to an estimated oracle latent: ˆz_p = f_c→p(ˆz_c).
- **Policy Training:** The RL policy is then trained using this bottlenecked latent (ˆz_p) as input. This process exposes the policy to the information limitations and potential reconstruction errors that will be present when using real sensors.
- **Frozen Networks:** All encoders and transformation networks remain frozen during this stage.
- **Rationale:** This two-stage approach allows for efficient adaptation to new sensor setups, requiring only a lightweight second stage of RL training rather than full retraining.

#### 3.6. F. Inference

During real-world deployment, only real tactile readings (c) are available.
- **Process:** The real tactile readings are first encoded by the tactile encoder (f_c) to obtain z_c. This real tactile latent is then mapped into the PFC latent space using the f_c→p transformation network to produce ˆz_p.
- **Policy Execution:** The adapted policy (π_θ) then takes non-tactile observations (o) and this reconstructed PFC latent (ˆz_p) as input to determine the action: a = π_θ(o, f_c→p(f_c(c))). All encoders and transformation networks remain frozen.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents both simulation and real-world hardware experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of SBLR.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments

Simulation experiments were designed to decouple the modality alignment gap from the dynamics gap of Sim2Real transfer. This was done by transferring policies from a "nominal simulation environment" (using PFC) to a "proxy-real environment" (using physics-based tactile simulation). The tasks included Peg Insertion, Gear Meshing, and Nut Threading from the Factory benchmark.

**Baselines and Oracles:**
- **SBLR (Ours):** The full proposed method.
- **DCP (Discrete Contact Position):** A baseline that uses a coarse, discretized contact position to avoid complex tactile simulation.
- **OT (Optimal Transport):** A baseline using optimal transport for modality alignment, adapted from image alignment.
- **No Tactile:** Policy receives only proprioception, isolating the contribution of tactile sensing.
- **Ablation (PFC + RF):** SBLR without the second RL stage (bottlenecked latent reconstruction).
- **Image Oracle:** Policy trained directly on tactile images in the proxy-real environment. Represents an upper bound for performance with direct access to proxy-real tactile readings.
- **PFC Oracle:** Policy trained and evaluated with direct access to PFC input. Represents an upper bound for performance with the oracle sensor.

**Results (Table I):**
- **SBLR Performance:** SBLR achieved average success rates of 87.4% (Peg Insertion), 86.5% (Gear Meshing), and 75.9% (Nut Threading).
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - SBLR significantly outperformed DCP (22.5-35.7% average) and No Tactile (32.0-49.2% average) across all tasks, indicating the necessity of rich tactile information.
 - SBLR also outperformed OT (52.3-75.4% average), suggesting that Rectified Flow and nearest-neighbor pseudo-pairing were more effective for modality alignment in this context.
- **Impact of Bottlenecked Latent Reconstruction (SBLR vs. Ablation):** SBLR consistently improved upon its ablation (PFC + RF). The performance gap was modest for Peg Insertion (87.4% vs. 80.3%) and Gear Meshing (86.5% vs. 83.3%), likely because the visuotactile sensor in the proxy-real environment provided much of the information available in PFC. However, the improvement was more substantial for Nut Threading (75.9% vs. 48.2%), highlighting the importance of adapting to the information gap for tasks requiring higher precision.
- **Comparison to Oracles:** SBLR approached the performance of the Image Oracle (86.6-88.2% average) and PFC Oracle (88.2-93.4% average) on Peg Insertion and Gear Meshing. On Nut Threading, SBLR had a larger gap to the oracles (75.9% vs. 87.6% Image Oracle, 93.4% PFC Oracle), indicating that while SBLR is effective, some performance margin remains for the most sensitive tasks.

#### 4.2. B. Real-World Experiments

Hardware experiments involved deploying the trained policies zero-shot on Peg Insertion and Gear Meshing tasks with two different visuotactile sensors: GelSight Mini and DIGIT. The observation space included an additional previous action, and dynamics randomization was applied during simulation training to address the sim-to-real dynamics gap.

**Baselines:**
- **SBLR (Ours):** Full method.
- **Ablation (PFC + RF):** SBLR without the second RL stage.
- **TacSL:** A physics-based tactile simulation baseline requiring calibration and image augmentation.

**Results (Table II):**
- **SBLR Zero-Shot Success:** SBLR demonstrated high zero-shot success rates:
 - GelSight Mini: 92.5% (Peg), 97.5% (Gear)
 - DIGIT: 92.5% (Peg), 85.0% (Gear)
- **Comparison to Ablation:** SBLR consistently outperformed its ablation, with larger gaps observed for DIGIT (e.g., 85.0% vs. 75.0% for Gear Meshing). This reinforces the finding from simulation experiments that the bottlenecked latent reconstruction stage is crucial for adapting to the specific information characteristics of real sensors, especially less informative ones like DIGIT.
- **Comparison to TacSL:** SBLR consistently outperformed the physics-based TacSL baseline across all tasks and sensors. For instance, on GelSight Mini, SBLR achieved 92.5% (Peg) and 97.5% (Gear) compared to TacSL's 77.5% and 82.5%, respectively. On DIGIT, SBLR achieved 92.5% (Peg) and 85.0% (Gear) compared to TacSL's 77.5% for both. This suggests that training directly on real tactile data from random contacts, without relying on physics-based models and their associated assumptions and calibration errors, leads to better Sim2Real transfer performance.
- **Cross-Sensor Applicability:** The high success rates across both GelSight Mini and DIGIT sensors demonstrate the framework's ability to adapt to different tactile sensors without requiring sensor-specific modeling or extensive retraining.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper, Sim2Real via Bottlenecked Latent Reconstruction (SBLR), offers a significant alternative to current practices for integrating tactile sensing into robotic manipulation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Elimination of Sensor-Specific Simulation:** The most direct impact is the demonstration that effective zero-shot Sim2Real transfer for tactile-rich tasks can be achieved *without* developing physics-based models for each specific tactile sensor. This addresses a major bottleneck in robot learning, as creating and validating such simulations is resource-intensive, requires specialized domain expertise, and is often prone to fidelity issues.
- **Reduced Development Effort and Cost:** By bypassing the need for sensor-specific simulation, SBLR substantially reduces the engineering effort, time, and computational resources typically required to set up new tactile sensors in a simulation environment. This lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners to experiment with and deploy a wider range of tactile sensors.
- **Enhanced Robustness to Sensor Diversity:** The framework's ability to adapt to different real tactile sensors (demonstrated with GelSight Mini and DIGIT) without requiring fundamental changes to the policy or retraining from scratch is a key strength. This promotes the use of diverse tactile modalities, allowing for sensor choices based on task requirements rather than simulation availability.
- **Effective Handling of Information Asymmetry:** The two-stage RL training procedure, particularly the "bottlenecked latent reconstruction" stage, is crucial. It explicitly addresses the inherent information gap between a rich oracle sensor and potentially less informative real-world tactile sensors. By exposing the policy to the expected information loss during training, it ensures greater robustness at deployment.
- **Improved Sim2Real Performance:** The experimental results show that SBLR outperforms a physics-based tactile simulation baseline (TacSL) in zero-shot hardware deployment. This suggests that the proposed method, by learning directly from real contact data for alignment, can yield more accurate and robust tactile representations for policies compared to relying on potentially imperfect physics models.
- **Potential for Broader Application:** The authors suggest that the framework could extend beyond its current application. It offers a generalized method to adapt any pretrained policy to new sensor setups or even new modalities by leveraging the lightweight second-stage RL for adaptation. This could be particularly impactful for large pretrained models (e.g., Vision-Language-Action models) where full retraining for each new sensor is prohibitively expensive.
- **Data Efficiency:** The alignment module relies on unpaired random-play data collected automatically in simulation and the real world. This type of data collection is less demanding than requiring paired data or on-task expert demonstrations, contributing to the practicality of the approach.

**Limitations:** The authors acknowledge that the method primarily addresses the sensor modality gap, but not the dynamics gap inherent in Sim2Real transfer, which still requires techniques like domain randomization. Additionally, for highly precise tasks, random interaction data might not sufficiently cover all critical contact scenarios, suggesting potential improvements through more targeted data collection methods.

In conclusion, SBLR represents a step towards making tactile sensing more accessible and practical for robotic applications by offering a simulation-free paradigm for Sim2Real transfer, thereby facilitating the rapid integration of novel and diverse tactile hardware.
