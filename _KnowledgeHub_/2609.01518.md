# A System for Fast, Resilient, and Adaptable Loco-Manipulation Behaviors on Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> IHMC's system is a robot-local, runtime-editable behavior architecture for humanoid loco-manipulation that combines object-centric affordance templates, a behavior tree with a persistent next-execution pointer, and behavior-time onboard perception, kept synchronized with an operator interface via 30 Hz CRDT updates. On the Alex humanoid it traverses a push door in 34 seconds and sorts six balls in 45 seconds under human disturbance.

## Problem

- Classical model-based humanoid and mobile-manipulator door systems take over a minute per traversal, lean on fiducial markers or motion capture, and lack robot-local authored recovery.
- Learned door policies are fast but adapt only through retraining, often run vision inference off-board, and cover a narrow set of door variants.
- Existing behavior-tree and affordance-template stacks require redeploy or restart cycles to change logic, and report either speed or authoring effort, never both.

## Method

- A ==runtime-editable behavior tree==, treated as one ==depth-first sequence== with a persistent next-execution index, ==fallback== try/catch nodes, ==goto nodes==, and ==execute-after concurrency dependencies==.
- Four-part node decomposition (UI, executor, runtime state, JSON definition) synchronized as ==Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs)== at 30 Hz over ==ROS 2 DDS== with clock-offset-corrected last-writer arbitration.
- ==Object-centric action definition== plus a behavior scene: ==YOLO== and ==FoundationPose== scene actions, a ==screw primitive== for constrained opening, visual door-state estimation, and depth-based table-edge detection yielding reusable approach frames.

## Results

- Alex traversed a left push lever door in **34 seconds** and a right pull door in **45 seconds**, versus **106 seconds** for hard-coded Atlas.
- Repeated trials gave **11/11** push and **12/12** pull approach-and-opening successes; a disturbed left pull traversal absorbed **five** human interventions and still finished in **65 seconds**.
- Authoring: **31:43** for a looping H1-2 opening (**32/32** repeats), **1:59:48** for a scratch push-door traversal, and **1:13:49** to compose bottle pickup with door carry-through.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Anchoring actions in perceived object frames rather than robot frames re-anchors each step, absorbing state-estimator drift that would compound across long ego-centric sequences.
> - Runtime editability turns the diagnose-fix-retest loop into minutes, making architecture, not policy learning, the lever for adapting behavior to new task variants.
> - Caution: reported reliability covers approach and opening only; full traversal remained limited by walking-controller quality, so architecture-level claims do not cover locomotion robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenges of deploying humanoid robots in human-centric environments for tasks that are physically demanding, hazardous, or repetitive. A capable robot in such environments requires an autonomy system that can coordinate complex functionalities including locomotion, whole-body manipulation, real-time perception, contact interaction, and operator supervision. The authors contend that the design of the behavior architecture itself can be a primary factor in achieving speed, reliability, and adaptability in robot performance. Their system focuses on runtime editability as a mechanism to accelerate behavior creation, adaptation, extension, and combination.

The work builds upon established theoretical foundations in robotics, specifically drawing inspiration from Affordance Templates for reusable, environment-centric behavior parameterization, the organizational and logical structure provided by Behavior Trees (though adapted in their implementation), and the principles of Coactive Design for human-robot team synergy.

Within the existing literature, this work is positioned in comparison to both classical, model-based mobile manipulator and humanoid systems, as well as more recent learning-based approaches for tasks such as door traversal. The authors highlight that their approach is distinct in its combination of robot-local execution, a runtime-editable behavior structure, and behavior-time perception, all validated through real-world demonstrations on humanoid robots. This contrasts with some learned systems that may rely on off-board computation for perception or focus on policy retraining rather than dynamic structural edits. The paper specifically compares its performance against systems like the DARPA Robotics Challenge-era Atlas, Nadia, and various wheeled manipulators and quadruped robots for door traversal, as well as emerging learned humanoid door policies such as DoorMan.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to develop an autonomy system that enables humanoid robots to reliably perform demanding tasks in human-designed spaces. The central claim is that the proposed behavior autonomy architecture is a primary enabler for fast, resilient, and adaptive humanoid robot behaviors.

To substantiate this claim, the authors delineate three primary research questions:
1. **Behavior Architecture Sufficiency:** To identify a concrete behavior architecture capable of delivering fast and robust performance across a spectrum of humanoid loco-manipulation behaviors.
2. **Comparative Performance:** To compare the proposed architecture's performance against prior IHMC baseline systems and established reinforcement learning door systems on shared metrics such as traversal time, operational reliability, and coverage of task variations.
3. **Runtime Editability Impact:** To quantify how a runtime-editable behavior structure influences the time and procedural steps required for an expert operator to create new behaviors and to adapt, extend, and compose existing ones.

These questions are supported by a set of hypotheses that guided the system's development:
1. Robot-local execution, combined with a synchronized user interface state, concurrent action layering, reactive tree logic, and behavior-time semantic perception, will result in door behaviors that are faster and more reliable than previous IHMC baselines and competitive with reported reinforcement learning systems for similar door tasks.
2. The runtime-editable nature of behaviors and perception modules will reduce the iteration cycle required to diagnose failures, modify logic, and re-test on the robot, offering an advantage over conventional redeploy, restart, or retraining workflows.
3. The decomposition of behaviors into reusable primitives, subtrees, and scene actions will enable the development of new door and loco-manipulation variants through minor edits to existing behaviors, rather than requiring construction from first principles.

Door traversal serves as a primary benchmark task due to its complexity, which necessitates coordination of precise foot placement, multi-phase manipulation (unlatching, opening, clearing), balance maintenance, and collision avoidance in a compact and repeatable setting. While doors are the main benchmark, the architecture is intended for broader loco-manipulation applications, demonstrated by tasks such as reactive ball sorting. The motivation emphasizes addressing the real-world utility gap for humanoid robots by enhancing their speed, resilience to variations and disturbances, and ease of adaptation by human operators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on a robot-local, runtime-editable behavior authoring and execution system. The core architecture integrates object-centric Affordance Templates, a tree-based structure for task logic, and a behavior-managed perception scene.

**System Architecture and Processes:** The system is decomposed into three main domains: Behavior Coordination, Perception, and Whole-Body Control. The Perception and Autonomy processes run onboard the robot, encompassing camera data ingestion, object detection (YOLO), pose estimation, and behavior tree/scene updates. The Whole-Body Control process, also robot-local, handles low-level hardware I/O, state estimation, and whole-body control. The Operator UI process runs on a separate computer, providing the behavior editor, 3D digital twin visualization, and system monitoring. This decoupling enhances robustness and allows high-rate data to remain local on the robot.

**Behavior Tree Structure:** The authors implement a "tree of behavior" that adopts concepts from Behavior Trees (sequence, fallback, condition, action nodes) but deviates from a strict formalism. The entire tree is treated as a large sequence, with executable elements being the leaves in depth-first order. A "next execution index," modifiable by the operator or the system, determines the next node to execute, similar to a state machine. Concurrency is managed via "execute after" dependencies, allowing flexible scheduling. Each node is decomposed into a robot-side executor, an operator-side UI component, a synchronized runtime state, and a persistent JSON definition.

**Robot-Operator Data Synchronization:** To enable runtime editability, the behavior tree and scene state are formulated as Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs). These are synchronized at 30 Hz between the robot and operator UI using ROS 2 DDS. Conflict resolution for concurrently modified (bidirectional) fields employs a latest-timestamp algorithm, supported by a clock-offset estimator. Unidirectional status fields (e.g., execution status) are mirrored from robot to UI. Bandwidth is conserved by sending compact partial data payloads unless a full payload is required.

**Node Library:** The system includes a comprehensive library of node types:
- **Control/Logic Nodes:** Sequence, Fallback, Condition, Goto, Checkpoint.
- **Physical Actions:** Neck Action, Spine Action, Walk Action, Arm Action, Screw Primitive Action, Pelvis Action, Ability Hand Action, Wait Action, Leg Action.
- **Perception/Scene Management Nodes:** Scene Action (for configuring YOLO, FoundationPose, persistent detections, managing scene objects), Door Traversal (generalized logic for specific door types).

**Object-Centric Action Definition:** A core design principle is defining physical actions (e.g., hand poses, footsteps) relative to perceived environmental objects or robot parts, rather than a fixed world or ego-centric frame. This approach reduces compounding errors from state estimator drift and control inaccuracies, and enhances behavior reusability and adaptability to varied starting conditions.

**Behavior-Time Perception:** The system features a dedicated "behavior scene" that manages active persistent detections and a privileged list of objects for use by behaviors. This provides behaviors with direct control over their perceptual dependencies. Specialized scene actions allow the creation of derived scene objects:
- **Door Panel Object:** Derived from stable YOLO detections of the opening mechanism and door panel, inferring panel orientation.
- **Door Frame Object:** Extends the Door Panel by estimating semantic door geometry (hinge location, push/pull type, open angle) from depth data using vertical 3D capsule sweeps and point counting.
- **Approach Table Object:** Detects table edges using depth point clouds via vertical capsule sweeps, projecting a squared-up reference frame onto the ground for invariant table approaches.
- **Composite and Hybrid Frames:** Perception-less objects (e.g., approach frames, hybrid frames) derived geometrically from existing scene frames to provide flexible reference points for actions.

**Shape-Contains Condition Node:** A GPU-accelerated CUDA kernel is used to count points and average color within virtual shapes (spheres, capsules) in the depth point cloud. This provides fast (under 10 ms), robust, semantic-free perceptual reactivity for condition nodes, enabling reactive behaviors like detecting open doors, obstacles, or specific colored objects in hand.

**Whole-Body Controller:** The behavior layer interfaces with an IHMC-developed whole-body controller based on linear momentum rate control and inverse dynamics. This controller supports asynchronous commands for various body parts and footsteps, integrating them into balanced whole-body motions.

**Operator Interface (RDX):** The user interface, built on the Robot Data eXplorer framework, provides a comprehensive environment for behavior authoring, 3D visualization of the robot and scene, direct teleoperation, and system monitoring.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the system focused on three primary lenses: speed, resilience, and adaptability, demonstrated across six task variants on Unitree H1-2 and Alex humanoid robots.

**Capability and Speed:**
- **Door Traversals:** An Alex right pull lever-handle door traversal was completed in 45 seconds (12s approach, 4s unlatch, 15s panel opening, 14s traversal). A left push lever door traversal on Alex was completed in 34 seconds. These timings are competitive with recent learned systems (e.g., DoorMan average 15.4s, Zhang et al. 10-11.5s on quadruped) and significantly faster than classical model-based approaches (e.g., Atlas 2021 hard-coded pull door at 106s). Limitations in Alex's spine yaw range were noted as impacting speed on pull doors.
- **Loco-Manipulation:** A reactive single-table ball-sorting task, involving sorting six balls by color under human disturbance, was completed in 45.2 seconds. An extended two-table ball sorting task, where the robot sorted nine balls across two tables with locomotion, was completed in 128 seconds.

**Reliability:**
- Repeated trials demonstrated 11 out of 11 successful push-door approaches and openings, and 12 out of 12 successful pull-door approaches and openings on a lab door.
- Full doorway traversal reliability was not fully quantified due to concerns about the walking controller's performance at the time of testing, which could have risked hardware damage. However, the successful approach and opening trials suggest high reliability for these core behavioral segments.

**Resilience:**
- A left pull door traversal, designed with fallback nodes and spatial occupancy checks, successfully recovered from four human disturbances (door pulled out of hand) during the opening phase and waited for a human to clear the doorway before proceeding. The task completed in 65 seconds overall.
- The single-table reactive ball-sorting behavior demonstrated resilience by detecting a failed pick and aborting a subsequent placement, returning to the search phase. It correctly sorted six balls despite continuous human interference.

**Fast Behavior Authoring:**
- **From Scratch Creation:**
 - On a Unitree H1-2, a looping standing right pull lever-handle opening behavior was authored from an empty tree to autonomous looping execution in 31 minutes and 43 seconds of active authoring time.
 - On Alex, the initial full pull lever door traversal, which involved significant platform-specific tuning, required 11 hours, 10 minutes, and 17 seconds of active authoring across five sessions.
 - A subsequent left push door traversal on Alex, built from scratch, was brought to first fully automatic success in 1 hour, 59 minutes, and 48 seconds, including a 9-minute fix for an arm taskspace error.
- These results suggest that novel behaviors can be authored in the hours regime, with a rapid diagnose-edit-retest loop.

**Fast Adaptation, Extension, and Combination of Behaviors:**
- **Behavior Composition:** An existing bottle pickup behavior was combined with a left push door traversal. The first autonomous success for picking up a bottle and carrying it through a door was achieved in 1 hour, 13 minutes, and 49 seconds of authoring. The autonomous run took 64 seconds.
- **Behavior Adaptation:** A lab right pull door traversal behavior was adapted to a break room left pull door. This involved using a mirroring feature and then 1 hour, 54 minutes, and 3 seconds of tuning, including adding a spine yaw motion for opening and re-authoring traversal footsteps for increased robustness due to a lack of a spring closer.
- **Behavior Extension:** The single-table reactive ball-sorting behavior was extended to sort colored balls between two tables, requiring locomotion between stations. This extension took 1 hour, 50 minutes, and 41 seconds of authoring (including pre-authoring). The resulting behavior autonomously sorted 9 out of 9 balls correctly, performing three accurate table approaches, in 2 minutes and 8 seconds.
- These sessions demonstrate the system's capacity for combining, adapting, and extending existing behaviors to new tasks within an hours-long timeframe, attributing this speed to the decomposition into reusable primitives, subtrees, and scene actions, and runtime editability.

**Comparative Analysis:** The paper presents quantitative comparisons, illustrating that its door traversal speeds are significantly faster than classical model-based systems and competitive with recent learned approaches, while uniquely providing measured behavior authoring and adaptation times. The runtime-editable workflow is contrasted qualitatively with an estimated learned policy pipeline (e.g., DoorMan), suggesting substantial time savings for creating and adapting behaviors due to localized, online edits versus full policy retraining cycles.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the advancement of humanoid robot autonomy by presenting a robust, runtime-editable behavior architecture. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Enabling Rapid Development:** The core finding regarding rapid behavior authoring (hours for novel behaviors, less for adaptation/composition) represents a notable step towards accelerating the deployment of humanoid robots. This capability for quick iteration and modification on real hardware can reduce development costs and time-to-solution, making humanoid robots more accessible for practical applications.
- **Enhanced Robot Capability and Resilience:** The system enables fast loco-manipulation behaviors that are demonstrated to be resilient to human-induced disturbances and task variations. This increases the reliability of robot operations in unpredictable environments, a crucial factor for real-world deployment.
- **Leveraging Human Expertise:** The Coactive Design principles embedded in the system's operator interface empower expert human operators to directly author, monitor, and refine robot behaviors. This synergistic approach effectively combines human intelligence with robotic execution capabilities, which is particularly impactful for complex and novel tasks.
- **Generalizability and Reusability:** The architecture's reliance on object-centric action definitions, reusable primitives, subtrees, and specialized scene objects (like Door Panel/Frame, Approach Table) facilitates the generalization of behaviors across different robot platforms and varied task instances. This reusability reduces the effort required to configure robots for new environments or tasks.
- **Competitive Performance with Transparency:** The system achieves loco-manipulation speeds competitive with advanced learning-based methods, while maintaining a transparent, model-based, and human-interpretable behavior logic. This allows for easier diagnosis of failures and targeted modifications, which can be challenging in purely black-box learned systems.
- **Foundation for Future Hybrid Systems:** The design allows for the integration of learning-based low-level routines as nodes within the behavior tree, suggesting a pathway for future hybrid autonomy systems that leverage the strengths of both model-based and learning-based approaches. This could extend the capabilities of humanoid robots to more diverse and unstructured environments.

Overall, this work enhances the practical utility of humanoid robots by providing a framework that is both performant and adaptable, streamlining the process by which complex behaviors are developed and deployed.
