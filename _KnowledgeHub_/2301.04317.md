# Teleoperation of Humanoid Robots: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive survey consolidates the current state of humanoid robot teleoperation systems, technologies, and methodologies, outlining their general architecture, specific components, and applications while also identifying critical challenges and future research directions.

## Problem

- Fully autonomous humanoid systems remain limited in their ability to robustly handle complex, unstructured, and hazardous environments.
- There was a recognized gap in the literature for a comprehensive, structured overview of existing humanoid robot teleoperation systems, technologies, and methodologies.
- A practical solution was needed to enable humans to control robots remotely for tasks such as disaster response, space exploration, and healthcare, leveraging human intelligence where autonomy falls short.

## Method

- The paper provides a comprehensive survey of humanoid robot teleoperation, synthesizing current research and categorizing technologies and methodologies.
- It structures the field by detailing components of the general teleoperation architecture, from human interface technologies and feedback mechanisms to retargeting, control strategies, and communication challenges.
- The survey identifies key evaluation metrics and outlines current applications, culminating in a discussion of ongoing challenges and future research directions for the field.

## Results

- A generalized architecture for humanoid robot teleoperation systems was identified, comprising a Human-Machine Interface, Retargeting and Control Algorithms, a Communication Channel, and the Robot Platform itself.
- Various human interface technologies were detailed, including motion capture systems (optical, IMU suits, exoskeletons) and physiological measurements, alongside feedback systems encompassing visual (VR), auditory, haptic, and thermal modalities.
- Current applications of humanoid teleoperation were surveyed across critical domains such as disaster response (e.g., Fukushima), industrial tasks, healthcare, and space exploration, demonstrating the practical utility of these systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Teleoperation provides an intermediate solution that combines human cognitive abilities with robotic physical capabilities, particularly for tasks in complex or hazardous environments.
> - The effectiveness of humanoid teleoperation systems critically depends on the seamless integration and bilateral flow of information across four core components: Human-Machine Interface, Retargeting and Control Algorithms, Communication Channel, and Robot Platform.
> - Major ongoing challenges in the field include reducing operator cognitive load, enhancing system robustness, optimizing the balance in shared autonomy, and establishing standardization for interfaces and evaluation metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction and Research Landscape

Humanoid robot teleoperation has emerged as a critical research area that enables humans to control robots remotely for tasks in hazardous environments, disaster response scenarios, space exploration, and healthcare. While fully autonomous humanoid systems remain limited in their ability to handle complex, unstructured environments, teleoperation offers a practical intermediate solution by combining human cognitive abilities with robotic physical capabilities.

The field has gained significant momentum following events such as the Fukushima disaster, the DARPA Robotics Challenge, the ANA Avatar XPRIZE, and various space exploration initiatives, which have demonstrated both the potential and limitations of current teleoperation technologies. This paper synthesizes the current state of research while identifying critical challenges and future research directions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Survey Objectives and Scope

This survey paper, authored by researchers from multiple leading institutions including the Istituto Italiano di Tecnologia, Florida Institute for Human and Machine Cognition, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, and others, addresses an important gap in the literature by providing a comprehensive overview of humanoid robot teleoperation systems, technologies, and methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. General Architecture of Humanoid Teleoperation Systems

Humanoid robot teleoperation systems typically follow a general architecture consisting of four primary components:

1. **Human-Machine Interface (HMI)**: Measures human movement and physiological signals and provides feedback to the operator
2. **Retargeting and Control Algorithms**: Translates human commands to robot movements
3. **Communication Channel**: Transfers information between the operator and robot
4. **Robot Platform and Environment Interaction**: Executes commands and gathers sensory information

This architecture creates a bilateral control system where information flows in both directions. The operator receives feedback about the robot's state and environment, while the robot receives control commands from the operator. The effectiveness of this system depends on the integration of these components and addressing challenges such as communication delays, control stability, and intuitive interfaces.

#### 3.2. Human Interface Technologies

Human interface technologies for teleoperation can be divided into two main categories:

1. **Human Measurement Systems**: These capture the operator's movements, physiological signals, and intentions.

 - **Motion Capture Systems**: Options range from full-body optical systems with markers to wearable inertial measurement unit (IMU) suits, exoskeletons, and even simple joysticks or haptic devices for partial body control.

 - **Physiological Measurements**: Beyond motion, systems may capture electrocardiogram (ECG), electromyography (EMG), electroencephalography (EEG), heart rate, perspiration, respiration, and eye-tracking data to assess operator state and intention.

2. **Feedback Systems**: These provide the operator with information about the robot and its environment.

 - **Visual Feedback**: From simple camera feeds to immersive virtual reality (VR) headsets with stereoscopic vision
 - **Auditory Feedback**: Environmental sounds and system alerts
 - **Haptic Feedback**: Force feedback devices, vibrotactile systems, and exoskeletons that can apply resistance to match robot experiences
 - **Thermal Feedback**: Temperature information from the robot's environment

The choice of interface significantly impacts the operator's comfort, intuition, and ability to effectively control the humanoid. The trade-offs between complexity, cost, and effectiveness remain a key research consideration.

#### 3.3. Retargeting and Control Strategies

Converting human movements to appropriate robot commands involves several technical challenges due to differences in kinematics, dynamics, and capabilities between humans and robots.

#### 3.4. Motion Retargeting Approaches

Motion retargeting bridges the gap between human and robot motion and can be implemented through:

1. **Joint-to-Joint Mapping**: Directly maps human joint angles to robot joint angles, typically requiring scaling and adaptation due to differences in proportion and joint ranges.

2. **Task Space Mapping**: Maps human end-effector positions (hands, feet) to robot end-effectors, which can accommodate significant differences in kinematics.

3. **Simplified Virtual Model Control**: Uses reduced models like inverted pendulums to capture essential human movement features.

#### 3.5. Robot Control Frameworks

Robot control frameworks for teleoperation typically incorporate:

1. **Whole-Body Control**: Coordinates multiple tasks (balance, manipulation, gaze) with appropriate prioritization.

2. **Balance and Locomotion Control**: Several approaches exist, including:

 - **Zero Moment Point (ZMP)**: Ensures dynamic stability by controlling the point where ground reaction forces have no moment in the horizontal direction
 - **Divergent Component of Motion (DCM)**: Separates stable and unstable dynamics for more robust control
 - **Contact Wrench Control**: Directly controls contact forces for more agile movement

3. **Shared Autonomy**: Blends direct teleoperation with autonomous capabilities where the robot handles low-level stability and safety while the operator provides high-level commands.

#### 3.6. Communication Challenges

Communication between operator and robot introduces several challenges that impact teleoperation performance:

1. **Time Delay**: Even small delays (>100ms) can significantly reduce operator performance and system stability. Delays are particularly problematic in space applications or internet-based teleoperation.

2. **Bandwidth Limitations**: Restrict the amount of sensory feedback and the update rate of commands, particularly for rich sensory data like video.

3. **Packet Loss**: Can result in missing commands or feedback, requiring robust handling mechanisms.

Multiple approaches address these challenges:

- **Predictive Displays**: Show the operator a simulated prediction of robot response before actual feedback arrives
- **Wave Variables**: Mathematical transformations that ensure stability despite delays
- **Model-Based Prediction**: Uses local models to predict robot behavior and provide immediate feedback
- **Data Compression**: Reduces bandwidth requirements while preserving essential information

#### 3.7. Feedback Mechanisms

Effective feedback is crucial for successful teleoperation, as it provides the operator with situational awareness and a sense of telepresence. Key feedback types include:

1. **Visual Feedback**: Ranges from standard monitors to immersive VR, with options for augmented reality overlays to enhance situational understanding.

2. **Force Feedback**: Transmits contact forces experienced by the robot to the operator through:
 - Kinesthetic devices (exoskeletons, haptic manipulators)
 - Vibrotactile feedback (vibration motors placed on the operator's body)
 - Surface haptics (devices that can change texture or stiffness)

3. **Auditory Feedback**: Provides environmental sounds, collision warnings, and system status information without overloading visual attention.

4. **Multimodal Feedback**: Combines multiple sensory channels for more complete situational awareness while managing cognitive load.

The challenge lies in providing rich feedback without overwhelming the operator while working within technical constraints of the system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics

Evaluating teleoperation systems requires considering both objective performance and subjective user experience. The survey identifies several key metrics:

1. **Task Performance Metrics**:
 - Completion time
 - Success rate
 - Precision of movements
 - Energy efficiency

2. **User Experience Metrics**:
 - Cognitive workload (measured via NASA TLX or other tools)
 - Situational awareness
 - Learning curve and skill acquisition rate
 - User comfort and fatigue

3. **System Metrics**:
 - Communication delays and jitter
 - Control stability
 - Robustness to disturbances
 - System reliability

Standardized evaluation frameworks remain under development, with researchers often creating custom metrics for specific applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Applications and Use Cases

Humanoid robot teleoperation finds applications in numerous domains:

1. **Disaster Response**: Following events like Fukushima, teleoperated humanoids can enter dangerous environments to perform tasks like valve manipulation, debris removal, and search and rescue.

2. **Industrial Applications**: Maintenance and repair in hazardous environments such as chemical plants, offshore platforms, and mining operations.

3. **Healthcare**: Telenursing and medical procedures, where the humanoid form improves patient comfort and trust while enabling complex manipulations.

4. **Space Exploration**: Teleoperated humanoids can perform tasks on other celestial bodies with reduced communication delays compared to fully autonomous systems.

5. **Telepresence**: Social and business interactions where the humanoid form enhances the sense of presence compared to traditional video conferencing.

Each application domain presents unique requirements and constraints that influence teleoperation system design.

#### 5.2. Current Challenges and Future Directions

The survey identifies several ongoing challenges and promising research directions:

1. **Reducing Cognitive Load**: Current interfaces often require significant mental effort from operators. Simplifying interfaces while maintaining functionality remains a key challenge.

2. **Improving Robustness**: Current systems often struggle with unexpected environmental conditions or robot hardware limitations.

3. **Shared Autonomy**: Finding the optimal balance between direct control and autonomous capabilities continues to be an active research area.

4. **Standardization**: The field lacks standardized benchmarks, interfaces, and evaluation metrics, making cross-system comparisons difficult.

5. **Ethical and Safety Considerations**: As teleoperation becomes more widespread, ensuring safety for both operators and those interacting with teleoperated robots becomes increasingly important.

6. **Telepresence Enhancement**: Improving the sense of embodiment and presence for operators through better feedback systems and more intuitive control.

Future research will likely focus on integrating machine learning approaches, developing more natural interfaces, and creating systems that can adapt to individual operator preferences and capabilities.

#### 5.3. Conclusion

Humanoid robot teleoperation represents a rapidly evolving field with significant potential for applications in hazardous environments, healthcare, space exploration, and beyond. This comprehensive survey provides a structured overview of the current state-of-the-art, identifying key technologies, approaches, and challenges.

The integration of advanced sensing, control algorithms, communication protocols, and feedback mechanisms is essential for creating effective teleoperation systems. While significant progress has been made, particularly following challenges like the DARPA Robotics Challenge, substantial research opportunities remain in improving intuitiveness, robustness, and performance.

As the field continues to mature, standardization efforts and cross-disciplinary collaboration will become increasingly important. The gap between human cognitive capabilities and robot physical abilities is gradually narrowing through teleoperation advancements, bringing us closer to robots that can effectively replace humans in dangerous environments while being controlled from safety.
