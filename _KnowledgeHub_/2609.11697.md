# ActSafeGuard: Differentiable and Training-Aligned Constraint Enforcement for Flow-Matching Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> ActSafeGuard is a differentiable, parameter-free safeguard layer for flow-matching robot policies that reformulates generation as discrete flow steps and rescales each step via an analytical ray-shooting operator, keeping actions inside a state-dependent convex feasible set. Active in both training and inference, its local Jacobian acts as an oblique projection giving boundary-sliding gradients, yielding 100% step safety while preserving or improving task success.

## Problem

- Flow-matching action heads generate chunks in unconstrained space, so predicted actions can breach joint, velocity, or workspace limits and damage hardware during execution.
- Training-time constrained policies optimize expected safety costs or soft penalties, giving only probabilistic guarantees and trading task objective against constraint satisfaction via Lagrangian tuning.
- Inference-time projection, clipping, or barrier-function filtering enforce hard limits but are absent from training, causing a training-inference mismatch that distorts the learned action distribution.

## Method

- ==Discrete-time flow matching== reformulates generation as N steps predicting a nominal update, so feasibility can be certified step by step from a feasible initial noise sample.
- ==Ray-scaling operator== computes the ray-boundary intersection with the nearest active polytope facet and applies a scaling factor in [0,1], shortening only constraint-violating steps.
- ==Boundary-sliding gradient==: the operator's local Jacobian is an ==oblique projection== onto the active facet's tangent space, reorienting unsafe directions instead of truncating them, with no learnable parameters.

## Results

- On RoboTwin bimanual tasks, mean success rises to **80.25%** static and **81.50%** dynamic with one backbone versus **75.25%** unconstrained, beating projection, truncation, and gauge-map baselines.
- Feasible training data alone is insufficient: unconstrained baselines reach only **51.42%** and **22.99%** mean step safety rate under position constraints, while all constrained variants achieve **100%**.
- Stop-gradient ablation on the scaling factor collapses place-shoe success from **98.0%** to **3.0%** under position plus velocity constraints, with worse smoothness and higher distribution discrepancy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hard feasibility at inference is not enough; the correction operator must be differentiable during training or the policy fails badly under state-dependent constraints.
> - Rescaling a predicted direction preserves a pretrained vector-field prior better than coordinate transforms that remap the whole action space into a canonical set.
> - Ray-boundary intersection is the only requirement, so the approach extends to time-varying and even non-convex feasible sets, but constraints still need manual specification.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial advancements with the emergence of embodied foundation models, specifically Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World-Action Models (WAMs). These models leverage large-scale visual-language representations and generative action heads to perform general-purpose robotic tasks, exhibiting broad semantic and motor priors. However, a significant challenge persists: the actions generated by these models often violate hard physical constraints inherent in robotic systems, such as joint position limits, velocity bounds, or workspace restrictions. Such violations can lead to unstable execution, hardware damage, or unsafe robot behavior in real-world deployments.

This problem is particularly acute for flow-matching based action heads, which generate action sequences in an unconstrained space, lacking explicit feasibility guarantees. Ensuring safety in robotic manipulation necessitates deterministic, per-step constraint satisfaction, as even a single infeasible action can have critical consequences.

Existing approaches to enforce safety can broadly be categorized into two paradigms:
1. **Training-time constrained methods:** These approaches integrate safety objectives (e.g., costs, regularization) directly into the policy optimization process. Examples include SafeVLA (Zhang et al. 2026) and SafeDojo (Tang et al. 2026). While these methods can make policies "safety-aware," they typically offer only probabilistic or expectation-based guarantees rather than deterministic per-step feasibility. Furthermore, incorporating constrained optimization often introduces a non-trivial trade-off between task performance and strict constraint adherence.
2. **Inference-time safety guards:** These methods modify or correct generated actions during deployment through techniques such as projection, clipping, action masking (Beaudin et al. 2026), or optimization-based filtering (Hu et al. 2025; English, Zheng, and Ewetz 2026). While capable of enforcing hard constraints during execution, these mechanisms are absent during policy training. This creates a "training-inference mismatch," where the policy is optimized in an unconstrained space but operates under an externally modified action distribution, which can distort learned trajectories and degrade task performance.

The work presented in this paper, ActSafeGuard, directly addresses the limitations of these existing paradigms by introducing a differentiable and training-aligned method. It seeks to integrate hard action feasibility directly into the policy learning process for flow-matching based models, thereby bridging the gap between training and inference-time constraint enforcement. This approach aims to provide deterministic per-step safety guarantees without compromising the task competence or generative flexibility of advanced foundation policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and minimally invasive mechanism for ensuring deterministic, per-step physical constraint satisfaction in actions generated by flow-matching based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World-Action Models (WAMs). This overarching goal is motivated by critical safety requirements in real-world robotic deployments.

Specific objectives and the motivations behind them include:

- **Achieve Deterministic Per-Step Feasibility:** Robotic systems require absolute adherence to physical limits. A single violation can result in hardware damage, system instability, or hazardous situations. Current flow-matching methods, by design, generate actions in an unconstrained latent space, which necessitates an explicit mechanism to guarantee that every generated action step respects hard physical constraints such as joint limits or velocity bounds.
- **Preserve or Enhance Task Success Rates:** While safety is paramount, it should not come at the cost of task performance. Many existing safety approaches either compromise success rates due to the statistical nature of their guarantees or introduce a training-inference mismatch that negatively impacts the policy's ability to achieve its objectives. A key objective is to ensure that the safety mechanism not only enforces constraints but also allows the policy to maintain or even improve its task-solving capabilities.
- **Maintain Action-Generation Quality:** The generative flexibility and smoothness of actions are crucial for effective robotic manipulation. Inference-time clipping or projection methods can often lead to jerky movements or sudden shifts in the action distribution. Therefore, an objective is to preserve the smoothness and distribution alignment of the generated trajectories, preventing degradation of the policy's natural motion characteristics.
- **Ensure Training-Inference Alignment:** A significant limitation of inference-time safety guards is the mismatch between the action distribution learned during training and the modified distribution observed during execution. This discrepancy can impede effective learning and hinder generalization. A core objective is to integrate the constraint enforcement mechanism directly into the training loop, ensuring that the policy learns within the feasible space it will operate in during deployment.
- **Provide Differentiable Constraint Enforcement:** To facilitate end-to-end learning and allow policies to adapt to the geometry of the feasible action space, the safety mechanism must be differentiable. This enables gradients from the loss function to propagate through the constrained action generation process, providing boundary-aware learning signals. This is critical for the policy to "learn" how to operate safely rather than just being passively corrected.
- **Minimal Intervention and Parameter-Free Design:** To be scalable and easily adoptable, the proposed solution should require minimal modifications to existing pretrained foundation models and introduce no additional learnable parameters. This reduces complexity and avoids the need for extensive retraining or architectural changes.
- **Generalize to Complex and Dynamic Constraints:** Real-world robotic tasks often involve state-dependent and multi-step constraints (e.g., velocity limits that depend on current joint positions or interactions between actions within a chunk). The method should be capable of handling such complex, high-dimensional feasible regions that may change dynamically.

The overall motivation stems from the need to move beyond merely "safe-ish" or "post-hoc corrected" robotic behaviors towards deterministically safe and competent embodied AI systems, thereby increasing their trustworthiness and applicability in real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ActSafeGuard is designed to integrate hard action feasibility directly into the learning and generation processes of flow-matching based policies. The core methodological innovation lies in a differentiable and parameter-free ray-scaling operator applied to discrete flow updates.

**Policy Formulation and Flow-Matching Background:**
The work frames language-conditioned robotic manipulation as learning a mapping from multimodal observations (`o_t`) and a language instruction (`l`) to a sequence of future actions (an action chunk `a_t:t+H-1`). The foundation model (VLA/WAM) produces a latent representation `e_t`, which is then decoded by a flow-matching action head `π_ϕ`. Flow matching (Lipman et al. 2023) trains a velocity field network `v_ϕ` to approximate the target vector field that transforms an initial noise sample `x_0` into an expert action chunk `x_1` along a linear interpolation path `x_τ = (1-τ)x_0 + τx_1`. Inference involves integrating `v_ϕ` using an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) solver.

**Constrained Action Space Definition:**
The feasible action space is characterized as a state-dependent convex polytope: `C(o_t) = {x_c | A(o_t)x_c ≤ b(o_t)}`. Here, `x_c` represents the constrained dimensions of the action chunk, and `A(o_t)`, `b(o_t)` are observation-conditioned constraint matrix and vector, respectively. The objective is to ensure `x_c` remains within `C(o_t)` throughout the action generation process.

**Discrete-Time Flow Matching with Feasible States:**
To enable step-by-step feasibility enforcement, ActSafeGuard reformulates the generative process as an N-step discrete flow. Instead of continuously integrating `v_ϕ`, the action head is parameterized to directly predict a discrete step `Δ_ϕ(x_k, τ_k, e_t)`. The target one-step update is `Δ* = (x_1 - x_0) / N`. The policy is trained with a discrete-time flow matching loss: `L_DFM = E[||Δ_ϕ - Δ*||^2]`. This discretization allows for the sequential enforcement of constraints: if the initial state `x_0` is feasible, and each discrete update `x_k → x_{k+1}` is guaranteed to respect the boundaries, then the entire generated trajectory remains feasible.

**ActSafeGuard Parameterization via Ray-Scaling:**
At each discrete step `k`, the pretrained action head provides an unconstrained velocity `v_ϕ`. ActSafeGuard transforms this into a safe update:
1. **Direction Vector (`d_ϕ`):** The unconstrained velocity is scaled by the number of discretization steps: `d_ϕ = v_ϕ / N`. This vector dictates the intended direction and magnitude of the nominal update.
2. **Safety Scaling Factor (`s_ϕ`):** A scaling factor `s_ϕ ∈` is computed using a ray-shooting operator. This operator determines the maximum `α ≥ 0` by which `d_ϕ` can be scaled from the current state `x_k` before hitting the nearest boundary of the feasible region `C(o_t)`. Specifically, `α = min_{i: a_i^T d_ϕ > 0} (b_i - a_i^T x_k) / (a_i^T d_ϕ)`. The safety scaling factor is then `s_ϕ = min(1, α)`.
 - If `α ≥ 1`, the nominal step `x_k + d_ϕ` is already within the feasible region, and `s_ϕ` is 1, leaving the step unchanged.
 - If `α < 1`, the nominal step would violate a constraint, so `s_ϕ` is set to `α`, shortening the step to exactly the boundary intersection `z = x_k + αd_ϕ`.
3. **Final Safe Discrete Step:** The modulated update is `Δ_ϕ = s_ϕ d_ϕ`.

**Safety Guarantee (Proposition 1):** By design, ActSafeGuard ensures that the generated trajectory `x_0 → ... → x_N` remains within the closed feasible region `C(o_t)`, provided the initial state `x_0` is feasible.

**Differentiability (Proposition 2) and Boundary-Sliding Gradients:**
A key aspect of ActSafeGuard is its differentiability. The piecewise function `s_ϕ` is differentiable everywhere except at boundary corners, where subgradient methods are applied during backpropagation. When `α < 1`, the local Jacobian of `Δ_ϕ` with respect to `d_ϕ` acts as an oblique projection `Q_hat_i`. This `Q_hat_i` projects gradients onto the tangent space of the active constraint facet. This means that gradients that would normally direct the policy outside the feasible region are instead reshaped to encourage movement along the boundary. This "boundary-sliding" behavior allows the action head to learn to re-orient unsafe nominal directions along feasible paths, rather than merely having its outputs truncated without corrective learning signals.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted in the RoboTwin simulation environment using a bimanual ALOHA-Agilex robot (14-DoF action space). Four manipulation tasks (lift pot, place shoe, hanging mug, place empty cup) were used. The policies receive multimodal observations (images, proprioceptive states) and language instructions, predicting absolute joint and gripper positions. Constraints were imposed on the 12-DoF arm-joint subspace:
- **Position Constraints (PosCons):** Static joint-limit box `q_min ≤ a_q ≤ q_max`.
- **Velocity Constraints (VelCons):** Dynamic joint displacement bounds `|q_t - a_q_t| ≤ v_max` and `|a_q_{t+i} - a_q_{t+i+1}| ≤ v_max`. When combined with PosCons, the feasible set becomes state-dependent.
ActSafeGuard was instantiated on two foundation backbones: `π_0.5` (VLA model) and `Fast-WAM` (world-action model). Baselines included `Projection` (inference-time step-wise), `Truncation` (post-hoc clipping), and `GaugeFlow` (training-aware, limited to static star-shaped regions). Evaluation metrics included Success Rate (SR), Step Safe Rate (SSR), Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) for distribution alignment, and Log Dimensionless Jerk (LDLJ) for trajectory smoothness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of ActSafeGuard across various tasks, constraint types, and foundation backbones, both in simulation and on a physical robot.

**Necessity of Hard Constraints:**
Initial experiments confirmed that merely training on feasible demonstration data is insufficient to guarantee deterministic safety. Unconstrained baselines, even when fine-tuned on in-distribution data, exhibited low Step Safety Rates (SSRs). For example, the `π_0.5` baseline achieved only 51.42% SSR under static position constraints (PosCons) and 48.09% under dynamic position + velocity constraints (PosCons + VelCons). The `Fast-WAM` baseline showed even lower SSRs, highlighting that neural fitting errors and sampling variations can push generated actions outside the empirically observed feasible envelope, necessitating explicit constraint enforcement.

**Performance Preservation (RQ1):**
ActSafeGuard consistently achieved a 100% Step Safety Rate across all evaluated tasks and scenarios, ensuring deterministic constraint satisfaction. Crucially, it maintained or even improved task success rates (SR) compared to other constrained methods and, in some cases, even outperformed the unconstrained baseline. For instance, with the `π_0.5` backbone, ActSafeGuard achieved a mean SR of 80.25% under PosCons and 81.50% under PosCons + VelCons, outperforming `Projection` and `Truncation` methods.

Trajectory quality metrics (MMD and LDLJ) indicated that ActSafeGuard better preserved the pretrained action distribution and smoothness compared to post-hoc correction methods. This outcome is attributed to ActSafeGuard's ability to eliminate the training-inference mismatch and leverage boundary-sliding gradients for more adaptive corrections during learning. In contrast, `GaugeFlow`, which transforms the action space, sometimes disrupted the pretrained vector-field priors, leading to slightly lower performance in certain tasks compared to ActSafeGuard's more minimal intervention.

**Generalizability to Complex Constraints (RQ2):**
ActSafeGuard demonstrated robustness and generalizability to complex, dynamic, and state-dependent constraints, specifically PosCons + VelCons. In this scenario, the feasible region forms a high-dimensional polytope that changes at every control step and couples multiple future actions. ActSafeGuard obtained the highest mean SR under PosCons + VelCons while maintaining 100% deterministic safety. This contrasted with `GaugeFlow`, which was not applicable to dynamic constraints due to its reliance on static star-shaped feasible regions. The ray-scaling formulation of ActSafeGuard proved advantageous because it only requires computing ray-boundary intersections with the current feasible polytope, making it adaptable to both static and dynamic constraints without complex iterative optimization or changes to the pretrained backbone.

**Ablation Study on Differentiability (RQ3):**
An ablation study isolating the effect of differentiability revealed its critical role. When a stop-gradient operation was applied to the safety scaling factor (`ActSafeGuard (w/ sg)`), task success rates collapsed significantly. For example, on the 'place shoe' task with PosCons + VelCons, SR dropped from 98.0% to 3.0%. While `ActSafeGuard (w/ sg)` still enforced 100% SSR during inference, the lack of boundary-aware gradients during training prevented the policy from learning how to effectively interact with dynamic feasible boundaries. This finding underscores that deterministic inference-time feasibility alone is insufficient; policies require differentiable learning signals to adapt and perform competently under constraints.

**Real-Robot Deployment:**
ActSafeGuard was successfully deployed on a physical AgileX ALOHA bimanual platform for two tasks: 'pick green cube' (joint boundaries) and 'guide the ball' (L-shaped Cartesian corridor for the end-effector). The results showed that ActSafeGuard strictly adhered to the prescribed boundaries, unlike the unconstrained baseline which violated the corridor in 'guide the ball'. ActSafeGuard achieved 100% success rates in both tasks (5/5 trials), matching the baseline in 'pick green cube' and outperforming it in the more constrained 'guide the ball' (baseline 4/5). This real-world validation further demonstrated ActSafeGuard's practical applicability and its ability to generalize even to non-convex feasible regions, provided ray-boundary intersections can be computed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of ActSafeGuard represents a significant contribution to the field of safe embodied AI, particularly for robotic manipulation governed by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World-Action Models (WAMs). Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Deterministically Safe Robotic Deployment:** The most direct impact of ActSafeGuard is its ability to provide a deterministic, per-step guarantee of physical constraint satisfaction. This addresses a critical bottleneck for deploying advanced generative models in real-world, safety-critical robotic applications, where even a single unsafe action can lead to substantial consequences, including damage or injury. By offering 100% step safety, ActSafeGuard can increase the trustworthiness and acceptance of embodied AI systems in industrial and service robotics.
- **Resolving the Training-Inference Mismatch:** ActSafeGuard effectively bridges the long-standing gap between policy training and execution in constrained environments. By integrating the safeguard layer directly into the training process, the policy learns within the same constrained action space it will operate in. This alignment is crucial for robust performance, preventing the degradation of task success and action quality often observed with inference-only correction mechanisms.
- **Enhancing Policy Learning with Boundary-Aware Gradients:** The differentiable nature of ActSafeGuard, particularly its mechanism for inducing boundary-sliding gradients through an implicit oblique projection, signifies a novel approach to learning safe behaviors. Instead of merely being truncated post-hoc, the policy actively receives signals that guide it to reshape unsafe nominal directions along feasible boundaries. This active learning approach enables policies to naturally adapt to the geometry of the feasible space, leading to improved task competence and smoother, more aligned trajectories.
- **Scalable and Minimally Invasive Solution:** ActSafeGuard is designed as a parameter-free, plug-and-play layer. This makes it highly scalable and adaptable, allowing easy integration into existing pretrained flow-matching backbones without requiring significant architectural changes or extensive retraining. This minimal intervention approach lowers the barrier to adopting advanced safety features for a wide range of VLA/WAMs.
- **Generalizability Across Constraint Types:** The demonstrated efficacy across static, dynamic, state-dependent, high-dimensional, and even non-convex (in real-robot trials) constraint regimes highlights the versatility of the ray-scaling formulation. This broad applicability positions ActSafeGuard as a robust solution for diverse robotic manipulation tasks with varying and complex safety requirements.
- **Advancing Research in Constrained Generative Models:** This work provides a practical and effective method for constrained generative modeling, specifically for flow-matching. It expands the understanding of how to incorporate hard constraints into diffusion or flow-based models in a training-aligned and differentiable manner, which could inspire similar developments in other generative AI domains.
- **Implications for Future Research:** The paper identifies limitations, such as the manual specification of constraint sets. This naturally points towards future research directions in automatically discovering, validating, and updating constraints from perceptual data or through interaction, further advancing the autonomy and adaptability of safe embodied AI systems.

In summary, ActSafeGuard provides a foundational component for building safe and reliable general-purpose robotic systems, addressing a critical safety gap and paving the way for more confident deployment of advanced AI in physical environments.
