# Learning Multi-Agent Loco-Manipulation for Long-Horizon Quadrupedal Pushing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and Google DeepMind developed a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework, enabling multiple quadrupedal robots to perform obstacle-aware, long-horizon pushing of large objects through coordinated whole-body motions. This framework achieved over 60% success rates in simulations and demonstrated successful real-world deployment on Unitree Go1 robots for tasks involving heavy cuboid and T-shaped blocks.

## Problem

- Quadrupedal robots struggle to manipulate large and heavy objects effectively, limiting their utility in demanding real-world applications.
- Existing multi-robot pushing methods are often restricted to short-horizon tasks, predetermined paths, or limited contact patterns, lacking obstacle avoidance and coordinated whole-body movements.
- Integrating long-horizon planning, adaptive obstacle avoidance, and diverse whole-body contact strategies in multi-quadruped systems remains a significant challenge.

## Method

- A hierarchical multi-agent ==reinforcement learning== (MARL) framework was proposed, comprising a high-level controller for object subgoals, a mid-level controller for individual robot velocity commands, and a low-level locomotion controller.
- The high-level controller utilizes an RRT planner for reference trajectories and a centralized adaptive policy to generate obstacle-aware object subgoals, while the mid-level employs a shared decentralized policy for robot-specific velocity commands.
- An Occlusion-Based (OCB) reward was designed for the mid-level policy to guide agents towards optimal contact points, complementing a comprehensive reward structure across both high and mid-levels.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved an average success rate exceeding **60%** across various simulated pushing tasks (e.g., **77.5%** for cuboid, **71.2%** for cylinder) and reduced completion time by **24.5%** compared to the best baseline.
- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of the OCB reward, improving success rate from **13.0%** to **57.0%** in short-horizon pushing, and demonstrated the high-level adaptive policy's ability to navigate around obstacles not accounted for by the initial RRT path.
- Successful real-world deployment on two Unitree Go1 robots validated sim-to-real transfer, showcasing adaptive obstacle avoidance for a **6.8** kg cuboid and controlled turning for a **3.3** kg T-block, effectively leveraging coordinated pushing strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The hierarchical MARL framework enables robust coordination among multiple quadrupeds for long-horizon, obstacle-aware loco-manipulation tasks that single robots cannot achieve.
> - The Occlusion-Based (OCB) reward is crucial for guiding agents to favorable contact points on the object, significantly improving pushing effectiveness and task success rates.
> - The adaptive high-level policy is essential for dynamically adjusting the object's path to avoid obstacles, demonstrating the necessity of integrating path planning with real-time reactive behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen significant advancements in quadrupedal locomotion, enabling robots to traverse complex terrains with stability and agility. However, the manipulation capabilities of these robots, particularly for handling large and heavy objects, have remained a challenge. Existing research has explored both prehensile manipulation (attaching grippers or robotic arms) and non-prehensile manipulation (using legs or the head). While these efforts allow quadrupeds to perform routine tasks, their utility in demanding real-world applications like search and rescue, construction, industrial automation, and room organization is limited by their inability to manipulate large objects over long distances in cluttered environments.

Previous attempts to address manipulation limitations have included adding support structures, coordinating whole-body movements, and utilizing multiple robots. However, many multi-robot approaches for quadrupedal pushing are restricted in scope, often focusing on short-horizon tasks, predetermined paths, or limited contact patterns (e.g., using only the head to push). These methods frequently lack obstacle avoidance capabilities or the ability to coordinate whole-body movements effectively across long distances. For instance, some multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) approaches focus on generating robot-centric commands for locomotion controllers, which can limit their applicability to longer-horizon manipulation. Optimization-based multi-robot methods sometimes rely on specific inter-robot connections, which can restrict generalizability.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by specifically addressing the gap in *obstacle-aware, long-horizon pushing* of *large objects* using *coordinated whole-body motions* of *multiple quadrupedal robots*. By proposing a hierarchical MARL framework, the authors aim to overcome the limitations of single-robot systems and prior multi-robot approaches that did not fully integrate long-horizon planning, adaptive obstacle avoidance, and diverse whole-body contact strategies. The paper positions itself as advancing beyond existing methods by enabling capabilities critical for practical, demanding applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable multiple quadrupedal robots to perform obstacle-aware, long-horizon pushing tasks involving large objects through coordinated whole-body motions. This encompasses several detailed goals:

- **Enhance Quadrupedal Manipulation Capabilities:** The paper aims to overcome the current limitations of quadrupedal robots in manipulating large and heavy objects. While locomotion has advanced, handling substantial payloads remains a hurdle that restricts their deployment in physically demanding scenarios.
- **Facilitate Long-Horizon Tasks:** Many real-world applications require robots to transport objects over considerable distances, not just short-range movements. The objective is to develop a control framework that can manage the cumulative errors and complexities inherent in long-duration manipulation.
- **Enable Obstacle Avoidance:** Real-world environments are often cluttered. A key motivation is to allow robots to navigate and manipulate objects while dynamically avoiding static obstacles, a capability often absent in existing multi-robot pushing frameworks.
- **Coordinate Multi-Agent Whole-Body Motions:** The research seeks to leverage the collective strength and dexterity of multiple robots. This involves not only coordinating their locomotion but also their interaction with the object using diverse whole-body contact patterns, moving beyond single-point pushing (e.g., head-only pushing).
- **Develop a Robust and Flexible Control Framework:** The authors aim to create a hierarchical control system that can adapt to varying object shapes, masses, and environmental conditions, ensuring successful task completion even with dynamic interactions between robots, objects, and obstacles.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the identified limitations in real-world utility of current quadrupedal robots. Applications such as search and rescue (moving debris), construction (repositioning materials), industrial automation (transporting components), and room organization (rearranging furniture) all demand robots capable of both high mobility and significant manipulation strength over extended periods in complex, dynamic environments. By addressing these challenges, the research seeks to expand the practical applicability of quadrupedal robotic systems in domains where robust, coordinated loco-manipulation of large objects is essential.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework comprising three layers of controllers to address the challenge of collaborative, obstacle-aware, long-horizon quadrupedal pushing.

**A. Hierarchical Framework Overview**

The framework operates with a high-level controller, a mid-level controller, and a low-level controller. The high-level and mid-level controllers operate at a frequency of 50 Hz, with the high-level policy updating more frequently to enable adaptive behavior. The low-level locomotion policy also runs at 50 Hz, while the underlying PD controller executes at 200 Hz in simulation and 2000 Hz on physical robots. Each layer is trained independently, leveraging frozen lower-level policies during the training of the layer above it.

**B. Low-Level Controller**

- **Purpose:** The low-level controller is responsible for individual robot locomotion, tracking velocity commands received from the mid-level controller.
- **Implementation:**
 - **Real World:** For physical robots (Unitree Go1), the manufacturer's built-in low-level controller is utilized due to its robustness in tracking velocity commands, especially when pushing heavy objects, and its superior sim-to-real performance compared to learned controllers.
 - **Simulation:** To facilitate efficient training of higher-level policies, a learned low-level policy is employed to mimic the behavior of Unitree's built-in controller. This policy is based on the Walk-These-Ways (WTW) framework, which learns locomotion behaviors with configurable body pose, gait timing, and reference velocity. Parameters from the built-in controller are reproduced in WTW to ensure similar behavior. This simulated policy is invoked in parallel on the GPU to reduce training time.

**C. Mid-Level Controller**

- **Purpose:** This layer consists of a shared decentralized goal-conditioned policy for all agents. It translates a common high-level subgoal into individual robot-specific velocity commands.
- **Input:** Each agent's mid-level policy receives the high-level subgoal (`a_h`), its local observations (`o_m,i`), which include the state of the target object (`s_i_object`), obstacles (`s_i_obstacle`), and other robots (`{s_i_j}`), all computed within the robot's local torso frame.
- **Output:** The policy outputs a mid-level action (`a_m,i`), which is a 3D velocity command (`v_x, v_y, v_yaw`) for robot `i`, sent to its low-level controller.
- **Training:** A single mid-level policy, shared across all robots, is trained using Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO). During training, object subgoals (`a_h`) are randomly sampled, and the low-level policy is frozen. Domain randomization for frictions is specialized to mitigate the sim-to-real gap for pushing tasks.

**D. High-Level Controller**

- **Purpose:** The high-level controller is responsible for generating subgoals for the target object, guiding the overall long-horizon task.
- **Components:**
 - **RRT Planner:** At the beginning of each episode, a Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) planner generates a geometrically feasible reference trajectory (`τ_r`) from the object's initial position to its final goal, considering the map information and obstacles. This planner does not account for robot pushing capabilities or multi-robot/object dynamics.
 - **Centralized Adaptive Policy:** This policy uses the reference trajectory from the RRT planner, the desired final object goal, real-time map information, the dynamic global object pose, and the states of all robots as input. It then outputs a high-level action (`a_h`), which is a subgoal position for the target object, to be pursued by the mid-level controllers.
- **Training:** The high-level adaptive policy is a centralized policy trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). During its training, both the mid-level and low-level controllers are frozen. To manage computational demands in simulation, random long curved trajectories are used as reference paths instead of real-time RRT planning during adaptive policy training, with obstacles randomly placed around these trajectories.

**E. Reward Design**

- **Mid-Level Reward (`r_m`):** Formulated as `r_m = r_m_task + r_m_penalty + r_m_heuristic`.
 - `r_m_task`: Rewards for moving the object towards and reaching the subgoal.
 - `r_m_penalty`: Penalizes close proximities between robots, robot fall-overs, and timeouts.
 - `r_m_heuristic`: A critical component including:
 - `r_m_approach`: Encourages agents to approach the object.
 - `r_m_vel`: Rewards for the object's velocity exceeding a threshold, promoting pushing actions.
 - **Occlusion-Based (OCB) Reward (`r_m_OCB`):** Guides agents to favorable contact points. It rewards agents for pushing on the object's convex hull perimeter that occludes their view of the subgoal. This is calculated as the dot product of a unit normal vector at the closest point on the object's convex hull to robot *i* and a unit vector directing from the object towards the subgoal.
- **High-Level Reward (`r_h`):** Composed of `r_h = r_h_task + r_h_penalty`.
 - `r_h_task`: Provides sparse reward for reaching the final target, dense reward for minimizing distance between the subgoal and the RRT trajectory, and dense reward for reducing the distance between the object and the final target. This encourages following the planned path while allowing adaptive deviations.
 - `r_h_penalty`: Penalizes close distances to obstacles and severe exceptions (fall-overs, collisions, object tilting, timeouts).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, evaluating the proposed hierarchical MARL framework against various baselines and through ablation studies.

**A. Simulation Setups**

- **Environments and Tasks:** Simulations were conducted in IsaacGym within cluttered environments featuring randomly placed 1.0 m × 1.0 m × 1.0 m obstacles. Unitree Go1 robots pushed target objects (4 kg cuboid, 3 kg T-shaped block, 10 kg cylinder with 1.5 m radius) that were larger and close to or exceeding the robots' 5 kg payload capacity. Tasks involved long-horizon pushing, with initial-to-target distances exceeding 10 m. Success was defined as the object's center being within 1 m of the target; failure included robot fall-overs, collisions, object tilting, or timeouts. Different numbers of agents (two for cuboid/T-block, up to four for cylinder) were evaluated.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Single-Agent (SA):** One robot using the proposed hierarchical policy and reward structure.
 - **High-Level+Low-Level (H+L):** A two-level hierarchy similar to, where the high-level policy proposes subgoals for robots, directly aided by low-level locomotion, without a mid-level pushing policy.
 - **Mid-Level+Low-Level (M+L):** A two-level hierarchy similar to, where robots are guided directly by the distant final target via mid-level and low-level policies, without a high-level subgoal generator. Includes basic heuristic rewards.
 - **High-Level+Low-Level with Fine-Tuned Rewards (H+L FT):** H+L architecture with the paper's fine-tuned reward functions (including `r_m_heuristic` components adapted for the high-level policy).
 - **Mid-Level+Low-Level with Fine-Tuned Rewards (M+L FT):** M+L architecture with the paper's complete fine-tuned reward functions.

**B. Simulation Results and Analysis**

- **Comparisons with Baselines (Table II):**
 - The proposed method achieved an average success rate exceeding 60% across all tasks (Cuboid: 77.5%, T-Shape: 63.5%, Cylinder: 71.2%) and demonstrated a 24.5% reduction in completion time compared to the best baseline.
 - **Single-Agent (SA):** Exhibited low success rates (<25%), indicating insufficient strength and manipulation range for large, heavy objects.
 - **H+L:** Also showed low success rates (e.g., 0.23% for Cuboid), suggesting difficulty in coordinating effective pushing despite agents reaching individual subgoals, due to complex object interactions.
 - **M+L:** Outperformed H+L in some cases but struggled with long-horizon tasks and exhibited high variability, attributed to the lack of higher-level guidance for distant targets.
 - **H+L FT & M+L FT:** Both showed performance improvements over their basic counterparts, confirming the efficacy of the proposed heuristic reward functions. However, M+L FT remained significantly inferior to the proposed method. H+L FT showed instability and poor adaptability with certain objects (e.g., T-shape), highlighting the necessity of the complete hierarchical architecture.
- **Ablation Study:**
 - **OCB Reward (Table III):** Ablation of the OCB reward for short-horizon pushing demonstrated its importance. With OCB, the success rate for a 20s timeout was 57.0% (vs. 13.0% without), and completion time was significantly lower. This indicates that OCB helps agents find optimal contact points and adapt pushing directions.
 - **High-Level Adaptive Policy (Fig. 4):** A scenario with obstacles directly blocking the RRT path showed that without the adaptive policy, the RRT path led to collisions (Fig. 4a). The adaptive policy allowed robots to deviate from obstacles in advance, successfully reaching the goal (Fig. 4b), emphasizing the policy's role in dynamic path adjustment.
- **Scalability Analysis (Fig. 5):**
 - For cylinder pushing, a single robot performed poorly.
 - Performance (success rate and completion time) improved with two and three robots.
 - With four robots, a slight decline in performance was observed, attributed to the cylinder's limited effective contact surface, increasing collision risk, and pushing interference. This suggests an optimal number of agents for specific object geometries.

**C. Real-World Setups**

- **Environments and Task:** Experiments were conducted in a 7.5 m × 7.5 m room equipped with 24 OptiTrack cameras for motion capture. Two Unitree Go1 robots, each with a 5 kg payload capacity, were used. Sim-trained high-level and mid-level policies were deployed, with the Unitree Go1's integrated locomotion policy serving as the low-level controller. Tasks included pushing a 1.5 m × 1.0 m × 0.5 m, 6.8 kg cuboid and a 0.5 m × 1.0 m × 0.5 m (main body) T-block weighing 3.3 kg. Target positions were set for long-horizon pushing, and obstacles were randomly initialized in the cuboid task.

**D. Real-World Results and Analysis (Fig. 6)**

- **Pushing T (Fig. 6a):** Robots successfully controlled the T-shaped block's turning and pushed it to the target. They consistently pushed from the two ends of the block, indicating an effective strategy for applying continuous forward force and directional control on smaller objects. The x-direction velocity commands often reached the upper bound, and yaw commands adjusted for turning.
- **Pushing Cuboid (Fig. 6b):** Robots navigated adaptively around obstacles and reached the target. The cuboid's large surface allowed varied pushing postures (forward pushing with heads for speed, sideways pushing for turning). High x-direction velocities were observed, with adaptive y and yaw commands for strategic adjustments.
- **Action Homogeneity Analysis:** Similar motion patterns and xy velocity commands were observed between robots. This was attributed to the shared mid-level policy, the necessity for both robots to maintain contact with the same object surface for efficient pushing, leading to similar states, and the yaw rate commands handling strategic adjustments and coordination.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that addresses several long-standing challenges in legged robot manipulation. The key significance lies in enabling multiple quadrupedal robots to perform complex, obstacle-aware, long-horizon pushing tasks involving large and heavy objects through coordinated whole-body motions.

The findings demonstrate that the proposed framework significantly outperforms baseline methods in simulation, achieving higher success rates and reduced completion times. The successful deployment on physical Unitree Go1 robots for tasks like Push-Cuboid and Push-T validates the sim-to-real transfer capabilities and the practical applicability of the learned policies. The ablation studies further highlight the importance of the OCB reward for effective contact point selection and the adaptive high-level policy for real-time path adjustments in cluttered environments.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for advancing robotic autonomy and multi-agent systems. By allowing quadrupedal robots to collectively manipulate objects larger and heavier than themselves over extended distances while navigating obstacles, the research moves closer to deploying these robots in demanding real-world applications such as:

- **Search and Rescue:** Robots could collaborate to move heavy debris, clearing paths or accessing trapped individuals.
- **Construction:** Facilitating the movement and placement of construction materials in hazardous or confined spaces.
- **Industrial Automation:** Handling and repositioning large components or equipment on factory floors.
- **Logistics and Room Organization:** Rearranging heavy items in warehouses or domestic environments.

This work contributes to the development of more versatile and capable legged robots, expanding their operational envelope beyond mere locomotion to include robust, coordinated, and intelligent object manipulation in complex, unstructured environments. It provides a foundational framework for future research in scalable multi-robot collaboration and hierarchical control for real-world physical interaction tasks.
