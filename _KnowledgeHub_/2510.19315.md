# Transformers are Inherently Succinct

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at RPTU Kaiserslautern-Landau and ETH Zürich established that unique-hard attention transformers (UHATs) are exponentially more succinct than Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and fixed-precision Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and doubly exponentially more succinct than finite automata. This inherent succinctness reveals that fundamental verification problems for UHATs, like non-emptiness and equivalence, are EXPSPACE-complete.

## Problem

- Existing theoretical work showed fixed-precision RNNs are strictly more expressive than fixed-precision transformers, creating a disparity with transformers' strong empirical performance.
- A lack of formal framework to measure how compactly neural architectures describe languages, beyond simply what classes of languages they can recognize.
- Unclear computational complexity for formal verification tasks (e.g., emptiness, equivalence) of transformers in the realistic fixed-precision setting.

## Method

- Introduced and formally defined '==succinctness==' as a measure of compactness for language descriptions by a formalism.
- Used Unique-Hard Attention Transformers (UHATs) as a representative model for self-attention, comparing their succinctness to LTL, RNNs, and finite automata.
- Established complexity bounds for UHAT verification problems by reduction from the EXPSPACE-complete 2^N-tiling problem and constructive translations between formalisms.

## Results

- Unique-hard attention transformers (UHATs) are exponentially more succinct than Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and fixed-precision Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).
- UHATs are doubly exponentially more succinct than finite automata, as demonstrated by a family of languages requiring polynomial-size UHATs but doubly exponential-size DFAs.
- The non-emptiness and equivalence problems for UHATs are proven to be EXPSPACE-complete, indicating severe computational intractability for general-purpose verification.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transformer attention mechanisms, even in simplified UHATs, can implement sophisticated counting functionalities, specifically doubly exponentially large counters.
> - Succinctness offers a theoretical explanation for transformers' empirical success by showing their ability to represent complex patterns very compactly, even if not recognizing a broader class of languages.
> - A direct correlation exists between greater succinctness and higher computational complexity for formal verification and decision problems, making them inherently intractable in the worst case.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of transformer neural networks, which are the foundational architecture for contemporary large language models. A significant body of prior theoretical work has explored various aspects of transformers, including their expressive power, their ability to generalize to unseen input lengths, their trainability, and the feasibility of their formal verification. A recurring finding in this area is that fixed-precision transformers—a model setting that closely reflects real-world hardware implementations—are primarily capable of recognizing various classes of subregular languages. This places them in a specific hierarchy below recurrent neural networks (RNNs), which, under similar fixed-precision assumptions, are capable of recognizing all regular languages. Consequently, RNNs are considered strictly more expressive than transformers in terms of the *set* of languages they can recognize.

The present paper introduces "succinctness" as an alternative analytical framework to evaluate and compare neural architectures. Unlike expressive capacity, which focuses on *which* languages a formalism can recognize, succinctness measures *how compactly* a formalism can describe a given language relative to other formalisms. This concept is classical in logic and automata theory, where greater succinctness often correlates with increased computational complexity for decision problems. For instance, Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) is known to be expressively equivalent to star-free languages (a subclass of regular languages), yet it can describe certain languages exponentially more compactly than finite automata, resulting in harder decision problems for LTL.

This work directly extends previous research on unique-hard attention transformers (UHATs), which have been demonstrated to have close connections to LTL and the star-free regular languages (e.g., Yang et al., 2024; Barceló et al., 2024; Jerad et al., 2025; Li and Cotterell, 2025). The paper differentiates itself by focusing specifically on the aspects of succinctness and the computational complexity of verification, areas not comprehensively addressed by these prior works. It also builds upon work by Sälzer et al. (2025) on transformer verification, significantly improving upon their findings by demonstrating a higher degree of succinctness for transformers relative to finite automata and providing the first succinctness comparisons against LTL and RNNs. The specific transformer model investigated is the unique-hard attention transformer (UHAT), chosen as a widely used abstraction of self-attention, with prior work demonstrating that expressivity bounds for UHATs can translate to corresponding bounds for softmax transformers with fixed precision. The study's conclusions are primarily valid within the fixed-precision arithmetic setting, which faithfully represents practical hardware implementations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to formally investigate "succinctness" as a measure for characterizing the expressive power of transformers. The authors aim to quantify how compactly fixed-precision transformers can describe languages when compared to established formalisms such as Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and Finite Automata.

The motivation for this shift in perspective from expressive *capacity* to *succinctness* stems from several observations:
- **Reconciling Empirical Success with Theoretical Limitations:** Despite existing theoretical results indicating that fixed-precision RNNs are strictly more expressive than fixed-precision transformers in terms of the *class* of languages they can recognize (RNNs can recognize all regular languages, while transformers are restricted to subregular languages), transformers have achieved remarkable empirical performance in large language models. This disparity suggests that the sheer expressive capacity might not be the most comprehensive metric for comparing these architectures. Succinctness offers an alternative lens, proposing that transformers' strength might lie in their ability to describe complex patterns very efficiently.
- **Complexity-Theoretic Refinement:** Succinctness is a classical concept in theoretical computer science that refines the notion of expressive power. Instead of merely asking "what languages can a formalism recognize?", it asks "how compactly can each such language be described within that formalism?". This adds a complexity-theoretic dimension, acknowledging that different formalisms, even if expressively equivalent, may require vastly different sizes of representations for the same language.
- **Implications for Computational Tractability:** A core principle linking succinctness and computational complexity is that more succinct formalisms typically lead to harder decision problems. This is because a compact description necessitates a decision procedure to "unfold" a larger amount of underlying structure to reach a conclusion. For instance, the known exponential succinctness of LTL over finite automata directly implies that decision problems for LTL (such as checking for trivial languages) are computationally harder than for automata. By studying transformer succinctness, the authors aim to predict and formally establish the inherent computational difficulty of analyzing and verifying transformer behavior.
- **Specific Questions Addressed:** The research seeks to answer precise questions regarding the succinctness hierarchy, such as whether transformers are exponentially or doubly exponentially more succinct than LTL, RNNs, and finite automata, and to quantify the blow-up when converting between these formalisms. A key technical motivation is to demonstrate that transformers can implement complex counting mechanisms (specifically, doubly exponentially large counters) using attention, which underpins their observed succinctness. This leads directly to investigating the computational complexity of fundamental verification tasks like emptiness and equivalence for transformers.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a theoretical approach grounded in formal language theory and computational complexity. The core methodology involves formalizing the unique-hard attention transformer (UHAT) model, defining succinctness metrics, constructing witness language families, and establishing complexity bounds through reductions and constructive translations.

**Formal Model of Transformers:**
The study focuses on the **Unique-Hard Attention Transformer (UHAT)**, a simplified yet representative model of self-attention. A UHAT is defined by:
- **Symbol Embedding:** A function `emb: Σ → Q^D` that maps input symbols to D-dimensional rational vectors.
- **Unique Hard-Attention (UHA) Layer:** Characterized by three affine transformations (A, B, C), a mask predicate (e.g., no masking, strict future, strict past), and a tie-breaking function (e.g., leftmost `min`, rightmost `max`). This layer computes attention scores via dot products and selects a single attention vector based on the maximum score among unmasked positions, using the tie-breaker for ties.
- **ReLU Layer:** Applies the ReLU function element-wise to a designated component of each input vector.
UHATs are constructed by composing a symbol embedding with a finite sequence of UHA and ReLU layers. For language recognition, a UHAT accepts a word if the dot product of its final output vector at the last position with a designated acceptance vector `t` is positive.
**Precision Assumptions:** While upper bounds are derived for arbitrary rational weights, the more significant lower bounds and the overall conclusions hold under the assumption of fixed-precision arithmetic, where numbers are represented with a constant number of bits. This aligns the theoretical model with practical hardware implementations.

**Comparison Formalisms:**
The UHATs are compared against:
- **Linear Temporal Logic (LTL):** Standard LTL formulas are used, allowing for temporal operators like `Until` (U) and `Since` (S). A language is recognized if the formula holds at the last position of an input word.
- **Boolean RASP (B-RASP):** This is an intermediate programming language expressively equivalent to UHATs, previously established by Yang et al. (2024). B-RASP programs consist of predicates derived from basic symbol lookups, position-wise Boolean operations, and attention operations that select values based on mask and score predicates. B-RASP serves as a convenient model for proving EXPSPACE-hardness due to its direct mapping to Turing machine computations.
- **Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs):** Defined as state-transition systems over rational vectors, with fixed precision assumptions. Under this assumption, an RNN with a D-dimensional hidden state using k-bit precision can be represented by a finite automaton with `2^(kD)` states.
- **Finite Automata:** Standard deterministic finite automata (DFAs).

**Key Methodological Steps:**
1. **Defining Succinctness:** The paper formally defines "f-more succinct" and "g-bounded expansion" to precisely quantify the relative compactness of language representations across different formalisms.
2. **EXPSPACE-Hardness Proof (Lower Bound):**
 - The 2^N-tiling problem, known to be EXPSPACE-complete, is used as the basis for reduction.
 - A B-RASP program is constructed that recognizes encodings of valid `2^N`-tiling solutions. This program leverages attention operations to implement "doubly exponentially large counters" and check complex horizontal and vertical adjacency constraints within a tiling configuration. The construction ensures the B-RASP program's size is polynomial in N.
 - A polynomial-time, language-preserving translation from a specific form of B-RASP programs (sufficient for the tiling reduction) to UHATs is provided (Lemma 9). This transfers the EXPSPACE-hardness from the tiling problem, through B-RASP, to UHATs.
3. **EXPSPACE-Membership Proof (Upper Bound):**
 - A crucial step is proving that for any UHAT, the precision required to evaluate it on any input is polynomial in its size (Proposition 12). This means the set of possible rational values during computation is finite and enumerable in exponential time.
 - Based on this, an exponential-time construction is provided to translate any UHAT into an equivalent LTL formula (Proposition 13). This significantly improves upon prior doubly exponential translations.
 - Since the non-emptiness problem for LTL is PSPACE-complete (Sistla and Clarke, 1985), applying this PSPACE algorithm to an exponentially sized LTL formula results in an EXPSPACE complexity for the UHAT non-emptiness problem.
4. **Succinctness Gap Characterization:**
 - A witness family of languages {L_n} is constructed using the `2^N`-tiling reduction (which itself encodes Turing machine counters that reach `2^(2^N)` states). This family requires polynomial-size UHATs but necessitates exponentially larger LTL formulas and doubly exponentially larger finite automata, formally demonstrating the succinctness advantages.
 - Conversely, a polynomial-time translation from LTL formulas to equivalent UHATs is provided (Proposition 16), establishing an upper bound on the succinctness gap in the other direction.
5. **Complexity of Verification Problems:** The established EXPSPACE-completeness for non-emptiness is then used to derive the EXPSPACE-completeness for the equivalence problem for UHATs.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a rigorous theoretical characterization of transformer succinctness and its computational consequences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings regarding the succinctness of transformers and the computational complexity of their verification problems:

1. **Transformers are Exponentially More Succinct than LTL:**
 - The paper formally proves that unique-hard attention transformers (UHATs) can describe certain languages exponentially more compactly than Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) (Theorem 15). This means there exist families of languages for which a polynomial-size UHAT is equivalent to an LTL formula that is exponentially larger.
 - This result is demonstrated by constructing a witness family of languages (L_n). These languages are built to encode solutions to the 2^N-tiling problem, which in turn can simulate a Turing machine with a 2^N-bit counter. The smallest accepted word in these languages has a length of at least 2^(2^N). While these languages can be recognized by UHATs of polynomial size (in N), any LTL formula recognizing such a language must have at least exponential size due to its inability to succinctly represent such large counters.
 - Conversely, the paper shows that UHATs have polynomially bounded expansion over LTL (Proposition 16). This implies that any LTL formula can be converted into an equivalent UHAT with at most a polynomial increase in size. This establishes that the succinctness gap is asymmetric, with UHATs being more succinct.

2. **Transformers are Doubly Exponentially More Succinct than Finite Automata:**
 - Using the same witness family of languages L_n, the research demonstrates that UHATs are doubly exponentially more succinct than finite automata (Theorem 17). A polynomial-size UHAT recognizing L_n implies an accepted word of length at least 2^(2^N). Since any finite automaton that recognizes a non-empty language accepts a word whose length is at most linear in the automaton's size, the smallest equivalent finite automaton for L_n must be at least doubly exponentially large in N.

3. **Transformers are Exponentially More Succinct than Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs):**
 - As a direct consequence of the succinctness findings with respect to LTL and the fact that fixed-precision RNNs can be converted to finite automata (with an exponential blow-up related to hidden state dimension and precision), UHATs are shown to be exponentially more succinct than RNNs (Corollary 18).

4. **Computational Complexity of Transformer Verification Problems:**
 - **Non-emptiness Problem is EXPSPACE-Complete:** The problem of determining whether the language recognized by a given UHAT is non-empty is proven to be EXPSPACE-complete (Theorem 4).
 - **EXPSPACE-Hardness (Lower Bound):** This is established by a reduction from the 2^N-tiling problem (known to be EXPSPACE-complete) to the non-emptiness problem for B-RASP programs, and subsequently demonstrating a polynomial-time translation from a relevant class of B-RASP programs to UHATs. The B-RASP construction shows how to use attention to enforce complex constraints over doubly exponentially long counter sequences.
 - **EXPSPACE-Membership (Upper Bound):** This is achieved by proving that all rational values arising during a UHAT's computation have a polynomial bit-length (Proposition 12), enabling a constructive translation from any UHAT to an equivalent LTL formula with at most an exponential blow-up in size (Proposition 13). Since LTL satisfiability is PSPACE-complete, applying this decision procedure to an exponentially sized LTL formula yields an EXPSPACE algorithm for UHAT non-emptiness.
 - **Equivalence Problem is EXPSPACE-Complete:** The problem of deciding whether two UHATs recognize the same language is also proven to be EXPSPACE-complete (Theorem 19). This follows from the EXPSPACE-completeness of non-emptiness (by reduction) and the ability to translate UHATs to LTL formulas, whose equivalence problem is PSPACE-complete.

5. **Improved LTL Translation:** The paper provides an exponential-time translation from UHATs to LTL formulas, which improves upon a previous doubly exponential translation by Yang et al. (2024).

6. **Restricted UHATs:** For UHATs constrained to use only strict future masking and rightmost tie-breaking (or strict past masking and leftmost tie-breaking), the non-emptiness problem falls within NEXP (Corollary 14). This is attributed to their equivalence with fragments of LTL that only use the `P` (past) or `F` (future) operator, whose non-emptiness problems are NP-complete.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of transformers and their computational properties, with significant implications for both fundamental theory and practical applications, particularly in the domain of AI safety and verification.

1. **Reframing Transformer Expressivity:** The work introduces succinctness as a crucial lens for evaluating neural architectures, complementing the traditional focus on expressive capacity. By demonstrating that fixed-precision transformers are remarkably succinct—exponentially more succinct than LTL and RNNs, and doubly exponentially more succinct than finite automata—the paper offers a theoretical explanation for the strong empirical performance of transformers, even when they may not be "more expressive" than RNNs in terms of the *set* of languages they recognize. This reframing suggests that the power of transformers might lie in their ability to describe complex patterns very compactly, rather than their ability to recognize an inherently broader class of languages.

2. **Highlighting Inherent Intractability for Verification:** The finding that fundamental verification problems for UHATs, such as non-emptiness and equivalence, are EXPSPACE-complete reveals their inherent computational intractability in the worst case. This establishes a high theoretical barrier for the formal verification of transformers, which is a critical concern in explainable AI and for ensuring the safety and trustworthiness of AI systems. It implies that general-purpose, worst-case complete verification algorithms for transformers will face severe scaling challenges, likely requiring double exponential time resources.

3. **Guiding Future Verification Research:** The identified complexity provides crucial guidance for future research in transformer verification.
 - **Subclass Identification:** The EXPSPACE-hardness proof relies on transformers encoding large counters. This suggests a promising avenue for research: identifying and characterizing subclasses of transformers that are unable to encode such complex counting mechanisms. These restricted subclasses might then admit more tractable verification algorithms, possibly with lower complexity bounds.
 - **Practical Techniques:** Despite the worst-case intractability, the paper challenges the field to adapt and develop practical verification techniques (e.g., symbolic methods, simulation, abstraction) from classical model checking and formal methods to address transformer verification for realistic scenarios, potentially leveraging domain-specific properties or approximation.

4. **Strengthening Connections to Classical Theory:** By rigorously comparing transformers to classical models of computation like LTL, RNNs, and finite automata, and by leveraging complexity-theoretic tools such as the tiling problem, the research firmly integrates modern deep learning architectures into the established theoretical framework of computer science. The improved exponential translation from UHATs to LTL provides a tighter and more accurate characterization of this relationship than previously available.

5. **Informative for Design and Learnability:** The theoretical bounds on succinctness and verification complexity could inform the design of future transformer architectures or their training procedures. Understanding which computational capabilities drive succinctness (e.g., doubly exponential counters via attention) can guide efforts to achieve specific representational efficiencies or to avoid unintended complexities. It also relates to open questions about the learnability of succinct transformers and their length generalization capabilities, areas that exhibit mixed empirical evidence.

In summary, this paper provides a foundational theoretical contribution by rigorously establishing the inherent succinctness of transformers and the corresponding high complexity of their formal verification, thereby offering new insights into their computational power and challenging the research community to develop innovative approaches for their analysis and assurance.
