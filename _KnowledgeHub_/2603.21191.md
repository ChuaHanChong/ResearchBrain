# On the Role of Batch Size in Stochastic Conditional Gradient Methods

> [!summary] Summary
> This research provides a theoretical analysis of stochastic conditional gradient methods for large-scale language model training under a fixed token budget, establishing convergence guarantees under the "mu"-Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz condition. It derives a critical scaling rule for effective batch-sequence size as proportional to total token budget to the power of 2/3 and proposes adaptive hyperparameter schedules, empirically validating these findings on a NanoGPT model.

## Problem

- Large-scale language model training is constrained by a fixed token budget, leading to a fundamental tradeoff between batch size and sequence length not fully captured by existing theoretical frameworks; empirically, a "critical batch size" exists beyond which increasing batch size degrades optimization efficiency and generalization, yet a robust theoretical explanation with explicit error laws is missing.
- Current hyperparameter transfer frameworks (e.g., "mu"P) primarily focus on local gradient stability, failing to provide global scaling laws for batch size, sequence length, and learning rate in relation to the total token budget.
- Most prior stochastic conditional gradient (SCG) analyses rely on general convex or nonconvex assumptions, which may not adequately model the structured nonconvexity found in deep neural network loss landscapes.

## Method

- Developed a theoretical framework for momentum-based ==Stochastic Conditional Gradient (SCG)== methods, deriving novel convergence guarantees under the ==μ-Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz (μ-KL) condition==, and translated these into explicit token-budget-aware error laws ($K = T/(BS)$) revealing the non-monotone relationship between optimization error and the effective batch-sequence scale (BS).
- Derived the ==BST scaling rule== ($BS \sim T^{2/3}$) for optimal effective batch-sequence size and corresponding scaling rules for the ==Frank–Wolfe stepsize== and momentum parameter.
- Proposed practical, adaptive scheduling strategies for batch size, sequence length, and stepsize, along with guidelines for hyperparameter transfer, and validated them empirically on a NanoGPT model.

## Results

- Established convergence guarantees for momentum-based SCG under the "mu"-KL condition, identifying an optimal regime where the achievable optimization error scales approximately as **T^(-1/3)**.
- Empirical studies on NanoGPT confirmed the practical relevance of the "mu"-KL condition and validated the **1/(BS)** scaling of gradient variance, while ablation studies demonstrated distinct batch size regimes and performance degradation when the effective batch-sequence size (BS) exceeded an optimal threshold under a fixed token budget.
- The derived BST scaling rule accurately predicted optimal Frank-Wolfe stepsizes and enabled effective hyperparameter transfer across varying token budgets and model sizes, outperforming fixed, "mu"P-style scheduling approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimization error in SCG methods under a fixed token budget exhibits a non-monotone relationship with the effective batch-sequence size (BS), transitioning through noise-dominated, batch-independent, and iteration-starved regimes.
> - An optimal effective batch-sequence scale exists, predicted by the "BST scaling rule" (BS ~ T^(2/3)), which efficiently balances stochastic noise reduction with the number of available optimization iterations.
> - The "mu"-Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz ("mu"-KL) condition provides a suitable theoretical framework for analyzing the structured nonconvexity of deep learning loss landscapes, confirmed by empirical validation on language models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The training of large-scale language models (LLMs) is a computationally intensive process primarily constrained by a fixed token budget ($T$) rather than a fixed number of optimization steps. This setting introduces a fundamental tradeoff concerning batch size ($B$) and sequence length ($S$). While increasing the batch size can enhance hardware utilization, empirical evidence suggests that beyond a certain threshold, it can lead to reduced optimization efficiency and potentially poorer generalization performance. This phenomenon of a "critical batch size" is well-documented in prior empirical studies, which also note its dependence on effective data size and training stage, prompting the development of adaptive training schedules.

Existing theoretical frameworks, such as the $\mu P$ framework, address hyperparameter transfer by ensuring local stability of gradient magnitudes and parameter updates across different model scales. However, these frameworks typically focus on the early stages of training and do not explicitly model how batch size, sequence length, and learning rate should scale globally with the total token budget to optimize long-horizon training performance. They do not provide explicit error laws as functions of ($B, S, T$) or characterize when increasing batch size becomes detrimental under a fixed token budget.

This paper addresses this gap by providing a theoretical analysis for stochastic conditional gradient (SCG) methods, a class of projection-free optimization algorithms increasingly relevant for modern norm-constrained training, exemplified by optimizers such as Muon. Unlike conventional gradient descent, SCG methods use a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) to find update directions. Previous analyses of SCG methods have often relied on assumptions of strong convexity or general nonconvexity and sometimes generalized smoothness conditions. This work distinguishes itself by analyzing SCG methods under a structured nonconvexity condition, specifically the $\mu$-Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz ($\mu$-KL) condition, which is argued to better capture the practical loss landscape geometry of deep neural networks and align with SCG's dual norm-based stationarity measure.

The work aims to move beyond empirical heuristics and local stability guarantees to offer a comprehensive, token-budget-aware theoretical framework for understanding and guiding hyperparameter selection and adaptation in large-scale deep learning, particularly for SCG methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to quantitatively understand and provide principled guidelines for the selection and adaptation of key hyperparameters—batch size ($B$), sequence length ($S$), and stepsize—in stochastic conditional gradient (SCG) methods for large-scale language model training, specifically under a fixed token budget ($T$).

The motivation for this study stems from several observations and open questions in the field:
1. **The Token Budget Constraint:** In large-scale LLM training, the total number of processed tokens ($T$) is typically fixed. Given that the number of parameter updates ($K$) is inversely proportional to the product of batch size and sequence length ($K = T / (BS)$), the choice of ($B, S$) and stepsize directly dictates how efficiently the token budget is converted into optimization progress. This coupling necessitates a deeper understanding of their joint effects on optimization error.
2. **The Batch Size Tradeoff:** Empirically, increasing the batch size initially improves hardware utilization and can accelerate training. However, beyond a certain "critical batch size," further increases often lead to diminishing returns, degrade optimization efficiency, and can negatively impact generalization. This non-monotone behavior is observed across various models and training stages, highlighting that batch size is not a static hyperparameter but a dynamic variable. Despite these empirical insights, a robust theoretical framework explaining *why* and *when* this degradation occurs, and providing explicit error laws, has been largely absent.
3. **Limitations of Existing Hyperparameter Transfer Frameworks:** Approaches like $\mu P$ have successfully enabled the transfer of learning rates across model scales by focusing on parameterizations that maintain local gradient stability. However, these methods do not inherently provide global scaling laws that consider the interaction between batch size, sequence length, and total token budget over an entire training trajectory. They ensure stable individual updates but do not address the cumulative optimization error under a fixed resource budget.
4. **Structured Nonconvexity in Deep Learning:** Most prior SCG analyses have focused on general convex or nonconvex settings. The authors propose that the $\mu$-KL condition, a form of structured nonconvexity, is more appropriate for characterizing the loss landscapes encountered in deep learning. Validating and leveraging this condition in SCG analysis is a key motivation to derive more practically relevant convergence bounds.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Derive novel convergence guarantees for momentum-based SCG methods under the $\mu$-KL condition, explicitly capturing the interaction between stepsize, batch size, and stochastic noise.
- Translate these guarantees into explicit error laws as functions of ($B, S, T$) to identify the regimes where increasing batch size is beneficial or detrimental.
- Propose principled, adaptive scheduling rules for batch size, sequence length, and stepsize that optimize performance under a fixed token budget.
- Provide empirical validation of these theoretical predictions using large-scale language models.

The overarching goal is to bridge the gap between empirical observations and theoretical understanding in large-scale optimization, offering actionable guidance for designing efficient training schedules.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combination of theoretical analysis and empirical validation to investigate the role of batch size in stochastic conditional gradient methods.

**Theoretical Framework:**
1. **Algorithm under Study:** The core algorithm analyzed is Algorithm 1, a momentum-based Stochastic Conditional Gradient (SCG) method. This algorithm updates a momentum buffer ($m_{k+1} = (1-\alpha)m_k + \alpha g(x_k; \xi_k)$) and then uses a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) to find a direction $d_{k+1}$ by minimizing the inner product with the momentum buffer, subject to a norm constraint ($\|d\| \leq 1$). The model parameters are then updated as $x_{k+1} = (1-\beta)x_k + \beta\eta d_{k+1}$.
2. **Problem Formulation and Assumptions:** The method aims to solve $\min_{x \in X} f(x)$. The analysis relies on four key assumptions:
 - **(A1) L-Smoothness:** The gradient $\nabla f(\cdot)$ is Lipschitz continuous with constant $L$ with respect to a general norm $\|\cdot\|$.
 - **(A2) Norm Equivalence:** There exists a constant $\rho > 0$ such that the dual norm is bounded by the Euclidean norm: $\|x\|_* \leq \rho\|x\|_2$. This is always true in finite-dimensional spaces.
 - **(A3) $\mu$-Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz ($\mu$-KL) Condition:** The objective function $f(x)$ satisfies $\|\nabla f(x)\|_* \geq \mu(f(x) - f^\star)$ for some $\mu > 0$. This condition relates first-order stationarity to suboptimality and is considered suitable for SCG due to its reliance on the dual norm. The authors empirically validate this assumption on a NanoGPT model, observing a linear relationship between dual gradient norm and training loss for lower loss values.
 - **(A4) Stochastic Gradient Noise:** The stochastic gradient estimator $g(\cdot; \xi)$ is unbiased, $E_\xi[g(x; \xi)] = \nabla f(x)$, and has $\sigma$-bounded variance, $E_\xi[\|g(x; \xi) - \nabla f(x)\|_2^2] \leq \sigma^2$. Crucially, the variance is modeled as $\sigma^2 = \sigma_\star^2 / (BS)$, where $B$ is batch size and $S$ is sequence length, reflecting that larger batch-sequence products reduce noise. This assumption is also empirically verified.
3. **Convergence Guarantees:** The paper derives in-expectation convergence guarantees (Theorem 4.1) for Algorithm 1 under these assumptions, explicitly tracking the influence of momentum ($\alpha$), smoothness ($L$), and stochastic noise ($\sigma$). The bounds specify the number of iterations ($K$) required to reach an $\epsilon$-suboptimal solution.
4. **Token Budget-Aware Error Laws (Corollary 4.1):** By substituting the relation $K = T / (BS)$ (where $T$ is the total token budget) into the iteration complexity bounds, the analysis transforms them into explicit error laws that depend on ($B, S, T$). This derivation reveals a non-monotone relationship between the achievable optimization error $\epsilon$ and the effective batch-sequence scale $BS$.
5. **BST Scaling Rule:** Based on the derived error laws, the authors identify a critical $BS$ value that separates different performance regimes. By balancing the dominant terms in the error expression, they derive the "BST scaling rule": $BS \sim T^{2/3}$, along with corresponding scaling rules for the Frank–Wolfe stepsize ($\beta \sim 1/K$) and momentum parameter ($\alpha = \text{Const}$).
6. **Adaptive Scheduling Strategies:** The theoretical insights are translated into concrete strategies for practitioners, including:
 - Guidelines for transferring hyperparameters (B, S, β) from a smaller model to a larger one under a fixed tokens-per-parameter (TPP) ratio.
 - Guidelines for adapting hyperparameters when only the token budget increases for the same model.
 - A multi-stage adaptive scheduling protocol where batch size and sequence length are increased during training as the effective token budget grows, with corresponding adjustments to the stepsize.

**Empirical Validation:**
The theoretical predictions are validated through experiments on a modded NanoGPT model trained on the FineWeb dataset.
1. **Assumption Verification:**
 - **Assumption 3.4 ($\sigma$-bounded variance):** Empirical gradient variance is measured as a function of batch size and sequence length. The results support the $\sigma^2 \sim 1/(BS)$ scaling.
 - **Assumption 3.3 ($\mu$-KL condition):** The dual norm of mini-batch gradients and training loss are tracked. A robust linear regression model confirms the linear relationship for low loss values, providing empirical support for the $\mu$-KL condition.
2. **Ablation Studies:** Experiments vary batch size ($B$) and sequence length ($S$) to find the optimal Frank–Wolfe stepsize ($\beta$) for a 124M NanoGPT model under a fixed token budget (1.3B). These studies aim to empirically identify the batch-independent regime and optimal operating points.
3. **Estimation of Problem-Dependent Constants:** The constants $L$, $\mu$, and $\rho$ are empirically estimated for various model configurations (varying number of layers and embedding dimensions). Power-law fits are derived to model how these constants change with model size and batch size. These estimates are then used in the BST scaling rule for hyperparameter transfer.
4. **Evaluation of Scheduling Strategies:**
 - **Restarted Scion:** The proposed adaptive strategy (increasing $BS$ and decreasing $\beta$ after an initial training phase) is compared against fixed schedules.
 - **Fixed Tuned-Batch Scion (μP-style):** Training with hyperparameters tuned on a smaller model, fixed throughout for a larger model.
 - **Fixed Large-Batch Scion:** Training with larger $BS$ from the beginning, with $\beta$ chosen either by $\mu P$ or the BST rule.
5. **Hyperparameter Transfer:** Experiments test the BST rule's predictive power for optimal $\beta$ and $\alpha$ when increasing the token budget for a fixed model size (124M) and when increasing model size (from 124M to 1B).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key theoretical and empirical findings:

**Theoretical Findings:**
1. **Convergence Guarantees for Momentum SCG under $\mu$-KL:** The paper established convergence guarantees for momentum-based SCG (Algorithm 1) under the $\mu$-KL condition, accounting for L-smoothness, norm equivalence, and bounded-variance stochastic noise. This provides a formal framework for analyzing these methods in structured nonconvex settings.
2. **Regime-Dependent Batch Size Behavior:** The token-budget-aware analysis (Corollary 4.1) revealed a non-monotone dependence of the achievable optimization error ($\epsilon$) on the effective batch-sequence scale ($BS$). Three distinct regimes were identified:
 - **Noise-dominated regime:** For small $BS$, increasing $BS$ improves performance by reducing stochastic noise.
 - **Intermediate regime:** The best achievable error becomes largely independent of $BS$, scaling approximately as $T^{-1/3}$. This is the optimal operating point for token efficiency.
 - **Large-batch regime:** Beyond a critical threshold, increasing $BS$ deteriorates performance linearly, as the algorithm becomes "iteration-starved" under a fixed token budget ($K = T/(BS)$).
3. **Critical BST Scaling Rule:** The theory predicts an optimal effective batch-sequence scale $BS \sim T^{2/3}$ (up to problem-dependent factors) that balances performance and hardware efficiency. This rule indicates how $BS$ should scale with the total token budget $T$.
4. **Optimal Hyperparameter Scaling:** Under the BST scaling rule, the optimal Frank–Wolfe stepsize scales as $\beta \sim 1/K$, and the momentum parameter $\alpha$ is predicted to remain constant, suggesting its transferability across scales.

**Empirical Findings:**
1. **Empirical Validation of Assumptions:**
 - Assumption 3.3 ($\mu$-KL condition) was supported empirically on NanoGPT, showing a linear relationship between dual gradient norm and training loss at lower loss values.
 - Assumption 3.4 (gradient variance $\sigma^2 \sim 1/(BS)$) was also empirically supported, with measured variance stabilizing rapidly during training and fitting the power-law relationship.
2. **Ablation Studies Confirm Regimes:** Ablation studies on NanoGPT (124M model, $T=1.3B$) showed that the optimal Frank–Wolfe stepsize stabilizes for sufficiently large batch sizes. The best performance was observed around $B=256, S=1024$, with minimal difference between $B=256$ and $B=512$, consistent with the predicted batch-independent regime. Further increases in $BS$ led to performance degradation.
3. **Problem-Dependent Constant Estimation:** The constants $L, \mu, \rho$ were estimated across different NanoGPT model configurations and found to vary slowly with model size, following fitted power-law relationships (Eq. 15).
4. **Effectiveness of Adaptive Scheduling (Restarted Scion):**
 - The proposed "Restarted Scion" strategy (increasing $BS$ and adjusting $\beta$ after an initial training phase) demonstrated competitive performance, often leading to accelerated improvement in validation loss compared to fixed schedules.
 - The $\mu P$-style fixed hyperparameter configuration (using values tuned for a smaller model without adaptation) consistently achieved the worst performance, highlighting its limitations for global, token-budget-aware optimization.
 - Increasing the batch size while keeping sequence length fixed ($B_1=512, S_1=2048$ vs $B_2=1024, S_2=1024$) was slightly more effective than increasing both, if context extension is not a priority.
5. **Saturation and Degradation with Excessive Batch Size:** Empirical results confirmed that increasing $BS$ beyond the values suggested by the BST scaling rule led to performance degradation. For instance, for a 1B model, a batch size of 1024 (as per BST) outperformed 2048 and 4096, with the latter showing more pronounced loss increases.
6. **Hyperparameter Transfer Validation:**
 - **Increasing Token Budget (124M model):** The BST scaling rule accurately predicted optimal Frank–Wolfe stepsizes ($\beta$) for increased token budgets, outperforming fixed $\mu P$-style stepsizes. The momentum parameter $\alpha$ was empirically found to transfer well, supporting the theoretical prediction of its constancy.
 - **Increasing Model Size (1B model):** The BST rule provided good estimates for both optimal momentum ($\alpha$) and Frank–Wolfe stepsize ($\beta$) when transferring from a 124M model to a 1B model, demonstrating its applicability across model scales.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a significant theoretical contribution to understanding the dynamics of large-scale optimization, particularly for stochastic conditional gradient methods under resource constraints. Its findings have several implications and potential impacts:

1. **Principled Optimization Guidance:** The work moves beyond empirical heuristics for batch size selection by providing a robust theoretical framework rooted in convergence analysis under the $\mu$-KL condition. The derived BST scaling rule ($BS \sim T^{2/3}$) offers a principled method for determining the effective batch-sequence scale, clarifying when increasing batch size is beneficial and when it becomes detrimental.
2. **Efficient Training Schedule Design:** The adaptive scheduling rules, along with the guidelines for hyperparameter transfer across different token budgets and model sizes, offer practical recipes for designing more efficient training schedules in large-scale machine learning. This is particularly relevant for LLM training, where compute resources are a primary bottleneck.
3. **Challenging Conventional Wisdom:** The findings challenge the simplistic view that large batches are inherently problematic for generalization or optimization. Instead, the research suggests that when batch size, sequence length, and learning rate are jointly adapted according to the BST scaling rule, large-batch training can remain token-efficient and effective.
4. **Bridging Theoretical Gaps:** The research bridges the gap between theories focusing on local stability (e.g., $\mu P$) and the observed global optimization behavior over an entire training trajectory under a fixed token budget. It disentangles the role of initialization and parameterization from long-horizon optimization efficiency.
5. **Applicability to Modern Optimizers:** By focusing on SCG methods, which underlie modern norm-constrained optimizers like Muon, the work provides directly applicable insights for a growing class of optimizers used in advanced deep learning architectures.
6. **Enhanced Understanding of Loss Landscapes:** The empirical validation of the $\mu$-KL condition in large-scale language models contributes to a better understanding of the geometric properties of their loss landscapes during training.

The potential impact includes enabling more effective resource allocation in LLM training, accelerating the development of larger and more capable models, and fostering the design of more robust and theoretically-grounded optimization algorithms. By providing a clearer understanding of the batch size tradeoff, the research offers a foundation for developing next-generation optimization strategies that are both theoretically sound and empirically validated.
