# QuietWalk: Physics-Informed Reinforcement Learning for Ground Reaction Force-Aware Humanoid Locomotion Under Diverse Footwear

> [!summary] Summary
> QuietWalk presents a physics-informed reinforcement learning framework for humanoid locomotion, utilizing an inverse-dynamics-constrained neural network to estimate ground reaction forces from proprioceptive signals. This approach achieved a 7.17 dB reduction in mean noise level on average across various surfaces while enabling robust and stable walking across diverse footwear and outdoor terrains.

## Problem

- Humanoid locomotion generates significant foot-ground impact transients, leading to acoustic noise, accelerated hardware wear, and potential damage to surfaces.
- Existing low-noise locomotion methods often rely on indirect kinematic proxies, which can compromise gait agility and stability, creating a trade-off with quietness.
- Reliably estimating ground reaction forces (GRFs) for direct impact control is challenging due to the fragility and noise of physical force sensors and the lack of physical consistency or real-time applicability in current estimation methods.
- Humanoid robots lack robust adaptation to diverse footwear, which significantly alters contact dynamics and hinders policy generalization across different end-effector configurations.

## Method

- **QuietWalk** develops an ==inverse-dynamics-constrained Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN)== that accurately estimates per-foot vertical ==ground reaction forces (GRFs)== using only proprioceptive signals, removing the dependency on external force sensors.
- Integrated the frozen, physics-consistent ==GRF predictor== directly into the ==reinforcement learning reward function==, enabling direct penalization of predicted impact forces for quiet locomotion.
- Designed a ==footwear asset modeling== and parameterization pipeline and employed a ==curriculum learning== strategy to train a single policy robust to diverse footwear (e.g., high heels) and surface conditions.

## Results

- The GRF predictor achieved high accuracy, with RMSEs of **14.49 N** (left foot) and **14.00 N** (right foot) and **R² values of 0.9887 and 0.9899**, respectively, on a held-out dataset.
- The Quiet RL policy reduced the mean noise level by **7.17 dBA** and peak noise level by **4.98 dBA** on average across four indoor surfaces, compared to a standard RL policy, approaching an engineering baseline controller.
- The policy demonstrated robust and stable locomotion across **four diverse footwear types** (barefoot, skate shoes, athletic sneakers, high heels) and various outdoor terrains (e.g., grass, gravel, cobblestone) without significant posture divergence or slipping.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Enforcing inverse-dynamics consistency within a neural network is critical for robust and accurate GRF prediction, reducing errors by 82-86% and significantly improving physical plausibility compared to purely supervised methods.
> - Directly penalizing predicted ground reaction forces via a physics-informed reward function effectively reduces both mean and peak noise levels (e.g., 7.17 dBA MNL reduction) without compromising gait stability or locomotion performance.
> - Footwear properties strongly interact with surface materials, causing up to a 20 dB difference in noise levels between optimal and adverse conditions, demonstrating the necessity of cross-footwear generalization for practical humanoid deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is expanding its application scenarios from controlled industrial environments to human-centered spaces like homes, hospitals, and offices. This shift introduces stringent requirements beyond basic locomotion, including enhanced acoustic performance and safety. A significant challenge in this context is mitigating foot–ground impact transients, which are a primary source of structural vibration and acoustic noise. Excessive impact forces not only generate undesirable noise but also contribute to accelerated hardware wear and potential damage to floor surfaces.

Existing research in low-noise or low-impact legged locomotion often relies on indirect methods. Some approaches penalize kinematic quantities, such as foot contact velocity or velocity changes, as proxies for impact reduction. While these methods have demonstrated some success in reducing noise, they can sometimes compromise gait agility and stability, creating a trade-off between quietness and robust, fast locomotion.

Direct measurement of ground reaction forces (GRFs), which are crucial for accurate impact control, typically involves fragile and noisy force sensors. This reliance on physical sensors can lead to training instability in reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks and limits deployability in real-world scenarios where such sensors might be impractical or prone to failure. Efforts to estimate GRFs using physics-based inverse dynamics or deep learning have been made, but these often lack physical consistency or struggle with real-time application in a closed-loop control system without external force sensing.

Furthermore, humanoids operating in diverse environments will encounter varying contact dynamics, particularly those induced by different types of footwear. While humans adapt their gait remarkably to changes in footwear, robust adaptation to such end-effector variations in robots remains a less explored area. Current robot control policies rarely account explicitly for the significant distribution shifts in contact dynamics introduced by different footwear, such as high heels, which can hinder policy generalization.

This work addresses these identified gaps by proposing a framework that integrates a physics-informed GRF estimation with reinforcement learning. It seeks to enable direct impact penalization without relying on fragile external sensors at deployment, improve physical consistency of force estimation, and provide a systematic approach to validate and achieve cross-footwear generalization, thereby enhancing the deployability and robustness of humanoid locomotion in human-centered environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to achieve low-impact, low-noise humanoid walking with explicit cross-footwear generalization capabilities. This is motivated by the increasing deployment of humanoid robots in human-centered environments (e.g., homes, hospitals, offices), where acoustic comfort, hardware longevity, and environmental safety are critical requirements.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

1. **Develop a reliable and deployable ground reaction force (GRF) estimator without external force sensors:**
 - **Motivation:** Ground reaction forces are a primary source of impact and noise during locomotion. Direct measurement using physical force sensors is often impractical for real-world deployment due to their fragility, noise, and cost. Existing estimation methods may lack physical consistency or real-time applicability in control loops.
 - **Objective:** To create an inverse-dynamics-constrained Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) that accurately estimates per-foot vertical GRFs using only proprioceptive signals (joint positions, velocities, accelerations), thereby removing the dependency on external force sensors for force feedback during deployment.

2. **Integrate physics-informed GRF feedback into reinforcement learning for impact-aware locomotion control:**
 - **Motivation:** Prior low-noise locomotion often uses kinematic proxies (e.g., foot contact velocity) in reward functions, which can lead to trade-offs between quietness, stability, and agility. Direct penalization of impact forces could achieve a more precise balance. However, traditional force-sensor signals are problematic for RL training.
 - **Objective:** To embed the frozen, inverse-dynamics-consistent GRF predictor into the RL training loop as a physics-consistent reward critic. This allows for direct penalization of predicted impact forces, enabling the policy to optimize for low-impact transients while maintaining gait stability and locomotion performance, with consistent force feedback between training and deployment.

3. **Ensure and systematically validate robust locomotion under diverse footwear conditions:**
 - **Motivation:** Human locomotion adapts to various footwear, which significantly alters contact dynamics. For humanoids to be truly versatile, they need similar adaptability. Footwear-induced changes, especially from items like high heels, introduce distribution shifts that can compromise policy generalization.
 - **Objective:** To develop a methodology for modeling footwear assets (geometry and inertia) to consistently reproduce footwear differences in simulation and to introduce cross-footwear robustness as a new dimension for humanoid locomotion control validation. This includes testing generalization across conditions ranging from barefoot to high heels on various surfaces.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a practical and robust solution for quieter, gentler, and more durable indoor humanoid locomotion by bridging the gap between physics-based dynamics, data-driven learning, and robust control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed QuietWalk framework consists of three primary components: an inverse-dynamics-constrained Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) for GRF prediction, a footwear asset modeling and parameterization pipeline, and a physics-informed reinforcement learning (RL) strategy for quiet locomotion.

#### 3.1. A. Inverse-Dynamics-Constrained PINN for GRF Prediction

This component focuses on estimating the bilateral normal ground reaction force (GRF) using only proprioceptive inputs, eliminating the need for external force sensors at deployment.

- **Input and Output:** The network takes a history of 6 frames (0.12 seconds at 50 Hz) of proprioceptive signals, specifically joint positions ($q$), velocities ($\dot{q}$), and accelerations ($\ddot{q}$), concatenated into a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{3n}$ (where $n=29$ is the joint dimension). The output is the bilateral normal GRF, $f_z = [f^{(L)}_z, f^{(R)}_z]^T \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Ground-truth GRF data for training is collected from plantar force-sensor insoles.
- **Network Architecture (Fig. 3):** The PINN is designed to explicitly incorporate physical laws rather than operating as a purely black-box model. It utilizes Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KAN) layers for nonlinear mappings.
 - **DynamicsKAN:** Predicts the inertia matrix $M(q) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ from $q$, enforcing symmetry and positive definiteness through a factorized form.
 - **PotentialKAN:** Learns a scalar potential $V(q)$ to derive the conservative generalized force $G(q) = \nabla_q V(q)$, preserving the conservative structure.
 - **Coriolis/Centrifugal Force:** The Coriolis/centrifugal generalized force $C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q}$ is computed from the learned $M(q)$.
 - **Sequence-Net:** Estimates the effective generalized actuation $\tau_{eff} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, which accounts for joint dynamic friction and unmodeled non-contact effects.
- **Inverse Dynamics Constraint:** The core of the physics-informed approach lies in satisfying the robot's inverse dynamics in joint space: $M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + G(q) = \tau_{eff} + J_n^T f$.
 - The contact-induced generalized force $\tau_c$ is calculated as $\tau_c = M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + G(q) - \tau_{eff}$.
 - The raw contact force $f_{raw}$ is then solved using a damped least-squares (DLS) regularized pseudoinverse: $f_{raw} = (J_n J_n^T + \lambda^2 I)^{-1} J_n \tau_c$, where $J_n \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times n}$ is the bilateral normal contact Jacobian and $\lambda$ is a damping coefficient for numerical stability.
 - Finally, the predicted normal GRF $f_z^{pred}$ is projected onto the non-negative orthant as $f_z^{pred} = \max(f_{raw}, 0)$ to ensure physical consistency (forces are non-negative).
- **Loss Function:** A weighted sum of multiple terms is minimized during training:
 - $L_{grf}$: Supervised regression loss between predicted and ground-truth normalized GRFs.
 - $L_{dyn}$: Inverse-dynamics residual, penalizing the mismatch between the calculated $\tau_c$ and $J_n^T f_z^{pred}$.
 - $L_{swing}$: Penalizes non-zero predicted forces during the swing phase, enforced by a binary contact mask.
 - $L_{smooth}$: A temporal smoothing regularizer, penalizing variations between consecutive predicted forces over the input sequence.

#### 3.2. B. Footwear Asset Modeling and Parameterization

This component ensures consistent representation of footwear effects across different simulation and hardware tests.

- **Process:** Based on the Unitree G1 foot model, three footwear assets (Skate shoes, Athletic sneakers, High-heeled shoes) are designed. Shoe meshes are aligned with the foot model, Boolean union is used to merge them, and key geometry (outsole thickness, toe curvature, heel height) is adjusted.
- **Consistency:** Effective leg-length changes due to outsole thickness are compensated. The merged geometry is exported in formats like STL, URDF, MJCF, and USD, with synchronized visual and collision geometries. Mass and inertia are recomputed under a unified density assumption to maintain consistent inertia distribution. This approach isolates footwear differences to changes in foot-end geometry and inertia.

#### 3.3. C. Physics-Informed Low-Impact RL for Quiet Locomotion

This component trains the locomotion policy in a simulator (Isaac Sim) using the frozen GRF predictor for reward shaping.

- **Policy and Control:** The policy operates at 50 Hz, taking 6 frames of proprioceptive history as input. It outputs joint position increments $\Delta q \in \mathbb{R}^n$, which are then tracked by a fixed PD controller to generate torque commands $\tau_{cmd}$.
- **Reward Function:** The total RL reward $r_t$ is composed of three parts:
 - $r_{task}$: Standard terms for velocity tracking and stability.
 - $r_{bonus}$: Auxiliary regularizers like joint energy and motion smoothness.
 - $r_{impact}$: The core quiet-locomotion reward, defined as $r_{impact} = -\alpha ( (f^{(L)}_z)^2 + (f^{(R)}_z)^2 )$. This term directly penalizes high predicted normal GRFs from the frozen PINN estimator, with $\alpha$ being a weighting coefficient.
- **Training Curriculum:** To prevent degenerate solutions and improve generalization, a staged curriculum is adopted:
 - Initial learning with a small $\alpha$ for stable walking.
 - Gradual increase of $\alpha$ to reduce impact while preserving task performance.
 - Progressive increase in terrain randomization difficulty, starting from flat ground.
 - Introduction of different footwear types (skate shoes, athletic sneakers, high heels) after initial barefoot training.
 - Final stage involves jointly sampling all footwear types to learn a single policy adaptable to various footwear-induced contact variations.
- **Deployment:** The GRF predictor is frozen during RL training. This ensures that the impact feedback signal used in training is consistent with what would be available during real-world deployment, where external force sensors are not required.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental validation of the GRF predictor's accuracy and stability, the low-noise performance of the physics-informed RL policy, and its robustness under diverse footwear and surface conditions.

#### 4.1. A. GRF Predictor Evaluation

The GRF predictor was evaluated on a held-out dataset comprising forward, lateral, backward walking, and in-place turning motions, using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Coefficient of Determination (R$^2$). An ablation study was conducted on the loss function terms.

- **Overall Performance (C1 - Proposed Method):** The full loss function (C1) achieved an RMSE of 14.49 N for the left foot and 14.00 N for the right foot, with R$^2$ values of 0.9887 and 0.9899, respectively. This indicates a high degree of accuracy and variance explanation.
- **Impact of Inverse Dynamics Constraint ($L_{dyn}$):** Removing the inverse-dynamics residual ($L_{dyn}$, C3) caused the most significant performance degradation. RMSE increased dramatically to 106.29 N (left) and 79.44 N (right), representing a 6-7x increase. R$^2$ values dropped to 0.3899 (left) and 0.6747 (right), indicating poor physical self-consistency and generalization across gaits. Qualitatively, C3 showed a clear breakdown of physical consistency.
- **Impact of Supervised Term ($L_{grf}$):** Removing the supervised regression term ($L_{grf}$, C2) resulted in magnitude calibration drift, although the temporal shape of the GRF prediction still matched the ground truth due to remaining dynamic constraints. RMSE was significantly higher (50.11 N left, 49.35 N right) and R$^2$ lower (0.8644 left, 0.8745 right) compared to the full model.
- **Impact of Swing Phase Constraint ($L_{swing}$):** Removing $L_{swing}$ (C4) led to non-zero "spurious forces" during the swing phase and mis-detection of contact peaks, increasing RMSE to 22.28 N (left) and 20.28 N (right).
- **Impact of Smoothing Regularizer ($L_{smooth}$):** Removing $L_{smooth}$ (C5) introduced high-frequency oscillations around swing segments and contact transitions, degrading temporal stability and increasing RMSE to 26.26 N (left) and 22.19 N (right).
- **Conclusion for Predictor:** The ablation study demonstrated that enforcing inverse-dynamics consistency is critical for reliable GRF prediction, reducing prediction errors by 82-86% and significantly improving R$^2$ values compared to a purely supervised predictor (C3).

#### 4.2. B. Quiet Locomotion Performance

Locomotion noise and gait performance were evaluated on a real robot at 1.2 m/s, across four surface conditions (concrete, carpet, wood, yoga mat) under the barefoot condition. Acoustic metrics included Mean Noise Level (MNL) and Peak Noise Level (PNL), both A-weighted (dBA).

- **Noise Reduction (Barefoot):** The proposed Quiet RL policy (D2) significantly reduced noise compared to a standard RL policy (D1).
 - Averaged over four surfaces, MNL decreased from 89.47 dBA (D1) to 82.30 dBA (D2), a reduction of 7.17 dB.
 - PNL decreased from 104.28 dBA (D1) to 99.30 dBA (D2), a reduction of 4.98 dB.
- **Comparison to Baseline:** D2 approached the performance of the official Unitree built-in controller (D3), which served as an engineering baseline. Averaged over surfaces, the gaps between D2 and D3 were 4.17 dB (MNL) and 2.34 dB (PNL). On specific surfaces like carpet, wood, and yoga mat, the PNL gap was as small as 0.47–1.86 dB.
- **Surface Dependency:** Denoising gains were larger on concrete and carpet, and smaller on wood and yoga mat, suggesting surface material is a dominant factor in noise generation.

#### 4.3. C. Cross-Footwear and Surface Robustness

The D2 policy was evaluated across four footwear conditions (barefoot, skate shoes, athletic sneakers, high-heeled shoes) and four surface conditions.

- **Footwear and Surface Interaction:** Clear structural patterns in noise levels were observed. Footwear with stronger cushioning/damping and larger contact areas (skate shoes, athletic sneakers) generally yielded lower MNL/PNL. High-heeled shoes, conversely, were noisier on most surfaces.
 - This indicates that higher effective contact stiffness and shorter contact transitions, often associated with high heels or hard surfaces like wood, concentrate impact energy at higher frequencies and amplify structural vibrations, leading to increased A-weighted levels and peak noise.
 - Compliant and highly damped contacts (e.g., athletic sneakers on a yoga mat) prolonged force rise time, suppressed high-frequency components, and reduced peak noise.
- **Range of Noise Levels:** A substantial noise difference (close to 20 dB) was observed between the most adverse combination (high-heeled shoes on wood) and the most favorable combination (athletic sneakers on a yoga mat). This highlights the strong interaction between footwear and surface properties and indicates that challenges remain for very high-stiffness/low-damping contact scenarios.
- **Outdoor Robustness:** The D2 policy demonstrated robust adaptation and transferability across diverse outdoor terrains (grass, gravel, cobblestone roads, smooth stone slab, asphalt) under different footwear conditions (Fig. 8). The robot maintained stable gait without significant posture divergence, repeated trips, or persistent slipping.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents QuietWalk, a physics-informed reinforcement learning framework that offers several advancements with potential implications for humanoid robotics.

The development of an inverse-dynamics-constrained PINN for vertical GRF estimation is a significant step towards enabling sensorless impact-aware control. By relying solely on proprioceptive inputs and enforcing physical consistency through the inverse dynamics residual, the method reduces dependency on fragile and noisy external force sensors. This improves the practical deployability and robustness of humanoid robots in real-world environments, where dedicated force sensors may not be feasible or reliable. The quantitative results demonstrate that incorporating inverse-dynamics consistency is critical for accurate and stable GRF prediction, surpassing purely data-driven methods in terms of physical plausibility and generalization.

Integrating this frozen, physics-consistent GRF predictor directly into the reinforcement learning reward function for impact penalization provides a more direct and effective approach to "quiet walking" compared to methods that use kinematic proxies. The reported reduction in mean and peak A-weighted noise levels on hardware, without sacrificing locomotion stability, suggests that this approach can achieve a better balance between acoustic performance and overall gait characteristics. This capability is essential for the seamless integration of humanoids into shared human spaces, mitigating disturbances caused by robot noise.

Furthermore, the introduction of footwear-induced contact variation as a critical dimension for robustness evaluation is a novel aspect of this work. By systematically modeling different footwear types and demonstrating robust adaptation across various shoe-ground combinations, the research addresses a crucial gap in robot generalization capabilities. The findings regarding the interaction between footwear properties and surface materials offer insights into the complex dynamics of foot-ground contact, informing future designs of robot feet and control strategies for enhanced adaptability. This robust cross-footwear performance could enable humanoids to operate effectively in environments where changes in end-effector properties or floor surfaces are common.

While the current framework primarily focuses on the vertical GRF component and acoustic measurements are based on a fixed setup for relative comparison, the work lays a foundation for future advancements. Potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** Quieter robots are less intrusive and more socially acceptable, improving user experience in homes, hospitals, and offices.
- **Increased Robot Longevity:** Reduced impact forces minimize wear and tear on robot joints and structures, leading to longer operational lifespans and lower maintenance costs.
- **Broader Application Scope:** Robots capable of adapting to diverse footwear and surfaces can perform tasks in a wider range of environments, from structured indoor settings to varied outdoor terrains.
- **Advancements in Physics-Informed AI:** The framework demonstrates a successful synergy between model-driven physical constraints and data-driven learning, pushing the boundaries of physics-informed AI in robotics control.

Future work outlined, such as extending force modeling to full 3D contact dynamics and improving acoustic measurement calibration, indicates promising directions for further enhancing the fidelity and applicability of such control policies. The sensorless approach particularly holds significant potential for realizing general-purpose, physically consistent locomotion controllers for the next generation of humanoid robots.
