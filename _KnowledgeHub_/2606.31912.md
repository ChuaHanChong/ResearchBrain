# Learning Locomotion on Discrete Terrain via Minimal Proximity Sensing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed a deep reinforcement learning framework that enables quadrupedal robots to robustly navigate discrete terrain by integrating minimal infrared proximity sensors directly into their feet. This foot-centric sensing approach facilitates reliable traversal of gaps up to 60 cm and stepping stones, achieving an average speed of 0.52 m/s while demonstrating superior robustness to common sensing and state estimation errors compared to global perception systems.

## Problem

- Legged robots struggle with robust locomotion over unstructured terrain containing discontinuities (gaps, edges) due to insufficient anticipatory contact awareness.
- Global perception systems (LiDAR, depth cameras) suffer from self-occlusion, latency, high computational cost, and vulnerability to state estimation drift, limiting their utility in dynamic legged locomotion.
- Existing proprioceptive and simple contact sensors are reactive, providing feedback only after impact, which is inadequate for proactive foothold selection and impact force modulation.

## Method

- Equipping quadruped robot feet with low-cost, low-latency ==STMicroelectronics VL53L5CX Time-of-Flight (ToF) proximity sensors== configured for a 4x4 grid view.
- Training a ==deep reinforcement learning== policy (==LSTM== with ==PPO==) in a high-fidelity simulation environment (==Isaac Gym==), using foot-centric proximity sensor readings alongside proprioceptive inputs.
- Employing a ==two-stage curriculum learning== strategy combined with explicit ==sensor degradation modeling== (noise, latency, missing readings) during training to facilitate robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- Policies employing foot-centric proximity sensing demonstrated superior robustness in simulation, maintaining significantly higher success rates on challenging terrains when subjected to perturbations like static joint offsets, robot model mismatches (e.g., **120%** shank length), and up to **400%** increased odometry noise, compared to base-centric perception.
- The ANYmal-D robot, equipped with the trained policy, successfully traversed real-world discontinuous terrains, including horizontal gaps up to **60 cm**, scattered stepping stones, and a **20 cm** wide balance beam, achieving an average speed of **0.52 m/s**.
- Emergent active perception behaviors were observed, such as utilizing foot sensor readings to detect upcoming gaps, guide foot extension towards platform edges, and infer surface normals to ensure controlled touchdowns on horizontal surfaces.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct, localized pre-contact sensing, particularly at robot feet, provides more reliable and robust environmental information than complex global perception systems prone to errors in state estimation or occlusions.
> - Accurate modeling of sensor noise, latency, and failure modes in simulation is critical for successful sim-to-real transfer of policies trained with minimal, imperfect sensory inputs.
> - Reinforcement learning can leverage these low-dimensional, foot-centric proximity signals to develop emergent, active perception behaviors for dynamic foothold selection and safe traversal of discontinuous terrain.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has seen substantial advancements, particularly with the advent of learning-based control methods, enabling robots to perform increasingly dynamic behaviors such as agile locomotion, object interaction, and whole-body manipulation. However, the performance of many legged systems remains constrained by an incomplete awareness of impending contact with the terrain. This limitation is particularly critical in unstructured environments characterized by terrain discontinuities like gaps, edges, or protrusions, where an inability to detect these features at appropriate spatial and temporal scales can lead to locomotion failure.

Current approaches to environmental perception in legged robots primarily rely on two main categories of sensors:

- **Global Perception Systems:** These include depth sensors such as LiDAR and RGB-D cameras, which offer rich global context about the environment. However, they are susceptible to various challenges, including self-occlusion by robot limbs or environmental occlusions, limited fields of view, time synchronization issues, and high computational costs associated with dense geometric reconstruction. Furthermore, state estimation methods built upon these sensors can suffer from small errors and latency, which are amplified during highly dynamic maneuvers, leading to drift and synchronization problems.
- **Proprioceptive Feedback:** This category involves joint measurements (e.g., torques, velocities, tracking accuracy) that provide information about the robot's internal state. While essential, proprioceptive feedback is inherently reactive, meaning corrections can only be initiated after an impact has occurred. Reinforcement learning (RL) policies often use raw sensor observations to implicitly infer latent state information. While teacher policies in simulation with full ground-truth access can learn agile skills, their performance degrades when distilled to student policies constrained by real-world onboard sensing, largely because these modalities do not provide direct, spatially resolved measurements of imminent contact.
- **Contact Sensors:** Sensors like pressure-sensitive resistors have been integrated into robot feet to provide explicit signals at the moment of impact. However, similar to proprioception, these sensors do not offer the necessary anticipatory information required for dynamic foothold selection and successful gap traversal. Additionally, simulating high-frequency contact forces realistically can be challenging for many simulators.

This research addresses the gap in providing direct, anticipatory, and localized pre-contact feedback. While short-range proximity and time-of-flight (ToF) sensors have been explored in other robotic contexts, such as tabletop manipulators for collision detection and smaller wheeled or legged robots, their application in learning-based, whole-body legged locomotion over unstructured terrain has remained relatively underexplored. This work investigates the integration of such minimal proximity sensors directly into the feet of a quadrupedal robot as a robust, low-power, and low-latency alternative or complement to existing perception systems. The paper positions its contribution as a method to achieve reliable locomotion by strategically placing task-relevant local sensors, thereby offering a scalable solution for operation in unpredictable, unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to enable quadrupedal robots to robustly navigate discrete terrain, such as gaps and stepping stones, by providing anticipatory information about imminent ground contact through minimal, localized sensing. This is motivated by the recognized limitations of existing perception paradigms in dynamic and unstructured environments.

The detailed motivations and objectives include:

- **Overcoming Limitations of Global Perception:** A key motivation is to bypass the inherent drawbacks of global perception systems (LiDAR, depth cameras), which suffer from latencies, occlusions (especially self-occlusion by the robot's own limbs), high computational demands for dense geometric reconstruction, and susceptibility to state estimation drift. The authors seek a sensing solution that is less computationally intensive and more robust to these issues.
- **Addressing the Reactivity of Proprioceptive and Contact Sensing:** Traditional proprioceptive feedback is purely reactive, initiating corrections only after impact. Similarly, contact sensors only provide information at the moment of physical contact. The research aims to provide "pre-contact" feedback, which is crucial for making proactive decisions regarding foothold placement, impact timing, and impact force modulation, particularly when traversing complex terrain discontinuities.
- **Providing Localized Geometric Awareness:** The paper is motivated by the need for spatially resolved, near-field terrain information that is directly relevant to foot placement. By embedding infrared proximity sensors directly into the robot's feet, the objective is to enable the robot to "screen underfoot terrain" and gain local geometric awareness independent of global line-of-sight constraints, which can be problematic for chassis-mounted sensors.
- **Facilitating Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A significant challenge in learning-based robotics is bridging the "sim-to-real" gap. The authors aim to characterize the noise, latency, and failure modes of the chosen proximity sensors, integrate these models accurately into the simulation environment, and demonstrate that policies trained under these conditions can be transferred to real-world robots with high fidelity.
- **Developing an Efficient and Robust Control Framework:** The work seeks to integrate these localized sensing signals into a reinforcement learning framework to generate emergent, agile, and safe terrain-seeking maneuvers. This includes developing a single unified policy capable of handling diverse terrain types without requiring specialized map-generation stacks, complex map-processing mechanisms, or terrain-specific training.
- **Offering a Low-Cost, Low-Power Solution:** The use of low-cost, high-frequency infrared proximity sensors is motivated by the desire to provide a computationally inexpensive and energy-efficient alternative or complement to more complex and power-hungry global perception suites.

The specific contributions outlined in the paper further detail these objectives:
- The design and deployment of readily integratable, low-cost proximity sensing modules for quadruped feet tailored for discrete terrain.
- A comprehensive characterization of these proximity sensors to model noise, latency, and failure modes for improved simulation fidelity and sim-to-real transfer, alongside evaluation of how learned policies cope with sensor inaccuracies.
- Experimental validation of the proposed sensing framework on real hardware, demonstrating reliable traversal of discontinuous terrain.

In essence, the research investigates a minimal sensing paradigm with learning-based control, hypothesizing that strategically placed, task-relevant local sensors can form a scalable and effective component of robotic systems operating in real-world, unstructured environments, reducing reliance on complex global perception.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves the hardware integration of minimal proximity sensors, their characterization for accurate simulation modeling, the development of a reinforcement learning framework, and rigorous evaluation in both simulation and real-world deployments.

#### 3.1. Sensorized Robotic Feet

The researchers modified the feet of a commercially available ANYmal-D quadruped robot. Each foot was equipped with an STMicroelectronics VL53L5CX Time-of-Flight (ToF) proximity sensor and its associated signal-processing electronics. Key specifications and integration details include:
- **Sensor Configuration:** The VL53L5CX sensor has a 45° × 45° Field of View (FoV), divided into a 4 × 4 grid (16 individual cells). It was configured to operate at 60 Hz to match the robot's motion controller frequency.
- **Data Transmission:** Data from the sensor to the robot is relayed via a USB buffer, with a measured delay ranging from 20 to 60 ms.
- **Measurement Capabilities:** For each of the 16 cells, the sensor reports the minimum distance to an obstacle within that cell with a 1 mm resolution, along with a binary measurement-validity flag. Readings exceeding 1 meter are clipped due to increased noise and reduced relevance for locomotion planning.
- **Physical Integration:** The sensor was snugly placed within the rubber ball foot, with a machined aperture to allow for its FoV. To enhance coverage of the anticipated foothold region during a typical foot swing, the sensor's optical axis was tilted approximately 15° outward relative to the sagittal plane.

#### 3.2. Sensor Characterization

To ensure reliability across a range of operational conditions, the ToF sensor's performance was characterized under varying distances and incidence angles on representative surfaces. This characterization revealed that:
- The sensor demonstrated high accuracy and reliability at short ranges (e.g., under 0.6 m) and when oriented nearly perpendicularly to the target surface.
- Increased distances and oblique incidence angles resulted in higher measurement noise and a greater rate of missing data (as depicted in Fig. 3).
- This empirical data formed the foundation for the sensor-noise model implemented in the simulation environment, aiming to bridge the sim-to-real gap.

#### 3.3. Control Methods

The control approach utilizes a reinforcement learning framework, directly incorporating the foot-centric proximity sensor feedback.
- **Policy Inputs:** The learning policy receives low-dimensional foot proximity sensor readings, which are simulated as ray casters mounted on the robot's feet with an FoV analogous to the real sensors. In addition, proprioceptive inputs such as joint positions, gravity vector, and body linear and rotational velocities are provided.
- **Policy Architecture:** The policy is implemented as a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, suitable for processing sequential data and capturing temporal dependencies.
- **Training Algorithm:** The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used for training.
- **Simulation Environment:** Training is conducted entirely within the Isaac Gym simulation environment, which provides high-performance GPU-accelerated physics simulation.
- **Training Stages and Curriculum:** A two-stage curriculum learning approach is employed:
 - **Stage 1:** Focuses on familiarizing the robot with fundamental terrain types, primarily stepping stones (dense grid, two-row, rough box fields, platform-gap crossings, mixed-row stones). Difficulty progressively increases within each terrain type by interpolating geometric parameters (e.g., decreasing foothold size, increasing spacing, greater vertical irregularity). This stage aims to enable basic locomotion reflexes under ideal sensing.
 - **Stage 2:** Introduces more challenging variations of Stage 1 terrains from the outset (e.g., smaller stones, larger minimum gaps, greater height variations) and expands the variety of initial robot poses. New terrain types like stairs and faceted ground are added to promote generalization across diverse gaits and walking styles. The objective is to obtain a single, unified policy capable of traversing all target terrains.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function consists of:
 - Sparse task rewards provided at the end of each episode upon reaching a target position.
 - Dense task rewards offering continuous guidance during learning.
 - Regularization rewards, following, to encourage efficient energy use and suppress undesirable behaviors like abnormal postures, excessive jumping, or stagnation.
- **Sensor-Specific Randomization for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To enhance policy transferability to the real robot, sensor degradation characteristics are explicitly modeled during training:
 - **Measurement Inaccuracies:** Static offset, proportional noise, and Gaussian noise are introduced to sensor readings.
 - **Missing Readings:** A fraction of readings is randomly marked as missing at each timestep and replaced with zeros.
 - **Temporal Delays:** Foot proximity sensor readings are delayed for a few steps, corresponding to the measured real-world delays (20-60 ms).
 - For each degradation type, the randomization level is sampled uniformly per episode, starting with very low levels in Stage 1 and increasing to mimic real-world levels in Stage 2, thereby reducing overfitting.

#### 3.4. Robustness Evaluation (Simulation)

To assess the benefits of foot-centric sensing, extensive simulations were performed, comparing the Foot Proximity Sensor policy against two base-centric benchmarks that derive "pre-contact" information from global observations:
- **Mock (Ideal Height Scan):** This benchmark uses a top-down ray caster in simulation that projects rays only against the terrain. It provides a noise-free 2.5D height scan (5m × 5m with 5cm resolution). Foot proximity readings are then emulated from this ideal map.
- **Mock (Aggre. Elevation Map):** This benchmark emulates an elevation-mapping pipeline that iteratively constructs a height scan from depth images captured by six chassis-mounted depth cameras. Cells within camera FoV are updated directly, while dead zones rely on prior maps transformed using estimated odometry (via zero-order integration of base velocities). Foot proximity readings are then emulated from this aggregated map.
- **Fair Comparison:** All simulated ray casters cast only against the terrain (no self-occlusions) to ensure an equitable comparison of sensor modalities. The same trained locomotion policy is used across all evaluations.
- **Performance Metric:** Success rate over 1000 runs on a challenging test terrain (80% stepping-stone, 20% gap terrain, at 90% curriculum difficulty). A run is successful if the robot reaches the target within 0.5m.
- **Perturbations:** The policies were evaluated under various out-of-distribution perturbations:
 - Static joint position offsets.
 - Robot model dimension mismatches (e.g., varying shank length by ±20%).
 - Odometry noise (scaled from 0% to 400% of training noise levels).

#### 3.5. Real-World Deployment

The trained policy was deployed on a physical ANYmal-D robot.
- **Evaluation Terrains:** Experiments were conducted on canonical sparse terrains: horizontal gaps (up to 60 cm), aligned or scattered rows of stepping stones (20x20 cm, with supporting bricks 20 cm lower and some stones 20 cm higher), and a straight 20 cm wide balance beam.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness and robustness of foot-centric proximity sensing for legged locomotion.

#### 4.1. Sim-to-Real Transfer

The approach successfully modeled the minimal proximity sensors in simulation, accounting for noise, latency, and failure modes. This detailed modeling facilitated the transfer of learned policies from simulation to the real ANYmal-D robot with high fidelity.

#### 4.2. Robustness Evaluation in Simulation

The comparative simulation study highlighted the superior robustness of foot-centric sensing compared to base-centric perception methods under various realistic perturbations:
- **Baseline Performance:** In the absence of any injected degradations, the base-centric sensing models (Mock Ideal Height Scan and Mock Aggregated Elevation Map) achieved reasonable locomotion performance.
- **Robustness to Static Joint Position Offset:** When a static bias was introduced to joint position readings (Fig. 6A), the performance of the base-centric sensing diminished significantly faster than that of the foot-centric proximity sensor. This degradation in base-centric systems was attributed to corrupted estimated foot positions, which are critical for querying proximity readings from a global map.
- **Robustness to Robot Model Dimension Mismatches:** Varying the robot's shank length by up to ±20% (Fig. 6B) demonstrated that the Foot Proximity Sensor maintained greater robustness. The Mock (Ideal Height Scan) performance declined more sharply due to errors in estimated foot positions caused by the unmodeled shank lengths.
- **Robustness to Odometry Noise:** The Mock (Aggre. Elevation Map) system, which relies on odometry to aggregate prior height maps, exhibited substantially more pronounced degradation with increasing odometry noise (Fig. 6C). The success rate of Mock (Aggre. Elevation Map) dropped from 51.7% to 24.3% when odometry noise increased from 0% to 400%, whereas the Foot Proximity Sensor maintained significantly higher performance across the noise spectrum. This result underscored the vulnerability of multi-component perception pipelines to drift and noise in state estimation.
- **Overall Conclusion from Robustness:** The comprehensive robustness evaluation indicated that sensing directly at the point of interest (foot-centric) provides more reliable and clear environmental information. Integrating intermediary components, such as those relying on other proprioceptive or exteroceptive sensors, increases the number of potential failure points and magnifies sensitivity to noise.

#### 4.3. Real-World Deployment Performance

The policy deployed on the ANYmal-D robot demonstrated effective traversal across all canonical sparse terrains used for evaluation (Fig. 7, 8A):
- **Traversal Capability:** The robot successfully navigated horizontal gaps up to 60 cm, various configurations of stepping stones (aligned, scattered, with varied heights), and a 20 cm wide balance beam.
- **Speed and Agility:** The robot maintained an average speed of 0.52 m/s, exhibiting a balance between stability and agility.
- **Emergent Active Perception Behaviors:** The foot proximity sensors enabled emergent, reflexive behaviors crucial for safe traversal.
 - **Gap Detection and Crossing (Fig. 8D):** During gap traversal, specific phases were observed:
 - As the foot lifted over a gap (0.80 s - 1.00 s), the measured distances sharply increased, indicating awareness of the trench below.
 - Subsequent foot movement (1.00 s - 1.26 s) led to a reversal in the relative ordering of zone readings, signaling the detection of the side surface of the next platform. This prompted the robot to extend its foot towards it as distances decreased.
 - Finally, another reversal in zone ordering (1.26 s - 1.40 s) combined with a sudden increase in distance indicated approach to the upper surface of the platform, leading to a controlled touchdown.
 - **Surface Normal Inference (Fig. 8E):** Analysis of the z-coordinate of the foot over time, colored by the minimum-reading zone, suggested that touchdown events typically occurred only when a nearby, approximately horizontal surface was detected. Conversely, sustained detection of a quasi-vertical surface correlated with continued foot-lifting behavior, actively guiding the robot towards an elevated stone. These observations demonstrate that the robot utilized the foot sensors to actively scan the environment and inform dynamic foothold selection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of legged robotics, particularly in the domain of robust locomotion over challenging, unstructured terrain. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Robust Locomotion in Complex Environments:** The work demonstrates that sophisticated and dynamic locomotion behaviors, even in complex environments with discrete terrain, do not inherently demand high-bandwidth, computationally intensive global vision pipelines. By integrating low-latency, foot-mounted infrared proximity sensors, the quadrupedal robot gains a "pre-contact reflex" that substantially improves its reliability in environments where traditional global sensors are prone to failure due to self-occlusion, perceptual aliasing, or state estimation drift. This is crucial for real-world deployment in unpredictable scenarios like navigating rubble, forests, or industrial sites with fragmented ground.

- **Simplification of Perception Stacks:** The approach offers a low-power and low-latency alternative or complement to complex global perception suites. By relying on minimal, localized sensing, it bypasses the need for extensive tuning, high computational resources, and sophisticated map-generation and processing mechanisms (e.g., multi-head attention) often associated with vision-based approaches. This simplification could lead to more energy-efficient, lighter, and less expensive robotic systems.

- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The detailed characterization and modeling of sensor noise, latency, and failure modes in simulation contribute to narrowing the sim-to-real gap. This rigorous approach to sensor randomization during training is vital for developing policies that are robust enough to transfer effectively from idealized simulations to the imperfections of real-world hardware and environments.

- **Insights into Optimal Sensing Strategies:** The findings underscore the principle that sensing "as close as possible to the point(s) of interest" can be the most effective strategy for acquiring clear and reliable environmental information. This suggests a paradigm shift from purely global, centralized perception towards a more distributed, task-informed, and localized sensing architecture, where sensors are strategically placed to gather the most relevant information for specific tasks. The emergence of active perception behaviors, where the robot uses its feet to scan the environment, further validates the efficacy of this localized sensing strategy.

- **Guidance for Future Robotic Design:** The research provides valuable insights for the design of future autonomous robots. It suggests that while algorithmic complexity is important, a critical path to achieving truly robust systems also lies in better-integrated, task-informed, and localized sensing. This could influence future hardware development towards more modular and distributed sensor networks on robotic platforms.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge several limitations and areas for future investigation. These include the sensitivity of ToF measurements to the optical properties of surfaces (e.g., highly absorptive or specular materials), and concerns regarding hardware longevity and susceptibility of sensor apertures to clogging by mud or debris in harsh environments. Future work is proposed to address these issues, potentially through IR-transparent housings or self-clearing mechanisms. Additionally, research into morphological optimization to determine the optimal density and placement of these sensors across the robot's limbs and undercarriage is suggested to achieve more holistic near-field awareness. Overall, this work demonstrates a practical and effective method to enhance quadrupedal locomotion robustness by intelligently integrating minimal, task-relevant sensors.
