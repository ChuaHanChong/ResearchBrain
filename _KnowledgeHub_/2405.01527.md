# Track2Act: Predicting Point Tracks from Internet Videos enables Generalizable Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Track2Act learns generalizable robot manipulation by predicting 2D point tracks of how image regions should move toward a goal, trained on diverse web videos of humans and robots. Predicted tracks are converted into open-loop rigid-transform actions, then corrected by a closed-loop residual policy trained on only ~400 embodiment-specific demonstrations, enabling manipulation of unseen objects and scenes without test-time adaptation.

## Problem

- Prior generalizable manipulation policies rely on large amounts of embodiment-specific demonstration data, limiting scalability to new objects, scenes, and tasks.
- Web video pretraining methods for robotics often predict only affordances or single interaction points, lacking the temporal action-trajectory detail needed for precise manipulation.
- Existing goal-conditioned policies trained purely from robot demonstrations fail to generalize to compositional or entirely novel object types unseen during training.

## Method

- **Track2Act**'s ==diffusion==-based point track prediction model forecasts future 2D point trajectories in an image conditioned on a goal, trained jointly on human and robot web videos including Something-Something-v2, Epic-Kitchens, RT-1, and BridgeData.
- A task-agnostic transformation module that converts predicted point tracks into a sequence of ==rigid-body transforms== of the manipulated object, yielding open-loop robot end-effector poses.
- A ==closed-loop residual policy== trained on roughly 400 embodiment-specific demonstrations that predicts corrective residual actions over the open-loop plan at each timestep to fix execution errors.

## Results

- The full residual-policy method reaches up to **70%** success rate under mild generalization to unseen instances, versus **0%** for the goal-conditioned BC baseline in harder generalization settings.
- Removing the residual correction and using pure open-loop track-derived actions substantially lowers success rate, showing residual correction is necessary for reliable closed-loop execution.
- Success rate degrades from about **70%** under mild generalization to roughly **20-40%** under compositional and type generalization, confirming these are the hardest generalization regimes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling large-scale web-video pretraining for perception/motion prediction from small-scale embodiment-specific policy correction is an effective way to cut demonstration-data requirements.
> - Point tracks are a more actionable and transferable intermediate representation than affordance heatmaps for converting passive video knowledge into executable robot actions.
> - Open-loop plans derived from video-only supervision are systematically noisy, so pairing them with even a small closed-loop residual corrector yields disproportionate reliability gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The vision of generalizable robot manipulation, where robots can operate reliably in novel, unstructured environments without extensive prior training, is a grand challenge in robotics and AI. This paper directly addresses a major bottleneck in achieving this: the severe limitation of real-world robot interaction data.

Historically, approaches to robot manipulation have often relied on:
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) on Robot Interaction Datasets**: Works like have attempted to scale BC. While promising in principle, collecting diverse, real-world robot data is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming, leading to datasets that often lack the task and scene diversity needed for true generalization. These datasets are typically restricted to lab-like, structured scenarios.
- **Test-time Training**: Recent efforts involve a human demonstrating a task, followed by hours of online exploration for the robot to learn. While allowing for "in-the-wild" deployment, this approach is inconvenient, time-consuming, and not "directly executable" out-of-the-box in new scenarios, thus limiting scalability and safety.
- **Learning Visual Representations for Manipulation**: Methods pre-train visual backbones on large image/video datasets but still crucially depend on significant in-domain robot data or test-time adaptation for policy learning, often leading to task-specific policies.
- **Structured Representations**: Approaches using point clouds or keypoints often require 3D datasets, sometimes from simulation, and are limited to specific, structured tasks. Flow-based methods often need 3D robot interaction data.
- **Leveraging Non-Robot Datasets**: Some works attempt to use human videos to predict affordances or hand-object masks. While these provide useful cues, they are often insufficient for direct task accomplishment and typically require conjunction with online learning or large, perfectly aligned human-robot datasets.

Track2Act differentiates itself by proposing a novel factorization of the manipulation policy. Instead of directly learning actions from limited robot data or relying on task-specific cues from human videos, it learns an *embodiment-agnostic interaction plan* (point tracks) from *diverse, uncurated web videos* (human and robot). This plan is then converted into robot-specific actions, which are refined by a *minimal set of embodiment-specific robot demonstrations* through a residual policy. This approach directly addresses the data scarcity and generalization limitations of prior work, aiming for direct deployability without test-time adaptation, and leveraging the immense scale of web data in a more structured way than previous attempts. It builds upon recent advances in general-purpose video tracking and diffusion models to realize its core idea.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a **generalizable goal-conditioned robot manipulation policy** that can execute diverse tasks directly out-of-the-box in novel, unseen scenarios without requiring any test-time adaptation. This vision is motivated by the desire for robots that can assist humans in everyday environments (e.g., offices, kitchens) with minimal setup and maximum reliability.

The key motivations driving the Track2Act framework are:
1. **Direct Executability and Generalization**: To enable robots to reliably perform tasks in new scenarios immediately, without requiring human demonstrations or lengthy self-practice, which are costly and impractical for widespread deployment.
2. **Overcoming Data Scarcity in Robotics**: Traditional robot learning methods require vast amounts of in-domain robot interaction data, which is exceptionally challenging and expensive to collect for diverse tasks and environments.
3. **Leveraging Abundant Web Videos**: Recognizing the vast, largely untapped resource of passive videos on the web (showing humans and robots manipulating objects), the authors aim to develop a method that can scalably learn from this diverse, "in-the-wild" data.
4. **Embodiment-Agnostic Interaction Planning**: To create an intermediate representation of an interaction plan that is independent of a specific robot's embodiment, allowing the learned knowledge from web videos to be universally applicable before being translated into robot-specific actions.
5. **Robust Closed-Loop Control with Minimal Robot Data**: To combine the broad generalization capability derived from web videos with the precision and contact reasoning required for real-world robot manipulation, using only a small, manageable amount of robot-specific data for fine-tuning.

In essence, Track2Act seeks to bridge the gap between large-scale, diverse, but embodiment-agnostic internet video data and the precise, embodiment-specific requirements of real-world robot control, aiming for a system that is both highly generalizable and directly deployable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Track2Act framework proposes a factorized policy that leverages diverse web videos for generalizable real-world manipulation. The approach consists of three main stages: Point Track Prediction, Inferring Coarse Manipulator Trajectory, and Closed-loop Manipulation with Residual Policy Correction.

#### 3.1. Point Track Prediction from Web Videos

- **Interaction Plan Definition**: The core idea is to predict an "embodiment-agnostic interaction plan" as a 2D trajectory (track) of points in an image over future time-steps, given an initial image ($I_0$), a goal image (G), and a set of initial points ($P_0$). This representation captures motion directly, making it more expressive than masks and easier to compute than full RGB frames.
- **Model Architecture**: A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) model is used to instantiate this track prediction as a denoising process. The model, $V_\theta(I_0, G, P_0, k)$, is trained to predict noise added to future point locations ($P_t$) at different diffusion time-steps ($k$).
- **Conditioning**: The DiT model is conditioned on embeddings of the initial image ($z_0$), goal image ($z_g$), and the diffusion time-step ($z_k$) using adaptive modulation (adaLN) layers. The input to the Transformer is a sequence of tokens corresponding to the $p$ initial points.
- **Training Data**: The model is trained purely on diverse, passive web videos, including human videos (EpicKitchens, Something-Something-v2) and robot videos (RT1, BridgeData). Ground-truth point tracks are generated by running an off-the-shelf point tracking approach (Co-Tracker) on 400,000 video clips. The model is designed to handle a variable number of randomized initial points ($p$), making it flexible for test-time point selection.
- **Embodiment Agnostic**: A crucial aspect is that this model learns to predict object motions without any knowledge of the specific robot embodiment, making it widely applicable.

#### 3.2. Inferring Coarse Manipulator Trajectory from Interaction Plan

- **From 2D Tracks to 3D Rigid Transforms**: In a robot's environment, given $I_0$, $G$, and a random set of points $P_0$, the trained track prediction model outputs future 2D locations ($\hat{P}_t$). Assuming a fixed camera and manipulation of a single object, a subset of these points with significant motion ($\tau_{obj}$) is identified.
- **3D Point Information**: With an RGBD camera, depth information for $P_0$ is available, yielding 3D points $P_0^{3D}$.
- **Optimization for Rigid Transforms**: A sequence of per-time rigid transforms $T_t$ (rotation $R_t$ and translation $t_t$) of the object relative to the first frame is inferred. This is achieved by solving an optimization problem (e.g., using PnP solvers, as described in Algorithm 1) that minimizes the reprojection error between the projected transformed 3D points ($KT_t P_0^{3D}$) and the predicted 2D tracks ($\{(x_i^t, y_i^t)\}_{i=1}^p$). This ensures the inferred 3D motion aligns with the predicted 2D movement.
- **Open-Loop End-Effector Poses**: The rigid transforms $T_t$ describe the object's motion. To obtain robot actions, a heuristic is applied: the robot end-effector first moves to the center of the initial 3D points on the object ($e_1$) with its initial orientation ($e_0$), performs a grasp, and then applies the rigid transforms to this grasped pose, yielding an open-loop action trajectory $\bar{a}_t = T_t e_1$.

#### 3.3. Closed-Loop Manipulation with Residual Policy Correction

- **Addressing Open-Loop Limitations**: The open-loop plan derived from predicted transforms may suffer from inaccuracies (e.g., grasping errors, slight prediction errors, lack of contact reasoning) because it uses no embodiment-specific data.
- **Residual Policy for Refinement**: A goal-conditioned residual policy, $\pi_{res}(I_t, G, \tau, [\bar{a}_t]_{t=1}^H)$, is introduced to correct these errors. Instead of predicting full actions, it predicts residual actions $\Delta a_t$ at each time-step, such that the executed action is $\hat{a}_t = \bar{a}_t + \Delta a_t$.
- **Multi-Step Prediction**: The policy predicts residuals for $h$ future time-steps ($\Delta a_{t:t+h}$), executing only the first action. This mitigates compounding errors common in behavior cloning.
- **Training with Minimal Robot Data**: The residual policy is trained via behavior cloning using a small amount of embodiment-specific robot demonstrations (approximately 400 trajectories across 10 tasks for a Spot robot). The input to this policy includes the current image ($I_t$), goal image ($G$), predicted point tracks ($\tau$), and the open-loop action sequence (or the open-loop plan, represented as end-effector poses) up to the current time.
- **Generalization Rationale**: The residual policy focuses on minor corrections, allowing it to generalize well with limited in-domain data, as the broad interaction plan is already provided by the web-trained track prediction model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted using a Boston Dynamics Spot robot equipped with a manipulator and an Intel RealSense RGBD camera, operating in diverse real-world office and kitchen scenes.

#### 4.1. Point Track Prediction Results

- **Qualitative Evaluation**: Visualizations in Figure 5 demonstrate that the track prediction model generates plausible future point tracks that correspond to objects being manipulated in the scene, aligning with the specified goal image. It effectively captures different groups of motions for multiple objects or camera movement, though for robot experiments, the focus is on single object manipulation under a fixed camera.
- **Quantitative Evaluation (Table 1)**: The model's performance was measured using a modified $\delta_x^t$ metric (area under the curve for point accuracy), averaged across the prediction horizon.
 - **Ours** achieved significantly higher accuracy ($\Delta$ scores of 0.67-0.77) across diverse web video datasets (EpicKitchens, SmthSmthv2, BridgeData, RT1 Data) compared to baselines.
 - **Flow-based baseline** performed poorly (0.21-0.42), attributed to its inability to capture large non-linear state changes.
 - **Video-based baseline** (predicting RGB video then tracking) also showed low accuracy (0.17-0.30), likely due to the difficulty of generating plausible RGB videos and subsequent tracking errors.
 - These results validate the effectiveness of directly predicting point tracks for capturing object motion in web videos.

#### 4.2. Robot Manipulation Results

- **Qualitative Evaluation (Figure 6, 7)**: The framework successfully enables the Spot robot to perform a wide array of manipulation tasks, such as closing microwaves, pulling drawers, flipping toasters, and pouring from jars, across unseen objects and scenes. The predicted tracks accurately guide the robot to achieve the goal configurations.
- **Quantitative Evaluation (Table 2)**: Success rates were evaluated across 20 rollouts for 25 tasks, categorized into four levels of generalization:
 - **Mild Generalization (MG)**: Unseen configurations of seen objects in seen scenes.
 - **Standard Generalization (G)**: Unseen object instances in seen/unseen scenes.
 - **Combinatorial Generalization (CG)**: Unseen activity-object type combinations.
 - **Type Generalization (TG)**: Completely unseen object types or activities in unseen scenes.
- **Performance of Ours (Residual Policy)**:
 - **Superior Generalization**: Achieved the highest success rates across all generalization levels: MG (70%), G (60%), CG (55%), TG (40%). This demonstrates strong generalization capabilities, especially in challenging unseen scenarios.
 - **Outperforming Baselines**: Significantly outperformed Goal-Conditioned BC (MG: 60%, G: 20%, CG/TG: 0%), highlighting the critical role of leveraging web data for generalization.
 - **Outperforming Other Web-Data Baselines**: Also outperformed Affordance-Conditioned BC (MG: 65%, G: 30%, CG: 10%, TG: 5%), Video-Conditioned BC (MG: 60%, G: 25%, CG/TG: 0%), and Hand-Object Mask-Conditioned BC (MG: 70%, G: 40%, CG: 25%, TG: 20%). This indicates that predicting direct motion through point tracks is a more effective way to utilize web data for manipulation compared to static affordances, hallucinated videos, or ambiguous masks.
- **Ablation Studies**:
 - **Ours (Open-Loop)**: Direct execution of rigid transforms without residual policy achieved lower success rates (MG: 35%, G: 25%, CG: 30%, TG: 25%). This demonstrates that the open-loop plan provides a good coarse trajectory, but the residual policy is crucial for correcting prediction inaccuracies and handling embodiment-specific details like grasping.
 - **Ours (Ablation; actions not residuals)**: Directly predicting actions (instead of residuals over an open-loop plan) performed worse than the full Track2Act (MG: 70%, G: 45%, CG: 30%, TG: 30%). This confirms the benefit of predicting residuals for refining a strong prior plan, which likely simplifies the learning task for the small robot dataset.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Failures

- The primary failure modes for the open-loop plan were inability to grasp objects correctly and inability to recover from intermediate errors.
- Even with the residual policy, higher generalization levels (CG, TG) still saw failures, mainly due to incorrect grasping, getting stuck in non-feasible motions, or executing trajectories that did not fully align with the specified goal image. This suggests remaining challenges in robust contact reasoning and long-horizon planning from a small robot dataset.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Track2Act" framework presents a significant advancement towards achieving generalizable robot manipulation, addressing several long-standing challenges in the field.

- **Bridging the Data Gap**: The most impactful contribution is demonstrating a highly effective method for leveraging vast, diverse, and freely available passive web videos (human and robot) to learn interaction plans that are otherwise infeasible to acquire through traditional robot data collection. This radically reduces the dependency on large, costly, in-domain robot datasets.
- **Achieving Strong Generalization**: The paper shows unprecedented generalization capabilities across diverse tasks, unseen objects, and novel scenes (Standard, Combinatorial, and Type Generalization) with a single, common goal-conditioned policy. This pushes the boundaries of what is possible in "out-of-the-box" robot deployment.
- **Direct Deployability**: By factoring the policy into an embodiment-agnostic planning component and a minimal embodiment-specific refinement component, Track2Act enables direct execution in new scenarios without requiring further test-time training or human intervention, enhancing the practical utility and scalability of robot systems.
- **Novel Interaction Plan Representation**: Predicting point tracks as an embodiment-agnostic interaction plan is a novel and effective way to extract meaningful motion information from diverse videos. It outperforms other web-data-leveraging approaches (affordances, video generation, masks) by providing more direct and expressive cues for object manipulation.
- **Efficient Robot Learning**: The use of a residual policy, requiring only a small amount (e.g., ~400 trajectories) of robot-specific data, drastically improves the sample efficiency of robot learning. This makes the system more accessible and faster to adapt to new robot platforms or specific operational nuances.

**Potential Impact**: Track2Act has the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of intelligent robots in everyday environments, from assisting in homes to automated industrial tasks. By democratizing the data source for robot learning, it opens avenues for a wider range of researchers and practitioners to contribute to robot skill acquisition without the prohibitive costs of large-scale data collection infrastructure.

**Limitations and Future Directions**: The authors acknowledge that the current framework is primarily demonstrated for short-horizon tasks involving the manipulation of a single object. Future work could explore extending Track2Act to tackle long-horizon tasks that require successive manipulations of multiple objects, which would significantly expand its applicability to more complex real-world scenarios. Further research into improving the robustness of the residual policy for highly novel situations, especially regarding contact and error recovery, remains an important direction.
