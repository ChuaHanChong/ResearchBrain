# Playful Agentic Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A multi-agent Code-as-Policy system, RATS, enables robots to autonomously acquire reusable skills through self-directed play. The system improved average task success rates by 20.6 percentage points on LIBERO-PRO and 17.0 percentage points on MolmoSpaces, and demonstrated effective sim-to-real and cross-environment skill transfer.

## Problem

- Existing agentic robot systems primarily acquire skills reactively in response to explicit task instructions, which limits proactive and continual skill development.
- Sparse feedback (e.g., binary success/failure) in complex robotic tasks provides insufficient information for effective continual learning and skill distillation.
- Earlier intrinsic motivation research often operated in predefined sensorimotor spaces, lacking the expressive power to articulate exploratory goals in language or store successful behaviors as callable code.

## Method

- Introduces **RATS** (Robotics Agent Teams), a multi-agent Code-as-Policy system enabling self-directed play for skill acquisition prior to specific tasks.
- RATS autonomously proposes self-generated tasks using a "==Goldilocks-driven==" strategy that balances novelty and learnability, focusing on the boundary of current capabilities.
- The system employs a Write-Execute-Verify-Diagnose loop for task execution and a Memory-Management Team to distill outcomes into a persistent skill library and failure memory.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **43.8%** on LIBERO-PRO, a **20.6** percentage-point improvement over the **23.2%** Code-as-Policy baseline, with object-based generalization showing **61.0%** and **63.0%** success.
- Demonstrated successful cross-environment transfer, with play-learned skills improving RoboSuite task success by **8.9** percentage points and two-arm lifting tasks by **24.0** percentage points.
- Showed effective sim-to-real transfer, improving real-world manipulation tasks by **8.8** percentage points on physical robots without real-world play, and MolmoSpaces-learned skills by **21.7** percentage points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-directed play, guided by intrinsic motivation, allows agentic robots to proactively accumulate reusable skills before explicit task arrival.
> - Structured feedback loops from an agent team (planning, per-step verification, failure diagnosis) provide dense learning signals crucial for distilling successful behaviors into reusable code.
> - Code-as-Policy agents are particularly well-suited for intrinsic motivation, enabling them to articulate and execute exploratory goals and store successful programs as persistent skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses the paradigm of agentic robot systems, which leverage large language and multimodal models to generate plans, utilize perception and control tools, and create executable Code-as-Policy programs. These systems offer a modular and inspectable alternative to end-to-end vision-language-action policies by allowing robots to observe feedback and iteratively revise their behavior. While current agentic systems have demonstrated capabilities in solving tasks, they are predominantly task-driven, meaning reusable skills are acquired reactively in response to explicit instructions. This limits their capacity for proactive and continual skill development.

This work draws inspiration from natural intelligence, specifically how children develop reusable skills through self-directed play before formal tasks are introduced. This concept has long been explored in developmental robotics and intrinsic motivation research, where agents prioritize novel yet learnable experiences. The authors posit that Code-as-Policy agents are particularly well-suited to operationalize these classical ideas. Unlike earlier systems that operated in predefined sensorimotor or feature spaces, coding agents can articulate exploratory goals in language, execute them as programs, analyze outcomes, and store successful behaviors as callable code. This convergence of agentic capabilities and play-based learning positions the work at the intersection of continual skill learning, curiosity-driven exploration, and Code-as-Policy robotics, aiming to bridge the gap between reactive task-solving and proactive, autonomous skill acquisition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate "Playful Agentic Robot Learning," a paradigm where an embodied coding agent utilizes self-directed play as a continuous stage for skill acquisition prior to the arrival of specific downstream tasks. The work aims to develop a system that can autonomously accumulate reusable skills, thereby enhancing its ability to solve future, potentially unseen, tasks.

The motivation for this research stems from several observations and limitations of existing agentic robot systems:
- **Reactive vs. Proactive Learning:** Current agentic robots typically learn and acquire skills only in response to external task instructions. This reactive approach limits their ability to generalize and adapt to novel situations efficiently. The authors are motivated by the biological precedent of play, which enables flexible skill development before explicit goal-directed behavior.
- **Sparseness of Feedback in Task-Driven Learning:** In complex robotics tasks, a binary success or failure signal at the task level often provides insufficient information for an agent to understand *what* was learned, *which* sub-step failed, or *what* reusable behavior should be distilled. This sparseness hinders effective continual learning and skill synthesis.
- **Leveraging Code-as-Policy Capabilities:** The emergence of Code-as-Policy agents, which can generate executable programs, provides a new avenue for intrinsic motivation. These agents can articulate and execute exploratory goals in natural language, observe the effects of their programs, and store successful executions as reusable code. This capability allows for a more structured and expressive form of play compared to earlier approaches.
- **Continual Skill Acquisition:** The goal extends beyond solving individual tasks. The research seeks to enable a robot to autonomously determine *what skills to practice and accumulate* in preparation for future, potentially unknown, tasks. This shifts the focus from merely task execution to self-directed curriculum generation and skill library development.
- **Need for Structured Play:** To make play informative for skill learning, the authors are motivated to design a structured agent team. This team is intended to provide dense feedback through planning, per-step verification, multi-attempt retries, and failure diagnosis, thereby enabling the system to identify successful policy components, localize missing capabilities, and systematically convert successful behaviors into persistent, reusable skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce RATS (Robotics Agent Teams), a multi-agent Code-as-Policy system designed to enable Playful Agentic Robot Learning. RATS operates in two phases: a play-time skill acquisition phase and an evaluation phase with the learned skill library.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup and Overview

In the play-time setting, the external task instruction (l) present in standard Code-as-Policy frameworks is removed. Instead, RATS autonomously proposes and practices self-generated tasks (τt) within a play environment (Eplay). The objective is to acquire skills that improve subsequent downstream task solving. RATS maintains a skill library (L = L0 ∪ Llearned, where L0 are initial primitives and Llearned are learned code skills) and a failure memory (M) storing lessons from failed attempts. After N play iterations, the frozen library L is used for evaluation.

RATS is organized into three collaborating teams:
- **Task Proposer Team:** Selects which play tasks to practice.
- **Execution Team:** Writes, runs, verifies, and diagnoses robot-control code to solve proposed tasks.
- **Memory-Management Team:** Distills outcomes into the skill library and failure memory.

#### 3.2. Task Proposer Team

This team implements intrinsic motivation for bottom-up task generation.
- **Candidate Task Generation:** An LLM, conditioned on the current scene context, the skill library (L), and recent attempt history, generates a pool of candidate task descriptions (Tt). For MolmoSpaces, this includes grounding object identifiers and filtering for visible/reachable objects.
- **Goldilocks-Driven Task Selection:** Tasks (τt) are selected by maximizing an analytical score that balances novelty and learnability. The score is a product of:
 - **Object-Skill Novelty N(τ):** Encourages exploration of rarely-tried (object, skill) combinations using a historical attempt count.
 - **Competence Frontier F(τ):** Favors tasks that are neither too easy nor too difficult, peaking at an average Wilson-bounded empirical success rate of approximately 0.5 for required skills. This helps focus practice on the boundary of current capabilities. Tasks matching recent failures are downweighted.
- **Environment Creation and Verification:** An Environment Creator agent converts the selected task into an executable instance (e.g., BDDL for LIBERO-PRO), including syntax/semantic validation and bounded repair. An Environment Verifier then performs deterministic pre-execution checks to ensure the environment instantiates correctly and is physically plausible.

#### 3.3. Execution Team

Once a task is proposed, this team executes it through a Write-Execute-Verify-Diagnose loop.
- **Planning Agent:** Generates an ordered plan, annotates steps with relevant skills (prioritizing verified ones from L), and predicts failure points. Input includes the task, scene, failure memory (M), primitives, and learned skills (L).
- **Planner Verifier:** Checks if the plan is physically grounded in the initial scene, identifying misperception, missing preconditions, or incorrect ordering. Provides feedback for plan refinement.
- **Policy Writer:** Translates the verified plan into executable Python code. On retries, it uses per-step verification results and feedback to make local revisions. A SubAgent can be spawned to practice isolated sub-actions if a persistent local bottleneck is identified.
- **Quality Checker:** Statically screens generated code for errors (syntax, unsafe patterns) before execution.
- **Goal Verifier:** Determines overall task success post-execution, using environment state or visual judgment.
- **Per-Step Verifier:** Provides localized pass/fail verdicts and explanations for individual plan steps, crucial for dense feedback.
- **Failure Diagnoser:** Analyzes failed executions (trace, state, verifier results) to categorize failure modes, identify the first failed step, and suggest concrete corrections for retry. It can route feedback to the Policy Writer (code-level fixes) or Planner (plan-level fixes).
- **SubAgent:** When needed, practices a single failed sub-action in isolation to return a reusable helper routine for the current retry (not automatically added to L).

#### 3.4. Memory-Management Team

This team ensures the persistent stores (L and M) remain useful.
- **Immediate Post-Episode Updates:**
 - **On Success:** Self-contained, semantically meaningful functions are extracted from successful code, assigned docstrings, and added to L as "experimental" skills. Usage statistics for invoked learned skills are updated.
 - **On Failure:** The failed episode is recorded in M and distilled into compact lessons (e.g., missing preconditions, retry corrections).
 - **Skill Reliability:** Skills in L progress through "experimental," "verified" (promoted after success criteria), and "deprecated" (marked after repeated failures) tiers. Retrieval prioritizes verified skills.
- **Periodic Memory Curation and Skill Proposal:** Every K iterations, a Memory Curator merges/rewrites redundant lessons and removes duplicate/redundant skills. A Skill Proposer drafts new candidate helper functions based on recurring missing capabilities, adding them to L as experimental skills.

#### 3.5. Evaluation with the Learned Skill Library

After play-time, L is frozen, and intrinsic exploration/memory update mechanisms are disabled. The system solves externally provided tasks in two modes:
- **Plug-and-Play Execution:** The frozen L is directly injected into a standard single-agent Code-as-Policy baseline (e.g., CaP-Agent0) as additional callable APIs. This isolates the value of the learned skills.
- **RATS Execution:** The full RATS Execution Team uses the learned skill library, benefiting from the Planner, verification-driven retry, and failure memory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluations demonstrate that skills acquired through autonomous play significantly enhance the performance and generalization capabilities of Code-as-Policy agents.

- **In-Domain Evaluation:**
 - **LIBERO-PRO:** RATS achieved an average success rate of 43.8%, a 20.6 percentage-point (pp) improvement over the CaP-Agent0 baseline (23.2%). It also outperformed all tested VLA baselines (OpenVLA, π0, π0.5), with the best VLA baseline reaching 12.8%. The gains were particularly pronounced on object-based generalization splits (61.0% and 63.0%), indicating effective transfer of learned skills beyond the exact tasks practiced during play.
 - **MolmoSpaces:** RATS improved average success from 21.0% (CaP-Agent0) to 38.0% (+17.0 pp), showing consistent gains across "Open," "Close," "Pick," and "Pick-and-Place" task categories.
- **Cross-Environment Transfer:** Skills learned during LIBERO-PRO play were successfully transferred to the RoboSuite environment, which was not seen during play. When these play-learned skills were plugged into CaP-Agent0, the average success rate on RoboSuite tasks increased by 8.9 pp (from 40.3% to 49.1%). Notably, significant improvements were observed in cross-embodiment tasks like two-arm lifting (+24.0 pp), despite skills being learned on a single-arm robot.
- **Ablation Study (Play Strategy and Test-Time Execution):** An ablation on LIBERO-PRO revealed that:
 - **Curiosity-driven play is crucial:** "Random Play" yielded minimal improvement over "No Play" (23.2% to 24.7%), whereas "Curious Play" (using RATS' task proposer) significantly improved performance to 32.3% (when used with CaP-Agent0).
 - **Complementary benefits:** Improvements from play-time skill acquisition and the RATS execution system are complementary. Improving only execution yielded 36.3%, and only play yielded 32.3%; combining both resulted in the highest performance of 44.3%.
- **Sim-to-Real Evaluation:** The skill library acquired during simulated LIBERO-PRO play demonstrated transferability to a physical robot without real-world play or fine-tuning. Adding RATS-learned skills improved the CaP-Agent0 baseline by 8.8 pp on real-world manipulation tasks. Further real-world experiments with MolmoSpaces-learned skills showed a 21.7 pp improvement on tasks like "Swap Cubes" and "Close Drawer."
- **Token Cost Analysis (Appendix E.2):** An analysis of LLM token consumption indicated that play-time costs are significant (approximately 30M tokens for 50 iterations), primarily driven by the "Write-Execute-Verify-Diagnose" loop, particularly the Failure Diagnoser and Policy Writer. A compute-matched comparison showed that proactive play-time computation, leading to a reusable skill library, was more effective in improving performance than simply allocating more reactive retry budget to the baseline agent at test time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a framework for "Playful Agentic Robot Learning" that shifts the paradigm from purely reactive, task-driven skill acquisition to proactive, self-directed skill development. The RATS system provides a concrete methodology for embodied coding agents to leverage intrinsic motivation and structured feedback for continual learning.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** By enabling robots to autonomously propose and practice skills, the framework moves towards more self-sufficient learning agents, reducing the reliance on human-provided task instructions and demonstrations for skill acquisition.
- **Improved Generalization and Transfer:** The demonstrated performance gains across diverse simulation environments and the cross-environment (sim-to-sim) and sim-to-real transfer capabilities suggest that play-learned code skills can generalize effectively. This implies a more robust and adaptable robotic system that can apply knowledge gained in one context to novel situations and even different robot embodiments.
- **Practical Skill Library Mechanism:** The work presents play-learned code skills as a practical "plug-and-play" mechanism. This allows for the improvement of existing agentic robot systems without requiring extensive fine-tuning of underlying models, by simply augmenting their operational capabilities with a curated set of reusable behaviors.
- **Complementary Learning Paradigms:** The findings highlight the complementary nature of proactive play-time skill acquisition and structured test-time execution. This suggests that combining these approaches can yield greater benefits than either strategy in isolation.
- **Efficiency of Proactive Learning:** The token cost analysis indicates that upfront investment in play-time computation to build a skill library is more effective than solely relying on reactive, increased inference-time retries. This has implications for optimizing computational resources in LLM-driven robotics.
- **Modularity and Inspectability:** By focusing on Code-as-Policy and distilling behaviors into explicit code skills, the approach retains the benefits of modularity and inspectability inherent in agentic systems, allowing for clearer understanding and debugging of robot behavior.

While primarily evaluated in simulation, the preliminary real-world transfer results are promising. The identified limitations, such as reliance on simulation diversity, potential issues with improper skill reuse, and increased inference costs, point to future research directions. These include scaling up physical deployment, enhancing environmental diversity for play, developing more sophisticated skill retrieval mechanisms, and exploring lighter feedback modalities for real-world application.
