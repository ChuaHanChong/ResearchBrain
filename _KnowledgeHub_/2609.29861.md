# GPT-6-Astra Lights Up Embodied Navigation: Evaluation in Zero-Shot Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> This report evaluates whether a general-purpose foundation model can navigate unseen indoor scenes on its own. Running GPT-6-Astra inside a minimal Codex harness that exposes only monocular RGB observations and primitive move/turn/tilt/stop actions over MCP, with no navigation fine-tuning, waypoint predictor or prebuilt map, the model reaches 79.0% success and 69.5% SPL on zero-shot R2R-CE-100.

## Problem

- Prior zero-shot VLN-CE agents lean on panoramic cameras, trained waypoint predictors or pre-built scene maps, so success does not isolate the underlying model's own ability.
- Supervised navigation policies require annotated instruction-trajectory data and navigation-specific training, obscuring how much capability a general foundation model already contributes.
- Existing LLM-based agents wrap models in human-designed workflows for planning, memory and action selection, so reported gains confound the model with the scaffolding.

## Method

- A minimal ==Codex-harness== interface: the harness only sustains a continuous session while the model interprets the instruction, selects observations, issues actions and decides when to stop.
- Two ==MCP== tools: observe() returns a 512x512 monocular RGB view without consuming a step; step(actions) executes ordered primitives - 0.25 m forward, 15 degree turns, 30 degree tilts, STOP.
- Evaluation protocol: the 100-episode Open-Nav R2R-CE validation-unseen subset, budgets of 500 primitive actions and 2,400 s per episode, run at both medium and ultra ==reasoning effort==.

## Results

- Ultra reasoning reaches **79.0%** SR and **69.5%** SPL, medium **76.0%** and **67.9%**, above SpatialAnt's **66.0%** zero-shot SR and Qwen-RobotNav's **72.1%** trained SR.
- Route fidelity diverges from success: median nDTW across 79 successes is **90.4%**, yet eight fall below **50%**; order-based instructions succeed **85.7%** versus **61.5%** landmark-based.
- Of 21 ultra failures, **13** stop at least 5 m from the goal and **seven** beyond 10 m; **15** episodes fail under both reasoning settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A general-purpose model with only monocular RGB and primitive actions can exceed the reported success rates of pipelines using panoramas, waypoint predictors and pre-built maps.
> - Success rate hides route quality: report nDTW, execution feedback and stopping correctness, since plausible landmark matches accompanied a 14.5 m final goal error.
> - More reasoning effort does not buy reliability; execution and goal-verification failures persist, and proprietary training data leaves any zero-shot claim unverifiable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

An agent is asked to carry out a multi-step indoor instruction from what it sees. It must decide when another view is useful, turn landmarks and spatial relations into turns and forward movement, and stop at the destination. This paper asks whether a general-purpose model can steer that interaction with an unfamiliar indoor environment, rather than relying on a separately trained navigation policy or a fixed navigation workflow. The authors give GPT-6-Astra monocular RGB and primitive actions, then let it decide when to observe, how to move, and when to stop. The harness sustains the session, and the model makes the navigation decisions. The paper is an evaluation, not a new navigation algorithm. In the tested setting, GPT-6-Astra with ultra reasoning reaches 79.0% success on R2R-CE-100.

Success rate (%) for zero-shot methods (left) and trained methods (right). Callouts compare SpatialAnt at 66.0% with GPT-6-Astra at 79.0% (+13.0 percentage points) and Qwen-RobotNav at 72.1% with GPT-6-Astra at 79.0% (+6.9 points). These are reported values, not matched-condition comparisons. GPT-6-Astra's score is from R2R-CE-100, while trained-method results use the full validation-unseen split.

### 2. The model gets the controls

Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) means following natural-language instructions through a visual environment to reach a destination. In the continuous version, the agent issues primitive movements rather than selecting a node on a pre-defined route graph. The interface has two tools. `observe()` returns a 512 × 512 monocular RGB view without advancing the simulator. `step(actions)` executes ordered primitive actions: forward movement of 0.25 m, left or right turns of 15°, camera tilts of 30°, and STOP. Maps, depth, goal coordinates, global pose, and reference trajectories are unavailable through the interface. Each episode has a 500-action budget and a 2,400-second limit.

R2R-CE-100 is a 100-episode validation-unseen subset spanning 10 scenes, with one run per episode in each reasoning setting. Here "zero-shot" means the authors apply no navigation-specific fine-tuning. Because the model is proprietary, the paper cannot establish whether it encountered navigation data during training. Success rate (SR) is the share of episodes in which the agent explicitly stops less than 3 m geodesic distance from the goal.

### 3. A high score, with a comparison caveat

On R2R-CE-100, GPT-6-Astra with ultra reasoning reaches 79.0% SR. [SpatialAnt](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.26837) reports 66.0%, a 13.0 percentage-point difference. This comparison is contextual, not a matched-condition demonstration of superiority. GPT-6-Astra uses monocular RGB without a pre-built scene map or trained waypoint predictor. SpatialAnt uses panoramic observations and scene reconstruction. The SpatialAnt results are also author-sampled, and their correspondence to the 100-episode subset is unverified.

The paired medium and ultra runs do not isolate reasoning effort, because the Codex versions differ. The authors also say that inference cost and latency constrain practical deployment.

### 4. A successful stop can still hide a bad route

In episode EP42, the instruction leads from the kitchen to a small living area and then to the bedroom on the left. After reaching a bedroom, GPT-6-Astra returns to the kitchen, crosses the living area, enters the bedroom, and stops 0.7 m from the goal. That counts as a success. Its nDTW, which measures agreement with the reference path, is 27.5%. Across the 79 successful ultra episodes, median nDTW is 90.4%, but eight score below 50%. The paper documents action adjustments in 16 episodes: 10 succeed and six fail. These are documented cases only and do not establish that adjustment caused success.

(a) nDTW versus SPL for 79 successful ultra episodes. Orange triangles mark recorded adjustments. (b) Of the 16 episodes with recorded adjustments, 10 succeed and six fail. (c) Selected views from EP42: the route returns to the kitchen before successful completion, with nDTW 27.5% and SPL 41.2%. Adjustment counts are descriptive and not causal.

Of ultra's 21 failures, 13 end at least 5 m from the goal. In EP176, eight forward actions produce pixel-identical observations, and the episode later exhausts its action budget. Fifteen episodes fail under both reasoning settings.

### 5. Reasoning still needs verification

A general-purpose model can direct a camera-and-action loop and achieve high benchmark success without navigation-specific fine-tuning. This is a simulator evaluation on a fixed benchmark, not a physical-robot deployment. Strong endpoint success coexists with route deviations, ineffective movement, and incorrect stops. The authors therefore propose building on general-purpose capabilities while measuring route fidelity, execution feedback, and stopping accuracy.
