# Semantic Radiance Fields as Simulators for Spatial Reasoning in Real-World Scenes

> [!summary] Summary
> Semantic Radiance Fields (SRF) turn posed RGB captures of real scenes into simulators for embodied spatial reasoning. Extending FruitNeRF, C independent sigmoid semantic heads on a shared Nerfacto density backbone lift pretrained SAM 3 segmentations into 3D, exposing render, semantic-probability and occupancy queries. A demonstrated orchard apple-reaching setup uses one field as renderer, segmentation oracle and collision detector.

## Problem

- Synthetic procedural simulators give programmatic control and ground-truth supervision, but their visual and geometric appearance departs from natural scenes, limiting transfer to real environments.
- Generative video simulators cover wide visual variety yet guarantee neither multiview consistency nor a persistent, queryable 3D state that an agent can interrogate.
- Existing semantic radiance fields were built for reconstruction and querying, and served reinforcement learning only for locomotion, never for tasks demanding object-identity reasoning.

## Method

- ==Multi-class semantic field==: C independent binary heads read the shared ==Nerfacto== density feature, ==sigmoid-activated== so a point may hold several classes, unlike SemanticNeRF's mutually exclusive softmax.
- ==2D-to-3D lifting==: ==SAM 3== prompted with a fixed class vocabulary yields per-image masks; per-class ==binary cross-entropy== plus photometric loss, weight lambda equal to 1, with semantic gradients detached from density.
- ==Query interface==: Render(pose) returns RGB, semantic and depth maps, Semantic(x) per-class probability, Occupancy(x) density for collision, distilled offline into an ==occupancy cache== closing a ==MuJoCo== physics loop.

## Results

- An orchard apple-tree field was reconstructed from **311** posed **6000x4000** images, downscaled fourfold to **1500x1000**, using **three** prompted classes: apple, branch and leaf.
- Training ran **500,000** iterations at **4,096** rays per batch with Adam, exponentially decayed **1e-2** learning rate and mixed precision, roughly **4** hours per scene on one H100.
- No policy training or quantitative benchmark is reported; evidence is qualitative side-by-side radiance versus semantic-blend renderings plus the enumerated set of simulator signals supplied to an agent.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Independent per-class sigmoid heads suit spatially overlapping classes, such as an apple partly occluded by leaves, where a shared softmax forces false exclusivity.
> - Blocking semantic gradients from the density field stops geometry collapsing onto class boundaries, a cheap safeguard whenever auxiliary heads are attached to a reconstruction backbone.
> - One grounded 3D field can replace renderer, label oracle and collision checker at once; the cost is hours of per-scene fitting and static geometry.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust embodied artificial intelligence (AI) systems necessitates training and evaluation in diverse, realistic, and semantically rich environments. Current paradigms for creating such environments present inherent trade-offs.

One approach involves **synthetic procedural simulators**, which offer programmatic control, ground-truth supervision, and the ability to introduce scene perturbations. However, these simulators often fall short in replicating the visual and geometric realism characteristic of natural, real-world scenes. This limitation can lead to a "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in synthetic environments perform poorly when transferred to physical robots.

Another emerging paradigm includes **generative simulators**, which leverage advanced generative models to produce extensive training data and encompass a wide array of visual scenes. While capable of generating visually complex scenes, these methods frequently lack guarantees of multiview consistency, meaning objects and scenes may appear differently from various viewpoints in a physically implausible manner. Furthermore, they do not inherently expose a persistent, queryable 3D state, which is crucial for agents needing to reason about object locations, occlusions, and interactions in a consistent 3D space.

Parallel to these, **neural scene representations**, exemplified by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), have advanced the capability of reconstructing photorealistic real-world environments from a collection of posed 2D images. These representations excel at novel-view synthesis, generating photorealistic images from unobserved camera positions. However, standard NeRFs do not natively encode semantic information; they lack the ground truth labels for objects or regions within the scene by default.

Recent extensions to NeRFs have begun to bridge this gap by lifting 2D semantic segmentations into the 3D radiance field, creating **Semantic Radiance Fields (SRFs)**. Prior work on SRFs has largely focused on their application for 3D reconstruction and querying, or for locomotion tasks in reinforcement learning (RL) where the primary concern is navigating free space rather than interacting with specific objects based on their identity.

This paper positions its work as a bridge between the limitations of existing simulators and the capabilities of SRFs. It addresses the gap where SRFs have not been fully leveraged for tasks requiring agents to perform **spatial reasoning that involves object identity**. By proposing SRFs as direct simulators, the authors aim to combine the geometric fidelity and visual realism of real-world reconstructions with the crucial semantic queryability and ground-truth supervision typically associated with synthetic environments. This approach offers a novel pathway for efficiently generating diverse, semantically rich, and realistic training and evaluation data for embodied AI systems focused on manipulation, navigation, and complex question-answering in real-world scenes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to propose and demonstrate the utility of Semantic Radiance Fields (SRFs) as comprehensive simulators for training and evaluating spatial reasoning agents in real-world environments.

This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing the "Diversity-Realism-Semantics" Trilemma in Embodied AI:** Training embodied AI systems, such as robots capable of navigation, manipulation, or complex interaction with their surroundings, requires exposure to a wide array of environments. These environments must be simultaneously:
 - **Geometrically faithful:** Accurately reflecting the 3D structure of the real world.
 - **Visually realistic:** Providing photorealistic appearance.
 - **Semantically queryable:** Allowing the agent to identify and locate specific objects or regions by their semantic labels (e.g., "apple," "branch," "leaf").
 Current simulator paradigms struggle to satisfy all three requirements concurrently, forcing researchers to make trade-offs. Synthetic simulators offer semantics but lack realism, while simulators based on real-world reconstructions provide realism but generally lack inherent semantic ground truth.

2. **Unifying Requirements with SRFs:** The authors contend that SRFs are a representation capable of unifying these often-conflicting requirements. By integrating 2D semantic segmentations from pretrained vision models directly into a 3D radiance field, SRFs jointly encode geometry, appearance, and per-class semantic identity. This allows for the reconstruction of real-world scenes that are both visually accurate and semantically annotated.

3. **Enabling Efficient Generation of Training Data:** The ability of SRFs to reconstruct diverse real-world scenes from posed RGB captures and subsequently support novel-view synthesis, semantic queries, and free-space queries from a single grounded representation enables the efficient generation of large and varied training and evaluation data. This is crucial for developing robust spatial reasoning models that generalize well to unseen real-world conditions.

4. **Bridging Sim-to-Real Gaps:** By directly using reconstructions of real-world environments, the proposed SRF-based simulator aims to reduce the "sim-to-real" gap. Agents trained within these realistic yet semantically annotated environments are expected to transfer more effectively to physical deployments.

5. **Extending SRF Applications to Object Identity Reasoning:** While SRFs have seen applications in reconstruction, querying, and some locomotion tasks, their potential for embodied tasks requiring explicit reasoning about object identity (e.g., "find the apple," "avoid the branch") has been underexplored. This work specifically motivates using SRFs to supply the necessary camera rendering, semantic ground truth, and occupancy queries for such object-centric reasoning tasks, thereby filling a perceived gap in the application of existing SRF technologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology involves extending existing Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) formulations to incorporate multi-class semantic information, resulting in a Semantic Radiance Field (SRF) capable of serving as a simulator.

#### 3.1. Multi-Class Semantic Radiance Field Formulation

- **Foundation:** The work builds upon the Nerfacto [Tancik et al., 2023] factorization of a neural radiance field. A density field $F_{\sigma}: \mathbf{x} \rightarrow (\sigma, h)$ maps a 3D position $\mathbf{x}$ to a density $\sigma$ and a latent feature $h$. An appearance field $F_c: (h, \mathbf{d}) \rightarrow c$ maps the latent feature and a view direction $\mathbf{d}$ to an RGB color $c$.
- **Semantic Extension:** To encode semantics, the authors attach $C$ independent binary semantic heads to the shared density backbone. Each head $F_s: h \rightarrow s \in \mathbb{R}^C$ produces an unconstrained logit $s_c$ for its respective class $c$.
 - **Independence of Classes:** Crucially, unlike some prior SRF methods that use a single softmax head enforcing mutual exclusion (i.e., a point belongs to only one class), these $C$ heads are independent. Each logit is activated by a sigmoid function, allowing a single 3D point to potentially belong to multiple classes simultaneously. This design choice provides flexibility for scenes where objects might overlap or have ambiguous boundaries.
 - **View-Independent Semantics:** Following previous work, the semantic head depends solely on the 3D position (via the latent feature $h$), ensuring that the semantic identity of a point is consistent regardless of the viewing direction.
 - **Gradient Flow Control:** To prevent the geometry of the scene from being distorted or "collapsed" onto class boundaries due to the semantic loss, gradients from the semantic loss are not propagated back through the density field. This ensures that the geometric reconstruction remains primarily driven by the photometric loss.

#### 3.2. Volumetric Rendering

- **Color Rendering:** The expected color $\hat{C}(r)$ of a pixel along a ray $r$ is computed using the standard alpha-compositing technique, accumulating color and density contributions from $K$ samples along the ray (Eq. 1).
- **Semantic Logit Rendering:** Similarly, the expected per-class logits $\hat{S}(r)$ at a pixel are obtained by applying the same volumetric accumulation process used for color, but to the per-class logits $s(t_k)$ (Eq. 3).

#### 3.3. Lifting 2D Segmentations to 3D

- **Ground Truth Generation:** The method does not assume pre-existing 3D semantic ground truth. Instead, for each input RGB image $I_i$, multi-class segmentation masks $M_i: \Omega \rightarrow \{0, 1\}^C$ are generated using a pretrained 2D vision model. In the experiments, SAM 3 [Carion et al., 2025] is employed, queried with a fixed vocabulary of class prompts (e.g., "apple," "branch," "leaf"). These 2D masks serve as the supervision signals for the semantic heads.
- **Loss Function:** The total training objective combines a standard photometric loss ($L_{photo}$) with a semantic loss ($L_{sem}$):
 - $L_{photo}$: An L2 norm (mean squared error) between the rendered RGB color $\hat{C}(r)$ and the ground-truth pixel color $C(r)$ for each ray (Eq. 5).
 - $L_{sem}$: This loss applies independent binary cross-entropy (BCE) for each class $c$ to the predicted per-class logits $\hat{S}(r)$ and the binary ground-truth label $y_c(r)$ derived from the 2D segmentation masks (Eq. 4). The sigmoid function $\sigma$ is applied to the logits $\hat{S}(r)$ to obtain probabilities before calculating BCE.
 - The total loss is $L = L_{photo} + \lambda L_{sem}$, with $\lambda$ set to 1 in all experiments.

#### 3.4. Query Interface

A trained SRF exposes three primary operations for downstream reasoning agents:
- `Render(P)`: Given a camera pose $P \in SE(3)$, it generates a posed RGB image, a corresponding semantic map (derived from the rendered semantic logits), and a depth map.
- `Semantic(x)`: For any given 3D point $\mathbf{x}$, it returns the per-class probability vector $\sigma(s(\mathbf{x})) \in^C$.
- `Occupancy(x)`: For any given 3D point $\mathbf{x}$, it returns the density value $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$, which can be used for collision detection.

#### 3.5. Example Application: Apple-Reaching in an Orchard SRF

To demonstrate the SRF's utility as a simulator, an apple-reaching task for a robot manipulator is outlined:
- **Scene Data:** An apple tree scene from the FruitNeRF dataset [Meyer et al., 2024], comprising 311 posed RGB images (6000x4000 px).
- **Semantic Annotation:** SAM 3 is used with prompts "apple," "branch," and "leaf" to generate per-image binary masks, which are then composited into a multi-class label image for training (background=0, apple=1, branch=2, leaf=3).
- **SRF Training:** The model is trained for 500,000 iterations with a batch size of 4,096 rays, using the Adam optimizer. Training for each scene takes approximately 4 hours on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU.
- **Simulator Integration:**
 - The trained SRF is distilled offline into an occupancy cache (e.g., a voxel grid or octree). This cache stores density and per-class probability information for efficient query of contact and reward signals.
 - A physics engine (e.g., MuJoCo) manages the rigid-body dynamics of the robot, providing camera and link poses at each step.
 - The SRF acts as the visual renderer, providing RGB observations and semantic maps to the agent.
 - The occupancy cache provides contact signals (e.g., collisions with branches) back to the physics engine, closing the simulation loop.
- **Task and Reward:** The agent's goal is to move its end-effector close to a target apple. The reward structure encourages approaching target objects (apples, identified via the semantic occupancy cache) and terminates episodes upon detecting collisions with designated avoidance classes (e.g., branches), using the contact signals.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents the successful development and demonstration of Semantic Radiance Fields (SRFs) as a framework for creating expressive and functional simulators for spatial reasoning agents.

The main findings and results are summarized as follows:

1. **Integration of Multi-Class Semantics into NeRFs:** The paper successfully implements a method to lift multi-class 2D semantic segmentations into a 3D neural radiance field. This is achieved by attaching independent binary semantic heads to the shared density backbone of a Nerfacto-based NeRF, allowing for flexible multi-label assignments to 3D points.
2. **Photorealistic and Semantically Rich Scene Representations:** The trained SRFs are shown to produce both photorealistic novel-view syntheses and corresponding semantic maps from arbitrary camera poses. Figure 1 visually supports this, displaying a radiance field rendering alongside a semantic field blend rendering, confirming the SRF's ability to jointly encode appearance, geometry, and per-class semantic identity.
3. **Comprehensive Query Interface:** The developed SRF provides a functional query interface comprising three key operations critical for embodied AI simulation:
 - `Render(P)`: Generates RGB images, semantic maps, and depth maps from novel viewpoints. This provides the visual input and ground-truth semantic supervision for agents.
 - `Semantic(x)`: Offers direct access to per-class probabilities at any 3D point in the scene, enabling precise object localization and identification for the agent or reward functions.
 - `Occupancy(x)`: Returns density values at 3D points, which can be utilized for collision detection, free-space planning, and determining traversability for agents.
4. **Proof-of-Concept as an Embodied AI Simulator:** The example application of an "apple-reaching" task in an orchard environment demonstrates the SRF's capability to function as a complete simulator. The SRF effectively serves multiple roles:
 - **Renderer:** Providing visual observations (RGB images) to the agent.
 - **Semantic Oracle:** Supplying ground-truth semantic maps for supervision and reward calculation.
 - **Collision Detector:** Facilitating collision detection through its 3D occupancy information, which can be distilled into an occupancy cache (e.g., voxel grid) for efficient querying by a physics engine.
5. **Efficiency of Training:** The training of a scene, based on 311 posed RGB images, requires approximately 4 hours on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU. This indicates that the method is computationally feasible for generating diverse real-world environments.
6. **Bridging Simulator Gaps:** The findings collectively illustrate that SRFs can bridge the functional gap between synthetic procedural simulators (which offer ground truth semantics but lack realism) and reconstruction-based simulators (which offer realism but lack inherent semantics), by providing both realism and semantic ground truth from real-world scene captures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of embodied artificial intelligence, particularly in the domain of training and evaluation environments. The development and demonstration of Semantic Radiance Fields (SRFs) as simulators hold several key significances and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Realistic and Semantically Rich Embodied AI Training:** The primary impact is the provision of a novel methodology for training and evaluating spatial reasoning agents in environments that simultaneously possess geometric fidelity, photorealistic appearance, and per-class semantic ground truth. This addresses a critical bottleneck in embodied AI, where agents often struggle to transfer learned skills from simplified synthetic environments to complex, unstructured real-world scenes. By providing a "real-world simulator," this work can potentially reduce the "sim-to-real" gap, fostering the development of more robust and generalizable AI systems.

2. **Unifying Disparate Simulator Paradigms:** The work unifies the strengths of two distinct simulator paradigms: the realism of reconstruction-based methods (like NeRFs) and the semantic queryability of synthetic procedural simulators. This unified representation simplifies the workflow for researchers by offering a single framework that meets diverse requirements for embodied agent development, from visual perception to object interaction and navigation.

3. **Efficient Generation of Diverse Training Data:** The ability to reconstruct diverse real-world scenes from readily available posed RGB images and then query them for various outputs (RGB, depth, semantics, occupancy) enables the efficient generation of vast quantities of varied training data. This is crucial for data-hungry machine learning models, especially in reinforcement learning, allowing for more comprehensive exploration of states and actions in complex environments.

4. **Broader Applicability for Spatial Reasoning Tasks:** The comprehensive query interface of the SRF (rendering, semantic query, occupancy query) supports a wide array of spatial reasoning tasks beyond the demonstrated apple-reaching example. This includes robotic manipulation, intelligent navigation, visual question answering, and tasks requiring fine-grained object interaction in complex, cluttered scenes. The SRF can serve as a foundational component for developing agents that reason about "what" objects are present, "where" they are, "how" they are occluded, and "how" the scene would appear from different viewpoints.

5. **Foundation for Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly points towards immediate extensions and future research.
 - **Integration with 3D Gaussian Splatting:** Applying the same lifting procedure to 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) could significantly reduce training times and enable real-time rollouts, which is a crucial step for interactive and dynamic simulation environments.
 - **Dynamic SRFs for Spatio-Temporal Reasoning:** The concept of extending SRFs with a temporal axis to create dynamic SRFs opens avenues for simulating environments with moving objects or deformable scenes. This would allow for training agents in spatio-temporal reasoning tasks, addressing dynamic manipulation and predictive control in evolving real-world scenarios.

In summary, this research advances the state of environments for embodied AI, offering a pragmatic and effective method to bridge the gap between simulation and reality. Its potential impact lies in accelerating the development of more capable and adaptable AI agents that can operate reliably in the complexities of the physical world.
