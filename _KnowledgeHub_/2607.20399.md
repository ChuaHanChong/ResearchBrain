# Towards Miniature Humanoid Tele-Loco-Manipulation Using Virtual Reality and Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Louisville developed a compliant full-body tele-loco-manipulation system for DYNAMIXEL-based miniature humanoids, combining VR telepresence for upper-body control with reinforcement learning for robust locomotion. The framework demonstrated stable bipedal posture and enabled teleoperated block relocation tasks, achieving an average of 2 out of 6 blocks moved despite an approximate 220 ms end-to-end motion latency.

## Problem

- High cost and operational complexity of full-sized humanoid robots limit research accessibility and the generation of teleoperated demonstration data for imitation learning.
- Miniature humanoids typically lack sophisticated combined locomotion and manipulation capabilities, as well as advanced, compliant control solutions.
- The scarcity of high-quality teleoperated demonstration data creates a bottleneck for advancing imitation learning schemes in humanoid robotics.

## Method

- Developed a full-body telepresence control stack for the ROBOTIS OP3 miniature humanoid, integrating ==VR telemanipulation== for upper-body control and an ==RL-based policy== for lower-body locomotion and balance.
- Implemented a custom ==PD-based torque controller== for DYNAMIXEL XM430 actuators and data-driven actuator modeling to enable compliant physical interactions and enhance safety.
- Utilized ==domain randomization== and actuator system identification (==Better Actuator Modeling==) during RL training to overcome the sim-to-real gap and generate robust locomotion policies.

## Results

- The telemanipulation system exhibited an end-to-end motion latency of approximately **220 ms** for arm movements and qualitatively tracked circular trajectories.
- The RL locomotion policy maintained robust stability, keeping normalized projected gravity consistently near **-1**, even under dynamic velocity commands for **80 seconds**.
- In integrated tele-loco-manipulation tasks, the robot successfully relocated an average of **2 out of 6** 40-gram cubes within a 10-minute trial, traversing about **5 meters** at an average velocity of **0.35 m/s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A modular approach integrating VR teleoperation and RL for locomotion can provide a functional and accessible full-body control stack for miniature humanoid robots.
> - Custom data-driven impedance control enables compliant and safe physical interactions on DYNAMIXEL-based platforms, which is crucial for teleoperated manipulation.
> - Extensive domain randomization in RL training is effective for developing robust locomotion policies that maintain stability in miniature humanoids even under dynamic conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen substantial advancements, particularly in the capabilities of full-sized robots. These developments often involve the integration of Virtual Reality (VR) for upper-body teleoperation and Reinforcement Learning (RL) for lower-body balance and locomotion. This approach allows a single remote operator to control a distant physical robot, facilitating manipulation and navigation in real environments. Such advanced control stacks are prominently utilized by commercial humanoid manufacturers for real-world deployment, contributing to potential applications in environments considered dull, dirty, or dangerous (e.g., firefighting, high-risk manufacturing, explosive ordinance defusal).

However, a significant challenge in this domain is the high cost and operational complexity of full-sized humanoid robots, which limits their accessibility to the broader research community. This inaccessibility results in a scarcity of high-quality teleoperated demonstration data, which is crucial for advancing imitation learning schemes that enable robots to perform tasks from high-level instructions.

Miniature humanoid platforms present an alternative due to their lower cost and easier handling. They are frequently used in research for prototyping and human-robot social interaction studies. Despite their prevalence, miniature humanoids typically possess fewer Degrees of Freedom (DoFs) and less biomimicry in their design compared to their full-sized counterparts, which constrains their general-purpose manipulation capabilities. Consequently, there has been limited research on combining locomotion and manipulation—termed "loco-manipulation"—in miniature humanoids using advanced control methods.

This paper addresses this gap by developing a compliant full-body telepresence control stack specifically for miniature humanoids. It aims to bridge the disparity in control sophistication between full-sized and miniature platforms by adapting the successful VR teleoperation and RL locomotion paradigm to smaller, more accessible hardware. By focusing on miniature humanoids, the work seeks to facilitate the collection of foundational data for future loco-manipulation efforts, which is currently a bottleneck for imitation learning in humanoid robotics. The research builds upon recent trends in VR telemanipulation, which have been driven by advancements in VR/haptic technology and competitions, as well as the rapid progress in RL for robust bipedal locomotion and sim-to-real transfer techniques. While previous work in miniature humanoid loco-manipulation has explored autonomous switching between manipulation and locomotion policies, this paper investigates an egocentric telepresence control method, aiming to leverage operator perception for improved generalization in complex tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the existing challenges in the field of humanoid robotics, specifically the high cost and operational difficulty associated with full-sized humanoid platforms. These factors render such robots largely inaccessible to academic research, creating a bottleneck for generating the vast amounts of teleoperated demonstration data necessary for advancing imitation learning—a key enabler for autonomous, high-level robot task execution. While miniature humanoids offer a more accessible and cost-effective alternative for research and prototyping, they typically feature simpler designs with fewer sensors and Degrees of Freedom, and consequently lack the sophisticated combined locomotion and manipulation capabilities observed in larger counterparts. This disparity limits their utility for general-purpose tasks and the generation of relevant loco-manipulation data.

In response to these challenges, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Develop a robust and generalizable framework for VR whole-body control of DYNAMIXEL-based miniature humanoids.** The initial target platform for this framework is the ROBOTIS OP3, chosen for its accessibility and prevalence in research. This objective directly addresses the identified gap in advanced control solutions for smaller robots.
2. **Create a software architecture for full-body tele-loco-manipulation control.** This involves integrating independent practices of VR telemanipulation for the upper body and RL for lower-body locomotion and balance into a cohesive system.
3. **Implement data-driven DYNAMIXEL XM430 actuator modeling for torque control.** This aims to overcome the limitations of standard position-controlled actuators by enabling compliant, torque-controlled physical interactions, which are crucial for safe data collection and real-world deployment.
4. **Design a full-body PD-based impedance controller for robot chains made of DYNAMIXEL actuators.** This controller is intended to provide tunable compliance and backdrivability, enhancing robot safety and adaptability during contact-rich interactions.
5. **Validate the developed framework and controller through experimentation on the ROBOTIS OP3 humanoid.** This objective involves empirical evaluation of the system's performance in both isolated telemanipulation and locomotion tasks, as well as integrated tele-loco-manipulation scenarios.

Overall, the motivation is to lower the barrier to entry for humanoid robotics research by providing an advanced, compliant tele-loco-manipulation system on an accessible miniature platform, thereby facilitating the collection of valuable demonstration data for future imitation learning and general humanoid capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology integrates independent practices of Virtual Reality (VR) telemanipulation and Reinforcement Learning (RL) locomotion to achieve whole-body control of miniature humanoids. The system is designed with hardware safety and compliance as central considerations.

#### 3.1. A. Embodiment Strategy

The core embodiment strategy involves superimposing a scaled virtual model of the robot over the operator's torso within a Head Mounted Display (HMD), creating an impression of robotic embodiment. Operator hand positions, tracked by VR controllers, are fed into an inverse kinematics (IK) solver to generate mimicked robot arm motions. To counteract the balance disturbances caused by arm movements, a robust RL-based walking/balancing controller is employed. Compliance is achieved through joint-side impedance control, requiring hardware modifications and custom software for DYNAMIXEL-based platforms like the ROBOTIS OP3.

#### 3.2. B. VR Robot Control

1. **Hardware:**
 - **Operator Interface:** A VIVE Pro 2 HMD for head tracking, VIVE controllers for hand poses, and four SteamVR Basestation 2.0s for optical tracking. A KAT Walk C2+ VR treadmill is mentioned for future locomotion retargeting. An i9-based PC with an RTX 4090 GPU manages VR processing.
 - **Robot Hardware (ROBOTIS OP3):** This miniature humanoid features twenty revolute Degrees of Freedom (DoF) across two 6-DoF legs, two 3-DoF arms, and a 2-DoF pan/tilt head, actuated by ROBOTIS DYNAMIXEL XM430-W350-R servos. It integrates an i7 NUC, a Robotis OpenCR board with a 9-DoF Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), and a generic VR180 dual-fisheye lens USB camera for enhanced telepresence. The system is currently tethered to wall power.

2. **Robot Software:**
 - **Development Environment:** Unity is used for VR scene creation and physics simulation. Wireless communication between Unity and the robot is facilitated by ROS and the Unity Robotics plugin for state data and command transmission.
 - **Arm Control:** A multi-objective IK solver translates VR controller positions into desired joint positions for the robot's arms, while also managing joint limits and self-collisions. Trapezoidal motion profiles are integrated to enforce velocity and acceleration constraints. The VR robot model is calibrated to match the operator's shoulder width and height.
 - **Torque Control:** A custom PD-based torque controller, operating at 200 Hz, tracks the desired arm positions. Torque control for DYNAMIXEL XM430 servos is implemented open-loop via hardware-level current control, assuming a linear relationship between input current and output shaft torque. The commanded torques ($\tau_{cmd}$) are calculated using Equation (1): $\tau_{cmd} = K_P (q_{des} - q) - K_D \dot{q}$, where $K_P$ and $K_D$ are diagonal matrices of joint stiffness and damping gains. Lower PD gains are used for arms and head to increase manipulative compliance, and this formulation is designed to be robot-agnostic.
 - **Neck Control:** HMD pan and tilt rotations are directly mapped to the robot's neck mechanism, allowing the operator to control the robot's camera orientation.

3. **User Interface:**
 - **Visual Feedback:** The robot's virtual torso model tracks the HMD position. A video stream from the dual fisheye camera (3840 × 1080 at 60 Hz) is projected into virtual spheres surrounding the robot, dewarping the image into a VR180 view to expand visual periphery (Figure 2). UDP via GStreamer provides GPU-accelerated H.265 transcoding, aiming for ~100 ms glass-to-glass latency and < 4 M bps bitrate. Binocular vision provides depth perception.
 - **Latency Compensation:** To mitigate motion sickness caused by mechanical latency in neck motions (~100 ms), spherical display rotations are dynamically adjusted in the opposite direction using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) filter.
 - **Arm Motion Retargeting Control:** Toggled by VIVE controller triggers. Releasing triggers halts movement by driving IK solver limits to zero; pressing them slowly ramps velocities/accelerations back up via an EMA filter. A translucent robot model shows real-time joint position feedback, allowing operators to compare commanded vs. actual robot states.
 - **Force Feedback Estimation:** Rough contact forces are estimated using Hooke's Law (Equation 2) based on the vectorial distance between virtual and translucent robot end-effectors, with a threshold to ignore small errors. These forces are visualized as 3D arrows on the translucent robot’s end effectors.
 - **Locomotion Interface:** The 2D touchpads on VIVE controllers are mapped to differential-drive style linear and angular velocity commands for the robot's lower body.

#### 3.3. C. RL Locomotion

1. **Formal Definition:** The RL policy is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), aiming to maximize a reward function $G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R(S_{t+k}, A_{t+k})$. An optimal policy $\pi^*$ maximizes the expected reward for a given state $S$ and for a given state $S$ taking an action $A$.

2. **Sim2Real and Actuator System Identification:**
 - **Domain Randomization:** To overcome the sim2real gap, various aspects of the robot and environment are randomized during training (Table I). This includes foot friction (0.5-0.9), gravity scale (0.95-1.3), link mass scale (0.7-1.3), and initial body velocity/position/orientation perturbations. This helps the policy adapt to changing mass and center of mass during telemanipulation. Pushing events (random velocity added every 10-15 seconds) are also randomized.
 - **Actuator Modeling:** Accurate actuator dynamics are crucial for zero-shot transfer. The Better Actuator Modeling (BAM) method was applied using a pendulum test bench to model DYNAMIXEL XM430 dynamics. Parameters like armature (0.045), friction loss (0.03), and effort limit (3.6) were estimated and validated in Isaac Sim (Figure 3).

3. **Observations and Rewards:**
 - **Observation Space:** Consists of five terms (angular velocity, projected gravity in body frame, user command, joint positions, previous action) with injected noise (Table II). A history of the previous nine timesteps is stacked, forming a 330×1 vector, which was found to reduce noise in inference actions.
 - **Reward Shaping:** Custom rewards and penalties (Table III) guide the policy towards stable and dynamic locomotion. Key terms include:
 - **Linear/Angular Velocity Error:** Rewards for tracking desired velocities.
 - **Gait Reward:** Rewards for dual and single support phases.
 - **Foot Clearance:** Rewards for sufficient foot height during swings.
 - **Penalties:** Applied for action smoothness/rate, base motion/orientation (especially $G_{x,y}$ components to encourage upright posture), foot orientation/slippage, high joint torque/velocity/acceleration/deviation, joint position limits, and termination. The "Foot Orientation" penalty on projected gravity xy components in the foot frame incentivizes the policy to quickly adjust feet to maintain balance when the center of mass changes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted three main experiments to validate the tele-loco-manipulation system: telemanipulation performance, locomotion stability, and an integrated tele-loco-manipulation task.

#### 4.1. A. Experimental Results

1. **Telemanipulation Validation:**
 - **Motion Latency:** Analysis of cross-correlation between human and robot hand positions, particularly in the y-direction, indicated an end-to-end motion latency of approximately 220 ms. This was qualitatively supported by visual inspection of transient data, showing a consistent ~200 ms displacement between human and robot motions across x, y, and z directions (Figure 4, left).
 - **Motion Accuracy:** Qualitative observation of circular trajectories demonstrated that the humanoid was able to track the operator's arm motions (Figure 4, right). Quantitatively, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for the normalized positions of the operator's and robot's right arms were calculated. For example, the right arm's y-dimension showed an MAE of 10.58% and an RMSE of 15.07% (Table IV).

2. **Locomotion Validation:**
 - **Stability:** The robustness of the Reinforcement Learning (RL) locomotion policy was evaluated by applying random whole-body velocity commands to the robot every three seconds over an eighty-second period. The normalized projected gravity, a metric for bipedal stability and posture, remained consistently near the desired value of -1 throughout the experiment (Figure 6). This indicated that the RL policy effectively maintained an upright posture and rejected disturbances, even during significant and abrupt changes in commanded body velocities.

3. **Tele-Loco-Manipulation Experiment:**
 - **Task Performance:** A block relocation experiment, requiring simultaneous bimanual manipulation and locomotion, was conducted over ten trials. The teleoperated robot successfully relocated an average of 2 out of 6 40-gram 3D-printed PLA cubes within a 10-minute trial period.
 - **Locomotion Metrics:** During these trials, the robot traversed an average distance of approximately 5 meters per trial, maintaining an average walking velocity of about 0.35 m/s.
 - **Qualitative Observations:** The system’s slow performance was attributed to a combination of operational difficulty and operator frustration. Specific issues included the RL-based locomotion policy not lifting its feet sufficiently high during walking, operators needing to cross their arms to generate adequate force for lifting cubes, and locomotion challenges compounded by the robot's tethered power connection.

#### 4.2. B. Discussion

The telemanipulation experiments indicated that while the system exhibited a notable motion latency of approximately 220 ms, the upper body was generally capable of reproducing the operator's circular arm movements. Much of the instantaneous positioning error (e.g., up to 15% RMSE/MAE) was attributed to this latency. The locomotion policy demonstrated robustness, with the normalized projected gravity consistently maintained near the desired value of -1, signifying stable torso orientation even under dynamic velocity commands. However, the integrated tele-loco-manipulation experiment revealed functional limitations. The system struggled to efficiently complete the block relocation task, managing only an average of 2 blocks in 10 minutes. This inefficiency was linked to several factors: insufficient foot clearance from the RL policy, necessitating compensatory operator actions to lift objects, and practical constraints such as the robot's tethered power supply affecting its maneuverability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a foundational tele-loco-manipulation system for DYNAMIXEL-based miniature humanoid robots, addressing a notable gap in advanced control capabilities for these accessible platforms. By integrating VR telepresence for upper-body manipulation with Reinforcement Learning (RL) for lower-body locomotion and balancing, the work provides a full-body control stack that allows for independent control of the robot's different segments. This modularity is posited to simplify future development efforts.

**Significance:**
- **Accessibility in Research:** The development of a compliant, advanced control framework for miniature humanoids like the ROBOTIS OP3 directly contributes to making sophisticated humanoid robotics research more accessible to a wider community. This potentially lowers the financial and logistical barriers compared to working with expensive, full-sized robots.
- **Data Generation for Imitation Learning:** By enabling teleoperated loco-manipulation on miniature humanoids, the system provides a means to generate valuable high-quality demonstration data. This data is critical for advancing imitation learning policies, which are currently bottlenecked by the scarcity of such data from full-sized robots.
- **Compliant Control for DYNAMIXEL Actuators:** The introduction of a custom-built PD-based torque controller and data-driven DYNAMIXEL actuator modeling is a significant contribution. This approach enables compliant interactions, enhancing safety during contact-rich tasks and offering a robot-agnostic solution that can be applied to various DYNAMIXEL-based robot morphologies.
- **Robust Locomotion:** The RL policy, trained with extensive domain randomization and base perturbations, demonstrates robust balancing capabilities, maintaining stable torso orientation even under dynamic manipulation and velocity commands.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement of Miniature Humanoid Capabilities:** The framework establishes a baseline for enhancing the general-purpose loco-manipulation capabilities of miniature humanoids, potentially expanding their application beyond prototyping and social interactions to more complex manipulation and navigation tasks.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The methods presented serve as a building block for future improvements in areas such as more sophisticated humanoid proprioceptive walking, advanced reward shaping for RL, and refined sim-to-real transfer techniques.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** Future enhancements like integrating a VR treadmill ("KAT WALK") and improving vision motion compensation could lead to more intuitive and immersive teleoperation experiences, increasing operator efficiency and reducing frustration.
- **Broader Application:** The generalizable nature of the DYNAMIXEL-based torque control and RL approach means the framework could be adapted and applied to other robots utilizing similar actuation schemes, extending its impact across the robotics landscape.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The research identifies several areas for improvement. The current system exhibits motion latency (around 220 ms) in telemanipulation. The telemanipulation system was noted to be "too soft" during trials, requiring operators to compensate for insufficient grip force, suggesting a need for actual grippers or end-effector sensors. The rudimentary actuator model necessitates excessive domain randomization, indicating a need for more accurate dynamic models and explicit gravity/friction compensation to improve joint impedance. The RL policy's gait struggled with foot clearance, and the tethered power connection hindered the robot's mobility. Addressing these limitations—through more advanced RL training on diverse terrains, improved sim2real transfer, better sensor integration, and untethered operation—is expected to significantly enhance the robustness, user-friendliness, and task efficiency of miniature humanoid tele-loco-manipulation systems, ultimately empowering them to tackle a broader suite of tasks.
