# BLINK: Multimodal Large Language Models Can See but Not Perceive

> [!summary] Summary
> The BLINK benchmark was introduced by researchers from the University of Washington and the Allen Institute for AI to evaluate the core visual perception capabilities of multimodal large language models. Evaluations using BLINK reveal a substantial performance disparity between MLLMs and humans on fundamental visual tasks, indicating that current models struggle with genuine perception beyond language-based reasoning.

## Problem

- Current multimodal large language model (MLLM) benchmarks often conflate true visual perception with language knowledge and general reasoning, allowing models to succeed by leveraging strong linguistic abilities on image captions rather than genuine visual understanding.
- Many traditional computer vision problems requiring deep geometric understanding, material properties, or precise spatial reasoning have been neglected in modern MLLM evaluations because they are difficult to describe or mediate through imprecise natural language.
- There is a critical gap between human-level intuitive visual understanding and machine capabilities, as tasks solvable by humans 'within a blink' continue to significantly challenge MLLMs.

## Method

- The **BLINK** benchmark comprises ==14 classic computer vision tasks== reformulated into ==multiple-choice questions== for MLLMs, specifically designed to test nuanced visual understanding that resists reduction to ==dense captioning==.
- Tasks incorporate diverse ==visual prompts== (e.g., circles, boxes, masks) directly on images to enable testing of fine-grained understanding that cannot be conveyed through text alone.
- The benchmark contains 3,807 questions and 7,358 images sourced from existing datasets and adapted, designed to assess ==visual commonsense== that is intuitive for humans but challenging for MLLMs.

## Results

- Humans achieved **95.70%** accuracy on BLINK, while the best proprietary MLLMs (GPT-4V at **51.26%**, GPT-4o at **59.03%**) scored significantly lower, demonstrating a profound performance gap.
- A text-only GPT-4 fed dense image captions performed at random guessing levels on BLINK (**38.09%** accuracy), while performing well or comparably to MLLMs on other benchmarks (MMBench, MMMU), confirming BLINK necessitates genuine visual perception.
- MLLMs performed relatively better on high-level spatial reasoning, art style, and counting, but consistently struggled with pixel-level and crop-level tasks demanding fine-grained visual details and local relationships.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) struggle significantly with fundamental visual perception tasks requiring geometric, intrinsic, or precise spatial reasoning, despite their advancements in vision-language integration.
> - Many existing MLLM benchmarks conflate perception with language and reasoning, as demonstrated by the ability of a text-only LLM to perform well on them given dense captions, a method that fails completely on BLINK.
> - The substantial gap between human performance and MLLMs on 'blink-of-an-eye' visual tasks highlights that current models lack the core visual 'commonsense' that underpins human understanding of the physical world.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of multimodal large language models has rapidly advanced, enabling models like GPT-4V and Gemini to interact with and generate content based on both text and images. This progress has led to an increasing need for benchmarks that can accurately assess their capabilities. Historically, early computer vision focused on interpreting images as projections of 3D scenes, delving into optical properties, 3D primitives, geometric reasoning, and visual correspondence. However, with the rise of MLLMs, the community's focus shifted towards vision-language connections, often evaluating models on tasks that emphasize language-based reasoning over deep visual perception.

This paper critically positions itself against the prevailing trend in MLLM evaluation. The authors argue that many popular existing benchmarks—such as MMBench, MMMU, MME, MM-Vet, Seed-Bench, MathVista, and HallusionBench—"conflate perception with language knowledge and reasoning." They contend that these benchmarks often inadvertently reduce complex visual understanding to a "dense captioning" task, where a robust language model, given a comprehensive textual description of an image (even if generated by a "blind" system), can often deduce the correct answer without true visual "perception." This implies that current MLLMs might be excelling more at leveraging their linguistic and reasoning capabilities on image captions rather than genuinely "seeing" and interpreting visual nuances.

"BLINK" directly addresses this perceived gap. It attempts to realign MLLM evaluation with the classical computer vision goal of "interpreting images as projections of 3D scenes" and understanding intrinsic visual properties. By reformulating 14 traditional computer vision problems into multiple-choice questions for MLLMs, BLINK seeks to isolate and test fundamental visual perception skills that are inherently difficult to mediate through imprecise natural language. This work highlights that while MLLMs have made remarkable strides in vision-language integration and generative abilities, their core perceptual understanding remains significantly underdeveloped compared to human intuition, thereby urging the community to shift focus toward improving these foundational visual competencies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and validate BLINK, a novel benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the fundamental visual perception abilities of multimodal large language models. The authors aim to shed light on a crucial aspect of MLLM capabilities that they argue has been overlooked or conflated in existing evaluations.

The motivation behind BLINK stems from several critical observations:
1. **Conflation of Perception and Language Reasoning:** The authors contend that current prominent benchmarks for MLLMs primarily test a combination of visual recognition, language understanding, and general reasoning. This often allows MLLMs to succeed by relying heavily on their powerful language models, which can infer answers from rich image captions, rather than genuinely "perceiving" visual details. They demonstrate this by showing that a "blind" GPT-4, fed only image captions, can perform surprisingly well on existing multimodal benchmarks.
2. **Lack of Focus on Core Visual Perception:** Many traditional computer vision tasks that require deep geometric understanding, material properties, or precise spatial reasoning have been neglected in modern MLLM evaluations because they "resist mediation through natural language." Language's inherent imprecision makes it challenging to describe fine-grained visual details like precise spatial keypoints or subtle reflectance differences.
3. **Human vs. Machine Discrepancy:** The tasks chosen for BLINK are those that humans can solve "within a blink"—intuitively and rapidly—yet current MLLMs struggle significantly with them. This highlights a critical gap between human-level visual understanding and machine capabilities, signaling that core perceptual abilities have not yet "emerged" in these large models.
4. **Stimulating Research for True Visual Intelligence:** By exposing this gap, the authors aim to stimulate the research community to focus on enhancing MLLMs' true visual perception. They hypothesize that MLLMs could benefit from integrating insights and techniques from specialized computer vision models that excel in these areas, moving beyond mere recognition and language-driven interpretation towards a more holistic and human-like understanding of visual scenes.

In essence, BLINK seeks to provide a targeted, challenging evaluation that pushes MLLMs beyond their current linguistic strengths to demonstrate genuine visual intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the development and application of the BLINK benchmark. The methodology encompasses a rigorous design process for the benchmark, a comprehensive evaluation setup, and in-depth analyses to validate its claims.

#### 3.1. The BLINK Benchmark Design (Blink)

The authors meticulously designed BLINK to overcome the limitations of existing benchmarks.
- **Task Selection:** BLINK comprises 14 classic computer vision tasks, recast into a multiple-choice question-answering format suitable for MLLMs. These tasks are chosen because they demand "nuanced understanding of the visual data" and "resist reduction using dense captioning." The tasks span:
 - **Low-level perception:** Visual Correspondence, Relative Reflectance, Semantic Correspondence, Functional Correspondence (often pixel-level or patch-level).
 - **Mid-level reasoning:** Relative Depth, Spatial Relation, Multi-view Reasoning, Jigsaw, Object Localization, Counting (often crop-level).
 - **High-level understanding:** Visual Similarity, IQ Test, Art Style, Forensic Detection (often image-level).
- **Question Format:** All tasks are presented as multiple-choice questions, with options being either text or images. Questions can involve single or multiple images.
- **Visual Prompting:** A key distinguishing feature is the incorporation of diverse visual prompts (circles, boxes, image masks) directly on the images to highlight specific regions or objects. This enables testing fine-grained understanding that text alone cannot convey.
- **Data Curation:** The benchmark contains 3,807 questions and 7,358 images, split evenly between validation and test sets. Data is sourced from a wide range of existing datasets (e.g., HPatches, IIW, Depth in the Wild, LVIS, TallyQA) and manually curated or adapted for the specific task format. The images encompass diverse settings (indoor, outdoor, urban, natural) and types (abstract, synthesized, authentic).
- **"Visual Commonsense":** Questions are designed to be intuitive and solvable by humans "within a blink" (typically a few seconds), requiring no domain-specific knowledge, thus focusing on fundamental perceptual abilities.
- **Quality Control:** All collected data were manually reviewed and filtered for ambiguity to ensure high quality and clarity.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Setup

The authors conducted an extensive evaluation of MLLMs on BLINK:
- **Models Evaluated:** 16 state-of-the-art MLLMs were tested, including popular open-source models (e.g., MiniGPT-4-v2, OpenFlamingo-v2, InstructBLIP, CogVLM, various LLaVA versions, Yi-VL) and powerful API-based models (Qwen-VL-Max, Gemini Pro, Claude 3 Opus, GPT-4V, GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4o).
- **Protocol:** Standard evaluation procedures from VLMEvalKit were followed, with temperature set to 0 (greedy decoding) and no image resizing. For models that do not natively support multiple image inputs, images were concatenated horizontally. Model outputs were parsed using predefined rules and, if necessary, an auxiliary GPT-3.5 model for option matching.
- **Human Performance:** Two co-authors independently annotated the tasks to establish a human baseline, with average accuracy reported.

#### 3.3. Analytical Approach

Beyond raw performance, the authors conducted several insightful analyses:
- **"Dense Captioning" Experiment:** To test the hypothesis that MLLMs rely on language, they converted images from BLINK, MMBench, and MMMU into dense captions using GPT-4V. These captions were then fed to a text-only GPT-4 model, and its performance was compared to the image-input MLLMs.
- **Visual Prompting Sensitivity:** For tasks involving visual prompts, they varied circle sizes (5px, 10px, 15px radius) and colors (red, gray) to assess their impact on GPT-4V's accuracy.
- **Specialist Model Comparison:** They evaluated six specialized computer vision models (e.g., DepthAnything for depth estimation, LoFTR for visual correspondence) on the relevant BLINK tasks to establish a potential upper bound for MLLM performance.
- **Error Analysis:** A random sample of GPT-4V's errors (10 per task, 140 total) was manually analyzed and categorized to identify common failure modes.

This multi-faceted methodology provides a robust framework for assessing MLLMs' perceptual capabilities and understanding the nature of their strengths and weaknesses.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on BLINK revealed significant insights into the perceptual abilities of current multimodal large language models:

- **Profound Performance Gap with Humans:**
 - Humans achieved an average accuracy of **95.70%** across BLINK tasks, demonstrating that these tasks are indeed straightforward for human visual perception.
 - In stark contrast, even the best-performing proprietary MLLMs, **GPT-4V**, achieved only **51.26%** accuracy, while **Gemini Pro** managed **45.72%**. These scores are merely **13.17%** and **7.63%** higher than random guessing (which is 38.09%), respectively.
 - Open-source MLLMs (7B and 13B parameters) performed even worse, hovering around **35-42%** accuracy, often comparable to or even below random chance on specific tasks like jigsaw, semantic correspondence, multi-view reasoning, object localization, and relative reflectance.

- **MLLM Strengths and Weaknesses by Task:**
 - MLLMs performed relatively better on **spatial reasoning, art style, and counting** tasks (mid-level perception), showing a notable improvement over random guessing.
 - They consistently struggled with **pixel-level and crop-level tasks**, indicating a difficulty in processing fine-grained visual details and understanding local relationships.

- **GPT-4V and GPT-4o's Distinctive Performance:**
 - While overall still far from human performance, **GPT-4V** showed a different performance profile compared to other MLLMs. It demonstrated significantly better results in visual similarity, art style, jigsaw, and multi-view reasoning, suggesting a more nuanced understanding of visual patterns and aesthetics.
 - **GPT-4o**, a more recent model, showed a substantial leap in performance (59.03% overall accuracy), particularly excelling in tasks like visual similarity, visual correspondence, and forensic detection, highlighting rapid improvements in newer models.

- **The "Dense Captioning" Hypothesis Confirmed for Other Benchmarks, Not BLINK:**
 - The crucial experiment of feeding text-only GPT-4 with dense image captions yielded striking results:
 - On **MMBench**, Caption + LLM performed *better* than GPT-4V (+5.7% accuracy), suggesting MMBench's tasks can be largely solved via sophisticated language reasoning on detailed captions.
 - On **MMMU**, Caption + LLM performed well, although slightly lower than GPT-4V (-9.6% accuracy), indicating a substantial portion of the necessary visual information is conveyed through captions.
 - On **BLINK**, however, Caption + LLM *failed spectacularly*, achieving performance equivalent to random guessing. This is the strongest evidence supporting the authors' claim that BLINK truly necessitates genuine visual perception beyond what can be conveyed or reasoned from text captions.

- **Impact of Visual Prompting:**
 - Experiments showed that **red circles** generally outperformed gray circles as visual prompts.
 - The optimal circle size was task-dependent, with **10px radius** performing best on average. This indicates that visual prompting is not a trivial factor and that MLLMs' robustness to prompt variations needs improvement.

- **Specialist Models as an Upper Bound:**
 - Evaluations of dedicated computer vision models for tasks like depth estimation (DepthAnything) and visual correspondence (DIFT) demonstrated their superior performance, often achieving human-level accuracy (e.g., 97.58% for DepthAnything on relative depth vs. GPT-4V's 59.68%).
 - This finding suggests that the inherent difficulty of BLINK tasks is not due to insolvability but rather the current MLLMs' lack of specialized perceptual capabilities, hinting at potential pathways for future improvement by integrating or distilling knowledge from such specialist models.

- **Error Analysis of GPT-4V:**
 - The most prevalent errors included **hallucinating fine-grained patterns and attributes** (24.2%) and **misinterpreting visual prompt locations** (20.0%).
 - Other common errors involved failures in recognizing spatial relations, reasoning errors despite correct image interpretation, and inability to capture overall scene impressions or ground mentioned objects. The consistency of errors across open-source MLLMs indicated that limitations often stem from their shared vision encoder architectures.

In summary, the main findings decisively show that while MLLMs are adept at processing vision-language cues and performing recognition-based tasks, they currently lack the fundamental, "blink-of-an-eye" visual perception abilities that humans possess, particularly for tasks requiring geometric, intrinsic, or precise spatial reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of BLINK carries significant implications for the advancement of multimodal AI research, particularly in pushing the boundaries of true visual intelligence in large models.

- **Re-defining Multimodal Evaluation:** BLINK serves as a crucial corrective for the current MLLM evaluation landscape. By rigorously demonstrating that many existing benchmarks conflate perception with language and reasoning, it forces a re-evaluation of what it truly means for an MLLM to "see." This will likely lead to a new generation of benchmarks that are more precise in isolating specific visual competencies.

- **Exposing a Critical Capability Gap:** The stark disparity between human performance (over 95% accuracy) and even the best MLLMs (around 50%) on tasks that are trivial for humans highlights a profound and often underappreciated limitation of current models. This gap in "core visual perception" suggests that MLLMs are not merely less precise than humans but fundamentally lack certain mechanisms for deep visual understanding. This finding is vital for temperating expectations about the current "seeing" capabilities of MLLMs and providing a clear target for improvement.

- **Guiding Future Research Directions:**
 - **Focus on True Perception:** BLINK will stimulate research efforts to develop MLLMs that can genuinely perceive beyond recognition and dense captioning. This could involve exploring new architectural designs, training objectives, or data modalities that imbue models with a deeper understanding of 3D geometry, material properties, and fine-grained visual details.
 - **Integration with Specialist Models:** The superior performance of traditional computer vision specialist models on BLINK tasks (e.g., DepthAnything, DIFT) provides a clear roadmap. Future MLLM research could focus on how to effectively integrate or distill the knowledge and capabilities of these specialists into general-purpose MLLMs, potentially through techniques like visual program distillation or specialized modules.
 - **Advanced Visual Prompt Engineering:** The sensitivity of MLLMs to visual prompts demonstrated in the analysis suggests that improving visual prompting techniques or making models more robust to prompt variations is a promising area.
 - **Error Rectification:** The detailed error analysis (e.g., hallucinating fine-grained patterns or misinterpreting prompt locations) offers specific targets for model improvement, guiding researchers to address fundamental deficiencies rather than just scale.

- **Implications for Artificial General Intelligence (AGI):** If the ultimate goal of AI is to achieve expert-level AGI, a human-like understanding of the visual world is indispensable. BLINK reveals that despite impressive progress in language and high-level reasoning, MLLMs are still far from possessing the intuitive visual "commonsense" that underpins human interaction with the physical world. This benchmark serves as a crucial stepping stone towards measuring and fostering such a foundational aspect of intelligence.

- **Impact on Real-World Applications:** Better core visual perception is not just an academic pursuit; it has significant practical implications. Applications in robotics, autonomous systems, medical imaging, and augmented reality, for instance, demand models that can accurately infer depth, object correspondence, and functional affordances. By driving improvements in these areas, BLINK contributes to building more reliable, robust, and truly intelligent AI systems that can operate effectively in complex real-world environments.

Ultimately, BLINK provides a simple yet effective testbed to bridge the gap between traditional notions of perception and the modern generative capabilities of multimodal LLMs, setting a clear challenge for the community to help these models "catch up with human-level visual perception."
