# Ground-level Viewpoint Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents Ground-level Viewpoint Navigation (GVNav), an approach that enables ground-level robots to follow natural language instructions by addressing the viewpoint disparity between human-height VLN research and real-robot perspectives. GVNav enhances waypoint prediction and incorporates adaptive information gathering, leading to improved navigation accuracy and robustness for low-height robots in both simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- A significant disparity exists between the human-height (approx. 1.5m) viewpoints used in most Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) research and the much lower viewpoints (approx. 0.3m) of practical robotic platforms.
- This viewpoint difference leads to challenges such as restricted field of view, increased visual obstructions, and mismatches between human instruction reference points and the robot's perception.
- Existing VLN models experience substantial performance degradation when deployed with low line-of-sight cameras, hindering their real-world applicability.

## Method

- Introducing **Ground-level** ==Viewpoint Navigation (GVNav)==, a framework designed to bridge the viewpoint discrepancy for low-height robots in continuous environments.
- Implementing an enhanced waypoint prediction method that uses data augmentation from low-height perspectives and depth-aware training to improve path planning.
- Developing an adaptive information gathering module that integrates weighted historical observations and uses an attention mechanism to create richer spatiotemporal context, compensating for limited instantaneous field of view.

## Results

- GVNav achieved superior performance compared to baseline methods across standard VLN metrics in both simulation and real-world experiments on a Xiaomi Cyberdog.
- The enhanced waypoint prediction module led to substantial improvements in navigation accuracy and generalization, particularly in handling ground-level depth information.
- The adaptive information gathering module demonstrated significant benefits in environments with visual obstructions, enabling the robot to maintain situational awareness despite its limited viewpoint, and real-world deployment confirmed robust navigation and accurate instruction following.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The disparity between human-height viewpoints used in most VLN research and the ground-level perspectives of many real robots creates a significant information gap.
> - Accurate waypoint prediction is particularly critical for effective navigation from low-height viewpoints, where visibility is often limited.
> - Integrating historical observations and adaptively selecting informative viewpoints can mitigate the impact of restricted field of view and visual obstructions common in ground-level navigation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) enables robots to follow natural language instructions to navigate through environments. While significant progress has been made in developing VLN systems in simulated environments, transferring these systems to real-world robots presents substantial challenges. One critical yet under-explored challenge is the disparity between the viewpoint heights used in VLN research and those encountered by real robots.

Most VLN research utilizes panoramic observations from a human-height perspective (approximately 1.5 meters), while many practical robot platforms—particularly small quadrupedal robots like the Xiaomi Cyberdog—operate with monocular cameras positioned much closer to the ground (around 0.3 meters). This viewpoint difference creates a significant information gap that impedes effective navigation.

The researchers introduce Ground-level Viewpoint Navigation (GVNav), an approach specifically designed to address the challenges of low-height viewpoint navigation in continuous environments. GVNav aims to bridge the gap between simulation and real-world deployment by enhancing waypoint prediction capabilities and developing an adaptive information gathering mechanism to handle visual obstructions common in ground-level perspectives.

### 2. Background on VLN

Vision-and-Language Navigation combines computer vision and natural language processing to enable agents to navigate environments based on human instructions. VLN research has traditionally followed two main approaches:

1. **Discrete Navigation**: Agents move between predefined nodes in a navigation graph, typically using panoramic observations at each node. This approach simplifies the navigation problem but limits applicability to real robots.

2. **Continuous Navigation**: Agents navigate in continuous space, making decisions based on egocentric observations. This approach aligns better with robotic applications but presents additional challenges in matching instructions to visual inputs.

Recent advancements have improved instruction following, visual grounding, and path planning capabilities. However, most research assumes ideal observation conditions with panoramic or high-positioned cameras, creating a disconnect with real robotic platforms.

### 3. The Viewpoint Discrepancy Problem

The core challenge addressed in this research is the viewpoint discrepancy between human instruction-givers and ground-level robots. This discrepancy manifests in several ways:

1. **Restricted Field of View**: Low-height robots have a limited field of view, preventing them from seeing over obstacles or gaining a comprehensive understanding of the environment.

2. **Visual Obstructions**: Objects like furniture and walls obstruct the robot's view much more significantly from a ground-level perspective.

3. **Reference Point Mismatch**: Human instructions often reference objects or landmarks visible from human height but not from the robot's viewpoint.

4. **Limited Spatial Context**: The restricted viewpoint makes it difficult for robots to build a complete spatial understanding of the environment, leading to navigation errors.

The authors quantified this performance gap by testing existing VLN models under both high and low line-of-sight conditions, finding significant performance degradation with lower viewpoints.

### 4. Ground-level Viewpoint Navigation Approach

GVNav addresses the viewpoint discrepancy problem through three primary components:

1. **Enhanced Waypoint Prediction**: Improving the ability to predict navigable paths from low-height viewpoints by augmenting training data and transferring connectivity graphs from public 3D scans.

2. **Adaptive Information Gathering**: Developing a module that integrates historical observations to create richer spatiotemporal context for instruction following.

3. **Topological Navigation Policy**: Utilizing a topological map-based navigation policy to enable effective backtracking and planning in continuous environments.

The approach is built upon the ETPNav framework but introduces critical modifications to handle the challenges of low-height viewpoint navigation.

### 5. Enhanced Waypoint Prediction

The waypoint predictor is a crucial component that determines where the robot can navigate given its current observation. To improve its performance with low-height viewpoints, the authors implemented:

1. **Data Augmentation**: Creating a large-scale dataset using 3D scans from HM3D, Gibson, and MP3D datasets, specifically capturing depth images from a low-height viewpoint to better represent the robot's perspective.

2. **Connectivity Graph Transfer**: Transferring connectivity graphs from public 3D scans to provide additional training data and enhance spatial priors for the waypoint predictor.

3. **Depth-Aware Training**: Training the waypoint predictor to better interpret depth information from a low-height perspective, enabling it to accurately determine navigable areas despite visual obstructions.

This enhanced waypoint prediction significantly improves the robot's ability to identify viable navigation paths from a ground-level viewpoint, addressing one of the key challenges in transferring VLN models to real robots.

### 6. Adaptive Information Gathering

To address the limited field of view and visual obstructions common in ground-level navigation, GVNav incorporates an adaptive information gathering module with two key features:

1. **Weighted Historical Observations**: The module leverages observations from multiple timestamps, creating a richer representation of the environment beyond what's currently visible.

2. **Attention Mechanism**: A trainable transformer encoder layer adaptively selects the optimal visual representation for each node in the topological map, assigning appropriate weights to different viewpoints to:
 - Mitigate feature collisions from redundant observations
 - Handle visual obstructions by focusing on the most informative viewpoints
 - Create a more comprehensive representation of the environment

This approach enables the robot to "remember" previously observed areas and use this information to navigate more effectively, compensating for its limited instantaneous field of view.

### 7. Experimental Setup

The researchers conducted extensive experiments to evaluate GVNav's performance:

1. **Simulation Environment**: The approach was first tested in simulation using the HM3D and RoboVLN datasets, which provide realistic 3D environments for VLN tasks.

2. **Real-world Testing**: GVNav was deployed on a Xiaomi Cyberdog quadruped robot equipped with an Intel RealSense D435 camera mounted at approximately 0.3 meters height.

3. **Test Environments**: Real-world testing was conducted in diverse indoor environments, including a gaming room, kitchen, laboratory, and office area, with varying complexity and visual characteristics.

4. **Baselines**: GVNav was compared against several baseline approaches, including standard ETPNav, to quantify the improvements specifically related to addressing the viewpoint discrepancy problem.

5. **Metrics**: Performance was evaluated using standard VLN metrics including success rate (SR), success weighted by path length (SPL), and navigation error (NE).

### 8. Results and Findings

The experimental results revealed several important findings:

1. **Performance Gap Confirmation**: The experiments confirmed a significant performance gap between VLN models trained on high line-of-sight data and their performance when tested with low line-of-sight data.

2. **Waypoint Prediction Improvement**: The enhanced waypoint prediction approach led to substantial improvements in navigation accuracy and generalization, particularly in handling ground-level depth information.

3. **Effectiveness of Adaptive Information Gathering**: The adaptive information gathering module demonstrated significant benefits in environments with visual obstructions, enabling the robot to maintain situational awareness despite its limited viewpoint.

4. **Ablation Study Insights**: Under challenging low line-of-sight conditions, the waypoint predictor had a more pronounced impact on performance compared to the navigator component, highlighting the critical importance of accurate waypoint prediction for ground-level navigation.

5. **Performance Metrics**: GVNav achieved superior performance compared to baseline methods across all metrics, with particularly notable improvements in success rate and navigation error in complex environments.

### 9. Real-world Deployment

The deployment of GVNav on the Xiaomi Cyberdog in real-world environments demonstrated the approach's effectiveness beyond simulation. Key observations from real-world testing include:

1. **Robust Navigation**: The robot successfully navigated complex indoor environments following natural language instructions, demonstrating the approach's robustness to real-world challenges.

2. **Handling of Visual Obstructions**: GVNav effectively handled visual obstructions by leveraging historical observations and adaptive information gathering, enabling the robot to navigate around obstacles and maintain orientation.

3. **Instruction Following**: The robot accurately interpreted and followed complex instructions involving spatial references and multi-step directions, even when key landmarks were temporarily out of view.

4. **Recovery from Errors**: The topological navigation policy allowed effective backtracking and recovery when the robot encountered unexpected obstacles or navigation errors.

The real-world deployment validated the practical applicability of GVNav and its ability to bridge the gap between simulation and real-world robotic navigation.

### 10. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its successes, the research acknowledges several limitations and areas for future improvement:

1. **Environmental Complexity**: While GVNav performs well in the tested environments, further research is needed to evaluate its effectiveness in more diverse and challenging settings.

2. **Instruction Complexity**: The approach may still struggle with highly complex or ambiguous instructions that require sophisticated reasoning about environmental features not visible from the robot's viewpoint.

3. **Long-term Memory**: Enhancing the system's ability to maintain and utilize long-term spatial memory could further improve navigation in large environments.

4. **Dynamic Environments**: The current approach primarily addresses static environments; extending it to handle dynamic elements (moving objects or people) would increase its practical applicability.

5. **Multimodal Sensing**: Integrating additional sensor modalities beyond RGB-D cameras could provide complementary information to further mitigate the viewpoint discrepancy problem.

### 11. Conclusion

The Ground-level Viewpoint Navigation (GVNav) approach represents a significant advancement in Vision-and-Language Navigation for ground-level robots. By specifically addressing the viewpoint discrepancy problem, GVNav bridges an important gap between VLN research and practical robotic applications.

The research demonstrates that enhanced waypoint prediction and adaptive information gathering can significantly improve navigation performance for low-height robots, enabling them to follow natural language instructions effectively despite their limited viewpoint.

The successful deployment on a quadruped robot in real-world environments validates the approach's practical utility and provides a foundation for future research on VLN for ground-level robots. This work contributes to the broader goal of developing more capable, intuitive, and reliable navigation systems for robots operating in human-centric environments.
