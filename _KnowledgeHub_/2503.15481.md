# Learning to Play Piano in the Real World

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from TU Dresden's LASR Lab present the first learning-based system capable of playing the piano using a real-world dexterous robotic hand. This work employs an iterative Sim2Real2Sim workflow combined with domain randomization to transfer policies trained in simulation, achieving an average F1-score of 0.881 on five simple songs during real-world execution.

## Problem

- The challenge of transferring learned dexterous manipulation policies from simulation to physical robots due to the "Sim2Real gap."
- Previous robotic piano playing efforts either used hand-designed controllers for real robots or confined learning-based approaches to simulation.
- Developing robots capable of human-level manipulation involving precise, adaptable, and contact-rich hand movements for complex tasks.

## Method

- A robotic system comprising a Ufactory xArm7 and an ==Allegro Hand== with custom 3D-printed fingertips, operating on an M-Audio MIDI keyboard.
- An iterative Sim2Real2Sim workflow that refines simulation parameters using real-world data to minimize discrepancies before policy training and deployment.
- Incorporation of domain randomization during simulation training to enhance policy robustness against variations and reduce the Sim2Real gap.

## Results

- The system achieved an average F1-score of **0.881** when playing five simple songs (e.g., "Happy Birthday," "Ode To Joy") on a real piano, with individual song F1-scores ranging from **0.821** to **0.919**.
- Policies trained with simulated key fences exhibited a smaller Sim2Real gap (**6.8%**) and better real-world performance (F1-score of **0.881**) compared to policies trained without fences (**11.2%** gap, F1-score of **0.840**).
- In simulation, policies achieved an average F1-score of **0.946**, demonstrating the agent's ability to learn the songs within the optimized virtual environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inclusion of narrow "fences" between piano keys in simulation environments improves training efficiency and reduces the Sim2Real gap by making the simulated physics more closely resemble real-world contact constraints.
> - A specific fingertip collision penalty in the reward function accelerates the learning process by guiding the agent towards physically plausible interactions, though it does not alter the final learned strategy.
> - The combination of iterative Sim2Real2Sim and domain randomization is an effective strategy for successfully deploying complex, learned dexterous skills, like piano playing, from simulation onto physical robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively pursuing the grand challenge of achieving human-level manipulation, which encompasses tasks requiring dexterity, precision, and adaptable movements. Previous research in robotic manipulation has explored various complex tasks, such as in-hand object rotation. While these studies have yielded efficient manipulation techniques, they often utilize non-anthropomorphic or unnatural motions that may limit their applicability to tasks requiring more human-like control.

Musical instrument playing, particularly the piano, presents a contrasting and compelling benchmark for dexterous manipulation due to its requirement for precise, contact-rich hand movements, timing, tone production, and close alignment with natural human hand kinematics. Historically, robotic engagement with musical instruments dates back to sophisticated, purpose-built robots designed to play instruments like the flute or early humanoid robots performing on the piano using pre-programmed movements. More recent works have utilized both custom-designed and general-purpose robotic hands for piano playing, but these largely relied on pre-programmed movements, lacking adaptability.

The emergence of machine learning, especially reinforcement learning (RL), has introduced new paradigms. Early RL-based approaches in piano playing demonstrated the ability to play simple notes with correct velocity in simulation using vision-based tactile sensors. Subsequent work, such as RoboPianist, showed that RL agents could be trained for advanced bi-manual music in simulation, though often requiring per-song training and manual fingering annotations. Efforts to improve multi-song performance and eliminate manual annotations include methods like optimal transport for fingering discovery and imitation learning. However, these advancements have predominantly focused on *simulation environments*.

A significant gap exists in transferring these learned, simulation-based piano-playing policies to *physical robots*. This "Sim2Real gap" arises from inaccuracies between simulated and real-world physics, sensor noise, and hardware variabilities. Prior work on mitigating the Sim2Real gap has included techniques such as residual physics learning, rapid motor adaptation, domain randomization (DR), and iterative Sim2Real2Sim approaches.

This paper addresses this specific gap by presenting the first system that deploys a *learning-based* piano-playing agent on a *real-world dexterous robot*. It integrates both Sim2Real2Sim and domain randomization techniques to bridge the simulation-to-reality divide, thereby moving beyond purely simulated demonstrations towards practical robotic musical performance. The work positions itself as a proof-of-concept aimed at establishing real-world piano playing as a benchmark for human-level manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate the first robotic system capable of learning to play the piano in the real world using machine learning approaches. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Advancing Human-Level Manipulation:** The paper aims to contribute to the broader scientific challenge of enabling robots to achieve human-level dexterity and manipulation capabilities. Piano playing is identified as a compelling and challenging testbed for this endeavor, as it requires a combination of strategic planning, precise movements, flowing actions, and contact-rich interactions that closely mimic human motor control.

2. **Bridging the Sim2Real Gap for Dexterous Tasks:** Previous research on robot piano playing has largely been bifurcated: either demonstrating hand-designed controllers on real-world robots or evaluating learning-based approaches exclusively in simulation. The core motivation is to bridge this gap by transferring policies trained in simulation to a physical robotic system. This addresses the practical difficulties of deploying learned controllers in the real world, where discrepancies between simulation and reality (the "Sim2Real gap") often lead to performance degradation.

3. **Iterative Sim2Real2Sim Approach:** To overcome the Sim2Real gap, the authors are motivated to employ an iterative Sim2Real2Sim approach. This method involves cyclically refining the simulation parameters based on real-world observations, training policies in the improved simulation, and then deploying these policies on the real robot to collect further data for subsequent simulation updates. This iterative refinement is intended to minimize the discrepancies between the training environment and the real world, thereby enabling more effective policy transfer. Additionally, domain randomization is used to train policies that are robust to environmental variations.

4. **Establishing Piano Playing as a Real-World Benchmark:** The authors explicitly aim to encourage the robotics community to adopt real-world piano playing as a compelling benchmark for human-level manipulation. By providing a proof-of-concept, they seek to demonstrate the feasibility and value of this task for evaluating and advancing dexterous robot control in complex, contact-rich scenarios. This motivation extends to fostering further research in this area.

5. **Facilitating Future Research:** To lower the barrier to entry and accelerate research progress, the authors are motivated to open-source their code and provide supplementary materials. This aims to reduce initial setup costs, enable other researchers to easily verify their approaches, and provide a baseline for comparing alternative methods in robot piano playing.

6. **Gaining Insights into Real-World Challenges:** Through the development and deployment of their system, the authors aim to identify and articulate important insights into the inherent nature and challenges of performing dexterous, contact-rich tasks like piano playing in the physical world. This includes understanding the impact of environmental features (e.g., key boundaries) and reward function design on learning and real-world transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a Sim2Real2Sim workflow combined with domain randomization to enable a multi-finger robot hand to learn and play piano pieces in the real world.

**Hardware Configuration:**
The robot setup consists of a Ufactory xArm7 robotic arm, which provides a parallel movement capability akin to a human forearm along the piano keys. Attached to the xArm7's end effector is an Allegro Hand v4.0. This hand comprises four fingers, each with four joints. However, the thumb joints are not utilized in this work due to their inability to reliably reach the piano keys, leaving 12 active joints. The default fingertips of the Allegro hand are replaced with custom 3D-printed fingertips, which are designed to approximate human finger dimensions and facilitate single-key presses, as the standard fingertips are wider than a piano key. The piano used is an M-Audio Keystation 49e MIDI Keyboard, a four-octave keyboard that generates MIDI events for each keypress and release, allowing the host computer to observe the currently pressed keys.

**Simulation Environment:**
The simulation environment is built upon the RoboPianist-Suite and utilizes the Mujoco physics engine. It includes a model of the Allegro hand with the custom fingertips and a piano model. A notable addition to the simulation environment is the inclusion of narrow, fixed boundaries, referred to as "fences," between the white keys. These fences are intended to better mimic the physical constraints of a real piano, preventing fingers from sliding laterally into adjacent keys without lifting. All joints in the simulation are controlled by PD controllers, incorporating maximum jerk constraints, a low-pass filter, and damping, allowing for fine-tuning of control parameters during the Sim2Real2Sim process.

**Markov-Decision-Process (MDP):**
- **Action Space:** The agent's action space is 13-dimensional and continuous, comprising one action for each of the 12 active hand joints and one additional action to control the xArm7's linear movement along the piano.
- **Observation Space:** The observation space is 369-dimensional (detailed in Table I) and includes:
 - Used joints of the Allegro hand (12 dimensions, radians).
 - Slider position for parallel movement (1 dimension, meters).
 - Previous joint positions (13 dimensions, mixed units).
 - Currently pressed keys (49 dimensions, one-hot encoded). A key is considered pressed based on MIDI events, meaning there are no observations for partially pressed keys.
 - Music sheet (294 dimensions, 6*49 one-hot encoded).
- **Fingering:** No manual fingering annotations are provided. The agent is encouraged to discover efficient fingering strategies implicitly through a component of the reward function.

**Reward Design:**
The total reward function, `r_total = r_energy + r_∆-action + r_keypress + r_optimal_transport + r_collision`, comprises several components:
- `r_energy`: Penalizes fast and forceful movements to reduce oscillations, conserve energy, and mitigate the risk of damaging the hardware.
- `r_∆-action`: A penalty proportional to the difference between consecutive actions, promoting smoother control signals.
- `r_keypress`: Rewards the agent for pressing the correct keys according to the music sheet.
- `r_optimal_transport`: Adopted from, this term encourages efficient fingering. Instead of Cartesian distance, it uses the one-dimensional difference in the horizontal axis to determine the correct fingertip, with the final reward calculated based on Cartesian distance. This design aims to prevent agents from reusing a finger merely due to its height advantage when another finger is already positioned correctly above the target key.
- `r_collision`: Provides a reward of 0.5 if there are no collisions or only collisions between the piano and fingertips; otherwise, it is 0. This term is included to accelerate convergence by guiding the agent towards physically plausible interactions.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The success of the agent is quantified using the F1-score, calculated as `2 * (recall^-1 + precision^-1)^-1`. Recall measures how reliably the agent presses the correct keys, while precision measures how reliably it avoids pressing incorrect keys.

**Sim2Real2Sim Workflow:**
The iterative workflow is depicted in Fig. 4:
1. An initial action sequence (e.g., hand opening/closing) is executed on the real robot to collect observational data.
2. This real-world data is used in conjunction with Bayesian Optimization to refine and optimize the parameters of the simulation environment, reducing the Sim2Real gap.
3. A piano-playing policy is trained within this updated simulation environment.
4. The trained policy is then deployed on the real robot, generating new observations and actions. These new real-world data points are subsequently used to further refine the simulation parameters in an iterative loop (returning to step 2).

**Domain Randomization (DR):**
To further enhance the robustness of the learned policies and minimize the Sim2Real gap, domain randomization is applied during training. The randomized parameters include the height of the piano, the starting position of the hand, joint damping, applied actuator force, the threshold for a key to be considered pressed, the stiffness of the piano keys' springs, and the friction between the keys and fingertips.

**Model Training:**
The agent's policy is represented by a neural network with three hidden layers, each containing 128 nodes. Training utilizes DroQ, an off-policy soft actor-critic algorithm. A new policy is trained for each song, with typical training duration being approximately 3 hours and 20 minutes over 3 million epochs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results demonstrating the system's ability to play simple piano pieces in the real world and provides insights into key design choices.

**A. Effect of Fences between White Keys:**
- **Setup:** This experiment compared two simulation environments: one with narrow, fixed boundaries ("fences") between white keys and one without. Policies trained in both environments were evaluated on their training progression and real-world Sim2Real transfer.
- **Results:** During simulation training, the environment with fences showed slightly faster progression compared to the fence-free environment. Both ultimately converged to a similar F1-score of 0.946. In real-world deployment, the policy trained with fences achieved an F1-score of 0.881, resulting in a Sim2Real gap of 6.8%. In contrast, the policy trained without fences achieved an F1-score of 0.840, corresponding to a larger Sim2Real gap of 11.2%.
- **Discussion:** The inclusion of fences in the simulation environment was found to be beneficial for both training efficiency and real-world performance. Fences simplify the task of pressing a single key and holding it without triggering adjacent keys during training. More critically, they make the simulation's physical behavior more closely resemble the real world by preventing unintended neighboring key presses from lateral fingertip force, which is common in soft-body physics simulations without such constraints. Fences compel the agent to fully lift its finger during transitions, a behavior consistent with real-world piano playing, thus reducing the likelihood of fingers getting stuck between keys in the real environment.

**B. Impact of Fingertip Collision Penalty:**
- **Setup:** This experiment compared the training progression of policies trained with and without the `r_collision` penalty in the reward function (which rewards collisions only between fingertips and keys).
- **Results:** Policies trained with the `r_collision` penalty exhibited a rapid initial increase in F1-score, reaching high performance quickly. Policies without the penalty showed a slower, more linear increase in F1-score. Both reward formulations eventually converged to similar final F1-score values after approximately 2 million episodes.
- **Discussion:** The `r_collision` penalty primarily accelerates the learning process rather than altering the final learned strategy. Its inclusion guides the policy towards physically plausible fingertip usage earlier in training by discouraging unrealistic collisions (e.g., forcing fingers *into* the piano through soft-body physics). Once the agent learns to interact with the piano primarily using its fingertips, the `r_collision` term becomes constant and no longer influences the optimal policy, explaining the similar long-term performance between the two groups.

**C. Real-World Performance and Generalizability:**
- **Setup:** The approach was evaluated for its generalizability across five simple songs: "Are You Sleeping," "Happy Birthday," "Ode To Joy," "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star," and the "C-Major Scale." Performance was measured in both simulation and the real world to calculate the Sim2Real gap for each song.
- **Results:** In simulation, all songs achieved F1-scores ranging from 0.937 to 0.964. In the real world, the F1-scores ranged from 0.821 to 0.919. The average F1-score achieved in simulation was 0.946, while the average in the real world was 0.881. The Sim2Real gap varied significantly across songs, from 2% to 12.3%.
- **Discussion:** The results indicate that the agent successfully learned to play all five simple songs in the real world. Variations in the Sim2Real gap are attributed to the specific characteristics of each song, such as the inclusion of a black key in "Happy Birthday" or the faster note sequence in "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star." This suggests that while the method generalizes to relatively simple pieces, more complex musical pieces continue to present challenges for the agent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a significant step towards achieving dexterous manipulation in robotics, particularly in contact-rich and human-centric tasks.

1. **Pioneering Real-World Learned Piano Playing:** The primary significance lies in demonstrating the first learning-based system capable of playing the piano with a real-world robotic hand. Prior work was largely confined to either hand-designed controllers or simulated learning, making this a novel bridge between learned policies and physical deployment for this complex task.

2. **Validation of Sim2Real2Sim Approach:** The successful application of the iterative Sim2Real2Sim workflow, coupled with domain randomization, provides a proof-of-concept for this paradigm in transferring complex learned behaviors from simulation to reality. This method's effectiveness in reducing the Sim2Real gap for dexterous tasks holds implications for other challenging robotic manipulation applications.

3. **Establishment of a Real-World Benchmark:** By successfully demonstrating learned piano playing on a physical robot, the authors propose it as a compelling and relevant benchmark for evaluating human-level manipulation. This encourages the robotics community to adopt this task, which demands strategic planning, precise movements, and fine motor control, as a standardized challenge for future research.

4. **Facilitating Community Research:** The decision to open-source the codebase and provide supplementary videos is a critical contribution. This action lowers the barrier to entry for other researchers, enabling them to replicate the results, verify new approaches, and use the established performance as a baseline for comparing alternative methods. This fosters collaborative progress in the field of dexterous manipulation.

5. **Practical Insights for Simulation Design:** The experimental findings, particularly regarding the benefits of "fences" in the simulation environment and the utility of the fingertip collision penalty in reward design, provide practical insights. These insights can inform the development of more accurate and effective simulation environments for other contact-rich robotic tasks, improving training efficiency and Sim2Real transfer.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The work implicitly outlines several promising avenues for future research, including the integration of vision and tactile sensing modalities to enhance perception and error correction, the exploration of advanced fingering techniques with more capable robotic hands (e.g., with finger abduction and wrist articulation), tackling more advanced and bimanual musical pieces, and minimizing whole-hand arm movements for more natural results. These directions point towards the broader impact of this research in pushing the boundaries of robotic dexterity.

While the current system's capabilities are limited to simple songs, this research establishes a foundational framework for future advancements, moving towards robots with higher proficiency and elegance in complex, human-like dexterous tasks.
