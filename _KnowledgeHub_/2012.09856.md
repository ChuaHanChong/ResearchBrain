# Reconstructing Hand-Object Interactions in the Wild

> [!summary] Summary
> RHO is an optimization-based method that reconstructs 3D hands and manipulated objects from single in-the-wild RGB images, fitting MANO hands to 2D keypoints, recovering object pose by differentiable rendering against predicted masks and monocular depth, then jointly optimizing with chamfer-distance and SDF collision terms plus contact-prior refinement. It yields MOW, a 500-image dataset spanning 121 object categories.

## Problem

- Existing 3D hand-object datasets are captured in labs with motion capture or multi-view rigs, covering few participants and at most tens of object instances.
- Feed-forward hand-object networks trained on limited lab object categories fail to generalize to unseen everyday objects in cluttered real-world imagery.
- Prior interaction-optimization methods need extra input such as scanned scene meshes or hand-labeled contact vertex pairs, limiting them to few object categories.

## Method

- **RHO** stage one fits ==MANO== hand parameters initialized by FrankMocap regression to detected 2D keypoints, minimizing a reprojection joint term with a shape regularizer.
- Object pose is optimized by differentiable rendering against a category-agnostic ==PointRend== instance mask plus a new depth loss versus monocular depth, resolving mask-only pose ambiguity.
- Joint optimization adds a hand-object ==chamfer== interaction loss and a voxelized ==signed-distance-field== collision loss, then a network refines hand pose using contact priors learned from **GRAB** MoCap.

## Results

- On HO3D, hand mean joint error falls to **9.7 mm** from **14.7 mm** and object chamfer distance to **19.9** from **26.8** versus the photometric-consistency baseline.
- On FPHA, hand error improves to **14.2 mm** from **18.0 mm**, though object error is slightly worse (**23.9** vs **22.3**) since the baseline trains on those objects.
- Ablation: interaction loss cuts hand-object distance from **414.8** to **71.5 mm** but raises collision to **39.8**; depth, penetration and refinement reduce collision to **6.5**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Segmentation models yield usable instance masks for object categories absent from their label set, so masks can drive pose fitting without correct recognition.
> - Silhouette fitting is pose-ambiguous; adding a monocular depth term supplies the geometric constraint needed to disambiguate object orientation.
> - Proximity losses and interpenetration losses trade off against each other, so plausible contact needs both terms plus a learned contact prior, not proximity alone.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Understanding how humans interact with objects, particularly through hand manipulation, is a foundational problem in computer vision and robotics. This understanding is considered essential for developing intelligent agents that can accurately perceive their environment, infer human intentions, learn object affordances, and acquire complex manipulation skills from human demonstrations.

The rich complexity of hand-object interactions necessitates 3D understanding, a task that is inherently challenging due to the ill-posed nature of reconstructing 3D information from a single 2D RGB image. This difficulty is further amplified in the context of hand-object interactions by several factors: significant mutual occlusion between the hand and the object, the vast diversity of small, everyday objects that are often not represented in standard recognition datasets, and the fine-grained nature of interactions involving complex contacts.

Previous research efforts have made progress in 3D hand pose estimation, 3D object pose estimation, and joint hand-object pose estimation. Approaches for hand pose estimation include direct 3D joint prediction from depth or RGB images, and fitting parametric hand models (e.g., MANO) to 2D keypoint detections. Object pose estimation often involves predicting 6-DoF poses for known 3D object models, or neural networks that generalize to new objects within limited categories. Joint hand-object pose estimation has been explored using multi-view inputs, RGB-D sequences, deep models trained on synthetic data, or networks focusing on egocentric scenarios. However, a common limitation across many of these techniques is their reliance on "in-the-lab" settings or synthetic datasets for training and evaluation.

The existing landscape of 3D hand-object datasets primarily consists of data collected in controlled laboratory environments. These datasets often utilize specialized camera setups, motion capture systems, or thermal sensors to obtain 3D annotations, including hand and object poses, and sometimes contact areas. While valuable, these "in-the-lab" datasets typically exhibit limited diversity in terms of participants, object categories, and interaction scenarios. This creates a significant "reality gap" when attempting to apply these methods to images captured "in-the-wild," where environments, objects, and actions are far more varied and less constrained.

Optimization-based methods have also been proposed for modeling 3D interactions between humans, objects, and scenes. These methods often require additional 3D input, such as reconstructed scene meshes, or extensive manual labeling for fine-grained interaction pairs, limiting their scalability and applicability to diverse, unconstrained scenarios. The challenge remains to develop robust methods capable of performing 3D reconstruction of hand-object interactions from single "in-the-wild" images, without extensive 3D supervision and capable of handling a broad range of unseen objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the understanding of hand-object interactions from unconstrained 2D images. This specifically entails the accurate 3D reconstruction of both the hand and the interacting object. The authors argue that a comprehensive grasp of these interactions necessitates a full 3D representation, which is a challenging task, particularly in "in-the-wild" scenarios.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in the existing research landscape:
1. **Mutual Occlusion:** During hand-object interactions, significant occlusions occur, making it difficult to infer the full 3D geometry and pose of both the hand and the object from a single 2D view. This ambiguity poses a substantial challenge for reconstruction methods.
2. **Object Diversity:** "In-the-wild" images frequently feature a wide array of everyday objects, many of which are not present in existing labeled recognition datasets. Traditional object recognition and pose estimation models, often trained on limited object categories, struggle to generalize to this broad diversity.
3. **Fine-Grained Interactions:** Human manipulation involves intricate, fine-grained contact between the hand and the object. Accurately modeling these complex contact points and regions is crucial for plausible 3D reconstructions but is difficult to achieve without explicit priors.
4. **"Reality Gap" in Datasets:** Current 3D hand-object datasets are predominantly collected in controlled laboratory settings, leading to a restricted diversity of participants, objects, and interaction types. This "in-the-lab" data often fails to represent the richness and complexity of real-world interactions, hindering the development of robust models for "in-the-wild" images. There is a need for high-quality 3D annotated data that reflects real-world variability.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth two key objectives:
1. **Develop a Novel Reconstruction Technique (RHO):** The first objective is to create an optimization-based method, termed RHO (Reconstructing Hands and Objects), that can reconstruct 3D models of both the hand and the object from single "in-the-wild" images. This technique aims to overcome the inherent ambiguities by effectively leveraging 2D image cues (such as hand keypoints and object masks) and incorporating 3D contact priors. A specific innovation for RHO is its ability to handle a broad range of object categories, even those not explicitly labeled in existing recognition datasets, by utilizing instance segmentation masks regardless of predicted category.
2. **Create a Diverse "In-the-Wild" Dataset (MOW):** The second objective is to construct a new 3D dataset, MOW (Manipulating Objects in the Wild), comprising 500 examples of hand-object interactions from "in-the-wild" images. This dataset is "3Dfied" using the RHO technique in conjunction with human intervention. The goal is to provide a resource with a significantly greater diversity of object categories (121 distinct categories) and manipulation actions compared to existing datasets, thereby enabling research into more generalizable hand-object understanding.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on RHO (Reconstructing Hands and Objects), an optimization-based technique designed for 3D reconstruction of hand-object interactions from single "in-the-wild" images. RHO integrates 2D image observations with 3D contact priors through a four-step process, which is also leveraged in a semi-automatic pipeline for dataset creation.

#### 3.1. Hand Pose Estimation

The initial step focuses on reconstructing the 3D hand mesh. This involves:
- **Parametric Hand Model:** The MANO parametric model is used to represent the hand mesh, defined by pose (θ) and shape (β) parameters.
- **Initial Estimation:** An off-the-shelf learning-based method, FrankMocap, is employed to provide an initial estimate of the weak-perspective camera model and the 3D MANO parameters.
- **2D Keypoint Fitting:** The initial hand pose is refined by optimizing the MANO parameters to minimize the difference between projected 3D hand keypoints and 2D hand keypoint detections obtained from OpenPose. This optimization uses a 2D keypoint distance term ($L_{joints}$) and a regularization term ($L_{reg}$) for hand shape. The weak-perspective camera is converted to a perspective camera model for 3D vertex reconstruction.

#### 3.2. Object Pose Estimation

This step recovers the object's 3D scale (s), rotation (R), and translation (T) using an analysis-by-synthesis approach with a differentiable renderer.
- **Object Mask Estimation:** To handle diverse, often unlabeled objects in the wild, the method uses PointRend (trained on COCO) to generate instance masks. Instead of relying on correct category predictions, the instance mask with the highest Intersection over Union (IoU) with a detected hand bounding box (from) is selected as the object of interest. This approach provides masks for a wide range of object categories.
- **Mask Loss ($L_{mask}$):** The L1 difference between the 2D mask rendered from the 3D object model and the estimated 2D object mask.
- **Depth Loss ($L_{depth}$):** A novel loss term introduced to address ambiguities from 2D mask-only fitting. It computes the L1 difference between the rendered depth map of the 3D object model and an estimated depth map from a monocular depth estimation method. This helps constrain the object's geometry and orientation in 3D.
- **Optimization:** The object pose (s, R, T) is optimized by minimizing a combined objective of $L_{mask}$ and $L_{depth}$. Multiple random initializations for object pose are used to mitigate local minima.

#### 3.3. Joint Optimization

To ensure a plausible 3D configuration, the individually estimated hand and object poses are jointly optimized to address issues like excessive distance or interpenetration.
- **Interaction Loss ($L_{dist}$):** This loss pushes the hand and object closer in 3D space by minimizing the Chamfer distance between their respective mesh vertices. This also aids in correcting the object's scale.
- **Collision Loss ($L_{collision}$):** To prevent implausible interpenetration, a collision loss is applied. It penalizes object vertices that are located inside the hand mesh, leveraging a differentiable Signed Distance Field (SDF) of the hand mesh.
- **Joint Objective:** The overall objective function for joint optimization is a weighted sum of the mask loss, depth loss, interaction loss, and collision loss.

#### 3.4. Pose Refinement

The final step refines the hand pose to achieve more realistic hand-object contact, moving beyond simple collision avoidance.
- **3D Contact Priors:** Instead of requiring per-vertex contact annotations, the method learns data-driven 3D contact priors from a large-scale hand MoCap dataset (GRAB). These priors indicate regions of likely object contact.
- **Refinement Network:** A small neural network is trained to refine the hand parameters (θ, β). Its input includes the initial hand parameters and a distance field (F) calculated from hand vertices to their nearest object vertices. The refinement is performed iteratively, with the distance field updated at each step.

#### 3.5. Dataset Construction (MOW)

The RHO technique, combined with human intervention, is utilized to create the MOW dataset.
- **Image Collection:** Static frames from the EPIC Kitchens and 100 Days of Hands datasets are used as sources for "in-the-wild" images, focusing on frames with significant hand-object bounding box overlap.
- **Three-Step Annotation Procedure:**
 1. **Model Selection:** Human annotators select an appropriate 3D object model from an existing collection (e.g., YCB dataset) or source a new one online (e.g., Free3D).
 2. **Reconstruction:** RHO is run semi-automatically, with annotators adjusting loss weights as needed to achieve a reasonable initial reconstruction.
 3. **Verification:** Annotators inspect the 3D reconstruction from multiple viewpoints, either accepting, returning for further adjustment (iterating with Step 2), or discarding the example. This iterative verification ensures annotation quality.
- **Output:** For each of the 500 annotated images, the dataset provides a 3D object model, 3D object pose, and 3D hand pose. Amodal masks and contact maps can be derived from these 3D annotations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative evaluations of the RHO method, along with characteristics and assessments of the MOW dataset.

#### 4.1. RHO Performance Evaluation

- **Quantitative Comparison in the Lab:**
 - RHO was quantitatively compared against a state-of-the-art method on the HO3D and FPHA datasets, which are collected in laboratory settings with 3D ground truth.
 - On HO3D, RHO achieved a mean average error (MAE) of 9.7 mm for hand joints (compared to 14.7 mm for) and a Chamfer distance of 19.9 for objects (compared to 26.8 for).
 - On FPHA, RHO yielded a hand joint MAE of 14.2 mm (compared to 18.0 mm for). The object MAE was slightly higher for RHO (23.9 mm vs. 22.3 mm for). The authors attribute this slight difference to's use of specific action splits for training and testing, whereas RHO was tested without 3D supervision on these datasets. Overall, RHO demonstrated performance that was comparable to or exceeded the state-of-the-art in these controlled environments.
- **Qualitative Comparison in the Wild:**
 - Qualitative comparisons on "in-the-wild" images from indicated that RHO produced more effective hand-object reconstructions than. The competing method struggled with diverse object categories encountered in the wild, likely due to its training on limited object categories in lab settings. RHO demonstrated an ability to generate reconstructions across varied viewpoints, activities, and objects.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of individual loss terms and the pose refinement stage to the overall performance, measured by hand-object distance and collision score (lower is better for both).
- **Individual Reconstruction:** Initially, individually reconstructed hands and objects were distant with no collision.
- **Interaction Loss:** Adding the interaction loss significantly reduced the hand-object distance (from 414.8 mm to 71.5 mm) but introduced a large collision score (39.8), indicating interpenetration.
- **Depth and Collision Losses:** Incorporating the depth loss and collision loss further reduced the collision score to 7.7 while maintaining a similar hand-object distance (76.4 mm).
- **Pose Refinement:** The final pose refinement stage resulted in a minor reduction in both collision score (from 7.7 to 6.5) and hand-object distance (from 76.4 mm to 75.8 mm), suggesting it contributes to more realistic contact.

#### 4.3. MOW Dataset Characteristics and Evaluation

- **Dataset Size and Diversity:** The MOW dataset comprises 500 images, encompassing 121 distinct object categories. It is noted for its greater diversity in manipulation actions, participants, and object categories compared to previous "in-the-lab" datasets (e.g., EPIC Kitchens, HO3D, GRAB).
- **Amodal Mask Accuracy:** To evaluate annotation quality, amodal masks derived from the 3D reconstructions were compared against human-labeled ground truth amodal masks on a sample of 100 images. The mean IoU scores were 0.77 for objects and 0.68 for hands, indicating a high overlap. Accuracy was higher for larger objects.
- **User Study:** A user study on the same 100 images involved users rating the quality of 3D reconstructions on a scale of 1 to 5 and verifying object model match. The average reconstruction quality score was 4.16, suggesting that most annotations were of good quality. Additionally, 92% of the 3D object models were found to match the true object in the image, with minor topological imprecisions accounting for some of the discrepancies in the remaining 8%.

#### 4.4. MOW Dataset Analysis

- **Object Variety:** The dataset exhibits a long-tail distribution of object categories, with some categories having many examples and many others having few, representing a realistic distribution of objects in daily interactions.
- **Grasp Variety:** Isomap embedding of 3D hand poses revealed that the dataset contains a variety of grasp types for each object, rather than only iconic grasps. This was evidenced by the presence of similar grasps across different object categories (e.g., pen and spoon) and distinct grasp types for the same object category (e.g., multiple ways of grasping a pen).
- **Grasp Structure:** Analysis indicated that the first dimension of the hand pose embedding correlated with the degree of hand closure, illustrating a gradient from fully closed to fully open grasps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of computer vision and robotics by addressing the challenges of 3D hand-object interaction understanding in unconstrained environments.

One significant aspect of this work is the **development of RHO**, a reconstruction technique capable of generating 3D models of hands and objects from single "in-the-wild" images. RHO's ability to leverage 2D image cues alongside 3D contact priors offers a method for resolving the ambiguities inherent in monocular 3D reconstruction, particularly in the presence of mutual occlusion. The method's approach to object mask estimation, which extracts segmentation masks for a broad range of object categories without requiring explicit category recognition, contributes to its generalizability across diverse "in-the-wild" scenarios.

The creation of the **MOW dataset** represents another contribution. By providing 3D annotations for 500 "in-the-wild" images, encompassing 121 distinct object categories and a variety of manipulation actions, MOW addresses the "reality gap" that exists with predominantly "in-the-lab" datasets. This dataset can serve as a benchmark for evaluating future hand-object reconstruction methods on more challenging and realistic data.

The analysis conducted on the MOW dataset, particularly concerning **grasp variety and structure**, offers insights into human manipulation behavior. The observation that the dataset contains diverse grasp types for various objects, and that a dimension of hand pose embedding correlates with grasp closure, indicates the potential for MOW to facilitate learning about human manipulation skills directly from real-world imagery.

The semi-automatic annotation pipeline, which combines the RHO technique with human verification, demonstrates a **scalable approach for generating high-quality 3D ground truth** in complex visual settings where full manual annotation would be labor-intensive.

The capability to accurately reconstruct 3D hand-object interactions from ordinary images has potential implications for several downstream applications. These include **enhancing the perceptual abilities of intelligent agents** to infer human intent, learn object affordances, and acquire complex dexterous manipulation skills. It could also contribute to advancements in human-computer interaction, virtual reality, and robotics, where a precise understanding of hand-object relationships is crucial for natural and effective interaction.
