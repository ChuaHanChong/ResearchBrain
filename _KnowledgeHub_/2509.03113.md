# Mitigating Multimodal Hallucinations via Gradient-based Self-Reflection

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NVIDIA, Data61, CSIRO, and ANU present Gradient-based Influence-Aware Constrained Decoding (GACD), an inference-based method that leverages gradient information to dynamically rebalance cross-modal influence and mitigate multimodal hallucinations in MLLMs. The approach effectively reduces co-occurrence and text-visual biases by up to 57% on co-occurrence metrics while improving accuracy and preserving informativeness across various MLLM architectures.

## Problem

- Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) frequently generate 'hallucinations' (factually incorrect or ungrounded content), particularly due to text-visual and co-occurrence biases, limiting their trustworthiness and adoption.
- Existing hallucination mitigation strategies often require expensive retraining, rely on auxiliary models, or lack the granularity to address biases at a token or object level during inference.
- Current inference-based methods provide limited insight into the sample-dependent severity of underlying biases, making targeted adjustments challenging.

## Method

- Proposes ==Gradient-based Influence-Aware Constrained Decoding (GACD)==, an inference-time solution that operates without finetuning or auxiliary models.
- Employs ==first-order Taylor expansion== to compute token-wise ==Jacobians (gradients)== that quantify the influence of individual visual features, prompt tokens, and prior output tokens on the next predicted token's logits.
- Implements ==Influence-Aware Constrained Decoding== by dynamically adjusting output logits, partitioning visual tokens into object-related and unrelated groups to target co-occurrence bias, and adaptively amplifying visual influence to counteract text-visual bias.

## Results

- Achieved significant reductions in hallucination rates, including up to **33%** for sentence-level and **57%** for co-occurrence hallucinations on the AMBER dataset, and up to a **92%** increase in accuracy on the LLaVA-QA90 dataset.
- Maintained or enhanced informativeness; recall dropped by an average of only **1.1%** across models, and detailedness improved by up to **45%** on LLaVA-QA90, demonstrating a balanced performance.
- Demonstrated broad applicability and superior performance across various MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA-v1.5, InstructBLIP, mPLUG-Owl2, Intern-VL2, Qwen2-VL) and benchmarks, consistently outperforming leading state-of-the-art mitigation methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gradients can provide a principled, granular, and sample-dependent measure of individual input token influence on MLLM outputs, enabling a 'self-reflective' understanding of model biases.
> - Multimodal hallucinations can be effectively mitigated at inference time by dynamically rebalancing cross-modal contributions and suppressing spurious visual correlations through adaptive logit adjustments.
> - Partitioning visual tokens based on their relevance to previously mentioned objects allows for targeted reduction of co-occurrence bias, while a dynamic amplification factor helps rebalance visual vs. textual influence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated advanced capabilities in generating coherent and context-aware content across various domains by integrating visual and textual information. However, a significant limitation of these models is their susceptibility to hallucinations, where generated text contains content not grounded in the visual input. This issue poses a barrier to the reliability and trustworthiness of MLLM outputs.

Previous research attributes MLLM hallucinations primarily to two biases:
1. **Text–visual bias:** An over-reliance on textual prompts and prior generated outputs, leading to neglect of visual information, particularly in longer sequences.
2. **Co-occurrence bias:** The generation of non-existent objects based on spurious statistical correlations observed in training data, where frequently paired objects are erroneously predicted together, even when only one is present.

Existing mitigation efforts fall into two main categories:
- **Training-based approaches:** These involve interventions during model optimization, often requiring extensive data collection, bias examination, or model retraining. Examples include methods using reinforcement learning with human or auxiliary model feedback. These are generally computationally expensive and may introduce additional sources of error or hallucination from auxiliary models.
- **Inference-based methods:** These operate at the decoding stage, offering a more cost-effective solution as they do not require model retraining or additional data. However, many existing inference-based methods have limitations:
 - They often provide limited insight into the severity or specific nature of underlying biases.
 - Some rely on auxiliary models (e.g., segmentation networks, object detection systems, or additional MLLMs), which can introduce extra computational overhead and potential errors.
 - Many employ heuristically tuned priors or apply uniform weighting across all visual features, lacking the granularity to adjust biases at the level of individual features, which is particularly critical for addressing co-occurrence bias.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing an inference-based method that provides a granular, sample-dependent bias estimation and mitigation strategy. It seeks to reduce hallucinations without external models, costly finetuning, or uniform weighting, aiming for a more principled and adaptable solution within the current MLLM research landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an inference-based method for mitigating multimodal hallucinations in MLLMs. The motivation for this objective stems from the observed prevalence of hallucinations, which compromise the factual grounding and trustworthiness of MLLM-generated content.

Specific objectives and their underlying motivations include:

1. **Addressing Text–Visual Bias:** MLLMs often exhibit an excessive dependence on textual prompts and previously generated text, leading them to "forget" or disregard visual information. This "text–visual bias" results in outputs that are semantically coherent but visually ungrounded. The motivation is to rebalance the contribution of visual features relative to text tokens during generation, ensuring outputs are more faithful to the visual input.

2. **Mitigating Co-occurrence Bias:** Training data can contain spurious statistical correlations, causing MLLMs to hallucinate objects that frequently co-occur with observed objects, even if the hallucinated object is absent in the current visual input. This "co-occurrence bias" is challenging to address with coarse, image-level interventions. The motivation is to suppress the influence of visual features that are spuriously correlated with an output, preventing the generation of non-existent but statistically linked objects.

3. **Developing a Principled Bias Estimation Mechanism:** Existing inference-based methods often rely on heuristic adjustments or lack a granular understanding of how specific inputs influence outputs. The motivation is to introduce a mathematically grounded approach for estimating the contribution of individual visual features and text tokens to the current output. This is achieved through first-order Taylor gradients, which provide a token-level "influence-awareness" for targeted bias intervention.

4. **Creating a Resource-Efficient and Adaptable Solution:** Many hallucination mitigation techniques require auxiliary models, task-specific supervision, or extensive finetuning, increasing computational cost and complexity. The motivation is to develop an inference-time method that is readily applicable to existing, pre-trained MLLMs without modifications, auxiliary models, or additional data collection. This aims to maximize practicality and broad applicability across diverse MLLM architectures.

5. **Achieving Granular and Sample-Dependent Mitigation:** Uniformly weighting visual features or applying global priors across all samples can be ineffective or lead to trade-offs between accuracy and informativeness. The motivation is to enable fine-grained, sample-specific adjustments to token influences, allowing for adaptive mitigation based on the particular biases observed in each generation step, rather than relying on generalized heuristics. This includes object-aware interventions for co-occurrence bias and dynamic rebalancing for text-visual bias.

In summary, the key motivation is to enhance the factual accuracy and visual grounding of MLLMs by developing a self-reflective, gradient-based decoding strategy that dynamically identifies and mitigates specific biases at a granular token level, all within a computationally efficient inference framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Gradient-based Influence-Aware Constrained Decoding (GACD), is an inference-time approach designed to mitigate multimodal hallucinations by understanding and rebalancing token-level influences without modifying the MLLM's parameters or requiring auxiliary models.

#### 3.1. Background on MLLMs

The paper first establishes the operational context of MLLMs, which generate a sequence of tokens ($y = [y_1, \dots, y_M]$) given a visual input ($t_v = [t_{v_1}, \dots, t_{v_S}]$) and a textual prompt ($t_p = [t_{p_1}, \dots, t_{p_N}]$). At each decoding step $m$, an MLLM with parameters $\theta$ computes a logit vector $z_m = \pi_\theta(t_v, t_p, y_{<m})$, from which the next token $y_m$ is sampled based on a categorical distribution derived from the softmax of $z_m$.

#### 3.2. Gradient-Based Token Influence Estimation

The core of GACD is its ability to estimate the contribution of individual input tokens (visual features, prompt tokens, and prior output tokens) to the current output logits. This is achieved by employing a first-order Taylor expansion of the logit vector $z_m$ around a reference sample point.
- **Taylor Expansion:** The logits $z_m$ are approximated as a linear combination of input tokens and a constant term.
- **Token-wise Jacobians:** The partial derivatives of $z_m$ with respect to each visual token ($t_v$), prompt token ($t_p$), and previous output token ($y_i$) are computed. These Jacobians, denoted as $g_{v_{ms}}$, $g_{p_{mn}}$, and $g_{y_{mi}}$, indicate how a small perturbation in a token's embedding space affects the predicted logit vector.
- **Influence Quantification:** The importance or "influence" of each input token is approximated by the Manhattan norm (L1 norm) of its corresponding Jacobian (e.g., $I_{v_{ms}} = \|g_{v_{ms}}\|_1$). These individual token influences are then aggregated to yield group-level influences for visual tokens ($I_{v_m}$), prompt tokens ($I_{p_m}$), and previous output tokens ($I_{y_m}$). This provides a sample-dependent, token-level measure of bias.

#### 3.3. Influence-Aware Constrained Decoding (GACD)

GACD utilizes these influence estimations within a constrained decoding framework, consisting of two main components:

- **Object-aware Visual Token Grouping:**
 - At each step $m$, nouns are identified in the previously generated sequence ($y_{<m}$) using a standard NLP library (spaCy). Each noun is treated as an "object mention."
 - To link these mentions to visual evidence, the visual token with the *maximal influence* ($I_{v_{is}}$) for each noun $y_i$ is identified.
 - A cumulative object mask ($M_{ms}$) is created, marking visual tokens that were most influential for any noun generated before step $m$.
 - The visual tokens ($t_v$) are then partitioned into two sets via a Hadamard product:
 - **Object-related tokens ($t_o$):** Visual tokens linked to previously mentioned nouns (i.e., those covered by $M_{ms}$).
 - **Unrelated-to-objects tokens ($t_u$):** The remaining visual tokens.
 - This grouping is applied specifically during noun prediction steps, which is where co-occurrence hallucinations are most relevant. For non-noun predictions, all visual tokens are treated uniformly as $t_u$.

- **Anchor-specific Influence-weighted Decoding:**
 - This component extends contrastive decoding by adjusting the next-token logits. It uses the original logits $z_m^* = \pi_{\theta^*}(t_v, t_p, y_{<m})$ and generates "anchor-specific negative guidance logits" $z_{o_m} = \pi_{\theta^*}(t_o, t_p, y_{<m})$. The negative guidance logits are computed considering *only* the object-related visual tokens ($t_o$), prompt, and previous outputs.
 - The adjusted logits are calculated as $\hat{z}_m = (1 + \alpha_m) z_m^* - \alpha_m z_{o_m}$, where $\alpha_m \ge 0$ is a decoding weight.
 - **Mitigation Mechanism:** Increasing $\alpha_m$ emphasizes the contribution of $t_u$ tokens (those unrelated to previously mentioned objects) by increasing the KL divergence between the full model's distribution and the negative guidance distribution.
 - For noun predictions, this suppresses spurious correlations from $t_o$, mitigating co-occurrence bias.
 - For non-noun predictions, where $t_u$ encompasses all visual tokens, this amplifies all visual tokens, addressing text–visual bias.
 - **Dynamic Weight $\alpha_m$:** The value of $\alpha_m$ is chosen to rebalance influences. Specifically, it is set such that the influence of $t_u$ matches the dominant text influence (maximum of $I_{p_m}$ or $I_{y_m}$). This aligns visual contributions with critical text inputs (prompt or previous outputs). A non-negativity constraint is also applied to $\alpha_m$ to ensure the influence of object-related visual and prompt tokens remains positive.

- **Sample-Dependent Early Stopping:**
 - To prevent hallucinations in long generations, a dynamic stopping criterion is introduced. If the visual influence ratio ($r_{v_m} := I_{v_m} / (I_{v_m} + I_{p_m} + I_{y_m})$) falls below a predefined threshold $\epsilon$ when the end-of-sequence (EOS) token is predicted, further generation is stopped. This prevents extending outputs with minimal visual grounding.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

Gradients are obtained directly using PyTorch's `torch.autograd.grad`. Noun tokens are identified using the `spaCy` library. Maximum $\alpha_m$ values are capped, and specific early stopping thresholds $\epsilon$ are empirically set for different MLLMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The GACD method was evaluated across a range of benchmarks and MLLM architectures, demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating hallucinations and enhancing visual grounding.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Generative Tasks:** Evaluated on image captioning (AMBER, MSCOCO) and visual question answering (LLaVA-QA90). Metrics included CHAIR scores (sentence-level hallucination C$_S$, instance-level hallucination C$_I$, recall R, co-occurrence hallucination cog, average length Len), and GPT-4V scores for accuracy (Acc) and detailedness (Det).
- **Discriminative Tasks:** Evaluated on VQA (AMBER discriminative VQA, POPE existence VQA). Metrics included accuracy and F1 score.
- **Baseline MLLMs:** LLaVA-v1.5, LLaVA-v1.6, InstructBLIP, mPLUG-Owl2, Qwen2-VL, and InternVL2.
- **Comparison Methods:** RLAIF-V (alignment-based), VCD, M3ID, AVISC (contrastive decoding methods), and Woodpecker (for POPE).

#### 4.2. Quantitative Results

- **Hallucination Mitigation:** GACD consistently reduced hallucinations across generative tasks.
 - **AMBER Dataset:** Sentence-level (hal) and instance-level (cha) hallucinations were reduced by up to 33% and 32%, respectively. Co-occurrence hallucinations (cog) saw reductions of up to 57%.
 - **MSCOCO Dataset:** Similar reductions were observed, with C$_S$ and C$_I$ decreasing across all tested MLLMs (e.g., C$_S$ for mPLUG-Owl2 reduced from 59.2 to 45.0, C$_I$ from 17.6 to 12.4).
 - **LLaVA-QA90:** Achieved accuracy gains of up to 92% (e.g., LLaVA-v1.5 Acc increased from 3.23 to 6.20), indicating improved alignment with visual inputs.

- **Information Preservation:** Unlike some methods that reduce hallucination at the expense of informativeness, GACD demonstrated information preservation.
 - **Recall (cov/R):** The recall metric, indicating the range of objects retrieved from visual inputs, dropped by an average of only 1.1% across AMBER and MSCOCO, and even increased by 3.1% for mPLUG-Owl2 on AMBER.
 - **Detailedness (Det) on LLaVA-QA90:** Showed increases of up to 45% (e.g., mPLUG-Owl2 Det increased from 4.33 to 6.28), suggesting better retrieval of relevant visual details and reduced visual forgetting.

- **Discriminative Tasks (AMBER, POPE):** GACD achieved a favorable balance between precision and recall, yielding higher F1 scores and improved accuracy.
 - **POPE Dataset:** For mPLUG-Owl2, accuracy improved from 72.5 to 84.2 and F1 from 77.5 to 83.7. Notably, GACD maintained performance on models like InternVL2 where other methods caused degradation.
 - **Category-wise Analysis (AMBER):** F1 scores improved across all question categories, with the most significant gains in "existence," "attributes," and "state" categories, which are strongly tied to visual cues.
 - **MLLM-specific Improvements:** The extent of improvement correlated with the baseline visual influence ratio; models with lower initial visual influence (e.g., LLaVA-v1.5, mPLUG-Owl2) benefited more substantially.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies and Analysis

- **Text-Visual Bias:** GACD effectively increased the overall visual influence to match dominant text levels (Fig. 3a, 4), reducing the "Text-Visual GAP" and leading to higher prediction confidence.
- **Co-occurrence Bias:** Analysis (Fig. 5) showed that GACD reduces hallucinations where spurious objects (e.g., 'table' with 'chair') share the same most influential visual token. For single-object images, GACD reduced hallucination rates and the percentage of shared influential tokens.
- **Component Analysis:**
 - **Visual Amplification (VA):** Significantly reduced hallucinations and improved object recall.
 - **Co-occurrence Hallucination Reduction (CR):** Further mitigated co-occurrence bias, building upon VA's benefits.
 - **Early Stopping (ES):** Shortened outputs in visually ungrounded scenarios, reducing hallucinations with minimal recall trade-off. Each component contributed positively to overall performance.
- **Gradient Computation:** Direct `torch.autograd.grad` was found to be substantially more efficient (e.g., 385ms vs. 20335ms for mPLUG-Owl2 on POPE) than Integrated Gradients while achieving comparable accuracy and F1 scores.
- **Norm Analysis:** The L1 (Manhattan) norm for quantifying token influence consistently yielded the best performance compared to L2 (Euclidean) and L$_{\infty}$ (infinity) norms, supporting its effectiveness in capturing individual token magnitudes.
- **Runtime:** GACD incurred a runtime increase comparable to other decoding-based methods (e.g., +101.44% for LLaVA-v1.5 on POPE), indicating reasonable computational cost.

#### 4.4. Additional Results from Supplementary Material

- **MMBench, MM-Vet, ScienceQA, VizWiz, MME:** GACD showed consistent overall improvements, particularly in "Coarse Perception," "Recognition," and "OCR" related tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving visual recognition. Performance varied for metrics heavily reliant on logical reasoning or generalization capabilities.
- **POPE (various settings):** GACD improved performance across random, popular, and adversarial settings, with more significant gains in adversarial scenarios that demand stronger visual input reliance.
- **Sampling Strategies & Model Size:** GACD improved performance for both greedy and non-greedy sampling, and consistently across different model sizes (7B and 13B), with smaller models showing larger performance boosts.
- **Confidence & Visual Influence:** GACD increased prediction confidence alongside visual influence, effectively mitigating hallucinations even in regions where baseline models exhibited low confidence.
- **Qualitative Examples:** Demonstrated GACD's ability to prevent hallucination in complex scenarios, such as occluded images.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of Multimodal Large Language Models by introducing Gradient-based Influence-Aware Constrained Decoding (GACD), an inference-based method for mitigating hallucinations. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Addressing Core Limitations of Hallucination Mitigation

GACD directly tackles key limitations of previous methods. It operates without the need for auxiliary models, costly finetuning, or additional data statistics, making it a resource-efficient solution. By deriving bias estimation from first-order Taylor gradients, it offers a principled and granular approach to understanding and adjusting token-level influences, a significant improvement over heuristic or uniform weighting methods.

#### 5.2. Enhancing Trustworthiness and Reliability of MLLMs

By effectively reducing both text–visual and co-occurrence biases, GACD improves the factual grounding of MLLM outputs in visual inputs. This directly leads to more trustworthy and reliable content generation, which is crucial for the adoption of MLLMs in sensitive applications. The demonstrated increases in accuracy and detailedness, coupled with reduced hallucination rates across diverse benchmarks, substantiate this claim.

#### 5.3. Practical Applicability and Generalizability

As an inference-time decoding strategy, GACD is readily applicable to existing "white-box" MLLMs without requiring architectural changes or retraining. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated across various modern MLLM architectures (e.g., LLaVA, InstructBLIP, mPLUG-Owl2, InternVL2, Qwen2-VL) and tasks (generative image captioning, VQA, discriminative tasks). This broad compatibility facilitates its integration into current MLLM pipelines.

#### 5.4. Providing Deeper Insights into MLLM Biases

The gradient-based token influence estimation not only enables mitigation but also provides a mechanism for quantitatively assessing bias severity at a sample-dependent, token-specific level. This interpretability can offer valuable insights into how MLLMs process and prioritize multimodal information, fostering a better understanding of their internal mechanisms and failure modes.

#### 5.5. Potential for Future Research

The insights gained from GACD regarding token influence and bias quantification could inform and guide future training strategies for MLLMs. By understanding *why* and *where* models hallucinate, researchers might develop more robust training objectives that inherently promote better visual grounding and reduce biases, moving beyond post-processing interventions.

#### 5.6. Broader Societal Implications

- **Positive Impacts:** Improved factual reliability can make MLLMs more dependable for applications like assistive technologies for the visually impaired (e.g., generating accurate image descriptions), educational tools (e.g., describing complex diagrams), and critical domains such as medical imaging analysis or remote sensing, where spurious outputs can have significant consequences. This could increase user trust and system safety.
- **Ethical Considerations/Limitations:** The method's requirement for gradient access limits its application to white-box MLLMs. Furthermore, while enhancing visual grounding is beneficial, its deployment in applications like surveillance or facial recognition systems could enable more intrusive and potentially privacy-infringing inferences from visual data, which warrants careful consideration regarding ethical deployment. The paper also acknowledges that the method's effectiveness depends on the baseline MLLM's original visual influence ratio and the inherent importance of visual information for a given task (e.g., existence questions benefit more than relational ones).
