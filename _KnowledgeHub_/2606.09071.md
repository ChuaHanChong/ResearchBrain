# REFLECT: Intervention-Supported Error Attribution for Silent Failures in LLM Agent Traces

> [!summary] Summary
> REFLECT is an inference-time error-attribution method for silently failing LLM agent traces. It diagnoses a candidate error step, injects a diagnosis-specific repair plan, and replays the agent from nearby rollback points while preserving the original prefix; a verified outcome flip is fed back to re-localize the step. It reaches the best same-auditor localization accuracy on four benchmarks, e.g. 76.3% exact match on WikiTableQuestions.

## Problem

- Trained step classifiers and LLM judges predict suspect steps without executing anything, so confidently wrong attributions on traces that complete without runtime exceptions go unverified.
- Retry and backtracking methods recover correct answers along independent trajectories, but success on a new path never identifies which step of the original trace was decisive.
- Constraint and anomaly detectors flag suspicious steps yet never test whether repairing a flagged step flips the final outcome, leaving attribution hypotheses stated but untested.

## Method

- Stage 1 Diagnosis: an auditor LLM reads the full trace and returns a provisional earliest-decisive-error step with a confidence score, fixing the prefix preserved during replay.
- Stage 2 Targeted Replay: a Diagnostician emits a structured repair plan (root cause, instruction, forbidden actions, expected tool), injected at multiple rollback points, with a faithfulness gate rejecting off-plan first steps.
- Stage 3 Verified Rollback Attribution: the rollback point whose replay flips the answer becomes the attributed step, and original-versus-corrected continuations contrastively refine the error explanation.

## Results

- Highest same-auditor exact match on all four benchmarks: 76.3% WTQ, 39.0% GAIA, 34.5% BBM, 70.0% SWE-bench, against 55.5/15.7/30.3/33.3 for the all-at-once auditor baseline.
- Correction-localization coupling: explanation-similarity gap between corrected and failed traces reaches +0.25 to +0.29, while ICS and Reflexion show at most +0.03 and +0.05 respectively.
- Ablation: post-correction re-localization adds +10.6pp exact match (65.7% to 76.3%); correct repair hints beat no-hint by +42.0pp correction accuracy and placebo by +32.0pp.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Correction is not attribution: a fix succeeding on an independent trajectory carries no evidence about which original step failed; prefix-preserving replay is what supplies that evidence.
> - Intervention content, not extra context tokens, drives repair - placebo and contradictory hints collapse accuracy, so semantic targeting is the load-bearing part of replay-based diagnosis.
> - The signal depends on structure: gains shrink on pure chain-of-thought traces, and oracle-free verification degrades to 0.56 AUROC where tool calls give no anchors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language model (LLM) agents has enabled them to address increasingly complex tasks through multi-step plan-and-execution traces. Despite advances in task performance, a significant challenge remains in reliably identifying the source of errors within these complex traces, particularly in scenarios termed "silent failures." Silent failures occur when an agent completes a task without overt runtime exceptions (e.g., tool crashes or malformed outputs) but still produces an incorrect final answer. As LLM agents become more capable, silent failures are becoming a predominant mode of failure, as outputs appear syntactically well-formed, but semantically incorrect.

Existing approaches to error localization in agent traces can be broadly categorized. Some methods, such as those employing classifiers or LLM judges (e.g., TRAIL, AgenTracer, TrajAD, ThinkPRM), attempt to predict suspect steps, but often lack execution-based verification, leading to potential hallucinated attributions. Other techniques, including reflection, retry, or backtracking strategies (e.g., Reflexion, ICS), aim to recover correct answers by re-generating parts of the trace. However, these methods typically focus on correction rather than attribution; they may find a correct solution without definitively identifying which original step was responsible for the failure, and often do not feed the outcome of the intervention back to refine the error localization. Furthermore, some systems perform signal checking or anomaly detection (e.g., AgentRx) by flagging suspicious steps but do not verify whether fixing the flagged step causally alters the outcome.

The presented work, REFLECT, positions itself by addressing these identified gaps. It aims to bridge the divide between error correction and error attribution by proposing a method that grounds attribution in execution outcomes, uses prefix-preserving replay, applies targeted diagnosis-specific interventions, and computes attribution at inference time. This approach contrasts with existing methods that either localize without execution grounding, achieve correction without coupling it to attribution, or perform signal checking without causal testing. By integrating intervention outcomes into the re-localization process, REFLECT seeks to provide more faithful and actionable error attributions, moving beyond mere predictions or general critiques. This directly supports the need for greater transparency and trustworthiness in LLM agent deployments, especially for high-stakes applications and development-time debugging.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for REFLECT stems from the observation that while LLM agents can perform complex tasks, their ability to review and reliably localize errors in completed execution traces is limited, particularly in the silent failure regime. This limitation presents significant practical challenges for the deployment and trustworthiness of AI agents:
- **Deployment Efficiency:** If human intervention is consistently required to inspect completed agent work and identify error sources, the productivity gains expected from AI agents are diminished.
- **Trust and Reliability:** For high-stakes tasks, agents cannot be fully trusted unless they can localize errors and explain failures, rather than merely producing plausible (but incorrect) answers or re-attempting tasks without understanding the root cause.
- **Dominance of Silent Failures:** As agents become more sophisticated, failures increasingly manifest as semantically incorrect outputs that appear syntactically well-formed, making manual debugging difficult.

The authors identify three recurring gaps in concurrent work that motivate their proposed solution:
1. **Localization without Execution Grounding:** Existing methods often predict suspect steps using classifiers or LLM judges, but these predictions are not verified through execution, potentially leading to confident but incorrect attributions.
2. **Correction without Attribution Coupling:** While methods exist to recover correct answers (e.g., via retry or backtracking), the success of such corrections is not typically used to identify the specific original step that caused the failure, treating correction as an end rather than evidence for attribution.
3. **Signal Checking without Causal Testing:** Constraint or anomaly detectors can flag suspicious steps, but they do not verify if a fix at the flagged step actually changes the outcome.

To address these limitations, the paper establishes four requirements for faithful error attribution in agent traces:
1. **Execution Grounding (R1):** Attribution must be based on observed execution outcomes, not solely on narrative explanations.
2. **Prefix-Preserving Replay (R2):** Evidence for attribution should come from replaying the same trace, preserving the original prefix, rather than initiating entirely independent retries.
3. **Targeted Intervention (R3):** Replay must be constrained by a specific diagnosis-driven intervention, not merely bare resampling.
4. **Inference-Time Computation (R4):** Attribution should be computed during inference on the test trace, rather than predicted by a pre-trained classifier.

The key objective of REFLECT is to propose a method that instantiates all four of these requirements within a unified pipeline. Specifically, the method aims to localize the earliest decisive error step in a completed, non-crashing trace by integrating diagnosis, targeted intervention via controlled replay, and the use of verified outcome flips as contrastive evidence to refine the final attribution. The goal is to produce "attribution records" that provide intervention-supported evidence for why a particular step caused a failure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

REFLECT (Reflective Fault Localization Engine for Agent Correction) is structured into three sequential stages that operate at inference time on a given test trace, addressing the four identified requirements for faithful error attribution.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup and Attribution Record

An agent trace $\tau$ is defined as a sequence of steps $(u_1, \dots, u_T)$ culminating in a final answer $y$. Each step $u_t$ captures the model's state, action (if any), and observation (if any). The problem assumes a completed trace with an incorrect final answer, without runtime exceptions (silent failure). The objective is to identify the earliest decisive error step $i^*$, defined as the earliest step whose correction enables a verified-correct continuation under replay. An "attribution record" is an operational object $(i^*, \Delta_{i^*})$, consisting of a targeted intervention at $i^*$ and a controlled replay that preserves the prefix $\tau_{<i^*}$ and flips the outcome from incorrect to correct. This record provides intervention-supported evidence for the attribution.

#### 3.2. Stage 1: Diagnosis

This initial stage generates an attribution hypothesis. An auditor LLM (referred to as the localizer) examines the full trace, the task input, the agent's final answer, and, in the primary oracle regime, the expected answer. It then predicts a provisional candidate for the earliest decisive error step, $\hat{i}_0$, along with a confidence score. Free-text reasoning and a suggested fix are also generated. All steps preceding $\hat{i}_0$ are considered the preserved prefix for subsequent replay. This stage focuses on identifying a plausible error location based on the available information.

#### 3.3. Stage 2: Targeted Replay

This stage tests the hypothesis from Stage 1 through controlled, prefix-preserving replay with a diagnosis-specific patch, satisfying R1 (execution grounding), R2 (prefix-preserving replay), and R3 (targeted intervention).
- **Error Classification:** The localized step is mapped to a coarse error family (e.g., formatting_error, hallucination) to guide the subsequent repair plan.
- **Repair Planning:** A "Diagnostician" component generates a structured repair plan $\pi$. This plan includes a root cause diagnosis, a specific correction instruction, actions to avoid (forbidden actions), and an expected next tool. The purpose of $\pi$ is to constrain replay to a targeted test of the hypothesized error, distinguishing it from unconstrained retries.
- **Rollback Selection:** Recognizing that initial localization may have slight inaccuracies, REFLECT replays from a small neighborhood of rollback points around $\hat{i}_0$, rather than just $\hat{i}_0$ itself. This increases robustness to minor localization errors.
- **Repair Loop (Algorithm 1):** In each diagnosis round, REFLECT builds a repair plan, selects rollback points, and reruns the agent from each prefix with the injected guidance (correction context).
 - **Faithfulness Gate:** A critical component, the faithfulness gate checks if the first regenerated step adheres to the repair plan. This can operate in a "soft" mode (logging verdict) or "hard" mode (rejecting unfaithful steps and retrying). This gate operationalizes the distinction between targeted intervention (R3) and uninformative retry; if the agent ignores the plan and succeeds via an unrelated path, it is not considered evidence for the hypothesized error.
 - **Verification:** After replay, a verifier determines if the replayed trace produces the correct answer. In the primary oracle regime, this involves comparing the replayed answer to a known expected answer.

#### 3.4. Stage 3: Verified Rollback Attribution and Explanation Refinement

This stage leverages successful correction outcomes to refine the attribution, thereby closing the loop from correction to attribution.
- **Verified Rollback-Point Attribution:** If a replay yields a correct answer, the rollback point that facilitated this outcome flip is adopted as the final attributed step index ($\hat{i}_{\text{final}}$). This directly grounds the attribution in intervention evidence.
- **Contrastive Explanation Refinement:** The original and corrected continuations from the attributed step are compared. This contrastive evidence is used to sharpen the error explanation, updating the error type, root cause description, and free-text reasoning based on the observed changes between trajectories.
- **Final Attribution Record:** The output is an attribution record ($\hat{i}_{\text{final}}, \Delta_{\hat{i}_{\text{final}}}$), where $\hat{i}_{\text{final}}$ is the re-localized step index, and $\Delta_{\hat{i}_{\text{final}}}$ is the intervention that flipped the outcome. If all repair rounds fail, the record defaults to the best available localization from the iterative diagnosis process.
- **Proxy Verification (Secondary Regime):** For settings without ground-truth answers, a "plan-anchored verifier" replaces the oracle. This verifier synthesizes "task anchors" from the question, audits macro-steps for deviations from these anchors, and estimates correctness without relying on an expected answer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of REFLECT was conducted across four localization benchmarks: WikiTableQuestions (WTQ) for table question answering, GAIA for multi-hop reasoning, BigBenchMistake (BBM) for chain-of-thought verification, and SWE-bench for software engineering. The experiments assessed REFLECT against eight baselines, including prompt-based, correction-based, scoring/constraint, and correction-validation methods.

#### 4.1. RQ1: Localization Accuracy

REFLECT achieved the highest Exact Match (EM) localization accuracy among same-auditor methods on all four benchmarks.
- On WTQ, REFLECT achieved 76.3% EM, compared to 55.5% for the best prompt-based baseline (AAO) using the same GPT-5.2 auditor.
- On SWE-bench, REFLECT showed 70.0% EM, significantly outperforming other baselines.
- Gains were more modest on BBM (34.5% EM), a benchmark with less structured chain-of-thought traces, where intervention signals are inherently weaker.
- Replacing the auditor LLM with Claude Opus 4.6 did not close this gap for tool-use benchmarks, although Opus performed better on BBM.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Correction-Localization Coupling

A key finding was the strong coupling between successful correction and explanation quality, quantified by the "coupling gap" ($\Delta$).
- REFLECT exhibited a substantial explanation quality difference between corrected and failed traces ( $\Delta$ ranges from +0.25 to +0.29). For corrected traces, explanation similarity (Sim-c) ranged from 0.48 to 0.61, while for failed traces (Sim-f), it was 0.23 to 0.32.
- In contrast, baselines like ICS and Reflexion showed much smaller gaps ($\Delta \le$ +0.05), despite achieving non-trivial correction rates. This indicates that for REFLECT, successful correction is accompanied by demonstrably better and more grounded explanations, validating the post-correction re-localization design. This coupling is attributed to the prefix-preserving replay, which provides structurally constrained contrastive evidence.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Intervention Signal Faithfulness

Direct tests of targeted intervention (R3) demonstrated its effectiveness.
- Injecting a correct repair hint in WTQ traces improved correction accuracy by +42.0 percentage points (pp) over a no-hint baseline and +32.0pp over a placebo hint, confirming that the agent responds to the semantic content of the intervention.
- This effect was robust across various wording and positional variants of the hint.
- Intervening at a deliberately wrong step significantly reduced accuracy, confirming that the localization target, not merely the act of intervention, drives correction.
- The correct hint increased the probability of the target tool by +17.3pp at the first resampled step and changed agent behavior immediately in 96.7% of cases.

#### 4.4. RQ4: Performance Without Oracle Verification (Proxy Regime)

In a secondary regime where ground-truth answers were unavailable, REFLECT (proxy) replaced the oracle verifier with a plan-anchored stepwise auditing mechanism.
- The proxy version retained a majority of the oracle's accuracy (e.g., 62.2% EM on WTQ, 66.1% on SWE-bench, 33.3% on GAIA).
- The performance of the plan-anchored verifier varied by benchmark, achieving high AUROC (0.97) on SWE-bench, moderate (0.84) on GAIA, and lower (0.56-0.68) on BBM and WTQ. The verifier performed best on traces with clear tool-call structures.
- Even with imperfect proxy verification, REFLECT (proxy) outperformed most oracle-aided baselines on WTQ and SWE-bench.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

An ablation study on WTQ quantified the contribution of REFLECT's components:
- The "Diagnostician" was the most impactful component for correction, contributing a +27.8pp increase in correction rate.
- "Multi-rollback" (replaying from multiple nearby points) improved localization accuracy by +2.2pp EM.
- The "hard gate" (faithfulness gate) maintained intervention specificity with minimal impact on EM.
- Post-correction re-localization provided a substantial +10.6pp boost in EM (from 65.7% to 76.3%), confirming that the attribution feedback loop, rather than correction alone, is crucial for REFLECT's advantages.
- Localization for verified cases substantially outperformed fallback cases (where correction failed) on GAIA and BBM, demonstrating that intervention evidence leads to stronger attribution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The REFLECT method represents an advancement in the field of LLM agent error localization by systematically closing the loop between error correction and attribution. By combining diagnosis-guided intervention with controlled, prefix-preserving replay, and utilizing verified outcome flips as contrastive evidence, REFLECT moves beyond predictive or correlational approaches to provide execution-grounded and causally supported error attributions.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Enhanced Debugging Capabilities:** REFLECT provides a more reliable and actionable mechanism for identifying the root cause of silent failures in LLM agent traces. This is particularly valuable in development-time debugging scenarios where ground-truth answers are available, allowing developers to understand *why* an agent failed, not just *that* it failed. This can accelerate the development cycle for agent-based systems.
- **Increased Trustworthiness and Explainability:** By providing intervention-supported attribution records, REFLECT contributes to making LLM agents more transparent and trustworthy. The method offers explanations that are grounded in observed changes in execution outcomes, which can be more robust than free-form rationales alone. This aligns with growing regulatory and societal demands for explainable AI.
- **Advancement in Agent Autonomy:** The ability for an agent system to not only correct its mistakes but also pinpoint their origin is a step towards more autonomous and self-improving AI. The quantitative demonstration of coupling between correction and localization in REFLECT, absent in other methods, suggests a deeper understanding of failure mechanisms.
- **Robustness to Silent Failures:** The specific focus on silent failures, which are increasingly prevalent and challenging to diagnose, addresses a critical practical limitation in current LLM agent deployment.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework of identifying and satisfying specific requirements (R1-R4) for faithful attribution provides a structured approach for evaluating and developing future error localization methods. The development of a proxy verification regime also extends the applicability of the method to deployment settings where oracle access is not available, pointing towards practical real-world usage.

Potential impacts include improved reliability of AI agents in various domains (e.g., data analysis, multi-hop reasoning, software engineering), reduced human oversight requirements for routine tasks, and a stronger foundation for building robust, self-diagnosing autonomous systems. However, the paper acknowledges limitations, such as the method's current evaluation on relatively structured silent-failure settings, the reliance on oracle access in the primary regime, the computational cost of re-executing agents, and the focus on single earliest decisive error steps. Addressing these limitations in future work could further broaden REFLECT's applicability and impact.
