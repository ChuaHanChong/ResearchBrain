# PivotRL: High Accuracy Agentic Post-Training at Low Compute Cost

> [!summary] Summary
> PivotRL is a post-training framework for Large Language Models that combines the computational efficiency of Supervised Fine-Tuning with the generalization capabilities of End-to-End Reinforcement Learning for long-horizon agentic tasks. It achieved an average of +14.11 percentage points in-domain accuracy improvement and robustly retained out-of-domain performance, requiring significantly less compute than End-to-End RL.

## Problem

- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is compute-efficient but often leads to "catastrophic forgetting" and poor out-of-domain (OOD) generalization for agentic LLMs.
- End-to-End Reinforcement Learning (E2E RL) maintains OOD capabilities and high in-domain accuracy, but its reliance on extensive multi-turn on-policy rollouts is computationally prohibitive.
- Naive attempts to adapt SFT trajectories for local RL fail due to uninformative turns yielding negligible learning signals and overly strict exact-match reward functions in generative action spaces.

## Method

- ==Offline Turn Selection (Pivot Filtering)==: Identifies informative "pivot" turns from SFT trajectories that exhibit mixed outcomes (positive reward variance) under a reference policy, ensuring strong learning signals.
- ==Verifier-Based Local Reward==: Utilizes a domain-specific verifier to assign binary rewards based on functional correctness rather than strict textual match, allowing credit for functionally equivalent actions.
- ==GRPO-style Optimization==: Employs a ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)==-style objective with group-normalized advantages from single-turn rollouts, augmented by ==KL-regularization== to preserve OOD capabilities and align with the reference policy.

## Results

- PivotRL achieved an average of **+14.11** percentage points in-domain accuracy improvement across four agentic benchmarks, outperforming standard SFT's **+9.94** points.
- It demonstrated robust out-of-domain (OOD) retention with a minimal average change of **+0.21** percentage points across eight OOD benchmarks, in contrast to SFT's severe average OOD regression of **-9.83** points.
- On SWE-Bench, PivotRL achieved comparable accuracy to E2E RL while requiring approximately **4x** fewer rollout turns and **5.5x** less wall-clock time, showcasing substantial computational efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Only groups of rollouts with positive reward variance (mixed success and failure) contribute non-zero group-normalized advantage updates, justifying the pivot filtering strategy.
> - The strength of local learning signals along the KL path is directly proportional to the reward standard deviation, indicating that turns with mixed outcomes provide richer training gradients.
> - Functional reward-based RL, when combined with KL regularization, enables the policy to shift probability mass towards acceptable actions while maintaining the relative ordering of non-acceptable actions, thus mitigating OOD degradation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has progressed to the point where they are increasingly applied to long-horizon agentic tasks. These tasks involve multi-turn interactions with environments, such as conversational tool use, agentic coding, terminal interaction, and web navigation. Post-training LLMs for these capabilities presents a fundamental challenge: achieving both high performance and robust generalization while maintaining computational efficiency.

Existing post-training methodologies, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and End-to-End Reinforcement Learning (E2E RL), exhibit a trade-off. SFT is computationally efficient as it relies on expert demonstrations, but it frequently struggles with generalization, leading to out-of-domain (OOD) degradation, a phenomenon often referred to as catastrophic forgetting. Conversely, E2E RL excels at maintaining OOD capabilities and often achieves higher in-domain accuracy. However, its high computational cost, due to the necessity of numerous multi-turn on-policy rollouts and environmental interactions for each parameter update, limits its scalability.

This work addresses the tension between these two approaches by proposing a novel framework that aims to combine the data efficiency of SFT with the generalization prowess of E2E RL. It situates itself within the evolving landscape of agentic LLMs and training recipes, building on advancements in scalable policy optimization, hierarchical modeling, and verifiable rewards. The paper directly tackles the problem of adapting behavior cloning (SFT) into more effective reinforcement learning paradigms, particularly for multi-turn tool use, without incurring the prohibitive costs of full-trajectory E2E RL. This research contributes to the ongoing efforts to develop LLMs capable of executing complex, real-world workflows more efficiently and reliably.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel post-training framework, named PivotRL, that can simultaneously achieve the compute efficiency of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and the generalization capabilities, particularly Out-of-Domain (OOD) retention, of End-to-End Reinforcement Learning (E2E RL) for long-horizon agentic tasks. The authors explicitly pose the central question: "Can we combine the data efficiency of SFT with the generalization capabilities of E2E RL, achieving both in-domain accuracy and OOD retention without incurring full-trajectory rollouts?"

The motivation stems from the observed limitations of current state-of-the-art post-training methods:

- **SFT's Efficiency vs. Generalization Deficit:** While SFT is compute-efficient, it primarily trains on specific demonstrated completions. This approach frequently leads to "catastrophic forgetting," where the model’s performance degrades significantly when encountering data outside its training distribution (out-of-domain or OOD). This limits its applicability to diverse, real-world agentic scenarios that require robust generalization.
- **E2E RL's Generalization vs. Compute Cost:** E2E RL is effective at preserving OOD capabilities and often yields higher in-domain accuracy by allowing exploration and learning from environmental feedback. However, it necessitates extensive on-policy rollouts, where the model interacts with the environment for many turns to gather experience for each parameter update. This process is computationally intensive and expensive, making it difficult to scale for very large models or complex environments.

The authors attempted a straightforward approach: repurposing SFT trajectories for local RL by sampling actions at intermediate turns and assigning a positive reward only for exact matches with the SFT demonstration. However, preliminary experiments revealed that this naive strategy failed to improve OOD accuracy over standard SFT. This failure was traced to two critical bottlenecks, which directly motivated the design of PivotRL:

1. **Negligible Learning Signals from Uninformative Turns:** When randomly selected intermediate turns are used for rollouts, the sampled actions often result in uniformly successful or uniformly failed outcomes. Under group-normalized RL objectives (like GRPO), such uniform outcomes lead to a normalized advantage close to zero, effectively providing no meaningful gradient update to the policy. The authors empirically observed that 71% of randomly sampled turns yielded a zero learning signal.
2. **Overly Strict Exact-Match Rewards:** In generative action spaces, such as those involving tool calls, shell commands, or search queries, many actions can be functionally equivalent or locally acceptable even if they do not precisely match the single demonstrated string in the SFT data. Relying solely on exact string matching for rewards leads to the erroneous discarding of functionally correct rollouts, thereby hindering effective learning.

PivotRL was designed to overcome these two bottlenecks by introducing mechanisms that filter for informative training signals and assign more flexible rewards.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PivotRL is designed as a novel framework for long-horizon agentic Reinforcement Learning (RL) that operates on existing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) trajectories. Its methodology comprises three core steps: offline turn selection for informative pivots, sampling local on-policy rollouts, and optimizing a verifier-based Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)-style objective.

**Overall Framework:**
The approach begins by extracting all assistant turns from expert interaction trajectories (`𝒟sft`) to create a `pivot candidate dataset (𝒟cand)`. Each entry in `𝒟cand` consists of a state `st` (the interaction history up to an assistant action) and the demonstrated assistant action `a*t`. The training paradigm is "turn-level," meaning an action is a complete assistant completion at a model-call boundary (e.g., a tool call, a bash command, a search step), not an individual token.

**1. Offline Turn Selection (Pivot Filtering):**
To address the bottleneck of uninformative turns, PivotRL first profiles the `𝒟cand` dataset offline under a frozen reference policy `𝜋0` (typically the policy used to initialize PivotRL).
- For each candidate state `s`, `K` local rollouts are sampled from `𝜋0(· | s)`.
- A domain-appropriate "verifier" (described below) scores these sampled actions, producing binary rewards `rfunc(s, a(k))`.
- The empirical reward mean `^𝜇(s)` and variance `^𝜎^2(s)` are computed from these `K` rewards.
- Only turns that are likely to provide strong learning signals are retained, forming the `𝒟pivot` (or `𝒟adv`) dataset. Specifically, a turn `(s, a*)` is included if:
 - `^𝜎^2(s) > 0`: The sampled actions exhibit mixed outcomes (some successes, some failures), indicating the turn is neither uniformly easy nor uniformly impossible under `𝜋0`. This ensures a non-zero advantage signal for group-normalized RL.
 - `^𝜇(s) < 𝜆diff`: The average reward is below a certain difficulty threshold `𝜆diff`, focusing training on states that are still challenging for the reference policy.

**2. Verifier-Based Local Reward:**
To overcome the limitations of strict exact-match rewards, PivotRL employs a domain-specific verifier to assign rewards.
- For a retained state `s`, `ℳ(s)` denotes the set of locally acceptable actions.
- The functional reward `rfunc(s, a)` is assigned as `1` if action `a` is in `ℳ(s)`, and `0` otherwise.
- The verifier's implementation varies by domain: it could be a normalized string/schema check, a task-specific equivalence rule, or a lightweight LLM judge. This allows functionally equivalent actions, even if textually different from the demonstration, to receive credit.

**3. Optimization Objective (GRPO-style):**
During training, PivotRL samples mini-batches of states `sb` from the `𝒟pivot` set.
- For each `sb`, `G` actions (`ab,i`) are sampled from the current policy `𝜋𝜃_old(· | sb)`.
- A short, single-turn rollout is executed for each sampled action to obtain its functional reward `rb,i = rfunc(sb, ab,i)`.
- The advantage `^Ab,i` for each action is computed using group-normalized rewards: `^Ab,i = (rb,i - (1/G)∑j=1^G r_b,j) / (std({rb,j}) + 𝜖std)`. This normalization is crucial, as Proposition 3.1 demonstrates that only groups with positive reward variance (i.e., mixed outcomes) yield non-zero advantages.
- The policy `𝜃` is updated using a GRPO-style objective `𝒥PivotRL(𝜃)`, which minimizes the standard PPO/GRPO clipped objective with the calculated advantages, augmented by a KL-regularization term (`𝐷KL = 𝛽KL(𝜋𝜃(· | s) ‖ 𝜋0(· | s))`) that constrains the new policy to stay close to the initial reference policy `𝜋0`. This regularization helps preserve OOD capabilities.

**Theoretical Justification:**
The paper provides theoretical results supporting its design choices:
- **Proposition 3.1:** Confirms that only rollout groups with positive reward variance (i.e., mixed success/failure outcomes) can contribute a non-zero group-normalized update. This directly justifies the pivot filtering strategy.
- **Theorem 3.2:** Shows that the Fisher norm of the natural gradient of the state-wise reward objective scales directly with the reward standard deviation. This implies that turns with larger reward variance induce stronger local learning signals along the KL path, further validating the focus on mixed-outcome pivots.
- **Theorem 3.3:** Demonstrates that functional reward-based RL (with KL regularization) shifts probability mass towards acceptable actions while preserving the conditional distributions (and thus relative ordering) of actions both within and outside the set of acceptable actions. This theoretical property explains how PivotRL mitigates OOD degradation by maintaining the reference policy's ranking of task-unrelated actions.

In summary, PivotRL combines an intelligent offline filtering strategy to identify informative training turns with a flexible, verifier-based reward mechanism, all within a computationally efficient, turn-level GRPO framework. This allows it to leverage SFT data effectively for RL while mitigating the high compute cost and OOD degradation issues of prior methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across four diverse agentic domains demonstrate the effectiveness of PivotRL relative to existing methods, particularly in terms of in-domain accuracy, out-of-domain (OOD) retention, and computational efficiency.

**In-Domain Accuracy:**
- PivotRL consistently yielded larger in-domain improvements compared to standard SFT when trained on identical data (same prompts and expert trajectories).
- Across conversational tool use (𝜏2-Bench), agentic coding (SWE-Bench Verified), terminal tool use (Terminal-Bench), and web search (BrowseComp) domains, PivotRL achieved an average in-domain accuracy improvement of +14.11 percentage points over the base model. This significantly surpasses the +9.94 points attained by SFT.
- Specifically, PivotRL outperformed SFT on 𝜏2-Bench (+5.37 points), Terminal-Bench (+6.25 points), and BrowseComp (+9.80 points). While SFT showed a higher score on SWE-Bench Verified (37.40 vs. 32.67), the subsequent comparison to E2E RL on SWE-Bench clarifies PivotRL's competitive standing regarding efficiency.

**Out-of-Domain (OOD) Retention:**
- A critical finding is PivotRL’s robust OOD retention, contrasting sharply with SFT’s OOD degradation.
- Across eight OOD benchmarks (IFBench, AIME25, MATH500, LiveCodeBench, Scicode, MMLU-Pro, MMLU-ProX, WMT24++), PivotRL introduced a minimal average change of +0.21 percentage points relative to the base model. No single benchmark dropped by more than -3.12 points.
- In contrast, SFT suffered a severe average OOD regression of -9.83 percentage points. The most dramatic regressions occurred after terminal-domain training with SFT, where AIME25 dropped from 86.04 to 21.56 (-64.48 points), and MATH500 dropped by -34.50 points. This highlights PivotRL's success in mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

**Compute Efficiency vs. End-to-End RL on SWE-Bench:**
- On SWE-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating long-horizon agentic capabilities, PivotRL demonstrated competitive accuracy with E2E RL, a standard training paradigm for software engineering agents.
- To reach comparable accuracy, PivotRL required approximately 4 times fewer rollout turns and 5.5 times less wall-clock time than E2E RL, using the same number of compute nodes. This indicates a substantial improvement in computational efficiency for achieving high performance in complex agentic tasks.

**Ablation Study:**
- An ablation study conducted on 𝜏2-Bench confirmed that both key mechanisms of PivotRL—pivot filtering and functional rewards—are necessary for its performance gains.
- Removing pivot filtering (training on all pivot candidates, `𝒟cand`, with functional rewards) reduced accuracy from 63.81% to 59.68%.
- Further removing functional rewards (training on `𝒟cand` with strict exact-match rewards) resulted in an accuracy of 57.34%, which is even lower than the same-data SFT baseline of 58.44%.
- The analysis of training dynamics showed that pivot filtering (`𝒟adv`) effectively preserved higher per-batch reward variance deeper into training, leading to more informative local updates and better optimization compared to random sampling (`𝒟cand`).

**Production-Scale Integration:**
- PivotRL has been adopted by NVIDIA’s Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B, serving as a primary workhorse for agentic post-training in production.
- Table 5 illustrates that Nemotron-3-Super, after an RL stage that included PivotRL environments, showed significant accuracy improvements on agentic benchmarks (e.g., 𝜏2-Bench from 48.00 to 64.00, SWE-Bench Verified from 12.87 to 61.33).

In summary, PivotRL demonstrates a method for achieving both high in-domain accuracy and strong OOD retention for agentic LLMs, while significantly reducing the computational cost typically associated with effective reinforcement learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of PivotRL represents a notable advancement in the post-training of Large Language Models (LLMs) for long-horizon agentic tasks, offering a resolution to the previously observed tension between computational efficiency and generalization capabilities.

**Significance:**
- **Balancing Efficiency and Generalization:** PivotRL successfully bridges the gap between compute-efficient supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and generalization-robust end-to-end reinforcement learning (E2E RL). It provides a framework that enhances in-domain performance while robustly retaining out-of-domain (OOD) capabilities, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting, a common challenge in LLM fine-tuning.
- **Computational Cost Reduction:** The method demonstrates that high-accuracy agentic post-training can be achieved with significantly reduced computational resources. By requiring approximately 4 times fewer rollout turns and 5.5 times less wall-clock time compared to E2E RL for comparable accuracy on benchmarks like SWE-Bench, PivotRL makes advanced agentic training more scalable and economically viable.
- **Theoretical Foundation:** The paper offers theoretical justifications for its core mechanisms. Proposition 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 explain why filtering for mixed-outcome "pivots" maximizes learning signals, while Theorem 3.3 clarifies how functional rewards enable conservative policy updates that preserve OOD performance. This theoretical grounding provides a deeper understanding of why PivotRL is effective.
- **Improved Action Space Coverage:** By utilizing on-policy rollouts and verifier-derived rewards, PivotRL achieves better coverage of the action space. This allows the model to learn from functionally correct actions that might not exactly match a single expert demonstration, leading to more robust and versatile agent behaviors.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Democratization of Advanced Agentic Training:** The reduction in computational cost could make sophisticated agentic LLM training accessible to a broader range of researchers and developers who may not have access to the extensive resources typically required for E2E RL. This could accelerate innovation in agentic AI.
- **Development of More Capable AI Agents:** By facilitating the training of LLMs that are both accurate in specific tasks and generalizable to unforeseen situations, PivotRL could lead to the development of more robust, reliable, and intelligent AI agents. These agents could perform complex, real-world workflows more effectively in domains such as software engineering, conversational tool use, and web navigation.
- **Enhanced Production-Scale LLMs:** The adoption of PivotRL by NVIDIA for its Nemotron-3-Super LLM in production-scale agentic post-training underscores its practical utility and impact. This demonstrates its ability to improve the performance of cutting-edge LLMs in real-world deployment scenarios.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future research, including exploring more advanced non-programmatic verifiers (e.g., LLM-as-a-judge frameworks, process reward models) and investigating online reward profiling approaches. These directions could further enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of agentic LLM training.

In conclusion, PivotRL presents a significant contribution to the field of LLM post-training by providing a compute-efficient yet highly accurate and generalizable method for developing agentic capabilities, with implications for both research and practical applications in AI.
