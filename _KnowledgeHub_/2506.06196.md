# Bridging Perception and Action: Spatially-Grounded Mid-Level Representations for Robot Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University and Google DeepMind developed an approach using spatially-grounded mid-level representations to enhance robot generalization for dexterous bimanual manipulation tasks, achieving a 69.6% average success rate across diverse challenges. This method integrates object-centric, motion-centric, pose-aware, and depth-aware information, improving performance by 24.5% over baseline diffusion policies and demonstrating zero-shot generalization to novel objects.

## Problem

- Current robot learning models struggle with fine-grained generalization to subtle real-world variations for dexterous bimanual tasks.
- High-level semantic or visual conditioning lacks the precise spatial and contextual details required for complex, contact-rich manipulation.
- No single mid-level representation is universally optimal, as different tasks require distinct forms of spatial grounding.

## Method

- Explicitly incorporates four types of spatially-grounded mid-level representations: object-centric, motion-centric, depth-aware, and pose-aware.
- Develops a **Mid-Level MoE** ==diffusion policy== architecture that uses ==attention-based gating== and ==cross-attention== to intelligently integrate these diverse expert-generated representations.
- Implements a two-stage training strategy, featuring a novel =="Self-Consistency" weighted imitation learning algorithm== to enhance precise utilization of the mid-level signals.

## Results

- The Mid-Level MoE policy achieved a **69.6%** average success rate across eight dexterous bimanual tasks, outperforming baseline diffusion policies by **24.5%**.
- The Mid-Level MoE architecture demonstrated superior robustness (Robustness Index of **0.86**) and effective multi-representation leveraging compared to simpler fusion methods.
- The proposed self-consistency training method improved real-world task success rates by an additional **10%** for Mid-Level MoE, enabling more precise utilization of mid-level features.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spatially-grounded mid-level representations provide actionable detail that effectively bridges high-level perception and low-level robot actions for dexterous tasks.
> - The utility of specific mid-level representations is highly task-dependent, and the Mid-Level MoE architecture offers a robust way to integrate this diverse spatial information, balancing sensitivity to relevant signals with robustness to noise.
> - The self-consistency training method enhances a policy's precision in utilizing mid-level features, improving overall performance for complex tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has made significant strides towards achieving generalized robotic capabilities, primarily by leveraging large-scale pre-training datasets and foundational models. Recent advancements, particularly in vision-language-action (VLA) models, have shown promise in enabling robots to understand and execute tasks based on high-level semantic and visual knowledge. However, as the authors highlight, these models still face substantial challenges in adapting to subtle real-world variations—such as changes in object spatial locations, the introduction of unseen objects, or different lighting conditions—especially for tasks requiring precise, dexterous, and bimanual manipulation.

Existing approaches to robotic generalization can broadly be categorized:

- **Scaling Data and Models:** Many efforts focus on increasing the breadth and diversity of robot data (e.g., RoboNet, Bridge Data, DROID, RoboCasa, Open X-Embodiment) to train increasingly large, generalist policies. While effective for broader task coverage, this approach is often prohibitively expensive for data collection and does not inherently guarantee robustness to fine-grained scene variations or the precision required for dexterous interactions.
- **High-Level Conditioning and Hierarchical Learning:** Another popular direction involves conditioning robot policies on higher-level representations. This includes goal images, video demonstrations, or natural language instructions (e.g., PaLM-E, Code as Policies, RT-H). These methods aim to provide abstract patterns and relationships, enhancing interpretability and controllability. However, the authors argue that the generalization properties enabled by these high-level forms of grounding are often insufficient for complex, contact-rich, or dexterous tasks. For instance, a language command or a simple bounding box might indicate *what* to do or *where* an object is generally located, but fails to provide the granular, actionable information about object geometry, orientation, or affordances necessary for precise physical manipulation. They are often "disconnected from the physics of a robot's interaction with the world."

This paper identifies a critical gap: the lack of representations that effectively *bridge the gap between high-level perception and low-level action*, providing both abstract understanding and precise, actionable detail. The authors hypothesize that the choice of mid-level representations is highly task-dependent, and that generalist policies require a nuanced balance between abstraction and specificity. For example, depth information is crucial for contact-rich tasks like handing over objects, while pose awareness is vital for assembly.

The contribution of "Bridging Perception and Action" lies in its systematic investigation of *spatially-grounded mid-level representations* that directly address this gap. By proposing and evaluating representations along dimensions like object-centricity, motion-centricity, pose-awareness, and depth-awareness, the work demonstrates how explicit spatial grounding can enhance adaptability and generalization for dexterous bimanual tasks. Furthermore, the paper introduces a novel architecture (Mid-Level MoE) and a training methodology (self-consistency) that intelligently combine and utilize these diverse representations, offering a more robust and precise approach to robot control than previous high-level grounding techniques. This research pushes the boundaries of robot generalization by focusing on the quality and specificity of intermediate representations, rather than solely on scale, thereby making significant strides towards enabling robots to perform complex tasks reliably in varied real-world environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of this research is to investigate how **spatially-grounded auxiliary representations** can significantly enhance the performance and generalization capabilities of robot policies, particularly for challenging dexterous bimanual manipulation tasks. The authors aim to demonstrate that these "mid-level representations" can provide a crucial link, offering both broad perceptual grounding and direct, actionable information to guide robot actions.

The motivation behind this work stems from several limitations observed in current robot learning paradigms:

- **Lack of Fine-Grained Generalization:** Despite significant advancements in large pre-trained robot models, they often struggle with adapting to subtle variations in real-world scenes, such as new object instances, slightly different spatial arrangements, or varying lighting conditions. This deficiency is particularly pronounced in dexterous tasks that demand high precision and intricate object interactions.
- **Inadequacy of High-Level Grounding for Dexterity:** Existing methods that use high-level conditioning, such as language commands or basic object bounding boxes, provide semantic understanding but lack the precise spatial and contextual details necessary for complex manipulation. A robot folding a shirt needs more than just a bounding box; it requires actionable information about folds, contact points, and orientations. Similarly, a simple "move arm" instruction (as in some language baselines) is too abstract for fine motor control.
- **Task-Dependent Representation Needs:** The authors hypothesize that no single type of representation is universally optimal across all tasks. Different tasks necessitate different forms of spatial grounding. For example, a task involving precise insertion might benefit from pose-aware or depth-aware information, while arranging objects in a cluttered scene might primarily leverage object-centric understanding. This highlights the need for a versatile system that can leverage multiple types of representations.
- **The Sensitivity-Robustness Tradeoff:** A key motivational aspect is the realization that policies relying on structured mid-level signals face a fundamental tradeoff: they need to be sensitive enough to adhere to accurate representations but also robust enough to handle noise or errors inherent in real-world perception systems. An overly sensitive policy might fail if a representation is slightly off, while an overly robust one might ignore valuable guidance. Addressing this balance is crucial for reliable deployment.

In essence, the authors seek to answer fundamental questions about the nature of robot grounding: What specific mid-level abstractions enable robust adaptation? How can multiple, task-specific representations be effectively integrated into a generalist policy? And how can a policy be trained to precisely utilize these representations while remaining resilient to their imperfections? By addressing these questions, the paper aims to pave the way for more capable, adaptable, and generalizable robotic systems for complex manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology focuses on explicitly incorporating spatially-grounded mid-level representations into a diffusion policy architecture to enhance generalization for dexterous bimanual tasks. The approach is structured around identifying key representation types, designing a specialized policy architecture to utilize them, and developing a training strategy that balances adherence to these representations with robustness to noise.

**A. Spatially-Grounded Mid-Level Representations**

The authors systematically investigate representations across four critical dimensions, each providing a specific type of grounding for robot policies:

1. **Object-Centric Representations:** These experts extract information about objects in the scene, such as their poses, sizes, shapes, and potential interaction points (e.g., bounding boxes, keypoints, grasp plans).
2. **Motion-Centric Representations:** These capture the dynamic aspects, focusing on the future motion of the robot or objects (e.g., 2D trajectory traces of the robot arm).
3. **Depth-Aware Representations:** These leverage 3D depth information to infer spatial relationships and physical constraints, crucial for contact-rich tasks (e.g., depth-aware trajectory traces).
4. **Pose-Aware Representations:** These encode relative and absolute spatial orientations of objects and the robot, vital for precise manipulation and alignment (e.g., pose-aware grasp plans, pose-aware trajectory traces).

For the implementation, these representations are generated by "specialist encoders." For object-centric bounding boxes, an off-the-shelf OWL-ViT model is used. For more dexterous tasks, custom ResNet34 models are retrained on relabeled data (from demonstrations and proprioception) to generate pose-aware representations on top of bounding boxes, or motion/depth-aware trajectory traces.

**B. Policy Architecture: Mid-Level MoE**

The core policy is built upon a diffusion policy framework, similar to ALOHA Unleashed, which directly outputs 12 absolute joint positions (6 for each arm) and 2 continuous gripper values. The policy takes 4 input images from different viewpoints (2 third-person, 2 wrist) fed through separate ResNet50 encoders, which are then processed by a transformer encoder to obtain image embeddings.

The novel aspect lies in how the mid-level representations are integrated:

- **Diverse Mid-Level Experts:** The architecture employs four distinct mid-level experts, each generating representations corresponding to the four identified axes of grounding (object-centric, motion-centric, pose-aware, depth-aware).
- **Attention-based Mid-Level Gating:** To combine these diverse expert outputs, the embeddings from each expert (R_i) are processed into a multi-dimensional tensor `z` using multi-headed attention (`z = MultiHead(R1, R2, R3, R4)`). This gating mechanism dynamically weights the contribution of each expert based on the current state, allowing the policy to prioritize relevant information.
- **Cross-Attention Mechanism:** The combined mid-level embeddings (`z`) are then fed into the transformer decoder via cross-attention with the visual (image) embeddings. This allows the policy to further refine its emphasis on specific representations based on visual and contextual information, enhancing both sensitivity to pertinent details and robustness to noisy or irrelevant signals.

**C. Training Strategy: Two-Stage Approach and Self-Consistency**

The training proceeds in two stages:

1. **Expert Training:** The specialist mid-level representation encoders are trained independently using supervised learning on data relabeled from robot demonstrations and proprioception. Once trained, their parameters are frozen.
2. **Policy Network Training:** The diffusion policy network is then trained end-to-end. Crucially, the authors introduce a novel concept called **Self-Consistency** to enhance the policy's ability to precisely follow the guidance from these mid-level representations.

 - **Motivation for Self-Consistency:** Direct behavioral cloning (BC) can lead to inconsistencies when mid-level predictions are noisy. The policy needs to learn *which* expert signals are reliable and *how closely* to adhere to them.
 - **Weighted Imitation Learning:** Self-consistency is implemented as a weighted imitation learning algorithm. The standard BC loss is modulated by "self-consistency weights" `w(x)`. The loss function becomes: `L_policy = E[(x,a)~D] [w(x) * L_BC(π(a|x),a)]`.
 - **Weight Calculation:** The weights `w(x)` reflect how well the policy's *achieved trajectory* (`τ`) aligns with the *mid-level expert output* (`E(s)`). Lower "adherence" (i.e., closer alignment) leads to higher weights. This effectively upweights demonstrations where the policy's actions are consistent with the "desired future behavior" encoded by the mid-level representations. The adherence metrics are specifically designed for each representation type (e.g., Euclidean distance for bounding boxes, combined position/orientation error for grasp plans, spatial/depth error for trajectory traces).
 - **Analogy to RL Advantage:** The authors draw an analogy to reinforcement learning, where `w(x)` acts like an `exp(R(s,a))` term, with `R(s,a)` being a "pseudo-reward" derived from the negative adherence error. This incentivizes the policy to generate actions that closely match the expert-generated mid-level plans. The `λ_E` coefficients for each expert's adherence metric are set inversely proportional to the maximum adherence error, ensuring balanced contributions.

**D. Experimental Setup**

The methodology is evaluated on eight diverse dexterous bimanual manipulation tasks: four in the ALOHA Unleashed simulation environment (Single Insertion, FMB Assembly, Fruit Bowl, Pen Handover) and four in a real-world setup with two 6-DoF ViperX robot arms (Kitchen Stack, Cup Stacking, Shirt Hanging, Shoelace Tying). Generalization is tested by varying object locations, adding distractors, and changing lighting conditions. Ablations are conducted against baselines like standard diffusion policy (no representations), language-conditioned policies (RT-H-like), and keypoint-based policies (MOKA-like), as well as different architectural choices (Early Fusion, Concatenation) and the presence/absence of self-consistency. Key metrics include task success rate, Sensitivity Score (SS) to quantify adherence to representations, and Robustness Index (RI) to measure performance under perturbed representations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments presented in the paper yield several significant findings regarding the effectiveness of spatially-grounded mid-level representations for robot generalization:

- **Overall Performance Superiority:** The proposed Mid-Level MoE policy consistently outperforms various baselines. It achieved an impressive average success rate of **69.6%** across all evaluation tasks. This is a substantial improvement over the standard diffusion policy (no representations) baseline at **45.1%** (a 24.5% increase), the language-grounded baseline (similar to RT-H) at **51.5%** (an 18.1% increase), and the keypoints-based baseline (similar to MOKA) at **58.0%** (an 11.6% increase). This highlights the benefit of more granular spatial grounding compared to higher-level semantic representations for dexterous tasks.

- **Task-Specific Benefits of Mid-Level Representations:** The ablation studies clearly demonstrate that the utility of specific mid-level representations is highly task-dependent.
 - **Motion-centric representations** (trajectory traces) were most effective for tasks like Single Insertion and Cup Stack.
 - **Object-centric representations** (bounding boxes) excelled in Fruit Bowl and Kitchen Stack.
 - **Pose-aware representations** (pose plans) showed superior performance in FMB Assembly and Shirt Hanging.
 - **Depth-aware representations** (depth-aware trajectory traces) were crucial for Pen Handover and Shoelace Tying, tasks requiring precise 3D understanding and contact.
 These findings align with the intuitive requirements of each task, emphasizing that a "one-size-fits-all" representation is suboptimal.

- **Mid-Level MoE Effectively Leverages Multiple Representations:** While individual representations show task-specific advantages, the Mid-Level MoE architecture successfully combines these benefits. Although it may not always outperform the *single best* representation for a specific task (e.g., slightly lower than bounding box for Kitchen Stack), it consistently achieves high performance across the *entire range* of diverse tasks. This demonstrates the architecture's ability to intelligently gate and combine information from multiple experts to achieve broad generalization.

- **Architectural Tradeoffs between Sensitivity and Robustness:** The comparison of different policy architectures (Mid-Level MoE, Early Fusion, Concatenation) revealed crucial insights into the sensitivity-robustness tradeoff:
 - Mid-Level MoE outperformed Early Fusion and Concatenation by an average of 11% and 7.5% respectively.
 - This superior performance correlates with Mid-Level MoE's lower average **Sensitivity Score (SS)** (0.2175), indicating a more focused adherence to *relevant* mid-level representations.
 - Furthermore, Mid-Level MoE exhibited a higher **Robustness Index (RI)** (0.86) compared to concatenation (0.75), suggesting better resilience to perturbations or noise in the representations. The combination of lower sensitivity to irrelevant signals and higher robustness to noisy ones contributes to its overall better and more reliable performance.

- **Self-Consistency Enhances Sensitivity and Performance:** Implementing the proposed weighted imitation learning (self-consistency) significantly improved the policy's ability to leverage mid-level features:
 - Policies trained with self-consistency showed a **25.5% lower average Sensitivity Score** compared to unweighted approaches, indicating a more precise and targeted utilization of pertinent features.
 - For the Mid-Level MoE architecture, this translated to an additional **10% higher success rate** on average across real-world tasks, suggesting that the benefits of more precise feature utilization outweigh the associated slight decrease in robustness.
 - However, a crucial finding was the tradeoff: weighted imitation learning also led to a 9% lower average robustness index. Notably, for less robust architectures like concatenation, self-consistency could *decrease* performance (e.g., a 26% drop for Weighted Concatenation) if the base architecture struggled with robustness to noise in the first place. This underscores the importance of a robust underlying architecture for self-consistency to be beneficial.

- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** The paper also demonstrated the zero-shot generalization capabilities of the method. Without any additional fine-tuning, the policy successfully stacked novel, unseen cups with an **85% success rate** and performed a pen handover with an unseen marker with a **90% success rate**. This suggests that the spatially-grounded mid-level representations capture sufficient abstract geometry and semantic information to handle significant variations in object instances.

In summary, the results strongly validate the paper's central hypothesis: explicitly incorporating spatially-grounded mid-level representations, integrated via a sophisticated Mixture-of-Experts architecture and trained with self-consistency, is highly effective for improving generalization and precision in dexterous bimanual robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the quest for more general-purpose and dexterous robotic manipulation. Its significance and potential impact can be seen across several dimensions:

- **Advancing Robot Generalization for Dexterous Tasks:** The paper directly addresses a critical limitation of current robot learning methods: their struggle with fine-grained adaptation and generalization for complex, contact-rich, and bimanual tasks. By systematically investigating and demonstrating the benefits of spatially-grounded mid-level representations, the authors provide a powerful framework for building robots that can operate reliably in diverse and unstructured environments. The substantial performance gains (up to 24% success rate improvement) on challenging real-world tasks underscore this impact.

- **Bridging the Perception-Action Gap with Actionable Grounding:** The core contribution lies in bridging the conceptual gap between high-level perceptual understanding (e.g., object recognition, semantic context) and low-level robot actions. Unlike purely semantic or coarse spatial cues, the proposed mid-level representations provide *actionable* detail—information about object geometry, pose, motion, and depth that is directly relevant to generating precise motor commands. This structured approach makes robot policies more interpretable and controllable, as errors can be traced back to specific representation failures or adherence issues.

- **Highlighting the Importance of "What to Ground":** The systematic analysis of different representation dimensions (object-centric, motion-centric, pose-aware, depth-aware) is crucial. It empirically demonstrates that the *type* of grounding matters significantly and is highly task-dependent. This insight can guide future research in designing task-specific or context-aware representation learning pipelines, moving beyond monolithic, end-to-end black-box approaches.

- **Novel Architectural Design for Multi-Modal Integration:** The Mid-Level MoE architecture, with its attention-based gating and cross-attention mechanisms, provides an effective solution for intelligently integrating diverse sources of spatial information. This design offers a robust way to fuse multi-modal inputs while managing the inherent tradeoff between sensitivity to valid signals and robustness to noise, a challenge pervasive in real-world robotics. Its superior performance over simpler fusion methods (concatenation, early fusion) validates the sophisticated integration strategy.

- **Innovative Training Methodology with Self-Consistency:** The introduction of "self-consistency" as a weighted imitation learning algorithm is a methodological innovation. By dynamically weighting demonstrations based on how well the policy's actions align with the expert-generated mid-level plans, it provides a powerful mechanism for improving the precision and reliability of policies, especially when ground-truth rewards are unavailable or expert signals are imperfect. This technique could have broader applicability in other domains of imitation learning or learning from imperfect demonstrations.

- **Enabling Zero-Shot Generalization:** The demonstrated ability to generalize to novel, unseen object instances without re-training is a strong indicator of the representations' capacity to encode abstract, transferable knowledge about physical interactions. This is a critical step towards truly adaptive and flexible robots that can handle unforeseen situations in complex environments like homes or factories.

**Potential Impact:**
The implications of this work are far-reaching. More dexterous and generalizable robots could revolutionize:
- **Manufacturing and Assembly:** Enabling robots to handle greater product variability and perform intricate assembly tasks without extensive re-programming.
- **Logistics and Warehousing:** Improving the efficiency and adaptability of robots in handling diverse packages and inventory.
- **Service Robotics and Healthcare:** Developing more capable robots for assistance in homes, hospitals, or elder care, where interaction with novel objects and varied human activities is common.
- **Exploration and Disaster Response:** Allowing robots to navigate and manipulate objects in unpredictable and unstructured environments.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge limitations such as frequency constraints in distilling representations into smaller policies, which points to the need for faster, more generalist representation generators. The reliance on hand-designed metrics for self-consistency is also a practical limitation, suggesting a future direction in automated metric discovery for scalability to broader task sets. Addressing these limitations will further amplify the impact of spatially-grounded mid-level representations. Overall, this research provides a strong foundation for developing the next generation of highly capable and adaptable robotic systems.
