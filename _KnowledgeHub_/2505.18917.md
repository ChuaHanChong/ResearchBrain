# Behavior Injection: Preparing Language Models for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The BRIDGE framework addresses the inconsistent performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) during Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning by injecting exploration and exploitation behaviors into supervised fine-tuning data. This data augmentation strategy consistently yields higher performance gains and faster reward growth in reasoning tasks, improving final accuracy on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution problems.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit inconsistent and often suboptimal performance improvements when subjected to Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning.
- The role of the LLM's initial state from Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) in determining RL efficacy has been underexplored.
- Traditional methods of enhancing LLMs, such as scaling pre-training data, face growing computational costs and data scarcity.

## Method

- Analyzed the Reinforcement Learning (RL) objective using "==per-step influence==" to identify optimal conditions: medium rollout accuracy and strong data co-influence.
- Introduced "**Behavior Injection**" (BRIDGE), a data augmentation method that instills explicit exploration and exploitation behaviors into Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) datasets.
- Prepared LLMs for RL by first fine-tuning them with SFT on these behavior-augmented datasets, then applying standard RL fine-tuning.

## Results

- Behavior Injection (BRIDGE) consistently led to superior performance gains and higher final accuracy in reasoning tasks across various LLMs and benchmarks, accelerating reward growth during RL fine-tuning.
- The method empirically validated theoretical insights, demonstrating a significant increase in "RL-informative rollout accuracy" and improved "data co-influence" in models.
- Ablation studies confirmed that injected behaviors, particularly in combination, synergistically enhanced both exploitation (reduced perplexity on demonstrations) and exploration (increased policy entropy).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs requires a "medium" rollout accuracy (neither too high nor too low) to generate informative learning signals.
> - The "data co-influence coefficient," measuring how gradients from one sample affect others, is a critical predictor of successful RL performance growth.
> - Explicitly injecting exploration (e.g., reflection) and exploitation (e.g., subgoal computation) behaviors into Supervised Fine-Tuning data makes LLMs significantly more "RL-ready."

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities across a wide array of tasks, especially when guided by Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting for multi-step reasoning. However, they continue to encounter difficulties with highly complex, multi-step reasoning challenges found in domains like mathematical competitions or real-world agentic scenarios. Traditional approaches to enhance LLMs often involve scaling up pre-training data, but concerns about data exhaustion and escalating computational costs are pushing researchers towards alternative strategies.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for post-training LLMs. RL enables reward-driven fine-tuning based on verifiable outcomes, ranging from simple correctness checks to complex interactions within executable environments. This has led to substantial advancements in areas such as mathematical problem-solving, logical reasoning, and interactive agent capabilities. Works like DeepSeekMath and OpenAI-o1 exemplify the significant gains achievable through RL-based optimization for general-purpose and specialized models.

Despite the promise of RL, its application to LLMs often yields inconsistent results: some models show remarkable performance improvements, while others plateau or even degrade. The existing literature on RL typically focuses on algorithmic innovations or data quality, often assuming models are trained from scratch. In contrast, RL for LLMs usually begins with a pre-trained or supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model. This paper identifies a critical gap in the research landscape: the role of the *initialization quality* of the SFT model in determining the efficacy of subsequent RL.

This work directly addresses this gap by shifting the focus from modifying RL algorithms to preparing the base LLM to be "RL-ready." It builds upon existing data-centric approaches that enrich LLM training data through synthetic derivations, curriculum sampling, or agentic data acquisition. However, it distinguishes itself by proposing a novel "behavior-level augmentation" strategy applied *prior* to RL, specifically during the SFT stage. The paper also contributes to the analysis of LLM fine-tuning dynamics, extending previous studies on SFT learning and data influence by investigating the RL learning objective itself to identify key factors driving performance growth. By explicitly injecting exploration and exploitation behaviors, a concept foundational in traditional RL but often underexplored in LLM post-training, this research offers a new perspective on enhancing LLM robustness and learning efficiency in RL environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to understand and mitigate the inconsistent performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) when subjected to Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning. Specifically, the authors aim to develop a method that systematically prepares LLMs for more effective and consistent performance gains during the RL stage.

The core motivation stems from an observed divergence in RL fine-tuning outcomes for LLMs: some models achieve substantial performance gains, while others show minimal improvement or even degradation. The authors hypothesize that this inconsistency is largely attributable to the *initialization state* of the LLM following Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), arguing that the quality and characteristics of this pre-RL model significantly influence the subsequent RL process. Traditional RL research, which often assumes training from scratch, has not adequately addressed this "warm-start" scenario prevalent in LLM applications.

To achieve their objective, the authors pursue two key motivational insights:

1. **Analysis of the RL Learning Objective:** The paper provides an in-depth analysis of the RL objective, particularly for the GRPO algorithm, using the concept of "per-step influence." This analysis identifies two critical conditions for effective performance growth during RL fine-tuning:
 - **RL-informative rollout accuracy:** The model's sampled trajectories during RL must exhibit a *medium* level of accuracy. If all rollouts are perfectly correct or entirely incorrect, the learning signal (and thus per-step influence) becomes negligible. A balanced mix of successful and unsuccessful attempts provides the richest learning opportunities.
 - **Strong data co-influence:** This factor quantifies how effectively the gradients from one training sample influence the model's performance on other samples. A strong co-influence implies that the model can generalize well from both successful and failed reasoning paths explored during RL training.

2. **Importance of Exploration and Exploitation Behaviors:** While exploration (trying new actions) and exploitation (leveraging known successful strategies) are fundamental to traditional low-dimensional RL, their explicit integration into LLM post-training has been underexplored. The authors are motivated by the idea that by intentionally instilling these behaviors in LLMs *before* the RL stage, they can make the models more "RL-ready." This would enable LLMs to generate more informative trajectories during RL rollouts and better capitalize on the learning signals, thereby fostering greater performance improvements.

Based on these motivations, the authors propose a data-centric strategy called "behavior injection" to enrich the SFT data, making the base model inherently more amenable to RL. Their ultimate goal is not to invent new RL algorithms, but to enhance the foundational model such that existing RL algorithms can operate more efficiently and achieve superior, more consistent results.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper, centered around "Behavior Injection" (BRIDGE), is designed to enhance the preparedness of LLMs for subsequent Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning. The overall approach integrates theoretical analysis of RL dynamics with a practical data augmentation strategy.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Foundation: Training Per-step Influence in RL Finetuning

The core of the methodology begins with a theoretical investigation into why LLMs respond divergently to RL. The authors leverage "per-step influence" analysis, a technique traditionally used to understand how individual training data points affect model performance. They adapt this to the context of RL, specifically for the GRPO (Generalized Reinforcement Learning with Policy Optimization) algorithm.

By applying Taylor expansion to the change in the RL objective function, they derive Proposition 3.1, which quantifies the per-step influence ($\Delta J$) of a single query-output group on the overall model performance. This proposition highlights two critical factors:
1. **Rollout Accuracy Rate ($\alpha$):** The term $\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}$ appears as a coefficient. This implies that learning is maximized when the accuracy ($\alpha$) of sampled outputs in a group is neither 0% nor 100%. If all rollouts are correct or incorrect, this coefficient becomes zero, meaning no effective learning signal is generated, irrespective of the other factors.
2. **Data Co-influence Coefficient ($K_\theta$):** This term, $K_\theta((q', o'), (q, o)) = \langle \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o'|q'), \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o|q) \rangle$, measures the inner product of policy gradients between a target query-output pair $(q', o')$ and a training pair $(q, o)$. It quantifies how strongly an update based on one training sample affects performance on another. A high positive $K_\theta$ for correct training samples and a small/negative $K_\theta$ for incorrect samples indicates effective learning from both successes and failures.

This theoretical analysis provides the guiding principles for the subsequent data augmentation strategy.

#### 3.2. BRIDGE: Behavior Injection Data Augmentation

Instead of directly manipulating the RL training distribution (e.g., through rejection sampling, which has drawbacks like increased cost, distribution distortion, and limited impact on $K_\theta$), the authors propose enhancing the base SFT model itself. The central idea is to inject desirable behaviors, analogous to exploration and exploitation in traditional RL, into the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data *prior* to RL.

The BRIDGE pipeline operates as follows (illustrated in Figure 1):
1. **DAG Representation:** For reasoning tasks, the authors adopt a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representation, where nodes represent reasoning steps and edges represent dependencies. This structured representation facilitates the generation and injection of specific behaviors.
2. **Behavior Construction:** Algorithm 1 outlines the process:
 - **Exploration Behaviors:** These are generated by attempting to solve a "locked node" (a node whose prerequisites haven't been met yet) and then reflecting on the premature attempt, leading the model to "step back." This encourages the model to try different paths, even if they are initially unsuccessful.
 - **Exploitation Behaviors:** These involve actively aggregating available information to solve an "unlocked node" (a solvable node not yet addressed) or computing a sub-goal. This reinforces known reasoning paths and improves the model's ability to leverage its current knowledge efficiently.
 - Specific examples include "subgoal computation" (breaking down complex equations), "information analysis" (aggregating relevant info for a node), and "reflection" (exploring a locked node and then retreating).
3. **Data Augmentation:** The constructed behaviors are injected into the vanilla Chain-of-Thought (CoT) dataset used for SFT. Subgoal computation is added to each step, while reflection and analysis are injected with a certain probability (e.g., $p=0.1$).
4. **SFT and RL Fine-tuning:** The LLM is first fine-tuned via SFT on this augmented dataset, creating a "behavior-injected SFT model." This model is then subjected to standard RL fine-tuning (using GRPO in this work).

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks:** Two reasoning benchmarks are used:
 - **iGSM (grade-school math):** Synthetic dataset where difficulty is controlled by the number of operations. Minor modifications were made for stability (e.g., removing modulo operations, filtering extreme answers).
 - **PromptBench (arithmetic and logical reasoning):** Synthetic dataset controlling difficulty by reasoning depth and redundant premises.
 These synthetic datasets minimize data contamination and primarily evaluate problem-solving abilities.
- **Base Models:** Qwen-2.5 (1.5B, 3B parameters) and Llama-3.2 (1B parameters) are used to validate the method across different model families and scales.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Vanilla:** Standard SFT without augmentation.
 - **Premise Permutation Augmentation (PP-Aug):** Shuffles premises to improve reasoning consistency.
 - **Reasoning Chain Augmentation (RC-Aug):** Generates new answers with different topological orders.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Strict match accuracy on both in-distribution (In-Dist) and out-of-distribution (OOD) problem sets to assess generalization.
- **Implementation Details:**
 - Standard SFT configurations (epochs, batch size, learning rate).
 - GRPO-based RL fine-tuning with specific parameters (batch size, learning rate, rollout number, temperature, KL coefficient). A format reward was added for Llama-3.2 models due to template deviation.
 - The "per-step influence" for different SFT models is estimated using a low-rank approximation technique (LoRA gradients with random projection) to overcome computational challenges.
- **Ablation Studies:** Conducted to evaluate the individual contributions of each injected behavior and the impact of the injection probability.
- **Exploration/Exploitation Analysis:** Policy entropy and perplexity are measured to characterize the effects of different behaviors on exploration and exploitation capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents compelling evidence for the effectiveness of Behavior Injection (BRIDGE) in preparing Language Models (LLMs) for Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance Gains in RL

- **Dominant Performance:** Across both iGSM and PromptBench reasoning tasks, and with all tested base models (Qwen-1.5B, Qwen-3B, Llama-1B), BRIDGE consistently achieved the highest performance gains (denoted by $\Delta$) from RL over the pre-RL SFT model. For instance, on iGSM, Qwen-1.5B with BRIDGE showed a 46.6% gain in in-distribution accuracy, significantly outperforming Vanilla (6.0%), PP-Aug (13.8%), and RC-Aug (13.0%). Similar trends were observed for OOD performance and across other models and benchmarks (Tables 1 and 2).
- **Highest Final Accuracy:** Beyond just performance gain, BRIDGE often resulted in the best overall accuracy after RL fine-tuning on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution sets, highlighting its effectiveness in improving the model's fundamental reasoning capabilities and generalizability.
- **Rapid Reward Growth:** Training curves (Figure 9 & 10 in Appendix B.5) demonstrate that BRIDGE leads to the fastest and highest reward growth during the RL training phase, indicating more efficient and effective learning.

#### 4.2. Validation of Theoretical Insights: Rollout Accuracy and Co-influence

The empirical results strongly validate the theoretical analysis regarding the importance of rollout accuracy distribution and data co-influence (Figure 3):
- **Shaping Rollout Accuracy (Middle Plots):** BRIDGE significantly increases the proportion of "medium accuracy" samples (i.e., rollouts with 0 < accuracy < 1) compared to baselines. This is crucial because, as per Proposition 3.1, only these samples provide an informative learning signal for RL. Baselines like RC-Aug tend to push accuracy towards 100%, leading to diminished learning signals.
- **Boosting Data Co-influence (Right Plots):** BRIDGE demonstrably improves the "approximated per-step influence" of the SFT models. This means that the model trained with behavior injection is better able to leverage both successful and failed trajectories from RL training to generalize effectively to new samples, thereby accelerating learning and performance gains.
- **Combined Effect:** The faster and higher reward growth observed with BRIDGE during RL training is directly attributed to its ability to simultaneously optimize both the rollout accuracy distribution and the data co-influence coefficient.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies on Injected Behaviors

- **Individual Contributions (Figure 4):** All three types of injected behaviors—subgoal computation, reflection, and information analysis—contribute positively to the RL performance gain and the per-step influence when added individually. Subgoal computation, in particular, enhances initial SFT model accuracy.
- **Synergy of Behaviors:** For certain models (e.g., Llama-1B), the combined effect of all behaviors in BRIDGE yields significantly greater performance gains in RL than any single behavior, suggesting a synergistic effect from a holistic approach to behavior injection.
- **Robustness to Injection Probability (Figure 5):** The exact probability of injecting reflection and analysis behaviors (across `p = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3`) does not critically impact the final performance, as long as the behaviors are introduced. This indicates that LLMs can adaptively learn to utilize these behaviors effectively, regardless of their precise density in the SFT data.

#### 4.4. Distinct Effects on Exploration and Exploitation

- **Exploitation Enhancement (Figure 6):** Subgoal computation and information analysis lead to a reduction in model perplexity on demonstration data. This indicates that these behaviors enable LLMs to generate ground-truth tokens with higher probability, effectively improving their exploitation capabilities by reinforcing known, correct reasoning paths.
- **Exploration Enhancement (Figure 6):** The reflection behavior, characterized by attempting and then retreating from locked nodes, results in a larger policy entropy during generation. This confirms its role in encouraging LLMs to explore a broader range of possible actions and reasoning steps.
- **BRIDGE's Balance:** The success of BRIDGE is attributed to its ability to enhance both exploration and exploitation, making LLMs significantly more "RL-ready" and capable of navigating complex reasoning spaces.

#### 4.5. Ineffectiveness of Rejection Sampling

- A comparison with accuracy-based rejection sampling (filtering queries with extreme rollout accuracies) showed that this alternative approach did not improve RL performance over the Vanilla baseline and could even degrade performance when combined with BRIDGE (Figure 11, Appendix B.6). This finding reinforces the paper's central argument: simply shaping the initial rollout accuracy distribution is insufficient; enhancing the model's inherent data co-influence through behavior injection is paramount for effective RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Behavior Injection" (BRIDGE) framework and its underlying theoretical analysis offer significant contributions to the field of large language model (LLM) research, with considerable potential for impact:

#### 5.1. Deeper Understanding of LLM-RL Dynamics

This work provides a novel theoretical and empirical explanation for the previously observed inconsistencies in LLM performance during reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning. By introducing the concept of "per-step influence" for RL and identifying the crucial roles of "RL-informative rollout accuracy" and "data co-influence," the authors shed light on the fundamental learning mechanisms at play. This analytical framework can guide future research in diagnosing and improving RL strategies for LLMs.

#### 5.2. Practical and Generalizable Enhancement for LLM Fine-tuning

BRIDGE offers a simple, data-centric, and task-agnostic method to significantly improve the effectiveness of RL fine-tuning. Unlike approaches that modify complex RL algorithms, BRIDGE focuses on preparing the LLM *before* RL, during the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage. This makes the method more accessible and easier to integrate into existing LLM development pipelines. The demonstrated superior performance gains, especially on out-of-distribution tasks, highlight its practical value in building more robust and generalizable LLM reasoners.

#### 5.3. Improved Efficiency and Performance

By making LLMs more "RL-ready," BRIDGE leads to faster reward growth and higher final performance in the RL stage. This translates directly to more efficient training processes and the development of more capable LLMs for complex reasoning tasks, which is critical as these models become increasingly sophisticated and compute-intensive.

#### 5.4. Inspiration for Advanced Agentic AI

The explicit injection of exploration and exploitation behaviors, a cornerstone of traditional RL, into LLM reasoning is a significant conceptual bridge. This approach could be highly impactful for developing more capable and adaptive LLM-based agents that need to navigate complex, multi-step environments, perform tool use, or engage in long-horizon planning. The framework provides a blueprint for how to instill fundamental agentic capabilities within LLMs.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Research and Data Curation

The analytical tools, such as the per-step influence computation and the low-rank approximation for data co-influence, can inspire future research into data curation, dataset analysis, and automated behavior discovery for LLMs. Researchers can use these tools to better understand which data points are most influential, how models learn from different types of feedback, and how to programmatically generate more effective training data.

#### 5.6. Limitations and Ethical Considerations

The authors acknowledge that the current evaluation is focused on math and common-sense reasoning tasks, suggesting future work on extending the method to broader agentic domains and larger datasets. From an ethical standpoint, the paper highlights a crucial concern: the misuse of behavior injection could lead to harmful consequences if unsafe or undesirable behaviors are intentionally or inadvertently instilled in LLMs. This underscores the importance of careful design and rigorous testing when applying such powerful data augmentation techniques.
