# Beyond Distributions: Geometric Action Control for Continuous Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Geometric Action Control (GAC) introduces a method for continuous reinforcement learning that generates actions directly through geometric operations on the unit sphere, addressing the geometric mismatch of traditional Gaussian policies and improving training efficiency and stability.

## Problem

- Gaussian policies, when combined with tanh squashing for bounded action spaces in continuous reinforcement learning, suffer from a geometric mismatch, leading to distorted action distributions and gradient vanishing near boundaries.
- Theoretically principled alternatives like von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distributions, while respecting bounded action spaces, are computationally expensive due to complex sampling methods (rejection sampling) and density computations (Bessel functions).
- Current maximum entropy RL algorithms often rely on explicit and often manually tuned entropy regularization to balance exploration and exploitation, which can be unstable and inefficient.

## Method

- **Geometric Action Control** (GAC) replaces traditional action sampling with a pipeline of ==direction== mapping, concentration control, and spherical mixing, operating directly on the unit sphere.
- A neural network predicts a mean direction vector, which is L2-normalized, and a separate network predicts a scalar concentration score that acts as a mixing weight for linear interpolation between the mean direction and uniform spherical noise.
- GAC integrates into the SAC framework by modifying the actor loss to directly use the learned concentration parameter as an intrinsic exploration signal, thereby replacing the explicit entropy regularization term.

## Results

- GAC achieved top performance on 4 out of 6 MuJoCo benchmarks, outperforming SAC by **37.6%** on Ant-v4 (5633 vs 4094) and TD3 by **59.5%** (5633 vs 3531) with lower variance.
- The GAC-Scale variant, featuring adaptive magnitude scaling, generalized effectively to six DMControl tasks, matching or surpassing SAC on 5 tasks and demonstrating significant gains (e.g., +**112%** on quadruped-run).
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical importance of spherical geometry for stability, the necessity of the adaptive concentration controller for state-dependent exploration, and the impact of the action magnitude on performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective and robust continuous control can be achieved by directly leveraging the geometric structure of bounded action spaces, such as the unit sphere, rather than relying on complex probabilistic distribution modeling.
> - A learnable, state-dependent concentration parameter, controlled by spherical mixing, can intrinsically regulate exploration and exploitation, eliminating the need for explicit entropy regularization and its associated tuning challenges.
> - Direct geometric operations on actions provide computational efficiency (O(d) per sample) and eliminate gradient pathologies (e.g., saturation) often encountered with tanh-squashed Gaussian policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Continuous control is a foundational and challenging problem in reinforcement learning (RL), with applications spanning robotics and autonomous systems. For over a decade, Gaussian policies have been the dominant approach for action generation in deep RL algorithms such as Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). Their widespread use stems from mathematical conveniences, including closed-form entropy and straightforward reparameterization.

However, a fundamental mismatch exists between Gaussian policies and real-world physical systems: Gaussian distributions possess infinite support, while physical action spaces are inherently bounded. The standard practice of applying squashing functions, such as tanh, to map Gaussian samples into bounded regions introduces several issues. These include distortion of the distribution's geometry, gradient vanishing near boundaries, and a breakdown of natural action space symmetry. The paper highlights empirical observations of significant gradient saturation in SAC policies during training, suggesting that issues often attributed to insufficient exploration may, in part, reflect this geometric mismatch.

In response to these limitations, alternative policy parameterizations have been explored. Von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distributions offer a theoretically principled solution by operating directly on the unit sphere, thereby naturally respecting bounded constraints. However, their practical adoption has been hindered by significant computational costs, including the need for rejection sampling (with O(dk) complexity, where k is the number of rejections) and density computations involving modified Bessel functions prone to numerical overflow. Other alternatives like normalizing flows or mixture distributions increase expressiveness but often at the expense of greater computational burden and training instability. Discretization of continuous actions provides an alternative but sacrifices resolution and requires careful binning strategies.

This paper positions itself by taking a different approach that questions the necessity of the distribution paradigm itself. Instead of seeking more sophisticated probability distributions or accepting the limitations of Gaussians, it proposes leveraging the inherent geometric structure of action spaces. This work aligns with a growing interest in geometric perspectives in RL, seen in areas like hyperbolic RL or Riemannian policy optimization, but aims to simplify rather than add complexity. It draws parallels with historical algorithmic simplifications in deep RL, such as PPO improving upon TRPO, suggesting that structural clarity can lead to superior practical performance. The proposed Geometric Action Control (GAC) method challenges the prevailing distributional paradigm by offering a distribution-free action generation mechanism rooted in direct geometric operations on the unit sphere.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and robust method for continuous action generation in deep reinforcement learning that addresses the fundamental geometric mismatch inherent in traditional Gaussian policies while avoiding the computational complexities of theoretically sound alternatives.

The core motivations are:

1. **Address the "Geometric Mismatch":** Gaussian policies, with their unbounded support, are fundamentally incompatible with the bounded action spaces of physical systems. The common solution of tanh squashing distorts the action distribution, leads to vanishing gradients near boundaries, and breaks the natural symmetry of the action space. The authors observe that a significant portion of pre-squashed samples in SAC policies fall into low-gradient regions, impacting learning stability and efficiency. The work aims to eliminate this mismatch by operating directly within a geometrically consistent space.
2. **Simplify Computational Complexity:** While von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distributions offer a theoretically elegant solution for spherical action spaces, their reliance on complex sampling methods (rejection sampling) and density computations (modified Bessel functions) makes them computationally expensive and difficult to implement reliably. The motivation is to achieve the geometric benefits of spherical distributions without incurring these computational costs.
3. **Challenge the Distributional Paradigm:** The paper questions whether explicitly modeling probability distributions over actions is necessary for effective continuous control. It proposes that actions in physical systems can be intuitively decomposed into direction and magnitude, suggesting that a direct geometric approach might be more effective and simpler than inferring actions from complex probability densities.
4. **Enhance Parameter and Computational Efficiency:** Standard Gaussian policies require 2d parameters (mean and variance for each dimension), leading to potentially high parameter counts. The objective is to develop a policy architecture that is more parameter-efficient while maintaining or improving performance. Furthermore, the goal is to reduce the per-sample computational complexity from O(dk) (for vMF) to a simpler O(d).
5. **Develop Intrinsic and Adaptive Exploration:** Traditional maximum entropy RL frameworks rely on explicit entropy regularization (e.g., SAC's temperature parameter α) to balance exploration and exploitation. The motivation is to integrate exploration directly into the action generation mechanism, making it an intrinsic geometric property modulated by a learnable, adaptive concentration parameter, thereby eliminating the need for manual entropy tuning or external regularization.

Through these objectives, the research seeks to champion a "Geometric Simplicity Principle," advocating that explicit modeling of action space geometry can be a more effective and efficient path for continuous control than relying on increasingly sophisticated probabilistic distributions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Geometric Action Control (GAC), replaces traditional action sampling with a geometric pipeline operating on the unit sphere.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work operates within the standard continuous control setting, where an agent interacts with an environment through bounded continuous actions, A ⊆ [-1, 1]^d. The objective is to maximize the expected return, augmented with an entropy term (J(π) = E_τ∼π [Σ γ^t (r_t + αH(π(·|s_t)))]) within the maximum entropy RL framework. GAC explicitly addresses the geometric mismatch where unbounded Gaussian policies are mapped to bounded action spaces via tanh squashing, which causes gradient saturation. GAC aims to align policy support with environmental constraints by design.

#### 3.2. Geometric Action Generation (GAC)

GAC generates actions through direct geometric operations on the unit sphere, discarding the need for explicit probability distribution modeling.

- **Core Insight:** Actions are generated via a pipeline of direction mapping, concentration control, and spherical mixing. This approach avoids density-based computations like log-probabilities or entropy, integrating exploration through structured spherical noise.
- **Direction Mapping:** A neural network, $f_\mu(s)$, outputs a raw vector, which is then L2-normalized to form a unit vector $\mu(s) \in S^{d-1}$. This explicitly constrains the policy's output to the unit sphere.
- **Concentration Control:** A separate neural network, $f_\kappa(s)$, predicts a scalar concentration score, $\kappa$. This score is passed through a sigmoid function, $w(\kappa) = \sigma(\kappa) \in (0, 1)$, to yield a mixing weight. This weight adaptively controls the trade-off between deterministic action and stochastic noise.
- **Spherical Mixing:** The final action is generated by linearly interpolating between the deterministic direction $\mu$ and uniform spherical noise $\xi \sim Uniform(S^{d-1})$. This mixed vector is then normalized and scaled by a task-dependent radius $r$:
 $a = r \cdot \text{normalize}(w(\kappa) \cdot \mu + (1 - w(\kappa)) \cdot \xi)$.
 The uniform spherical noise $\xi$ is sampled as a normalized Gaussian noise. This design ensures that exploration is intrinsic to the action generation process, making it an endogenous control signal rather than an external perturbation.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Justification

GAC's spherical mixing operation is theoretically analyzed to demonstrate how the mixing weight controls concentration without complex density functions.

- **Expected Direction Control (Theorem 1):** The expected unnormalized sample vector, $E_\xi[v]$, is shown to be $w(\kappa)\mu$. This indicates that $w(\kappa)$ directly controls the expected alignment with the mean direction, implying vMF-like concentration control as $\kappa$ (and thus $w(\kappa)$) increases, without using Bessel functions.
- **Concentration Analysis:** While a closed-form density is intractable, an approximation of the expected cosine similarity between normalized samples and the mean direction is derived. For typical operating ranges of $w(\kappa)$, this quantity closely tracks $w(\kappa)$, empirically validated with a correlation of approximately 0.95.
- **Exploration Control and Entropy Connection:** The concentration parameter $\kappa$ implicitly relates to entropy in directional statistics. For vMF distributions, entropy is inversely related to concentration, with a leading term of $-\kappa$. GAC leverages this conceptual relationship by using $-\kappa(s)$ as an exploration signal in its actor loss, effectively balancing exploration and exploitation without explicitly computing or regularizing entropy.
- **SAC Convergence:** The paper provides a proof sketch outlining how GAC integrates into the SAC framework and maintains properties essential for convergence. This includes the continuity of GAC's action generation, the regularization provided by the exploration controller, and the implicit policy improvement through gradient-based optimization.

#### 3.4. Integration with SAC

GAC replaces the standard Gaussian policy within the SAC framework. The actor loss is modified to incorporate the learned concentration $\kappa(s)$ directly, replacing the entropy regularization term: $L_{actor}(\phi) = E_{s \sim D} [\kappa(s) - \min_{i=1,2} Q_{\theta_i}(s, a)]$. The soft Q-function update also integrates $\kappa(s)$ into the target value: $y(r_t, s') = r_t + \gamma [\min_{i=1,2} Q'_{\theta_i}(s', a') - \kappa(s')]$.

#### 3.5. Practical Considerations

- **Parameter Efficiency:** The fixed-radius GAC requires $d+1$ parameters (d for direction, 1 for concentration), a 50% reduction compared to 2d for diagonal Gaussian policies. The adaptive scaling variant uses $2d+1$ parameters, comparable to Gaussians but with explicit geometric structure.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Sampling in GAC involves normalization and linear interpolation, yielding O(d) complexity per sample, which is faster than the O(dk) for vMF rejection sampling. Empirical timing shows GAC is approximately 22% faster than SAC.
- **Hyperparameter Selection:** A fixed radius $r$ (default 2.5) is introduced to scale actions appropriately, as unit vectors in high dimensions have small expected per-dimension magnitudes. GAC exhibits low sensitivity to $r$ within a reasonable range.
- **Adaptive Magnitude Scaling (GAC-Scale):** For tasks requiring more flexible magnitude control, an extension allows for a learnable per-dimension magnitude vector $r \in \mathbb{R}^d$, produced by a neural network head with softplus activation. This preserves geometric structure while providing anisotropic control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of GAC was conducted on six standard MuJoCo benchmarks and six challenging DMControl suite tasks, comparing against SAC, TD3, and PPO.

#### 4.1. MuJoCo Benchmarks (Fixed Radius)

- **Overall Performance:** GAC demonstrated strong performance, achieving the best results on 4 out of 6 tasks (Walker2d-v4, HalfCheetah-v4, Ant-v4, Humanoid-v4) and remaining competitive on others (Hopper-v4, Pusher-v4). PPO consistently underperformed.
- **Learning Efficiency:** GAC exhibited faster initial learning on environments like Ant-v4 and HalfCheetah-v4, reaching near-optimal performance earlier in training compared to SAC and TD3. This is attributed to the geometric structure that removes the need for entropy tuning.
- **High-Dimensional Control:** GAC scaled effectively to complex, high-dimensional tasks. On Humanoid-v4 (17D action space), GAC matched TD3 and slightly exceeded SAC during training, achieving 5823 ± 121. In post-training evaluation, it reached 6591 ± 53. On Ant-v4 (8D), GAC outperformed SAC by 37.6% (5633 ± 158 vs. 4094 ± 1039) and TD3 by 59.5% (3531 ± 1263), with notably lower variance.
- **Trade-off (Stability vs. Expressiveness):** While GAC provided stable learning and low variance on many tasks, it underperformed on Hopper-v4 and Pusher-v4. This suggests that the spherical normalization, which favors near-unit-norm actions, can limit performance in tasks requiring highly asymmetric or contact-rich control patterns.

#### 4.2. Generalization to DMControl Suite (Adaptive Scaling)

- **Enhanced Generalization:** Using the GAC-Scale variant (with learnable per-dimension magnitude $r$), the method generalized effectively to six complex DMControl tasks. It matched or surpassed SAC on 5 out of 6 tasks, achieving significant gains on quadruped environments (e.g., +34% on quadruped-walk, +112% on quadruped-run compared to SAC).
- **Adaptive Magnitude Learning:** Analysis of the learned scaling vector $r$ indicated that values stabilized within a theoretically motivated range (1.5-3.0) after initial exploration. For Walker tasks, $r$ converged to nearly uniform values, similar to the fixed baseline. In contrast, Quadruped tasks showed more heterogeneous scaling patterns across dimensions, reflecting a need for anisotropic control.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (on HalfCheetah-v4)

- **Criticality of Target Magnitude (r):** Reducing the fixed radius $r$ from 2.5 to 1.5 resulted in a 43% performance drop, indicating insufficient actuation. Increasing $r$ to 3.5 caused only a minor decline (-4.1%), suggesting robustness to slight over-scaling. These results underscore that appropriate action magnitude is essential for effective control.
- **Importance of Adaptive Exploration ($\kappa$):** Removing the learnable $\kappa$ controller reduced performance by 10.8% and slowed convergence. This demonstrated that the learned $\kappa$ is crucial for state-dependent exploration, balancing confidence and diversity without explicit entropy regularization.
- **Necessity of Geometric Structure:** Removing spherical normalization led to divergence within 5k steps due to unbounded gradients, while using raw, unbounded outputs immediately collapsed training. These ablations confirmed that GAC's spherical geometry is fundamental for stability, acting as an implicit regularizer and preventing numerical issues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents Geometric Action Control (GAC) as a method for continuous reinforcement learning that challenges the prevailing reliance on complex probability distributions. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

1. **Conceptual Shift in Policy Design:** GAC fundamentally re-conceptualizes continuous control by replacing probabilistic sampling with direct geometric operations on the unit sphere. This demonstrates that robust and efficient control can be achieved by respecting the geometric structure of action spaces rather than modeling complex distributions. This "Geometric Simplicity Principle" suggests new avenues for developing algorithms that are efficient, interpretable, and theoretically grounded.
2. **Improved Efficiency and Stability:**
 - **Parameter Efficiency:** GAC reduces the number of policy parameters by 50% for fixed-radius settings compared to diagonal Gaussian policies, while maintaining or improving performance.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** The O(d) complexity per action sample, achieved by avoiding rejection sampling, Bessel functions, and explicit density calculations, makes GAC computationally lighter than vMF distributions and faster than SAC.
 - **Stability:** GAC eliminates gradient pathologies associated with tanh-squashed Gaussians, ensuring consistent gradient flow and bounded actions, which contributes to more stable training.
3. **Enhanced Performance on Complex Tasks:** GAC consistently matches or exceeds state-of-the-art methods like SAC and TD3 across diverse MuJoCo and DMControl benchmarks. Notably, it achieves substantial gains (e.g., 37.6% over SAC on Ant-v4, up to 112% on complex quadruped-run tasks) and exhibits lower variance, particularly in high-dimensional and complex coordination tasks. The adaptive scaling variant further extends its capabilities to asymmetric control challenges.
4. **Adaptive and Intrinsic Exploration:** By integrating exploration directly through a learnable concentration parameter $\kappa$, GAC eliminates the need for manual entropy tuning or external temperature scheduling. This adaptive, state-dependent exploration emerges naturally from the value-based objective, simplifying the optimization pipeline and improving interpretability.
5. **Foundation for Future Geometric RL:** The success of GAC suggests that exploring geometric structures could be broadly applicable across other areas of RL. This includes potential applications in value function approximation, hierarchical control, multi-agent coordination, or developing adaptive geometric structures that interpolate between different manifolds based on task characteristics.

While the current geometric prior (spherical manifold) shows some limitations on highly asymmetric tasks, the work opens up a new research direction for developing RL algorithms that prioritize structural clarity and geometric consistency over statistical modeling complexity.
