# EFGCL: Learning Dynamic Motion through Spotting-Inspired External Force Guided Curriculum Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> External Force Guided Curriculum Learning (EFGCL), developed at The University of Tokyo, enables legged robots to learn challenging dynamic whole-body motions like jumps and flips by providing gradually decaying physical assistance. This curriculum-based approach stabilizes reinforcement learning and demonstrates successful sim-to-real transfer to a quadrupedal robot.

## Problem

- Legged robots struggle to acquire high-risk, dynamic whole-body motions (e.g., flips) using reinforcement learning due to frequent failures.
- Inefficient exploration and sparse rewards in such tasks lead to unstable learning and difficulty in policy convergence.
- Current guidance methods like imitation learning or reward shaping often require costly reference trajectories or task-specific tuning, potentially introducing designer bias.

## Method

- External Force Guided Curriculum Learning (EFGCL) introduces transient, "spotting-inspired" external forces to physically assist the robot during early training phases.
- An adaptive curriculum gradually decays these assistive forces based on the agent's success rate, transitioning from assisted to autonomous execution.
- The framework utilizes PPO and a teacher-student architecture for sim-to-real transfer, employing a sparse reward structure for task definition.

## Results

- EFGCL enabled the acquisition of complex backflip and lateral-flip motions, which were unlearnable by a PPO baseline.
- For the jump task, EFGCL accelerated learning by approximately a factor of two compared to the PPO baseline and produced more natural postures.
- Policies trained with EFGCL successfully transferred from simulation to a physical KLEIYN quadruped robot, reproducing dynamic jumps and flips.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct physical assistance in early training dramatically increases the density of successful trajectories, leading to faster and more stable estimation of the value function.
> - The method's effectiveness is robust to heuristic variations in the assistive force's application point, magnitude, and timing.
> - Inspired by human "spotting" in gymnastics, providing physical guidance rather than indirect reward shaping or costly trajectory following proves effective for high-risk motor skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of dynamic whole-body motions for legged robots using reinforcement learning (RL) presents significant challenges. While robust methods exist for tasks like rough-terrain locomotion and obstacle traversal, highly dynamic motor skills, such as those found in sports, remain difficult to learn. This difficulty stems from the high risk of failure inherent in such motions, which impedes efficient exploration and often leads to unstable learning.

Existing approaches to guide RL for dynamic motions include imitation learning and reward shaping. Imitation learning, which involves mimicking reference trajectories, has been applied to dynamic tasks. However, its effectiveness is often contingent on the quality of these reference trajectories, which can be costly to generate. Methods like Opt-Mimic generate trajectories through optimization, incurring costs in robot modeling and objective function design. Approaches such as WASABI use physically guided demonstrations, but their quality depends on human operator skill. Consequently, a trade-off exists between reference trajectory quality and generation cost.

Reward shaping attempts to facilitate exploration by designing intermediate rewards that guide behavior. This approach is highly task-dependent and non-trivial, requiring specific knowledge of which aspects of a motion to reward. Furthermore, intermediate rewards can introduce designer bias, potentially excluding optimal motion sequences and degrading learning performance. This has led to recommendations for using sparse reward functions and promoting learning through mechanisms external to the reward itself. However, a general framework for efficiently exploring dynamic motions with a high risk of failure, particularly with sparse rewards, has not been widely established.

The paper draws inspiration from "spotting" in artistic gymnastics, a technique where a coach physically supports an athlete during skill practice. This method provides physical guidance rather than relying solely on reward design to shape behavior. The proposed External Force Guided Curriculum Learning (EFGCL) aims to integrate this principle into robot reinforcement learning, addressing the gap in efficient, physically guided exploration for dynamic whole-body motions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work arises from the inherent difficulties in training legged robots to perform complex, dynamic whole-body motions using reinforcement learning. These difficulties include:
1. **High risk of failure:** Dynamic movements often involve states that are prone to failure (e.g., falling), making it challenging for an RL agent to discover successful trajectories through random exploration.
2. **Inefficient exploration:** The sparse nature of success in high-risk tasks leads to a lack of positive feedback, hindering the agent's ability to learn effectively and stabilize its policy.
3. **Unstable learning:** The absence of early successful experiences can result in poorly estimated value functions, causing gradient-based policy updates (such as those in PPO) to become ineffective or even detrimental, leading to learning stagnation or collapse.

To address these challenges, the authors drew inspiration from the human learning process, specifically "spotting" in artistic gymnastics. In this technique, a coach physically assists an athlete, enabling them to safely experience and successfully complete difficult maneuvers. This physical guidance provides direct, experiential feedback, contrasting with indirect forms of guidance like reward shaping or imitation learning based on reference trajectories.

The key objectives of the proposed research are:
1. **Introduce a new learning paradigm:** To establish a method for acquiring dynamic motor skills that employs physical guidance via external forces, as an alternative to guidance solely through reward design. This aims to provide a more direct form of assistance during the critical early stages of learning.
2. **Propose External Force Guided Curriculum Learning (EFGCL):** To develop a structured approach that integrates this physical guidance into an RL framework. EFGCL aims to gradually reduce external assistive forces over time through a curriculum, allowing the agent to transition from assisted execution to autonomous performance. The objective is to demonstrate that this physical guidance significantly improves exploration efficiency and enables the acquisition of motions that conventional RL methods struggle with.
3. **Demonstrate real-world effectiveness and transferability:** To validate the proposed approach through experiments on a physical quadrupedal robot, confirming that the learned policies can be successfully deployed from simulation to real-world environments.

Ultimately, the motivation is to create a general and cost-effective strategy for improving learning efficiency in dynamic whole-body motion tasks by allowing agents to physically experience successful motion executions early in training, without relying on task-specific reward shaping or complex reference trajectories.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, External Force Guided Curriculum Learning (EFGCL), integrates physical guidance in the form of external assistive forces into a reinforcement learning framework, specifically PPO, to facilitate the learning of dynamic whole-body motions.

#### 3.1. Overview of EFGCL

EFGCL modifies the Markov Decision Process (MDP) itself by introducing external forces that aid task execution in the early stages of learning. This assistance is then gradually diminished through a curriculum process. The goal is for the agent to initially experience successful trajectories under assistance, then progressively transition to generating autonomous motions, eventually acquiring a policy that performs the target motion without any external aid. This approach seeks to stabilize learning while maintaining sparse reward functions, avoiding complex reward engineering.

#### 3.2. Design of External Assistance

External assistance is defined by three components: application points (P), corresponding force vectors (F), and application timing (T). The purpose of this assistance is not to teach an optimal trajectory but to guide the agent towards high-reward states, thereby artificially increasing the density of successful trajectories experienced early in training. This, in turn, helps the value function to assign high values to near-optimal behaviors, stabilizing policy updates. The assistive forces are heuristically designed for each task.

- **Jump:** Assistive forces are applied vertically to all four scapula links, with a magnitude calculated to provide the initial velocity required for the target jump height, modeled as simple projectile motion.
- **Backflip:** Forces are applied vertically to the front left and front right scapula links (p_Scapula_FL, p_Scapula_FR) with a fixed magnitude of 175 N.
- **Lateral-flip:** Forces are applied vertically to the front right and back right scapula links (p_Scapula_FR, p_Scapula_BR) with a fixed magnitude of 300 N.
- **Timing:** For all tasks, the forces are applied for a short duration: T = {(1.0 s, 1.1 s)}.

#### 3.3. Success-Rate Based Adaptive Curriculum Scheduling

To ensure stable policy transitions, EFGCL employs an adaptive curriculum that adjusts the decay of assistive forces based on the agent's training progress. PPO training continues at each curriculum stage until the success rate for the current task exceeds a predefined threshold (ζ = 0.6). Once this threshold is met, the assistive force is decayed by a scaling factor α_i, calculated as α_i = max(0, 1 − ε × i), where ε = 0.01 is the decay step size. This mechanism automatically adjusts the difficulty increase, maintaining small gaps between successive MDPs and supporting stable incremental learning.

#### 3.4. Reinforcement Learning Framework

The core RL algorithm utilized is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), known for its training stability and suitability for high-dimensional action spaces. PPO uses Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) to estimate advantages. The paper highlights that in high-failure environments, successful trajectories are rare, leading to small advantage estimates and consequently slow or stalled learning. EFGCL aims to mitigate this by ensuring early successful experiences.

#### 3.5. Time-Encoding for Observations

Since the assistive forces are time-dependent, the agent needs to infer the timing of assistance. To avoid issues with monotonically increasing elapsed time as a direct input, a bounded, monotonically increasing function τ(t, λ) = t̃^3 / (1 + t̃^3) (where t̃ = t/λ and λ = t_start) is used to encode time in the observations.

#### 3.6. Teacher-Student Learning Architecture

The study adopts a Teacher-Student learning architecture to handle partial observability on the real robot.
- **Teacher Policy:** This policy operates in simulation, receiving both proprioceptive observations (joint positions/velocities, gravity vector, root angular velocity, command input, time encoding) and privileged observations (task-specific information like root height, max height, pitch/roll angle). The Teacher Policy is trained via reinforcement learning using PPO, with EFGCL applied during this training phase.
- **Student Policy:** This policy is designed for deployment on the real robot and receives only proprioceptive observations (onboard sensor information). It is trained through supervised learning, where it learns to mimic the actions of the Teacher Policy and reconstruct privileged observations, minimizing action-matching and reconstruction losses.

#### 3.7. Robot Platform and Tasks

Experiments were conducted using the quadrupedal robot KLEIYN, an 18 kg, 600 mm tall robot with 3 DoF per leg and 1 DoF in the torso. Isaac Gym served as the simulation environment. Three dynamic whole-body motion tasks were defined:
- **Jump:** Achieving a target maximum height.
- **Backflip:** Rotating 2π (360 degrees) around the pitch axis.
- **Lateral-flip:** Rotating 2π (360 degrees) around the roll axis.

#### 3.8. Reward Function Design

All tasks share a common sparse reward structure to prevent arbitrary performance gains from task-specific tuning. The reward consists of:
- `ρ_task`: Progress towards the task-specific target (max height for Jump, rotation angle for flips).
- `ρ_stand`: Encourages stable posture after landing.
- `r_ang`: Regularizes rotation about non-target axes.
- `r_common`: Enforces general physical constraints (e.g., collision penalties, termination penalties, joint velocity/acceleration penalties).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of EFGCL involved comparisons with a PPO baseline across multiple random seeds, an ablation study on force design, and an analysis of value function estimation.

#### 4.1. Learning Performance and Convergence

- **EFGCL Performance:** EFGCL demonstrated stable convergence and achieved high reward values across all three dynamic tasks: Jump, Backflip, and Lateral-flip. The learning process with EFGCL was consistent across random seeds.
- **PPO Baseline Performance:** The conventional PPO baseline failed to acquire the more complex flipping tasks (Backflip and Lateral-flip). For the Jump task, the baseline exhibited high variance in reward accumulation and often resulted in suboptimal or unnatural postures. In contrast, EFGCL facilitated the acquisition of natural and stable dynamic motions. The Jump task was learned approximately twice as fast with EFGCL compared to the baseline.

#### 4.2. Adaptive Curriculum Efficacy

The success-rate-based adaptive curriculum scheduling in EFGCL effectively managed the decay of assistive forces. The system automatically adjusted the speed at which assistance was reduced based on the agent's current success rate, ensuring that the difficulty increase was gradual and stable learning was maintained. This adaptive mechanism contributed to the stable progression and high convergence rates observed in EFGCL.

#### 4.3. Real Robot Deployment (Sim-to-Real Transfer)

Policies learned using EFGCL in simulation were successfully distilled into student policies and deployed on the physical KLEIYN quadrupedal robot. The robot was able to reproduce the dynamic motions of jumping, backflipping, and lateral-flipping in the real world, consistent with the behaviors observed in simulation. This demonstrates the practical transferability of the learned skills.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study on Assistive Force Design

An ablation study was conducted on the Backflip task to assess the sensitivity of EFGCL to heuristic design choices for the assistive force. The results indicated that learning remained robust across a wide range of parameters:
- **Application Point:** Successful policies were acquired even when the assistive force was applied to different links (e.g., thigh or calf instead of scapula).
- **Magnitude:** Learning succeeded with force magnitudes varying between 140 N and 210 N (the main experiment used 175 N).
- **Timing:** Successful learning occurred across various application timings.
- **Failure Cases:** Learning failed only in extreme conditions where the assistance was physically insufficient (e.g., 100 N) or excessively high (e.g., 250 N), or when the application timing was too brief (e.g., 1.0 s to 1.05 s).
These findings suggest that EFGCL does not require precise tuning of assistive force parameters, indicating robustness to reasonable heuristic designs.

#### 4.5. Accelerated Critic Value Estimation

Analysis of the value function estimates during the Jump task revealed that EFGCL significantly accelerated the learning of the critic. With EFGCL, the value estimates for successful reference motions converged to a stable distribution, closely resembling the final value function, as early as 200 training iterations. In contrast, the PPO baseline required over 1,000 iterations to achieve a comparable level of accuracy and exhibited higher variance in its value estimates. This result supports the hypothesis that physically experiencing successful states early in training expedites and stabilizes the estimation of the value function.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented on External Force Guided Curriculum Learning (EFGCL) offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic reinforcement learning, particularly for dynamic whole-body motions.

#### 5.1. Novel Learning Paradigm

EFGCL introduces a novel learning paradigm centered on physical guidance via external forces. This approach differentiates itself from conventional methods such as reward shaping, which manipulates the reward signal, and imitation learning, which relies on expert demonstrations or reference trajectories. By physically assisting the robot, EFGCL enables the agent to directly experience successful task executions early in training. This direct physical experience is a distinct form of guided exploration, inspired by human learning techniques like "spotting" in gymnastics.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Exploration Efficiency and Stability

The study demonstrates that EFGCL substantially improves exploration efficiency and learning stability in tasks involving high-risk, dynamic motions. Standard reinforcement learning approaches, like PPO, often struggle or fail entirely when successful experiences are rare. EFGCL mitigates this by artificially increasing the density of successful trajectories, leading to faster and more stable value function estimation. This accelerated value learning, in turn, provides more informative gradients for policy updates, enabling the acquisition of complex behaviors (like backflips and lateral-flips) that were unlearnable with the baseline method and significantly speeding up learning for other tasks (e.g., Jump accelerated by a factor of two).

#### 5.3. Robustness to Heuristic Design

A key finding is the robustness of EFGCL to variations in the heuristic design of assistive forces. The ablation study indicated that precise tuning of force application points, magnitudes, or timings is not critical. As long as the assistance generally facilitates the target motion without being physically insufficient or excessive, the learning process remains effective. This robustness reduces the burden on human designers, making the method more practical and accessible for implementing complex dynamic skills.

#### 5.4. Sim-to-Real Transferability

The successful deployment of EFGCL-trained policies on a physical quadrupedal robot (KLEIYN) validates the method's practical applicability. The ability to reproduce complex dynamic motions in the real world, consistent with simulation, underscores the potential for EFGCL to be utilized in developing agile and dynamic behaviors for robotic systems.

#### 5.5. Complementary to Existing Methods

EFGCL is presented as a paradigm that complements existing guided exploration techniques. While it can function effectively with sparse rewards and minimal task-specific tuning, the authors acknowledge that for continuous and highly structured motions (e.g., dancing), combining physical guidance with trajectory-tracking rewards or imitation learning might be more effective. This indicates that EFGCL could be integrated into a broader toolkit of RL strategies.

#### 5.6. Future Research Directions

The current reliance on heuristic design for assistive forces, while robust, points to an important area for future work: developing automatic optimization or generation methods for these forces. Such advancements could further reduce human intervention and extend the applicability of EFGCL to even more complex and varied dynamic target motions. Exploring combinations with other guidance mechanisms for continuous behaviors also represents a promising avenue.

In summary, EFGCL represents a significant step towards enabling legged robots to learn sophisticated dynamic whole-body motions, offering a robust, efficient, and physically intuitive approach to guided exploration in reinforcement learning.
