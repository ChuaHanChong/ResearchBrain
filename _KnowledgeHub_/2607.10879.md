# 3D Scene Graph Prediction: Generating Hierarchical Models from Partially Observed Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from MIT's Laboratory for Information & Decision Systems developed a top-down framework for predicting hierarchical 3D scene graphs from partially observed environments. The system utilizes a mixed-domain graph diffusion model for room-level generation and an object-level diffusion model, demonstrating superior performance in scene completion and generalization to out-of-distribution data by handling partial constraints at inference time without specialized training.

## Problem

- Robots often operate in partially observed environments, making it challenging to predict unknown portions for efficient exploration and navigation.
- Existing 3D scene generation methods largely focus on object arrangements within single rooms, lacking the hierarchical room-level layouts and traversability information critical for robotic tasks.
- Current scene completion efforts struggle with a lack of diverse training data for partial observations and heterogeneous sensing modalities, hindering generalization to real-world scenarios.

## Method

- A top-down hierarchical framework is introduced to synthesize ==Building–Room–Object (BRO) scene graphs==, first generating room-level structures and then object-level layouts conditioned on the rooms.
- A **mixed-domain graph diffusion model** is proposed for the room layer, jointly predicting room semantic labels, 3D floor plan geometry, and ==traversability connections== between rooms.
- A ==masking strategy== is integrated to handle partial observations and constraints at test time without requiring retraining on partial data, allowing the model to condition on known regions and predict unknown ones.

## Results

- The proposed method quantitatively outperformed LLM-based and occupancy-based baselines in layout realism, graph structure, geometry, and label/edge prediction on in-distribution 3D-FRONT partial scene graphs.
- It demonstrated substantial outperformance over baselines, particularly Semantic Scene Completion (SSC), on out-of-distribution Matterport3D partial scene graphs, showing strong generalization capabilities.
- The framework successfully integrated with Hydra and MiDiffusion, qualitatively demonstrating the ability to complete floor plans and synthesize object layouts from real-world, robot-collected partial scene data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal room-level generation involves a mixed representation, with discrete modeling for room connectivity (edges) and continuous modeling for room semantics (labels), balancing convergence speed and geometric realism.
> - A graph diffusion model can effectively generalize to partial observations by applying a masking strategy at inference time, enabling robust prediction in incomplete environments without extensive partial training data.
> - Hierarchical scene graph generation, from room-level layouts to object placements, provides a more comprehensive and robot-relevant understanding of environments than single-layer or LLM-based approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of generating realistic 3D indoor scenes has garnered increasing interest within computer vision and robotics. Prior research in this area frequently concentrates on the arrangement of objects within a single room, often driven by applications such as interior design. However, the generation of higher-level scene structures, including room-level layouts and traversability information, has remained less explored, despite its relevance for robotics applications like exploration and navigation.

Three-dimensional Scene Graphs (3DSGs) have been adopted in robotics as a concise, hierarchical representation of 3D environments, encompassing different levels of abstraction such as objects, rooms, and buildings. Systems like Hydra facilitate the real-time construction of such multi-layer scene graphs from sensor data. A significant challenge in robotic exploration is the common occurrence of occlusions and partial observability. This makes it difficult for autonomous systems to predict the unknown portions of an environment, which in turn hinders the planning of efficient exploration strategies or anticipating the spatial structure beyond observed areas. Additionally, scene completion efforts are often constrained by a lack of diverse training data, as collecting in-distribution data for all possible scenarios can be impractical due to heterogeneous sensing modalities and environmental conditions.

Existing scene synthesis methods primarily focus on generating layouts from scratch, typically limited to flat object arrangements within individual rooms. These methods have utilized various techniques, including procedural modeling, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), autoregressive models, and diffusion models. More recently, Large Language Model (LLM)-based planners, have been explored. While some works address room-level floor plans, they generally offer limited support for incorporating partial constraints.

In the realm of world modeling and scene completion, Semantic Scene Completion (SSC) for outdoor environments has focused on predicting missing semantic voxels or point clouds. For indoor settings, approaches using symbolic representations with scene graphs and LLMs have been investigated for navigation, and 3DSG hierarchies have been leveraged for structural priors. However, current LLMs and Vision Language Models (VLMs) have shown limitations in complete scene prediction and precise geometric generation, primarily due to insufficient 3D spatial grounding.

Diffusion models, have gained prominence across various synthesis tasks, including 3D scenes, and graph-conditioned layouts. These models generally operate by progressively adding noise in a forward process and then learning to denoise in a reverse process. Continuous data often uses Gaussian noise in Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), while discrete data employs categorical diffusion models. Some approaches, like MiDiffusion, propose mixed discrete-continuous methods for 3D scenes. Graph generative models, which predict node and edge attributes, have also evolved from autoregressive architectures to score-based and diffusion frameworks, that handle equivariant properties.

This work specifically addresses the challenge of predicting the unexplored parts of an indoor environment from partial observations, with a focus on robotics applications. It differentiates itself by proposing a top-down framework for synthesizing *hierarchical* 3D scene graphs that explicitly models both room-level structure (floor plan, traversability) and object-level layouts. Critically, it introduces a mixed-domain graph diffusion model designed to handle partial constraints at test time without requiring retraining on partial data, thereby aiming to improve generalization and practicality for real-world robotic deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing demand for robotic systems that can autonomously understand, anticipate, and interact with their environments. While existing scene generation methods have made progress, particularly in object layout within single rooms, they often overlook the generation of high-level scene structures crucial for robust robotic operation.

The key motivations are:
- **Addressing Partial Observability:** Robots often operate in partially observed environments. The ability to accurately predict unobserved portions of a scene is fundamental for informed decision-making in downstream tasks such as active exploration, efficient navigation, and object search.
- **Generating Hierarchical Scene Structures:** Robotic tasks often benefit from a structured understanding of an environment. Existing methods typically focus on individual objects or single rooms. This work is motivated by the need to generate comprehensive, hierarchical 3D Scene Graphs (3DSGs) that represent an environment at multiple levels of abstraction (e.g., building, rooms, objects) and their interrelationships, including traversability.
- **Overcoming Data Scarcity for Partial Scenes:** Collecting exhaustive training data that covers all possible partial observation scenarios and heterogeneous sensing modalities is often infeasible. The motivation is to develop a method that can handle partial constraints at inference time without requiring specific training on partially observed data.
- **Improving Generalization:** Many current generative models struggle with geometric precision and generalizing to out-of-distribution inputs. A motivation is to develop a robust generation framework that can generalize well to real-world, robot-collected data, which may differ from synthetic training datasets.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this research are:
- **Develop a Top-Down Hierarchical Framework:** To create an integrated pipeline capable of synthesizing hierarchical 3D scene graphs, starting from a high-level (room layer) and progressing to lower levels (object layer).
- **Propose a Mixed-Domain Graph Diffusion Model for the Room Layer:** To jointly predict diverse attributes of rooms, including their semantic categories, geometric floor boundaries, and connectivity/traversability between rooms.
- **Enable Partial Constraint Handling without Retraining:** To design the room-layer model with a masking strategy that allows flexible conditioning on incomplete inputs (e.g., partial floor plans) at test time, thus eliminating the need for retraining on partially observed training data.
- **Integrate an Object-Layer Predictor:** To couple the room-layer prediction with an existing object-layer diffusion model (MiDiffusion) to achieve end-to-end building-scale scene completion. This object-layer model should predict object categories, locations, sizes, and orientations within each generated room, conditioned on the room's geometry and type.
- **Validate Performance and Generalization:** To conduct extensive experimental validation against state-of-the-art occupancy-based and LLM-based floor plan generation methods on standard benchmarks (3D-FRONT). Furthermore, to demonstrate the framework's ability to generalize to out-of-distribution partial scene graphs (Matterport3D) and real-world robot-collected data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a top-down framework for synthesizing hierarchical Building–Room–Object (BRO) scene graphs. This process is decomposed into two main stages: room-level scene graph generation, followed by object-level scene graph generation conditioned on the generated room structures.

**A. Room-level Scene Graph Generation**
The room-level scene graph, denoted G_R, consists of room nodes, each characterized by a semantic label (z_R,i) and a geometric feature (x_R,i). The geometric feature comprises a 3D floor plan center (t_R,i) and a 2D latent-space encoding of the room shape (s_R,i). Undirected edges (e_R,ij) represent traversability between rooms. The model is designed to handle a maximum number of rooms (N), using an "empty" label for padding if fewer rooms are present.

1. **Graph Diffusion in Mixed Domains:**
 The core of the room-level generation is a mixed-domain graph diffusion framework that jointly models variables across three domains: semantic labels (G_z), geometric features (G_x), and edge connectivity (G_e). The authors derive a variational diffusion loss (L_vb) that, by assuming independent domain-specific noise injection, allows the forward and reverse processes to factorize across domains. This decomposition enables the reuse of domain-specific diffusion implementations (e.g., DDPM for continuous variables, D3PM for discrete categorical variables) while learning a joint denoising network. The overall loss is a weighted sum of domain-specific losses, with empirical weights (λ_k) assigned to control the importance of each domain.
 For semantic labels (G_z), both discrete (categorical) and continuous (one-hot encoding with Gaussian noise) parameterizations are explored. For edges (G_e), both discrete (binary) and continuous (relaxation to `[-1, 1]` with Gaussian noise) parameterizations are considered.

2. **Denoising Network Architecture:**
 The denoising network takes the noisy graph G_t at time t and predicts the parameters for the reverse diffusion process (e.g., categorical distributions for discrete variables, Gaussian means for continuous variables).
 - **Shape Feature Latent Space:** The 2D room shape (s_i_t) is represented in the latent space of a pre-trained 2D point cloud autoencoder, with the latent space regularized to `[-1, 1]`.
 - **Feature Encoder:** Node inputs combine semantic labels and geometric features. Discrete features (labels, edges) are encoded either as one-hot vectors processed by an MLP or as learned embeddings. Continuous features are processed by an MLP. These embeddings are concatenated to form node embeddings.
 - **Graph Transformer Backbone:** A fully connected graph transformer, adapted from DiGress, serves as the backbone to capture pairwise interactions and maintain permutation-invariance.
 - **Feature Extractors:** Domain-specific MLPs are used to extract the reverse-process parameters for each domain.

3. **Shape Post-processing:**
 The predicted latent shape feature (s_i_0) is decoded into a 2D point cloud. This point cloud undergoes a post-processing pipeline to convert it into a clean, closed polygon:
 - Outlier removal: Points with nearest-neighbor distances exceeding a threshold (0.5 m) are removed.
 - Boundary ordering: An approximate Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) is solved on the remaining points to recover a cyclic vertex ordering.
 - Boundary simplification: The Ramer–Douglas–Peucker algorithm (with 0.5 m tolerance) is applied to simplify the ordered polyline.
 - Boundary denoising (3D-FRONT only): For the 3D-FRONT dataset, an additional step projects the simplified polygon to an axis-aligned one using a least-squares fit with Manhattan-edge constraints, formulated as a mixed-integer quadratic program (MIQP).

4. **Partial Conditioning:**
 The framework is designed to handle partial observations at test time without retraining.
 - Known (fully explored) rooms (G_known_0) are corrupted by the standard forward diffusion process (G_known_t).
 - Unknown regions are predicted by the denoising network (G_unknown_t).
 - Partially explored rooms (G_partial_0) are handled by a function (ρ) that decodes the room boundaries, takes their union in boundary space, and re-encodes the result to update only the geometric feature (x), while semantic labels and edges remain fixed by masking.
 - The final graph G_t at each step is constructed by combining masked known, partially updated, and predicted unknown regions, ensuring all constraints are satisfied.

**B. Object-level Scene Graph Generation**
For the object layer, the authors integrate MiDiffusion, an existing mixed discrete-continuous diffusion model. This model synthesizes object layouts (semantic labels, positions, bounding boxes, orientations) independently for each room, conditioned on the parent room's geometry. MiDiffusion also employs a masking strategy, which is compatible with the room-level approach for handling partial object observations.

**C. Datasets and Baselines**
- **Datasets:**
 - **3D-FRONT:** Used for training, validation, and in-distribution testing. Consists of 5,107 synthetic apartment scenes with 36,284 rooms across 17 categories. Filtered for traversability and room size, and augmented with 90-degree rotations. Partial scene graphs for testing are generated by sampling random rectangular patches.
 - **Matterport3D (MP3D):** Used for out-of-distribution evaluation. Partial scene graphs are constructed from 6 single-floor apartment scenes using Hydra with RGB-D images.
 - **Real-world data:** Two apartment datasets collected using Intel RealSense cameras, processed by Hydra, for demonstration of the full pipeline.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Occupancy-based:** Lee et al. (SSC), which uses a discrete diffusion model for voxel occupancies.
 - **LLM-based:** AnyHome and Holodeck. AnyHome predicts room connectivity and then uses HouseGAN++. Holodeck directly predicts axis-aligned room corner coordinates. For LLM-based methods, partial observations are converted into text instructions.

**D. Evaluation Metrics**
The room-graph prediction is evaluated across four aspects:
- **Layout Realism:** Measured by FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) and KID (Kernel Inception Distance) between rendered top-down projections.
- **Graph Structure:** Measured by MMD (Maximum Mean Discrepancy) over degree and clustering coefficient statistics.
- **Geometry:** Measured by mean pairwise room overlap (IoU%) and mean distance between connected rooms.
- **Labels/Edges:** Measured by room-label KL divergence, edge-matrix L1/L2 differences, and room-count KL divergence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were structured to evaluate the proposed framework through an ablation study, scene completion tasks using both in-distribution and out-of-distribution inputs, and a demonstration of real-world integration.

**A. Ablation Study: Unconditioned Room-level Scene Graph Generation**
An ablation study was conducted on the 3D-FRONT dataset to compare different parameterizations for node labels (semantic categories) and edges (traversability) within the room-level scene graph generation module. Four variants were trained: Discrete Labels + Discrete Edges, Discrete Labels + Continuous Edges, Continuous Labels + Discrete Edges, and Continuous Labels + Continuous Edges.
- **Edge Representation:** Discrete edge representation consistently demonstrated superior performance across almost all metrics, particularly those related to graph structure and edge prediction accuracy. The authors suggest this is due to faster convergence and a better match for the binary nature of room connectivity compared to continuous representations.
- **Node Label Representation:** Continuous node labels showed improved performance in geometric aspects and overall layout realism. Discrete node labels, while slightly better for room-count and label distribution metrics, generally resulted in a trade-off.
- **Conclusion:** The model configuration with "Continuous Labels + Discrete Edges" ("Cont. L + Disc. E") was identified as providing the optimal overall trade-off across the evaluated metrics and was selected for subsequent experiments.

**B. Scene Completion**
The selected model ("Cont. L + Disc. E") was compared against state-of-the-art baselines for floor plan completion.

1. **In-distribution 3D-FRONT Partial Scene Graphs:**
 The model was evaluated on partial scene graphs from the 3D-FRONT test set, generated by sampling rectangular patches, consistent with the training setup.
 - **LLM-based Baselines (AnyHome, Holodeck):** These methods frequently struggled to satisfy geometric constraints imposed by the partial inputs. AnyHome produced layouts that deviated from the 3D-FRONT distribution, likely due to its backbone being trained on a different dataset. Holodeck’s reliance on axis-aligned rectangles limited its ability to generate diverse and realistic complex room shapes. Both methods also failed to produce outputs for a significant number of cases when language constraints could not be satisfied.
 - **Occupancy-based Baseline (SSC):** SSC showed greater precision in local room-boundary alignment. However, it sometimes generated predictions inconsistent with input semantic labels and could not predict room connectivity.
 - **Our Method:** The proposed method generated layouts that were quantitatively closer to the ground truth and more effectively captured complex room shapes. While occasionally exhibiting slight room overlap near boundaries (a noted failure mode), it substantially outperformed LLM-based approaches in layout realism and generally surpassed all baselines across most other metrics, including graph structure, geometry, and label/edge prediction. SSC was unable to provide metrics for graph structure or connectivity.

2. **Out-of-distribution MP3D Partial Scene Graphs:**
 The methods were tested on out-of-distribution partial scene graphs derived from the Matterport3D (MP3D) dataset, constructed using Hydra.
 - **Domain Gap Impact:** As anticipated, all metrics showed some degradation compared to the in-distribution results due to the domain gap between 3D-FRONT (synthetic) and MP3D (real-world).
 - **Our Method vs. SSC:** Our method demonstrated substantial outperformance over SSC across nearly all metrics, with the exception of IoU (where SSC's design inherently led to zero overlap). This indicated a promising generalization capability to inputs significantly different from the training distribution.
 - **LLM-based Baselines:** AnyHome and Holodeck continued to perform poorly on geometric prediction with out-of-distribution inputs, consistent with their performance on 3D-FRONT.

**C. Integration with Hydra and MiDiffusion**
The research demonstrated the integration of the proposed hierarchical scene graph completion pipeline with Hydra (a real-time scene graph construction system) and MiDiffusion (the object-layer generator).
- **Pipeline:** Hydra extracts partial room geometry and connectivity from robot-collected data. These serve as inputs to our room-level completion module. Subsequently, MiDiffusion synthesizes object layouts within each completed room.
- **Real-World Demonstration:** A qualitative example (Figure 1 in the paper) illustrated the pipeline's ability to take partial observations from a robot's trajectory, complete the floor plan, and generate a layered scene graph, including synthesized objects in previously unexplored areas.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for 3D scene graph prediction that addresses several limitations of existing methods, particularly in the context of robotic applications and partially observed environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancement in Hierarchical Scene Generation:** The work moves beyond generating flat object layouts or single-room structures by providing a comprehensive, top-down framework for synthesizing hierarchical 3D Scene Graphs (3DSGs). This includes generating room-level layouts (semantics, boundaries, traversability) and subsequently object-level layouts, which is more aligned with human understanding of environments and crucial for complex robotic tasks.

- **Robustness to Partial Observations:** A key contribution is the development of a mixed-domain graph diffusion model that can handle partial constraints at test time without requiring specific retraining on partially observed data. This masking-based strategy significantly improves the practical applicability of the system for real-world robotic exploration where complete scene data is rarely available.

- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstrated ability of the proposed method to generalize substantially better to out-of-distribution partial scene graphs (from synthetic 3D-FRONT to real-world Matterport3D) compared to baseline approaches is significant. This suggests a higher potential for deployment in diverse and novel environments, reducing the reliance on extensive and perfectly matched training data.

- **Direct Relevance for Robotics:** By focusing on predicting unexplored parts of an environment, the framework directly supports critical robotic tasks such as intelligent exploration planning, efficient navigation, and contextual object search. The lightweight, hierarchical 3DSG output is an ideal representation for robotic reasoning and decision-making.

- **Modular and Integrable Pipeline:** The modular design, integrating a novel room-level diffusion model with an existing object-level diffusion model (MiDiffusion) and a real-time scene graph construction system (Hydra), allows for the creation of end-to-end, comprehensive scene completion pipelines from raw sensor data. This modularity facilitates future improvements and adaptations of individual components.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework opens new avenues for research into joint room-object evaluation metrics and the direct integration of predicted scene graphs into downstream planning and control tasks for autonomous agents. This could lead to more intelligent and adaptive robotic systems capable of operating effectively in complex, dynamic, and unknown environments.
