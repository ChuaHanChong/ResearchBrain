# TAMS: Task-Aware Multi-View Adaptive Streaming for Wireless Telerobotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TAMS is a task-aware multi-view adaptive streaming system for wireless telerobotic manipulation. It infers the manipulation phase online from end-effector speed and gripper events, then splits the uplink budget across three camera streams using an offline-calibrated phase-by-view weight matrix with a per-stream bitrate floor. On a 6-DoF pick-and-place testbed it raises primary-view SSIM, cuts completion time from 68.9 s to 43.9 s, and lifts success from 48% to 71% at 3 Mbps.

## Problem

- Conventional adaptive bitrate policies react only to throughput, delay, and loss, ignoring what manipulation objective the operator is pursuing at each moment.
- Multi-view teleoperation shares one constrained uplink, yet resources are rarely split by the task-dependent utility of each camera view across manipulation phases.
- Semantic frame-analysis alternatives add computation unsuitable for resource-constrained robot platforms, while fixed prioritization hurts global context during coarse reaching and transport phases.

## Method

- Online ==task phase inference== labels six phases (Reach, Align, Grasp, Transport, Pre-release, Release) from smoothed end-effector speed, vertical speed, gripper transitions, and dwell timers, holding phase during pauses.
- An offline-calibrated ==normalized view weight matrix== over phases and the Eye-in-Hand, Global-Top, Global-Side views, built from eight raters ranking per-phase view necessity under imagined bandwidth scarcity.
- ==Bitrate allocator== reserves a minimum floor per stream, distributes the remaining measured uplink budget by phase weights, and degrades proportionally below the floor, driving three independent ==x264/RTP GStreamer== encoders.

## Results

- Averaged over six phases, primary-view SSIM reached **0.968** / **0.904** / **0.794** for TAMS versus **0.951** / **0.879** / **0.778** static and **0.944** / **0.862** / **0.764** equal across the three networks.
- Mean completion time at 3 Mbps was **43.9 s** versus **52.1 s** static and **68.9 s** equal, with a significant interaction term of **-23.47 s** and effect size d = **-1.38**.
- Success rate at 3 Mbps rose to **71%** versus **60%** static and **48%** equal; at 12 Mbps all strategies saturated near **90-92%**, showing gains only under scarcity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Coupling communication control to task state, not just network state, is the lever; the same bandwidth buys more task performance when spent phase-selectively.
> - Compact robot proprioception (speed, gripper transitions) suffices for phase inference, so semantic video analysis is unnecessary overhead for closed-loop streaming control.
> - Static prioritization of a close-up view backfires during coarse phases; benefits appear only under scarcity, so evaluate such policies at genuinely constrained bandwidths.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Telerobotics involves a human operator controlling a robotic system remotely, extending human capabilities into environments that may be hazardous or inaccessible. Applications include disaster response, industrial inspection, nuclear maintenance, and space operations. A critical component of telerobotic manipulation is the provision of timely and high-quality visual feedback, as operators rely on transmitted video to estimate object pose, align grippers, observe contact events, and monitor task progress. The quality and timeliness of this visual feedback directly influence manipulation accuracy, task efficiency, and operator workload.

A significant challenge in practical wireless telerobotic manipulation is the constraint of limited and dynamic uplink resources. Current industry standards, such as those by 3GPP TS 22.104, specify high communication service availability and specific end-to-end latency ranges for mobile robot remote control with video feedback. These challenges are exacerbated when multiple task-relevant cameras must share a single wireless uplink, which can experience bandwidth fluctuations, latency variations, and packet impairments. Prior research efforts have explored areas such as haptic-enhanced teleoperation and the development of network-aware benchmarks for telerobotic systems (e.g., TeleSim and VISTA). These advancements highlight the need for adaptive streaming policies that consider both network conditions and the real-time performance requirements of telerobotic tasks.

However, conventional adaptive bitrate streaming policies are typically driven by network performance metrics such as throughput, delay, and packet loss. These policies often do not explicitly account for the operator's current manipulation objective or the task-dependent utility of individual camera views in a multi-view setup. For instance, global views are more useful for workspace awareness and coarse motion guidance, while a close-up end-effector view is more critical for precise actions like grasping. This lack of task-awareness in resource allocation can lead to inefficient use of bandwidth. The TAMS system addresses this gap by developing a multi-view adaptive streaming approach that integrates task phase inference with bitrate allocation, thereby tailoring visual feedback quality to the immediate requirements of the ongoing manipulation task.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a real-time multi-view streaming system, named Task-Aware Multi-View Adaptive Streaming (TAMS), for wireless telerobotic manipulation operating under dynamic uplink constraints. The core motivation stems from the limitations of existing adaptive streaming policies, which primarily react to network conditions without considering the specific demands of the operator's current manipulation objective or the varying utility of different camera views throughout a task.

Specifically, the paper identifies two key issues left unresolved by conventional streaming policies:
1. **Lack of Task-Guided Bitrate Adaptation:** Bitrate adaptation is rarely informed by the operator's current manipulation objective. This means that valuable bandwidth may be allocated uniformly or based on static priorities, regardless of which visual information is most crucial for the current phase of the task.
2. **Inefficient Resource Distribution:** Uplink resources are not distributed according to the task-dependent utility of each camera view. Different phases of a manipulation task (e.g., reaching, grasping, transporting) require distinct visual cues, making some camera views more critical than others at different times.

To address these issues, TAMS is motivated by the following detailed objectives:
- **Real-time Task Phase Inference:** To infer the current manipulation phase from lightweight robot-side signals, such as end-effector kinematics and gripper events. This approach avoids the computational overhead of semantic analysis on video frames, making it suitable for resource-constrained robotic platforms.
- **Adaptive Bitrate Allocation:** To allocate video bitrate dynamically based on the inferred task phase, prioritizing the camera view most relevant to the operator in that specific phase. This aims to enhance fine visual detail where needed.
- **Preservation of Situational Awareness:** While prioritizing a primary view, the system aims to maintain a minimum baseline visibility for secondary views. This prevents the complete loss of global situational awareness, which could otherwise hinder overall task performance.
- **Lightweight and Interpretable Policy:** To design a runtime policy that is computationally efficient, interpretable, and relies on a fixed mapping between task phases and camera views calibrated offline. This contributes to its deployability and predictability.

The main contributions of this paper are formulated as:
1. **A task-aware formulation of multi-view bitrate allocation:** This involves defining wireless telerobotic video streaming as a task-dependent allocation problem where camera view importance varies across manipulation phases, ensuring optimal utility of bandwidth under dynamic constraints while preserving minimum visibility.
2. **A lightweight real-time streaming system for telerobotic manipulation:** This involves the design and implementation of TAMS, which integrates online phase inference from robot kinematics and gripper events with bitrate allocation across multiple camera streams, circumventing video frame semantic analysis.
3. **A human-in-the-loop evaluation under constrained wireless conditions:** This involves empirically validating TAMS on a 6-DoF telerobotic manipulation testbed, measuring improvements in primary view SSIM, task completion time, and trial success rate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

**A. TAMS System Overview**
TAMS is structured around three coupled real-time stages:
1. **Task Phase Inference:** Determines the current manipulation phase using robot-side signals.
2. **View Weight Determination:** Retrieves phase-specific view importance from a pre-calibrated matrix.
3. **Bitrate Allocation:** Distributes the available uplink budget among camera streams based on the inferred phase and view weights.

**B. Teleoperation Platform**
The system was implemented and evaluated on a human-in-the-loop teleoperation platform.
- **Robot Side:** A UFACTORY Lite 6 manipulator with a two-finger electric gripper. Three cameras provide visual feedback: an Eye-in-Hand (EIH) camera near the end-effector, a Global-Top (GT) camera, and a Global-Side (GS) camera.
- **Operator Side:** An HTC VIVE Focus 3 headset for visual feedback and a tracked six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) controller for input.
- **Software and Hardware:** The host workstation runs Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2), managing robot control, phase inference, video streaming, and the VR interface. An NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU accelerates video decoding.
- **Control System:** The 6-DoF controller enables Cartesian teleoperation. Smoothed controller velocities are mapped to robot commands, subject to velocity limits and workspace bounds. A deadman switch, clutching mechanism, and gripper control (open/close) are integrated. The control channel operates independently of the video uplink.

**C. Video Streaming Implementation**
Each camera view is managed as an independent GStreamer pipeline.
- **Sender Pipeline:** Frames from `appsrc` are converted, encoded (x264enc for H.264), packetized into RTP payloads, and transmitted over UDP.
- **Receiver Pipeline:** `udpsrc` receives RTP/UDP packets, `rtpjitterbuffer` manages jitter, `rtph264depay` unpacketizes, `avdec_h264` decodes, and `videoconvert` prepares frames for display.
- **Configuration:** All streams use consistent frame rate (30 fps), resolution (640x480), transport protocol (RTP/UDP), and decoder settings. TAMS independently updates the H.264 target bitrate for each stream, allowing direct encoder control rather than joint encoding.

**D. Task Phase Inference Module**
This module estimates the current manipulation phase using lightweight robot-side signals:
- **Inputs:** Smoothed end-effector speed ( `v_bar_t`), smoothed vertical speed (`v_bar_z,t`), and binary gripper state (`g_t`).
- **Manipulation Phases:** The pick-and-place task is segmented into six phases: Reach, Align, Grasp, Transport, Pre-release, and Release.
- **Inference Logic (Algorithm 1):**
 - **Gripper Transitions:** An open-to-closed gripper transition identifies GRASP, and a closed-to-open transition identifies RELEASE.
 - **Velocity-Based Conditions:**
 - If the gripper is open and sustained downward motion (exceeding `T_align` dwell time) occurs, it's ALIGN. Otherwise, it's REACH.
 - If the gripper is closed and sustained downward motion (exceeding `T_pre` dwell time) occurs, it's PRE-RELEASE. Otherwise, it's TRANSPORT.
 - **Pauses:** When end-effector speed falls below a threshold, the system holds the last inferred phase to prevent oscillatory bitrate changes.
- **Calibration:** Velocity thresholds and dwell times were calibrated offline on the Lite 6 testbed and remained fixed during experiments.

**E. View Weight Determination**
- **Concept:** A fixed view weight matrix, `W_p,v`, is used, where `p` is the phase and `v` is the camera view. Each row sums to 1 (`sum_v W_p,v = 1`), indicating the desired relative importance of view `v` during phase `p`.
- **Motivation:** The utility of camera views changes with the task phase (e.g., global views for coarse motion, EIH for fine manipulation).
- **Offline Calibration:** A small calibration sample (N=8) performed the pick-and-place task under unconstrained visual quality. Participants then rated the necessity and identified the most critical view for each phase.
- **Resulting Matrix (Table I):** The matrix assigns the largest bitrate share to a single dominant view per phase, while secondary views retain non-zero shares for situational awareness. This matrix is fixed at runtime to minimize computational overhead and maintain interpretability.

**F. Bitrate Allocation Module**
This module allocates the available uplink budget `B_t` among the EIH, GT, and GS streams based on the inferred phase `s_t` and the corresponding row of `W_p,v`.
- **Nominal Allocation:** `b_nom_v,t = W_s_t,v * B_t`.
- **Minimum Bitrate Floor:** A minimum bitrate `b_min` is enforced for each stream.
 - If `B_t >= V * b_min` (where V=3 is the number of streams), the floor is reserved, and the remaining budget is distributed proportionally: `b_target_v,t = b_min + W_s_t,v * (B_t - V * b_min)`.
 - If `B_t < V * b_min`, all streams are degraded proportionally: `b_target_v,t = B_t / V`.
- **Properties:** The policy protects the most important view, preserves minimum visibility for secondary views, and maps directly to independent encoder controls.

**G. Experimental Methodology**
- **Use Case:** A telerobotic pick-and-place task, requiring grasping a block and placing it in a container within 120 seconds, with success defined as correct placement within 90s and no more than two drops.
- **Experimental Design:** A 3x3 within-subjects design with two factors: Streaming Strategy and Network Condition. Each of 16 participants (novice teleoperators) completed three repetitions per condition, totaling 432 trials. Condition order was counterbalanced.
- **Streaming Strategies:**
 - **Equal Allocation:** Distributes bitrate evenly across all three views.
 - **Static Allocation:** Assigns fixed weights of (0.50 EIH, 0.25 GT, 0.25 GS) throughout the task.
 - **TAMS:** Uses phase-specific view weights from Table I.
- **Network Conditions:** Three progressively constrained uplink conditions (S1: High, S2: Medium, S3: Low) were emulated using Linux `tc`, affecting one-way latency, jitter, packet loss, and video bandwidth (12 Mbps, 6 Mbps, 3 Mbps, respectively). The control channel was unconstrained. A safety factor of 0.92 was applied to video bandwidth for encoder target calculation.
- **Measures:** Primary-view Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), task completion time, and trial success rate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Bitrate Tracking**
Experimental results demonstrated that the H.264 encoder bitrate successfully tracked the target bitrates determined by TAMS. During a representative trial under the 6 Mbps network condition, TAMS dynamically shifted bitrate to the EIH stream during Align, Grasp, Pre-release, and Release phases. Conversely, during Reach and Transport phases, bitrate was allocated more to the global streams (GT/GS). The measured RTP/UDP traces consistently followed these patterns, with only brief transients observed immediately after phase transitions. This confirms that the intended phase-specific allocation was effectively computed and realized by the video pipeline.

**B. Visual Fidelity (Primary View SSIM)**
TAMS consistently achieved the highest Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) for the primary view across nearly all manipulation phases and network conditions.
- **Average SSIM:** Averaged over the six task phases, TAMS attained SSIM values of 0.968, 0.904, and 0.794 under S1, S2, and S3 network conditions, respectively. These values surpassed those of Static allocation (0.951, 0.879, 0.778) and Equal allocation (0.944, 0.862, 0.764).
- **Performance under Constraints:** The advantage of TAMS became more pronounced under the bandwidth-limited S2 and S3 conditions.
- **Adaptive vs. Static Prioritization:** Static allocation, which fixed EIH as the primary view, sometimes resulted in degraded performance when a global view (e.g., GT during Reach) was the actual primary view according to the task phase. For instance, under S1 during Reach, TAMS achieved an SSIM of 0.960, compared to 0.918 for Static and 0.940 for Equal. This indicates that TAMS's phase-adaptive prioritization avoids the trade-offs associated with fixed prioritization strategies, ensuring higher quality for the currently most relevant view.

**C. Task Performance**
Improvements in visual fidelity translated into better task outcomes.
- **Task Completion Time:** Completion time increased with network degradation across all strategies, but TAMS mitigated this increase.
 - **S1 (High Bandwidth):** Mean completion times were 23.5 s for TAMS, 23.4 s for Static, and 24.7 s for Equal. No statistically significant difference was observed.
 - **S2 (Medium Bandwidth):** TAMS achieved a mean completion time of 28.1 s, outperforming Static (31.7 s) and Equal (34.8 s).
 - **S3 (Low Bandwidth):** TAMS demonstrated a mean completion time of 43.9 s, significantly lower than Static (52.1 s) and Equal (68.9 s). A linear mixed-effects model indicated a significant TAMS×S3 interaction (β̂ = −23.47 s, SE = 3.22, p < .001), with large effect sizes (d = −1.38 for S3, d = −0.72 for S2).
- **Trial Success Rate:** A consistent pattern of higher success rates for TAMS was observed, especially under challenging conditions.
 - **S1:** All strategies performed near ceiling (90–92%).
 - **S2:** TAMS achieved an 88% success rate, compared to 79% for Static and 73% for Equal.
 - **S3:** TAMS achieved a 71% success rate, significantly outperforming Static (60%) and Equal (48%). This represents a 23 percentage point improvement over Equal allocation under the most constrained network condition.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TAMS system addresses a fundamental challenge in wireless telerobotic manipulation by demonstrating the efficacy of task-aware multi-view adaptive streaming. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Improved Efficiency and Reliability in Telerobotics:** By dynamically allocating bandwidth based on the current manipulation phase, TAMS demonstrably reduces task completion times and increases trial success rates, particularly in environments with limited and fluctuating network resources. This suggests that task-aware communication can enhance the operational efficiency and reliability of remote robotic operations in critical applications such as disaster response, remote maintenance, and space exploration.
- **Advancement in Adaptive Streaming Policies:** The work moves beyond conventional network-driven adaptive bitrate streaming by explicitly integrating task-level context. This represents a methodological advancement, highlighting that the utility of visual feedback is not solely determined by network quality but also by the specific information required for the ongoing task.
- **Practicality for Resource-Constrained Systems:** The design of TAMS emphasizes lightweight task phase inference using robot-side kinematics and gripper events, rather than computationally intensive semantic analysis of video frames. Combined with an offline-calibrated, fixed view weight matrix, this approach yields a real-time, interpretable, and resource-efficient system suitable for deployment on robotic platforms with limited processing power.
- **Enhanced Operator Experience:** By prioritizing the most relevant camera view while maintaining baseline visibility from others, TAMS aims to provide operators with the most critical visual information at each stage of a task. This can potentially reduce cognitive load and improve decision-making under stress, contributing to a more intuitive and effective teleoperation experience.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays groundwork for several future research directions. These include exploring online adaptation of view weights (beyond offline calibration), developing richer task inference mechanisms, validating the system with real mobile network traces (beyond emulation), and conducting larger-scale user studies. These extensions could further enhance the adaptability and robustness of task-aware streaming systems.
- **Generalizability of Task-Aware Resource Allocation:** The core principle of allocating communication resources based on dynamic task requirements could be applicable to other cyber-physical systems involving multi-stream data transmission where different information streams hold varying importance at different operational stages.
