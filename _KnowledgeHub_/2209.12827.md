# Advanced Skills by Learning Locomotion and Local Navigation End-to-End

> [!summary] Summary
> A new deep reinforcement learning approach for legged robots optimizes for reaching a target position within a timeframe, with task success evaluated at episode end. This enables robots to learn advanced locomotion and local navigation skills, such as jumping over 1.2m gaps and climbing 0.95m pits, significantly surpassing traditional velocity-tracking methods.

## Problem

- Traditional hierarchical control for legged robots decomposes navigation into sub-tasks (path planning, velocity tracking), limiting the robot's solution space and hindering complex dynamic maneuvers.
- Velocity tracking objectives for locomotion controllers implicitly bias policies towards specific gaits (e.g., trotting), preventing the emergence of diverse or energy-efficient behaviors.
- Continuous position tracking tasks typically encourage overly aggressive "bang-bang" motions, requiring extensive tuning with additional penalties to achieve controlled movement.

## Method

- An ==end-to-end deep reinforcement learning== framework, trained in massively parallel simulation using ==PPO==, generates a neural network policy for locomotion and local navigation.
- A novel ==position-based task formulation== specifies a 3D target location and the remaining time to reach it, with the primary success reward applied ==sparsely at the end of the episode==.
- The reward function combines this ==sparse task completion reward== with continuous ==penalties== for undesirable motion qualities (e.g., joint accelerations, torques) and a temporary ==exploration bias==.

## Results

- The final position tracking approach (`P_f`) significantly increased maximum solvable terrain difficulties, achieving jumps over **1.2m** gaps (**700%** improvement) and climbs out of **0.95m** pits (**850%** improvement) compared to velocity tracking.
- A novel, asymmetric three-phased gait emerged, demonstrating improved energy efficiency at lower speeds and enabling dynamic maneuvers like jumping and climbing without requiring explicit air-time rewards.
- Learned policies successfully transferred to a real ANYmal quadrupedal robot, exhibiting high-speed stair climbing, gap jumping (**0.6m**), and advanced obstacle navigation, validating the sim-to-real capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reformulating the task objective from continuous command tracking to a sparse, time-dependent goal allows the robot to explore a much broader solution space, leading to the emergence of complex, dynamic skills.
> - Decoupling the main task reward from continuous motion quality penalties enables the policy to optimize for both goal achievement and efficient, smooth movement without direct conflicts.
> - The explicit time-to-target component in the command is crucial for preventing aggressive "bang-bang" behaviors, implicitly learning local speed planning, and facilitating nuanced movement strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics aims to enable robots to navigate complex, unstructured environments that are often inaccessible to wheeled robots. A common approach in this domain involves decomposing the navigation problem into several sequential sub-tasks: high-level path planning, mid-level path following, and low-level locomotion control. Typically, the locomotion controller is tasked with accurately tracking a commanded velocity, which is provided by the path-following module. This modular design has been widely adopted in both model-based control methods, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) and trajectory optimization, and more recently, in learned locomotion controllers using techniques like imitation learning and deep reinforcement learning (DRL).

Despite significant advancements, particularly with DRL enabling robust locomotion in diverse real-world settings (e.g., on stairs, rocks, roots), these decoupled approaches often impose implicit constraints on the robot's behavior. For instance, requiring a locomotion policy to maintain a constant base velocity can bias it towards specific gaits, such as trotting in quadrupedal robots, even when other gaits might be more energy-efficient or suitable for dynamic maneuvers. Furthermore, adherence to a pre-computed path or constant velocity command can hinder the robot's ability to execute agile movements like jumping over gaps, climbing, or modulating speed for optimal obstacle traversal. Such scenarios might necessitate temporary deviations from a planned path or changes in velocity that would otherwise incur high penalties in traditional velocity-tracking schemes.

Previous works have explored agile locomotion through trajectory optimization, which can directly generate joint commands for tasks like jumping or climbing. However, these methods are computationally intensive and may lack the robustness of learned policies for real-time deployment in unstructured environments. Other learning-based approaches have tackled elements of agile locomotion by splitting the problem into high-level planners and low-level trackers, or by training end-to-end policies for local navigation. However, these too frequently rely on velocity commands for the locomotion layer, thus inheriting the limitations associated with that paradigm.

This research addresses these limitations by proposing an end-to-end learning framework that redefines the task objective. Instead of focusing on continuous velocity tracking or path following, the work targets a more holistic goal: reaching a specified position within a given timeframe, with success evaluated primarily at the episode's conclusion. This approach seeks to provide the robot with greater autonomy in selecting its path and gait, thereby expanding the solution space and potentially enabling more complex, agile, and energy-efficient behaviors that are difficult to achieve with conventional, sub-task-oriented methods. It positions itself as an advancement in end-to-end learned robot control, emphasizing task-level objectives over immediate command tracking, and drawing parallels to the integrated decision-making observed in biological systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end control policy for legged robots that enables advanced locomotion and local navigation skills by reformulating the task objective. The authors aim to move beyond traditional hierarchical control approaches that decompose navigation into sub-tasks such as path planning, path following, and velocity-tracking locomotion.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in the prevalent sub-task decomposition strategies:
1. **Limited Solution Space:** By breaking down the navigation problem into distinct sub-tasks, the robot's capabilities are constrained. Individual tasks, particularly low-level velocity tracking, do not consider the full solution space of possible robot behaviors. This can prevent the robot from executing complex, dynamic maneuvers (e.g., jumping over gaps, climbing) that might require temporary deviations from a commanded velocity or planned path.
2. **Implicit Constraints on Locomotion:** Velocity tracking, a common objective for locomotion controllers, implicitly biases the policy towards gaits that maintain a constant base velocity, such as trotting. This can limit the emergence of more diverse or energy-efficient gaits, as observed in animals, which frequently switch gaits or modulate their speed. Policies are often forced to compromise between minimizing penalties (e.g., torques, accelerations) and accurately tracking velocity, hindering optimal motion quality.
3. **Inefficiency and Aggressiveness with Continuous Position Tracking:** While a simple continuous position tracking task might seem like an alternative, it typically encourages the robot to reach the goal as fast as possible (a "bang-bang" solution), leading to overly aggressive and potentially dangerous motions. Mitigating this with additional penalties (on velocities, torques) requires careful tuning and still penalizes beneficial behaviors like slowing down in dangerous situations.
4. **Need for Local Planning:** Traditional approaches require a separate local planner to generate trajectories. An end-to-end approach, if successful, could implicitly learn this capability within the locomotion policy itself, simplifying the control architecture.

To address these issues, the authors propose a novel position-based task formulation where:
- The robot must reach a target position within a specified time.
- The task's success is evaluated primarily at the end of an episode, based on the final distance to the target. This temporal sparsity means the policy is not continuously penalized for deviations from an optimal path or speed throughout the episode.
- The robot is given the freedom to select its path and locomotion gait to achieve the goal, as long as it reaches the target within the allocated time. This setup allows the policy to optimize for other factors, such as energy efficiency or smoother motion, without compromising the primary task objective.
- The time dependence in the command (remaining time to reach target) is critical. It allows the robot to plan its speed and motion over the episode, preventing the "bang-bang" behavior associated with continuous position tracking and enabling more controlled, nuanced movements.

Ultimately, the key objectives are to demonstrate that this end-to-end, sparse-reward, position-based formulation can enable a quadrupedal robot to:
- Achieve higher success rates on more challenging terrains, including those requiring complex maneuvers like jumping and climbing.
- Learn novel, potentially more energy-efficient gaits compared to velocity-tracking policies.
- Develop implicit local navigation capabilities, such as obstacle avoidance.
- Successfully transfer these learned advanced skills from simulation to a real quadrupedal robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) framework to train a neural network policy for end-to-end locomotion and local navigation. The training is conducted entirely in simulation, followed by deployment on a real quadrupedal robot without further adaptation.

**A. DRL Framework:**
The authors utilize Isaac Gym for massively parallel simulation, which allows for training with a large number of concurrent robot instances. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is selected as the DRL algorithm.

**B. Observation Space:**
The policy receives a comprehensive set of observations, including:
- Joint positions and velocities.
- Base linear and angular velocities.
- Previous actions.
- Terrain measurements sampled around the robot.
- **Commands:** These are crucial to the proposed approach, defined as the three-dimensional location of the target in the robot's base frame and the remaining time to reach that location. This time component is a key differentiating factor.
Sensor noise, mimicking real robot sensors, is added to the observations to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.

**C. Action Space:**
The neural network outputs target joint positions, which are then passed to the robot's motors. A proportional-derivative (PD) controller on the robot transforms these target positions into joint torques, directly controlling the robot's movement.

**D. Reward Structure:**
The reward function is designed with a hierarchical structure to encourage task completion while simultaneously optimizing motion quality.
1. **Task Reward ($\mathbf{r_{task}}$):** This is a sparse reward, active only near the end of the episode. It is calculated as an inverse squared distance to the target:
 $r_{task} = \frac{1}{T_r} \frac{1}{1 + ||x_b - x_b^*||^2}$, if $t > T - T_r$, else $0$.
 Here, $x_b$ and $x_b^*$ are the current and target base positions, $t$ is the current time, $T$ is the maximum episode length, and $T_r$ is the duration for which the reward is active. $T_r$ is set long enough (1 second) to ensure the policy learns to stop stably at the target, preventing unstable "last-moment" dashes. This structure decouples the main task from instantaneous path or velocity considerations.
2. **Penalties ($\mathbf{r_{penalties}}$):** These are continuously applied throughout the episode to encourage desirable motion quality and robot safety:
 $r_{penalties} = - c_1||\ddot{q}||^2 - c_2||\tau||^2 - c_3 N_c - c_4||a - a_{-1}||^2 - c_5 \sum_{feet} ||\ddot{x}_f||^2$
 The penalties target joint accelerations ($\ddot{q}$), joint torques ($\tau$), number of collisions ($N_c$), abrupt action changes ($a - a_{-1}$), and a novel penalty for feet accelerations ($\ddot{x}_f$) to reduce ground impact. A key aspect is that minimizing these penalties does not directly conflict with the main task reward, allowing the policy to optimize motion quality once the primary goal of reaching the target is satisfied.
3. **Exploration Reward ($\mathbf{r_{bias}}$):** A temporary directional reward is introduced at the beginning of training to bias the policy towards walking towards the target. It is removed once the $r_{task}$ reaches 50% of its maximum value, preventing it from constraining the final learned behavior.
4. **Stalling Penalty ($\mathbf{r_{stall}}$):** To counteract the effect of the PPO discount factor (which can incentivize delaying negative penalties), a small penalty is applied if the robot's linear velocity is below a threshold (0.1 m/s) while it is still far from the target (0.5 m). This encourages continuous movement or waiting at the target, rather than stalling mid-way.

**E. Terrains:**
The policies are trained on a curriculum of progressively challenging terrains:
- **Standard Terrains:** Slopes, stairs, random rectangular obstacles, and rough surfaces, derived from prior work.
- **New Challenging Terrains:**
 - **Gaps:** Requiring the robot to step or jump across a defined gap.
 - **Pits (Climbing Task):** Requiring the robot to climb out of a deep pit, potentially using its knees.
 - **High Obstacles:** Requiring the robot to navigate around obstacles in its path, learning local planning.

**F. Episode Process:**
At the start of each episode, a valid 3D target location is sampled for each robot (1-5m radius, 0.5m height above ground). The episode length is fixed at 6 seconds, with the task reward becoming active for the last 1 second ($T_r = 1s$). A terrain is considered successfully solved if the robot ends the episode within 0.5m of the target without crashing.

**G. Training Stability:**
The sparse task reward posed challenges for value function prediction. To mitigate instabilities, the authors made specific modifications:
- **Increased Batch Size:** Doubled the number of consecutive steps per robot per policy iteration, resulting in a batch size of approximately 200,000 samples (4096 parallel robots * 48 steps).
- **Reduced Episode Length:** From 20 seconds to 6 seconds.
- **Removed Value Function Bootstrapping:** As the task termination time is explicitly defined and provided as an observation, bootstrapping (which mimics an infinite horizon) was removed, leading to more stable training.
Training typically lasts for 2000 iterations, taking approximately 1 hour.

**H. Baselines for Comparison:**
The proposed approach (`P_f`: final position tracking) is compared against:
- **Velocity Tracking (`V`):** A standard baseline where the policy attempts to match commanded velocities, similar to.
- **Continuous Position Tracking (`P_c`):** The task reward $r_{task}$ is provided at every time step ($T_r = T$), without further tuning.

**I. Hardware Deployment:**
For sim-to-real transfer to the ANYmal quadrupedal robot, the simulation environment is augmented with a neural network that mimics the robot's series elastic actuators. To account for real-world motor limitations, torques are clipped based on an affine function of motor velocity. Commands on the real robot can be provided via actual position targets or through a joystick, which translates into "pseudo velocity" commands by scaling the remaining time to the target.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from extensive simulation experiments and hardware deployments, demonstrating the capabilities of the proposed end-to-end learning approach compared to baseline methods.

**A. Maximum Terrain Difficulty:**
The proposed final position tracking approach (`P_f`) significantly surpassed both velocity tracking (`V`) and continuous position tracking (`P_c`) in terms of the maximum difficulty of terrains that could be reliably solved (with a 95% success rate).
- **Stairs:** `P_f` successfully navigated stairs with a rise of 0.4m, an 80% increase over `V` (0.22m) and twice that of `P_c` (0.2m).
- **Slopes:** `P_f` handled slopes up to 48 degrees, a 35% improvement over `V` (35 degrees) and more than double `P_c` (21 degrees).
- **Random Steps:** `P_f` cleared random steps with a height of $\pm$0.2m, a 40% increase compared to `V` ($\pm$0.14m). `P_c` managed $\pm$0.16m.
- **New Terrains (Obstacles, Gaps, Pits):** This is where `P_f` demonstrated its most substantial advantages.
 - **Obstacles:** `P_f` navigated obstacles up to 0.85m in length, a 140% increase over `V` (0.35m). `P_c` failed to reliably navigate obstacles.
 - **Gaps:** `P_f` jumped over gaps 1.2m wide, a 700% increase over `V` (0.15m). `P_c` managed 0.65m.
 - **Pits:** `P_f` climbed out of pits 0.95m deep, an 850% increase over `V` (0.1m). `P_c` managed 0.65m.
These results indicate that `P_f` enables the robot to learn complex behaviors such as jumping, climbing, and sophisticated obstacle avoidance that were not feasible with the other methods.

**B. Energy Consumption:**
The energy consumption, proxied by the sum of squared torques, was evaluated across various velocities on flat terrain.
- At high velocities, all three approaches (`V`, `P_c`, `P_f`) exhibited similar energy consumption levels.
- At slow speeds, `P_f` and `P_c` generally outperformed `V`. The velocity tracking policy (`V`) consumed more energy at lower speeds, suggesting that maintaining a constant base velocity is more energetically costly in this regime.
- `P_c` slightly outperformed `P_f` at higher velocities in simulation, which the authors attribute to `P_c` being trained to run as fast as possible, while `P_f` was primarily trained with velocities around 1 m/s.
Hardware experiments confirmed similar trends, with `P_f` showing better low-speed efficiency than `V`. The continuous position tracking policy (`P_c`) was not transferred to hardware due to its tendency for aggressive motions.

**C. Emergent Gaits:**
The task definition significantly influenced the learned locomotion gaits.
- **Velocity Tracking (`V`):** Consistently resulted in a trotting gait, as is common in prior work, likely due to the emphasis on maintaining a constant base velocity. This often required additional rewards (e.g., for air time) to encourage larger steps.
- **Final Position Tracking (`P_f`, our approach):** A novel three-phased asymmetric gait emerged. The robot moved diagonally (e.g., front-left), with a sequence of contacts: back leg, then center legs, then front leg. This gait allowed for stronger base motion and larger, smoother steps, removing the necessity for explicit air-time or clearance rewards. The motion was qualitatively described as more dynamic and natural.
- **Continuous Position Tracking (`P_c`):** Also adopted a three-phased gait, but with an inverted contact schedule, moving diagonally towards its right-back side.
A notable artifact of the `P_f` policy was that it consistently learned to walk in only one fixed diagonal direction (e.g., front-left or back-right) across different training runs, preferring to turn rather than walk sideways or backward.

**D. Hardware Deployment:**
Policies trained with the `P_f` approach were successfully deployed on a real ANYmal quadrupedal robot.
- The robot demonstrated abilities previously challenging or impossible, including running on flat terrain, climbing stairs at high speed, jumping over a 0.6m gap, climbing onto a 0.55m box, and navigating around obstacles.
- Success rates on challenging terrains in hardware were sometimes lower than in simulation, partially attributed to the reliance on external state estimation and perception modules not specifically designed for such dynamic maneuvers (e.g., motion capture was required for climbing).
- The system supported natural joystick control, where joystick commands were converted into "pseudo velocity" commands by scaling the remaining time to a fixed target.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of legged robot locomotion and navigation by challenging the conventional paradigm of task decomposition and continuous command tracking. The findings highlight the impact of task formulation on the learned capabilities of reinforcement learning policies.

**Redefining Task Objectives:** The core contribution is the introduction of a position-based, time-dependent task reward that is evaluated sparsely at the end of an episode. This paradigm shift, by decoupling the primary task from continuous motion quality penalties, allows the robot's policy to explore a much broader solution space. It demonstrates that providing freedom in *how* a task is accomplished can lead to fundamentally different and more capable behaviors.

**Enhanced Agility and Dexterity:** The ability of the robot to successfully perform complex and dynamic maneuvers such as jumping over significant gaps, climbing high boxes using its knees, and proactively navigating around large obstacles represents a substantial advancement. These capabilities surpass those achieved by traditional velocity-tracking or even continuous position-tracking methods, indicating a new level of agility and dexterity for learned legged robot control.

**Improved Energy Efficiency and Natural Gaits:** The emergence of novel, asymmetric three-phased gaits that are more energy-efficient, particularly at lower speeds, suggests that the new task formulation enables policies to optimize motion quality beyond mere command tracking. The qualitative observation of more "dynamic" and "natural" movements aligns with the aim of achieving animal-like agility, without needing explicit rewards for gait characteristics like air-time.

**Simplification of Control Architecture:** By implicitly learning local navigation capabilities, the approach reduces the need for a separate, often complex, local planning module. This move towards a more end-to-end control policy streamlines the robot's control stack and could lead to more robust and responsive systems in unstructured environments.

**Broader Applicability:** The paper suggests that this re-thinking of task definition is not limited to locomotion but can be applied to a wider range of robotic tasks, such as fall recovery scenarios. This implies a potential framework for designing more effective reward functions in DRL for complex robotic behaviors, allowing policies greater autonomy in optimizing their execution strategies.

**Future Research Directions:** The work also identifies important areas for future development, including addressing training instabilities, overcoming the learned unidirectional walking bias, reducing reliance on high-fidelity state estimation and perception for extreme maneuvers, and extending the framework to handle sequences of waypoints. Addressing these challenges could further enhance the robustness and generality of the approach.

In summary, this research underscores that substantial improvements in robot agility and intelligence can be achieved not just through architectural changes or more data, but by fundamentally re-evaluating the objectives given to learning agents. It serves as an encouragement for the robotics community to explore alternative task formulations to unlock the full potential of advanced robotic platforms.
