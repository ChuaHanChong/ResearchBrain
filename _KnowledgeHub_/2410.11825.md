# Learning Smooth Humanoid Locomotion through Lipschitz-Constrained Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Simon Fraser University, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, UC Berkeley, and Stanford developed a method using Lipschitz-constrained policies to produce smooth humanoid locomotion. This approach enables zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on various real-world robots, demonstrating robust and stable movement on diverse terrains.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained in simulation often produce "jittery" or "bang-bang" control actions, which are infeasible for real robot actuators.
- Existing smoothing techniques, such as explicit smoothness rewards or low-pass filters, are often non-differentiable with respect to policy parameters, complicating optimization.
- Current smoothing methods typically require extensive and tedious manual hyperparameter tuning for different robotic platforms, hindering generalizability and ease of use.

## Method

- ==Introduces Lipschitz-Constrained Policies (LCP)== by imposing a Lipschitz constraint on the learned policy to encourage smooth action changes.
- Integrates this constraint as a differentiable gradient penalty into the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, directly penalizing the squared L2-norm of the policy's gradient with respect to observations.
- Trains policies solely in IsaacGym, a high-performance GPU-based physics simulator, and leverages Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA) for robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- LCP reduced Action Jitter from **42.19** (no smoothing) to **3.21**, achieving smoothness levels comparable to or better than explicit smoothness rewards in simulation.
- Policies trained with LCP successfully demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on four distinct real-world humanoid robots, including Fourier GR1T1, Fourier GR1T2, Unitree H1, and Berkeley Humanoid.
- The deployed policies maintained smooth and effective locomotion across varying real-world terrains and exhibited robust recovery behaviors when subjected to external perturbations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Bounding the gradient of a policy's output with respect to its input observations effectively encourages smooth action changes.
> - A differentiable gradient penalty offers a principled and efficient method to enforce policy smoothness compared to non-differentiable heuristics.
> - Applying the gradient penalty to the entire observation input (current state and historical observations) yields better smoothness and task performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of intelligent, human-like machines capable of autonomous operation is a grand challenge in robotics, with reliable mobility being a foundational component. This paper addresses a critical problem within the increasingly prevalent paradigm of learning-based robot control, specifically for legged and humanoid robots.

Historically, robot locomotion relied heavily on **traditional model-based control methods** like Model Predictive Control (MPC). While effective, these methods demand precise and labor-intensive modeling of system structure and dynamics, a complex undertaking for high-dimensional, unstable legged systems.

The emergence of **model-free reinforcement learning (RL)** offers a compelling alternative, providing an end-to-end approach that significantly reduces the need for meticulous manual design and modeling. RL has shown remarkable success in controlling complex robotic systems, including quadruped and humanoid robots. However, a major bottleneck for RL in real-world robotics is its data inefficiency, requiring a vast number of interactions. This necessitates the use of **sim-to-real transfer techniques**, where policies are trained in high-fidelity simulations and then deployed on physical robots. Common sim-to-real strategies include domain randomization, high-fidelity simulators, and teacher-student frameworks (such as the Regularized Online Adaptation, ROA, used in this paper).

A persistent challenge in sim-to-real transfer, particularly for RL policies, is the phenomenon of "jittery behaviors." Due to simplified simulation dynamics and idealized motor models, policies trained in simulation often learn discontinuous or "bang-bang" control strategies. These policies generate rapid, high-frequency action changes that real-world actuators cannot produce, leading to instability, high energy consumption, and failure to transfer.

To mitigate this, prior research has largely relied on two main **smoothing techniques**:
1. **Smoothness Rewards:** Incorporating penalty terms in the reward function for high joint velocities, accelerations, or energy consumption. While effective, these often require extensive, tedious manual tuning of hyperparameters for different robots and tasks, and their non-differentiable nature with respect to policy parameters makes gradient-based optimization challenging, often relying on sample-based methods.
2. **Low-Pass Filters:** Applying filters to the policy's output actions before execution. These can dampen or limit the policy's exploration during training, potentially leading to sub-optimal performance and requiring extra effort for new robotic platforms. Like smoothness rewards, they are generally non-differentiable with respect to the policy's internal parameters.

This work, "Learning Smooth Humanoid Locomotion through Lipschitz-Constrained Policies (LCP)," directly addresses the limitations of these existing smoothing techniques. By proposing a **simple, general, and differentiable method based on Lipschitz continuity and gradient penalties**, LCP carves out a new and more principled approach. While gradient penalties have been previously used in contexts like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for stability or in adversarial imitation learning to regularize discriminators, their application as a direct, differentiable regularizer for *policy smoothness* in reinforcement learning for real-world robot locomotion is novel and significant. This positions LCP as a valuable advancement in the quest for robust and deployable RL-based robot controllers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **develop a general and differentiable method for encouraging reinforcement learning policies to produce smooth behaviors**, thereby facilitating successful sim-to-real transfer and enabling robust locomotion control on real-world humanoid robots.

The key motivations driving this objective are:

- **Overcoming Jittery Behaviors:** Policies trained in simplified simulations often exhibit "jittery" or "bang-bang" control, characterized by rapid and large changes in actions between consecutive timesteps. Such behaviors lead to excessive torque demands that real robot actuators cannot meet, making direct deployment infeasible. The primary motivation is to find a principled way to mitigate these undesirable behaviors.
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Smoothing Methods:** Current techniques, such as explicit smoothness rewards (penalizing high joint velocities, accelerations, or energy) and low-pass filters on actions, suffer from significant drawbacks:
 - **Non-differentiability:** They are often implemented in a way that is not directly differentiable with respect to the policy's neural network parameters, complicating optimization within gradient-based RL frameworks. This often necessitates sample-based gradient estimators, which can be less efficient.
 - **Tedious Hyperparameter Tuning:** Both methods frequently require extensive, trial-and-error tuning of numerous hyperparameters to balance smoothness with task performance, a process that is time-consuming and platform-specific.
 - **Damping Exploration:** Low-pass filters, in particular, can overly restrict the policy's output space, hindering exploration during training and potentially leading to sub-optimal learned policies.
- **Establishing a General and Differentiable Technique:** The authors aim to introduce a method that is simple to implement, easily integrated into existing RL frameworks, and broadly applicable across diverse robotic platforms without requiring platform-specific tuning or extensive reward engineering. By leveraging differentiability, they seek a more elegant and efficient optimization process.
- **Enabling Robust Real-World Deployment:** Ultimately, the goal is to enable the development of locomotion controllers that are not only smooth but also robust to real-world disturbances, capable of recovering from external forces and traversing irregular terrains, making humanoid robots truly useful in complex environments.

In essence, LCP seeks to provide a principled, computationally efficient, and generally applicable solution to a fundamental problem in robot learning, bridging the gap between simulated training and reliable real-world performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of Lipschitz-Constrained Policies (LCP) revolves around imposing a **Lipschitz constraint** on the learned policy to induce smooth behaviors. This constraint is integrated into the reinforcement learning optimization process as a **differentiable gradient penalty**.

**Conceptual Foundation:**
1. **Lipschitz Continuity:** The paper introduces Lipschitz continuity as a mathematical property to quantify function smoothness. A function is Lipschitz continuous if its rate of change is bounded by a constant K (the Lipschitz constant). Crucially, a corollary states that if the gradient of a function is bounded (i.e., `||∇_x f(x)|| <= K`), then the function is Lipschitz continuous. This forms the basis for LCP: by penalizing large gradients of the policy's output with respect to its input observations, the policy's actions are encouraged to change smoothly.
2. **Motivating Experiment:** The authors conducted a simple experiment showing that policies trained with traditional smoothness rewards (which implicitly encourage smoothness) exhibit smaller gradient magnitudes compared to those trained without. This observation directly inspired the explicit regularization of the policy's gradient.

**Mathematical Formulation as a Differentiable Objective:**
- The ideal objective is to maximize the RL return `J(π)` subject to a constraint on the maximum gradient norm of the policy: `max_s,a ||∇_s log π(a|s)||^2 <= K^2`.
- Since calculating the maximum gradient norm across all states is intractable, this constraint is approximated by an **expectation over samples** collected during policy rollouts: `E_{s,a~D} ||∇_s log π(a|s)||^2 <= K^2`. `D` is a dataset of state-action pairs.
- To enable gradient-based optimization, this constrained problem is converted into an unconstrained one using a **Lagrange multiplier**. By setting the Lagrange multiplier `λ_gp` as a manually specified coefficient, the optimization objective becomes: `max_π J(π) - λ_gp * E_{s,a~D} ||∇_s log π(a|s)||^2`.
- This additional term, `λ_gp * E_{s,a~D} ||∇_s log π(a|s)||^2`, serves as a **gradient penalty (GP)**, directly penalizing the squared L2-norm of the policy's gradient with respect to the input observations.

**Reinforcement Learning Framework and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
- **Policy Architecture:** All policies are modeled as neural networks.
- **Optimization Algorithm:** Policies are trained using the **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm.
- **Training Environment:** Training is performed *solely in simulation* using **IsaacGym**, a high-performance GPU-based physics simulator.
- **Domain Randomization:** Standard domain randomization techniques are employed during simulation training to improve sim-to-real transfer robustness by varying physical parameters (e.g., base mass, center of mass, motor strengths).
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer Technique:** The framework leverages **Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA)**. ROA involves:
 - An encoder (`μ`) that embeds privileged information (`e_t`, such as robot dynamics parameters) into a latent vector (`z_μ`).
 - An adaptation module (`ϕ`) that learns to estimate this latent embedding (`z_μ`) solely from a history of proprioceptive observations.
 - The LCP gradient penalty term `L_gp(π)` is seamlessly integrated into the ROA training loss, as shown in the Appendix, which includes PPO loss and adaptation module losses. The total loss for ROA is modified to `L(θ_π, θ_μ, θ_ϕ) = -L_PPO(θ_π, θ_μ) + λ ||z_μ - sg[z_ϕ]|| + ||sg[z_μ] - z_ϕ|| + λ_gp L_gp(π)`.
- **Observations and Actions:**
 - **Observations (`o_t`):** Include gait phase (`ϕ_t`), steering commands (`c_t`), measured joint positions and velocities (`s_robot_t`), and the previous action (`a_{t-1}`).
 - **Privileged Information (`e_t`):** Input to the policy during training for robustness, includes base mass, center of mass, motor strengths, and root linear velocity.
 - **Commands (`c_t`):** Desired linear velocities (x, y) and yaw velocity, sampled randomly during training.
 - **Actions:** Policy outputs target joint rotations, converted to torque commands by PD controllers with manually specified gains.
- **Reward Curriculum:** A curriculum is applied to the cumulative discounted reward, dynamically adjusting the scaling factor for regularization rewards to encourage exploration initially and then refine behaviors later.

**Evaluation Platforms:**
The effectiveness and generality of LCP are demonstrated on a diverse set of real-world humanoid robots:
- **Fourier GR1T1 & GR1T2:** Human-sized, 21 joints (19 actively controlled).
- **Unitree H1:** Human-sized, 19 actively controlled joints.
- **Berkeley Humanoid:** Smaller (0.85m height), 12 degrees of freedom.

This comprehensive approach integrates a novel regularization technique with established RL and sim-to-real methods, enabling robust and smooth locomotion across various humanoid platforms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive experimental validation for LCP, comparing it against established smoothing techniques in both simulation and real-world deployments.

**A. Effectiveness for Producing Smooth Behaviors (Simulation):**
- **Quantitative Metrics:** LCP significantly reduces "jitter" metrics (Action Jitter, DoF Position Jitter) and improves smoothness metrics such as DoF Velocity, Energy Consumption, and Base Acceleration compared to policies trained with "No Smoothing."
- **Comparison to Baselines:** LCP achieves smoothness levels comparable to, or even better than, policies trained with explicit "Smoothness Rewards," despite LCP not directly optimizing these specific smoothness metrics in the reward function. For instance, LCP achieved an Action Jitter of 3.21, compared to 5.74 for Smoothness Rewards and 42.19 for No Smoothing (Table I(a)). This demonstrates that bounding the gradient of the policy is an effective proxy for encouraging overall smooth behavior.
- **Training Progress:** Figure 4 illustrates that LCP consistently produces low values for action rate, DoF acceleration, DoF velocity, and energy consumption throughout training, mirroring the performance of smoothness rewards.

**B. Impact on Task Performance (Simulation):**
- **Comparable Task Returns:** LCP achieved task returns (26.03) comparable to policies trained with Smoothness Rewards (26.56). This indicates that LCP effectively balances smoothness with the ability to achieve the primary locomotion task.
- **Superiority over Low-Pass Filters:** Policies using Low-pass Filters showed lower task returns (24.98), likely due to the damping effect that can impair exploration and hinder optimal policy learning.
- **Trade-off with No Smoothing:** While "No Smoothing" policies achieved the highest task returns (28.87), their extremely jittery behaviors made them unsuitable for real-world deployment (Action Jitter of 42.19), highlighting the necessity of smoothing.

**C. Effect of the GP Coefficient (λ_gp):**
- **Sensitivity to λ_gp:** Ablation studies on the gradient penalty weight (λ_gp) revealed its critical role.
 - Small `λ_gp` (e.g., 0.001) still results in some jitter, though significantly better than no smoothing.
 - Excessively large `λ_gp` (e.g., 0.01) leads to overly smooth and sluggish behaviors, causing a substantial decline in task return (from ~26 to ~16) and slower learning speeds (Figure 6).
 - An optimal `λ_gp` of **0.002** was identified as striking an effective balance between smoothness and task performance, showcasing that careful tuning is still required, similar to other regularization techniques.

**D. Gradient Penalty Input Components:**
- **Whole Observation vs. Current Observation:** Applying the gradient penalty to the *entire* observation input (current state and history of past observations) achieved superior performance compared to applying it only to the current observation. The latter can still lead to non-smooth behaviors due to changes in the history component of the input.

**E. Sim-to-Sim Transfer:**
- Before real-world deployment, policies trained in IsaacGym were tested in Mujoco, a different simulator. The results showed a slight decrease in task return for human-sized robots (Fourier GR1 and Unitree H1) but generally good transfer performance, providing confidence for physical deployment.

**F. Real-World Deployment (Zero-Shot):**
- **Generalizability Across Robots:** LCP-trained models were successfully deployed *zero-shot* on all four distinct real-world humanoid robots: Fourier GR1T1, Fourier GR1T2, Unitree H1, and Berkeley Humanoid. This is a crucial finding, demonstrating the method's ability to generalize across different robot morphologies with the same training setup (Figure 1, Figure 7).
- **Robustness to Terrains:** The policies maintained smooth and effective locomotion across varying real-world terrains: smooth planes, soft planes, and rough planes (Table III). The jitter metrics remained consistently low across these challenging surfaces.
- **Robustness to External Forces:** The robots demonstrated robust recovery behaviors when subjected to unexpected external perturbations, as evidenced in the supplementary video, highlighting the learned policies' resilience.

In summary, LCP proves to be an effective, general, and differentiable alternative to traditional smoothing methods. It enables robust and smooth locomotion policies for diverse humanoid robots, facilitating successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, a significant step forward for practical robot deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Lipschitz-Constrained Policies (LCP) represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly for legged and humanoid locomotion. Its potential impact spans both fundamental research and practical applications:

**1. A Principled and General Smoothing Technique:**
- **Novel Application of Gradient Penalty:** LCP pioneers the direct application of gradient penalties (previously common in GANs for stability) as a differentiable regularizer for *policy smoothness* in reinforcement learning. This provides a more mathematically grounded and elegant approach compared to heuristic smoothness rewards or non-differentiable filters.
- **Generality Across Platforms:** The successful zero-shot deployment on diverse real-world humanoid robots (Fourier GR1, Unitree H1, Berkeley Humanoid) is a testament to LCP's generality. This contrasts sharply with prior methods that often require extensive re-tuning or platform-specific design, making LCP a highly practical tool for robot engineers and researchers.

**2. Overcoming a Major Sim-to-Real Hurdle:**
- **Directly Addresses Jittery Behaviors:** The "jittery" or "bang-bang" control issue is a pervasive problem that often prevents RL policies trained in simulation from being deployable on real hardware. LCP provides a robust solution to this critical domain gap, ensuring that learned behaviors are physically executable and safe.
- **Facilitates Robust Locomotion:** By enforcing smoothness, LCP enables policies that are not only stable but also robust to real-world complexities like varied terrains and external disturbances, crucial for robots operating in unstructured environments.

**3. Enhancing RL Training Efficiency and Accessibility:**
- **Differentiability for Efficient Optimization:** LCP's differentiable nature allows for seamless integration into modern automatic differentiation frameworks and gradient-based optimization algorithms (like PPO). This can lead to more efficient training and potentially faster convergence compared to non-differentiable methods that rely on less efficient sample-based gradient estimators.
- **Reduced Manual Effort:** By replacing the need for meticulous hand-tuning of numerous smoothness reward coefficients or filter parameters, LCP significantly reduces the manual engineering effort required to train deployable robot controllers. This lowers the barrier to entry for developing complex robotic skills.

**4. Broader Implications for Robot Learning:**
- **Beyond Locomotion:** While demonstrated for locomotion, the principle of Lipschitz-constrained policies could potentially be extended to other robot control tasks where smooth and physically feasible actions are essential, such as manipulation, human-robot interaction, or delicate assembly tasks.
- **Energy Efficiency:** The results show that LCP can lead to lower energy consumption (Figure 4, Table I), which is a crucial practical benefit for real-world robots, extending battery life and reducing wear and tear on actuators.
- **Foundation for Complex Skills:** Establishing a solid foundation for smooth and robust basic locomotion opens the door for training more dynamic and agile skills (e.g., running, jumping, parkour) in humanoids, which the authors identify as future work. The ability to learn these complex skills reliably in simulation and transfer them to the real world relies heavily on the underlying control policy's smoothness.

In conclusion, LCP represents a significant methodological contribution that provides a simple yet powerful solution to a long-standing challenge in robot learning. Its generality, differentiability, and demonstrated real-world performance make it a valuable tool that can accelerate the development and deployment of advanced humanoid robot capabilities.
