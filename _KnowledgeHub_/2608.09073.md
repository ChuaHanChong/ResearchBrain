# Latent World Models with Monotone Planning Costs for Image-Goal Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> A latent world model for image-goal navigation: a frozen DINO encoder plus a trainable AdaLN ViT predictor rolls out future patch features, and CEM plans by cosine distance to the goal embedding. Two objectives, an autoregressive rollout loss and a Monotone Cost Ranking loss, close the teacher-forcing gap and make the cost rise with trajectory deviation, cutting orientation error 2.7x versus the same-encoder baseline.

## Problem

- Reactive navigation policies predict actions directly from observation and goal without simulating futures, so they cannot compare multiple long-horizon candidate plans at test time.
- Pixel-space video-diffusion world models plan over generated frames, which is computationally expensive and forces modeling of visual detail irrelevant to navigation.
- Latent baselines train with single-step teacher forcing, mismatching multi-step self-conditioned MPC rollout, and regression losses leave the cosine planning cost non-monotone.

## Method

- ==Autoregressive rollout loss== unrolls the predictor on its own outputs with an L2-plus-cosine per-step term, under a ==counter-curriculum== ramping horizon from 2 to 8 steps while annealing learning rate and batch size.
- **Monotone Cost Ranking**, a 1,000-step pairwise margin-ranking phase over AR(1)-perturbed action sequences at three noise levels, forces larger dead-reckoned trajectory deviation to incur higher embedding-to-goal cosine cost.
- ==AdaLN ViT predictor== conditions every transformer block on 4-D ==SE(2)== actions via per-layer scale-and-shift, replacing per-patch action concatenation, with ==RoPE== and causal masking over frozen ==DINO== patch features.

## Results

- On GNM the full recipe reaches **7.63** degrees AOE and **11.11** degrees MAOE, best overall, versus **20.27** for the same-encoder DINO-WM baseline and **14.57** for pixel-space NWM.
- Ablations isolate both objectives: rollout beats teacher forcing **9.12** versus **12.10** degrees AOE, and MCR lowers AOE for every base variant and both DINOv2 and DINOv3 encoders.
- Action-contrastive training with temporal permutation negatives degrades planning to **21.17** degrees AOE, baseline level; zero-shot Husky deployment succeeded in **5 of 5** trials versus **2 of 5** for OmniVLA.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prediction accuracy alone is insufficient for sampling-based MPC: the induced cost must order candidate plans monotonically, or gradient-free search converges to suboptimal actions.
> - Train the model under the exact self-conditioned regime used at inference; matching the rollout distribution matters more than the choice of frozen visual backbone.
> - Discriminative contrastive objectives can conflict with geometry-preserving predictive ones, since separating hard temporal-permutation negatives distorts the manifold planning costs depend on.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot navigation, particularly image-goal navigation, has seen significant advancements through learning-based methods. These methods generally fall into two categories: reactive policies and world-model-based approaches.

Reactive policies, such as ViNT, NoMaD, and large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models like OmniVLA, directly map current observations and a goal image to actions. While demonstrating strong generalization capabilities, these methods do not explicitly simulate future states or the consequences of actions, thereby limiting their capacity for long-horizon planning and comparison of multiple potential action sequences.

World-model-based methods aim to overcome this limitation by learning a model of the environment's dynamics to predict future observations under candidate actions. Navigation World Models (NWM) represent an early example, performing Model Predictive Control (MPC) over predicted video frames. However, pixel-level prediction is computationally intensive and models visual details that may not be directly relevant for navigation. DINO-WM addressed the efficiency concern by predicting future visual features in a latent space, specifically DINOv2 patch features, enabling planning in a more abstract representation. This approach aligns with the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) paradigm, which focuses on predicting future representations rather than raw observations. Recent work, such as X-Mobility, has further explored decoupled latent world models for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

Despite these advancements, existing latent world models like DINO-WM typically employ single-step teacher forcing during training. This creates a significant train-test mismatch, as MPC requires multi-step autoregressive rollouts where the model conditions on its own potentially imperfect previous predictions, leading to an accumulation of errors. Furthermore, the effectiveness of sampling-based planners like the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) hinges on a planning cost function that reliably ranks candidate action sequences. Standard regression losses (e.g., MSE or cosine similarity) do not inherently guarantee that the induced cosine planning cost will be monotonically related to trajectory deviation from the goal.

This work contributes to the landscape by proposing a latent world model that specifically addresses the challenges of rollout consistency and reliable cost function monotonicity within the image-goal navigation context. It builds upon the efficiency gains of latent-space planning (like DINO-WM) but enhances its predictive and planning capabilities through novel training objectives, aiming to achieve more robust and accurate navigation performance, including in real-world, zero-shot settings. The paper also investigates and reports a negative result regarding action-contrastive learning in this specific context, contributing to the understanding of objective function interactions in world models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and effective latent world model for image-goal robot navigation that can accurately predict future visual observations and reliably rank candidate action sequences for planning. The authors identify two specific requirements for such a world model when used with sampling-based planners like CEM:

1. **Rollout Consistency:** During inference, Model Predictive Control (MPC) involves multi-step autoregressive rollouts, where the world model predicts future states conditioned on its own previous predictions. Existing latent world models, such as DINO-WM, are often trained with single-step teacher forcing, where each prediction is supervised by ground-truth features. This creates a discrepancy between the training and testing distributions (exposure bias), leading to an accumulation of errors over multiple prediction steps during planning. The motivation here is to align the training regime with the inference regime to reduce this train-test mismatch, thereby ensuring predictions remain accurate even when conditioned on the model's own prior outputs over an extended horizon.

2. **Monotone Cost Landscape:** The planning cost, defined as the cosine distance between the predicted final embedding and the goal embedding, needs to reliably order candidate action sequences. For sampling-based planners, it is crucial that action sequences deviating further from a goal-reaching trajectory receive a higher planning cost. If the cost landscape contains local minima or is otherwise non-monotone, the planner can be misled into selecting suboptimal action sequences, even if the model's individual predictions are locally accurate. The motivation is to directly shape this cost landscape to be monotone, ensuring that increasing perturbations from the optimal path lead to proportionally higher costs, thus guiding the planner more effectively towards the true goal.

Additionally, the authors were motivated to investigate whether **explicit action-contrastive learning** could further improve planning by making latent space predictions more discriminative across different action sequences. This objective aimed to sharpen the distinction between optimal and suboptimal actions in the latent space.

Finally, a key underlying motivation is to achieve **state-of-the-art image-goal navigation performance** in both simulated benchmarks and through zero-shot deployment on a **physical robot**, demonstrating the practical applicability and generalization capabilities of the proposed latent world model.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research addresses the image-goal navigation problem, where a robot needs to reach a target location specified by a reference image using only RGB observations and on-robot odometry for action labels during training. Actions are continuous body-frame SE(2) displacements (∆x, ∆y, sin ∆θ, cos ∆θ).

#### 3.1. Architecture

The model comprises two main components:
- **Frozen DINO-family encoder:** A pre-trained DINOv2-ViT-S/14 or DINOv3-ViT-S/16 encoder maps raw RGB observations (O_t) into a spatial grid of patch features (z_t = ϕ(O_t)). The encoder weights remain fixed throughout training, serving as a robust visual feature extractor. Features are layer-normalized before use.
- **AdaLN ViT predictor:** A trainable causal Vision Transformer with Rotary Positional Encodings (RoPE) and Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN) serves as the action-conditioned dynamics model. It autoregressively predicts the next-step feature map (ˆz_t+1) conditioned on a context window of recent patch-feature maps and corresponding actions. Actions are encoded by a small MLP into per-layer scale-and-shift parameters that modulate each transformer block, directly integrating action conditioning into the prediction process. This predictor can be unrolled for H steps autoregressively to produce future embeddings (F_H_θ).

#### 3.2. Training Objectives

Training is divided into two phases: a base phase for autoregressive rollout supervision, followed by a Monotone Cost Ranking (MCR) phase.

- **Autoregressive Rollout Loss (L_rollout):**
 - The per-step prediction loss (L_pred) is a combination of L2 and cosine similarity terms: L_pred(ˆz, z) = ||ˆz - z||^2_2 + λ_cos (1 - cos(ˆz, z)), with λ_cos = 0.5.
 - Instead of teacher forcing, the model is trained with an autoregressive rollout loss over K steps. It starts from a short ground-truth seed sequence and then conditions subsequent predictions on its own previously predicted outputs. This directly mirrors the self-conditioned rollout used during MPC inference, addressing the train-test mismatch.
 - To prevent instability or collapse at long horizons, a counter-curriculum is employed. The rollout horizon K gradually increases from 2 to 8 steps as training progresses, with corresponding adjustments to the learning rate and batch size.

- **Monotone Cost Ranking (MCR) Loss (L_mcr):**
 - This phase directly enforces monotonicity in the planning cost landscape. Action sequences are generated by perturbing the ground-truth actions at three different noise levels (σ ∈ {0.05, 0.15, 0.35}) using AR(1)-correlated Gaussian noise.
 - For each candidate action sequence, its cosine planning cost (c_i) and its trajectory distance (d_i), defined as the mean L2 deviation of its dead-reckoned path from the ground-truth trajectory, are computed.
 - A pairwise margin-ranking loss (L_mcr) is then applied. For any pair of action sequences (i, j) where d_i > d_j, the loss enforces that c_i > c_j by a gap-proportional margin m_ij = max(0.02, 0.5 |d_i - d_j|).
 - The total MCR objective combines this ranking loss with the rollout loss applied to the ground-truth action sequence to maintain prediction quality.

- **Action-Contrastive Training (Investigated Variant):**
 - The authors also investigated adding an InfoNCE-based action-contrastive loss (L_act) as an auxiliary term to L_rollout.
 - The positive sample is a K-step rollout under ground-truth actions, while negatives are rollouts of the same scene's features under action sequences from other batch elements (cyclic permutation). The objective aims to make predictions for ground-truth actions distinct from those for incorrect actions. The paper reports this variant as detrimental to planning performance.

#### 3.3. CEM Planning at Inference

At test time, the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) is used for planning. Given current observations and a goal image, CEM samples candidate action sequences from a unicycle kinematic prior with AR(1)-correlated noise. These candidates are evaluated by performing H-step autoregressive rollouts using the trained latent world model. The planning cost for each sequence is the cosine distance between the pooled predicted final embedding and the pooled goal embedding. CEM iteratively refits a Gaussian distribution to the top-k elite samples. The best action from the final iteration is executed in a receding-horizon manner, with replanning at every timestep. No pixel reconstruction is performed during inference.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Dataset:** GNM dataset, a large-scale cross-embodiment robot navigation corpus.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** A held-out split of 1,316 trajectories is used. Our model and DINO-WM use CEM with a 6-step planning horizon and 4 context frames. NWM also uses CEM. Reactive policies use their native inference procedures.
- **Metrics:** Average and Mean Average Orientation Error (AOE/MAOE, in degrees) and Average and Mean Average Displacement Error (ADE/MADE, in meters) are used, with lower values indicating better performance.
- **Real-World Deployment:** Zero-shot deployment on a Clearpath Husky A200 robot equipped with a ZED2i camera, Velodyne VLP-32 lidar, and NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin. A ROS 2 Nav2 stack handles low-level execution and obstacle avoidance based on the model's predicted waypoints.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several key findings based on experiments on the GNM navigation dataset and zero-shot real-world robot deployment.

#### 4.1. State-of-the-Art Performance on GNM Dataset

- The proposed method, particularly the MCR variant with a DINOv2 encoder, achieved the lowest Average Orientation Error (AOE) of 7.63°, Mean Average Orientation Error (MAOE) of 11.11°, and Mean Average Displacement Error (MADE) of 1.64 m across all evaluated baselines.
- It demonstrates improvements over existing latent-space world models, reducing AOE by 2.7x compared to the same-encoder DINO-WM baseline.
- The model also outperformed pixel-space world models like NWM (AOE 7.63° vs. 14.57°) and competitive reactive policies such as OmniVLA (img) and NoMaD (GC) on orientation and worst-case displacement metrics. Reactive policies showed slightly lower ADE, which the authors attribute to their imitation learning objective favoring ground-truth paths, whereas their model optimizes goal-proximity.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Autoregressive Rollout Training

- Ablation studies consistently showed that autoregressive rollout training outperformed single-step teacher forcing. For the DINOv2 encoder, this led to a reduction in AOE from 12.10° (TF) to 9.12° (Base) before the application of MCR. This finding supports the hypothesis that matching the training distribution to the self-conditioned inference regime significantly improves multi-step planning accuracy.

#### 4.3. Impact of Monotone Cost Ranking (MCR)

- MCR provided the most substantial and consistent per-stage improvement across all variants and encoders. For instance, with the DINOv2 encoder, MCR reduced the AOE from 9.12° (Base) to 7.63° (MCR). This indicates that explicitly shaping the latent cosine cost landscape to correctly rank trajectory deviations is critical for reliable performance of sampling-based MPC.

#### 4.4. Encoder Choice

- While both DINOv2 and DINOv3 encoders demonstrated similar qualitative patterns in the benefits of rollout training and MCR, DINOv2-ViT-S/14 slightly outperformed DINOv3-ViT-S/16 when using the full MCR recipe (AOE 7.63° vs. 8.54°).

#### 4.5. Negative Result for Action-Contrastive Training

- Contrary to the initial hypothesis, the investigation into order-sensitive action-contrastive training (ACT) with temporal permutation negatives revealed that it consistently degraded CEM planning performance. With the DINOv3 encoder, ACT increased AOE to 21.17°, largely negating the gains from rollout training. Even when combined with MCR (ACT+MCR), performance remained significantly behind MCR alone. The authors attribute this to the contrastive pressure from distinguishing maximally hard negatives (permutations of the same action multiset), which can distort the latent geometry essential for accurate autoregressive rollout and cost-based planning.

#### 4.6. Zero-Shot Real-World Robot Demonstration

- The model was deployed zero-shot on a Clearpath Husky A200 robot in unseen indoor and outdoor environments.
- Qualitative results indicated that the proposed WorldModel produced more goal-directed trajectories and reached final observations that more closely matched the target views compared to the OmniVLA baseline. OmniVLA tended to generate overly short trajectories or move towards obstacles.
- In limited-scale physical trials (5 scenarios), WorldModel successfully reached the target in all five, while OmniVLA succeeded in two, suggesting improved real-world transferability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in image-goal robot navigation, particularly within the paradigm of latent world models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Improved Latent World Model Design:** The work addresses critical limitations of previous latent world models, specifically the train-test mismatch arising from teacher forcing and the unreliable nature of planning costs in an unconstrained latent space. By introducing an autoregressive rollout loss with a counter-curriculum and a Monotone Cost Ranking (MCR) objective, the paper provides a more robust framework for training planning-ready world models.
- **Enhanced Navigation Performance:** Achieving state-of-the-art results on the GNM benchmark, outperforming both pixel-space world models and leading reactive policies, demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed approach. This suggests that explicit future prediction and a carefully shaped cost function in latent space can lead to more accurate and reliable navigation behavior.
- **Efficiency in Planning:** By performing planning entirely within the latent feature space of a frozen encoder and avoiding pixel reconstruction, the method maintains computational efficiency. This makes it suitable for on-robot deployment where computational resources are often constrained.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization to Physical Robots:** The successful zero-shot deployment on a physical robot in novel environments highlights the model's generalization capabilities and practical utility. This reduces the need for extensive robot-specific data collection and fine-tuning, accelerating the deployment of navigation solutions.
- **Understanding Objective Function Interactions:** The systematic investigation into action-contrastive training and the identification of its detrimental effect, especially with order-sensitive permutation negatives, provides valuable insights into the complexities of training world models. This negative result suggests a tension between discriminative and geometry-preserving predictive objectives, opening avenues for future research into mitigating such conflicts through architectural modifications or specialized training strategies.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The principles of ensuring rollout consistency and shaping planning costs are fundamental for any model-based reinforcement learning or control system. This work contributes to a deeper understanding of these aspects for continuous action spaces and high-dimensional visual inputs, potentially influencing the design of more advanced robotic agents capable of complex tasks.

Despite its contributions, the paper acknowledges limitations, such as untested performance in highly dynamic, multi-agent environments and the inherent challenge of error accumulation over very long planning horizons in autoregressive models. Addressing these areas could further amplify the impact of this research.
