# AI4AI-Bench: Benchmarking LLM Agents in Algorithmic Design for Recursive Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> AI4AI-Bench isolates whether LLM agents can improve training algorithms, the algorithmic-design level of recursive self-improvement. Ten frozen repositories spanning ten algorithm families give an agent four hours on one GPU to rewrite training code; the patch is rerun for twelve hours and scored by a hidden evaluator on a scale where 0.1 is the shipped recipe. Across 29 configurations of six systems the mean is 0.166, the best 0.250.

## Problem

- Kaggle-derived suites are won by feature engineering and ensembling while the learning algorithm stays a library call the agent never edits, so nothing transfers forward.
- Post-training and ML-system benchmarks let data assembly and initialization dominate, and their scores conflate execution-level speedups with genuine changes to how the model learns.
- Prior algorithm-search work operates in compact spaces on short proxy tasks; opening one training script instead yields edits that behave like hyperparameter search and lose to CMA-ES.

## Method

- Ten frozen repositories, one per algorithm family: ==SFT==, ==agentic GRPO==, ==on-policy distillation==, ==Bradley-Terry reward modeling==, ==DPO==, ==DDPO==, ==NPO unlearning==, ==DiGress graph diffusion==, ==Model Soup==, ==OWL pruning==.
- A ==two-stage contract== separates four-hour exploration against a cheap proxy from a clean-start twelve-hour replay of the submitted patch alone, scored by an evaluator frozen in advance.
- A **progress-coordinate ladder** puts ten incommensurable metrics on one scale, 0.1 = shipped recipe and 1.0 = optimum, and a judge model sorts each diff into four run-side and four learning-side families.

## Results

- Across **290** cells the mean score is **0.166** and the best system, Claude Opus 5, averages **0.250**; **124** cells fall below **0.1**, worse than the shipped algorithm.
- Of **263** classifiable submissions, **141** stay on the run side and only **122** touch the learning procedure; those that do average **0.226** versus **0.126**, a gap of **0.100**.
- Raising reasoning effort lifts the learning-side share from **8%** to **64%** and the mean from **0.094** to **0.196**, while spend is uninformative: Opus leads at a **$181** median against GPT-5.6 Sol's **$434**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Measuring self-improvement requires asking what a patch touches, not only whether the score went up; line count cannot distinguish a new learning rule from a refactor.
> - Reasoning effort buys willingness to attempt the hard layer rather than better attempts there; the score roughly doubles while learning-side attempts rise eightfold.
> - The rare strong submissions all built an instrument first: a solver, a 500x faster evaluation rig, a localized bug, before changing the algorithm.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the broader field of artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the concept of Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) in AI systems. RSI refers to an AI system's ability to enhance the process that produces its successors, allowing subsequent systems to inherit these improvements. The paper delineates three levels at which an AI system can automate improvements:
1. **Systems engineering level:** Optimizing computational kernels, parallelism, and communication. This level is constrained by hardware limitations.
2. **Data level:** Improving data mixtures, synthesis, and filtering. This level is bounded by the finite stock of human-generated data and diminishing returns in loss reduction.
3. **Algorithmic design level:** Enhancing objectives, update rules, regularization, and schedules. This level is distinct because improvements here change the fundamental "exchange rate" between compute and capability, yielding compounding gains across all future training runs, including those that produce the next agent.

The authors argue that existing benchmarks do not adequately isolate and measure an agent's ability to operate at this critical algorithmic design level. Previous benchmarks often focus on:
- **Kaggle-derived suites:** These emphasize feature engineering and ensembling on fixed datasets, where the core learning algorithm remains untouched.
- **PostTrainBench/RSIBench-Data:** These focus on post-training optimizations or data-centric decisions, freezing aspects of the training stack.
- **MLS-Bench:** While improving ML system components, it conflates execution-level improvements with changes to the learning algorithm.
- **Autoresearch:** Although allowing agents to edit training files including optimizers, its tasks involve short runs on single scripts, and agent edits have been observed to behave more like hyperparameter searches.
- **Systems-focused benchmarks (e.g., CUDA kernel generation):** These aim to optimize how computation is mapped to hardware, not how the model learns.
- **Data-centric methods (e.g., data curation, instruction synthesis):** These modify the input data, not the learning process itself.

AI4AI-Bench distinguishes itself by explicitly targeting the algorithmic design level. It aims to provide a unified protocol where an agent's success is measured by its ability to modify the fundamental learning rules and objectives within existing research repositories, rather than by tuning hyperparameters, assembling data, or optimizing execution efficiency. This direct focus on algorithmic change is presented as crucial for achieving true compounding in RSI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a benchmark, AI4AI-Bench, that specifically isolates and measures an AI agent's capability in algorithmic design for recursive self-improvement. The motivation is rooted in the conviction that the algorithmic design level is the most impactful for achieving compounding gains in AI systems. A better objective or update rule, once discovered, fundamentally improves the efficiency of computation-to-capability conversion for every subsequent training run. Examples like Adam, layer normalization, DPO, and GRPO are cited as single innovations that have yielded continuous benefits.

The key motivations are detailed as follows:
- **Isolating Algorithmic Design:** The authors observe a gap in current benchmarking practices, where no existing suite exclusively evaluates an agent's ability to innovate at the algorithmic level. Many benchmarks conflate improvements from data, system engineering, or hyperparameter tuning with genuine algorithmic breakthroughs. AI4AI-Bench is designed to disentangle these, ensuring that an agent's score directly reflects its capacity to modify the core learning mechanism.
- **Enabling Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI):** The hypothesis is that for RSI to truly compound, most of the improvements must originate from advancements in algorithmic design. By providing a benchmark focused on this, the research aims to facilitate the development and measurement of AI agents capable of contributing to this crucial aspect of self-improvement.
- **Mimicking Real-World ML Scientist Workflow:** The benchmark protocol reflects the asymmetric workflow of a machine learning scientist: rapid ideation and preliminary testing (4 hours of agent exploration with a proxy metric) followed by rigorous, time-consuming verification (up to 12 hours of clean-start training with a hidden final metric). This setup encourages agents to develop scientifically sound hypotheses and implement robust changes, rather than merely performing quick, localized tweaks.
- **Providing a Comprehensive and Diverse Testbed:** The benchmark includes ten distinct research repositories, each representing a different family of training algorithms (e.g., supervised fine-tuning, multi-turn agentic RL, preference optimization, machine unlearning, weight averaging). This diversity ensures that the evaluated agents are tested on a broad spectrum of algorithmic challenges, covering various ML paradigms and problem types.
- **Measuring *What* Agents Change, Not Just *Whether* They Win:** Beyond just scoring performance, a critical objective is to classify the nature of the changes agents make. This allows for a deeper understanding of where improvements originate – whether from "run-side" optimizations (e.g., budgets, hyperparameters) or "learning-side" modifications (e.g., loss functions, update rules, supervision signals). This classification is intended to verify whether an agent genuinely engages in algorithmic design.

In essence, the research aims to create a rigorous, relevant, and transparent evaluation framework to push the boundaries of AI agent capabilities in fundamental machine learning algorithm development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The AI4AI-Bench benchmark is structured around a unified protocol designed to evaluate AI agents on their ability to improve existing training algorithms.

**Task Formulation:**
Each task is defined by a tuple (C, a₀, q, m, d):
- **C:** The repository's source code in its frozen baseline state.
- **a₀:** The base model the procedure starts from.
- **q:** An inexpensive proxy metric available to the agent during development.
- **m:** The final, authoritative metric used for scoring.
- **d:** The direction of improvement for `m` (↑ for higher is better, ↓ for lower is better).

An agent operates under an **exploration budget** (T_e = 4 hours on one B300 GPU), observing (C, a₀, q). Its output is a rewritten source code, C'. The agent has no access to `m` during exploration. Following this, the submitted C' undergoes a **verification budget** (T_v = 12 hours) in a fresh environment, yielding a model a(C'). The final score s(C') is computed as m(a(C')) by a fixed evaluator E, which is inaccessible to the agent. The procedure is identical for the baseline (unmodified C) to ensure a fair comparison.

**AI4AI-Bench Algorithmic Tasks:**
The benchmark comprises 10 distinct research repositories, each representing a different family of training algorithms (Table 1 lists these). The selection criteria for these tasks were:
1. They must implement a training algorithm actively used by its authors, not a simplified example.
2. A frozen starting model (a₀) must be provided.
3. The final metric must be recomputable reproducibly within a half-day budget on a single B300 GPU.
Two tasks (Weight Averaging, One-Shot Pruning) do not involve iterative training but still present algorithmic design challenges, and their protocols are adapted accordingly. The metrics across these tasks are incommensurable (e.g., aesthetic score, perplexity, solve rate), necessitating a standardized scoring method.

**Protocol:**
- **Exploration Stage (4 hours):** The agent works within the repository on one B300 GPU. It can read/edit code, launch training runs, and query the fast proxy metric (q) freely. The submission is solely the modified source code (C'), with no trained weights or cached state carried forward.
- **Verification Stage (up to 12 hours):**
 - For 8 training tasks: The submitted C' is executed from scratch. The three most recent valid checkpoints are kept, and the best-performing one (according to `m`) determines the score.
 - For 2 non-training tasks (Weight Averaging, One-Shot Pruning): The code is executed once to produce the final model, which is then scored. The 12 hours serve as a ceiling for this execution.
- **Evaluation Boundary:** The critical separation between agent-accessible proxy `q` and inaccessible final metric `m` ensures the agent cannot directly optimize for the final score during development.

**Baselines:**
The performance of an agent's submission (C') is compared against the repository's original, unmodified code (C). This baseline execution uses the identical procedure, hardware, budget, and evaluator. This strict comparison isolates the impact of the agent's code modifications.

**Scoring:**
To address the incommensurable nature of different task metrics and provide a dense reward signal for reinforcement learning, a normalized score `σ(x)` is used. Each task defines:
- **x_⊥:** Score of an uninformative model.
- **x_b:** Baseline score (s(C)).
- **x_∗:** Optimum attainable score.
- **φ:** A strictly increasing function that maps the raw metric to a linear utility (e.g., `−log` for perplexity).

The score `σ(x)` is calculated as:
- 0.1 * (φ(x) - φ_⊥) / (φ_b - φ_⊥) if φ(x) ≤ φ_b (scores below baseline, scaled to 0-0.1)
- 0.1 + 0.9 * (φ(x) - φ_b) / (φ_∗ - φ_b) if φ(x) > φ_b (scores above baseline, scaled to 0.1-1.0)
The score is clipped to, with 0 assigned if no model is returned. A score of 0.1 indicates performance equivalent to the shipped algorithm, providing a clear pivot point. All reported aggregate scores are means of `σ`.

**Experiments:**
The study evaluated 29 configurations across 6 AI systems (three GPT-5.6 variants, two Claude 5 variants, and Kimi K3), each attempting all 10 tasks, totaling 290 cells. The systems varied in their base models, harnesses (Codex, Claude Code), and "reasoning effort" levels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several key findings regarding AI agents' performance in algorithmic design:

- **Overall Low Performance:** The mean score across all 290 cells was 0.166, with the best system achieving an average of 0.250. On a scale where 0.1 represents the repository's shipped algorithm and 1.0 is the optimum, even the strongest systems closed less than a fifth of the distance to the optimum. A significant portion (124 of 290 cells, over two-fifths) resulted in models worse than the baseline (score below 0.1).
- **System Ordering and Compressed Range:** While systems showed a clear performance ordering (Claude Opus 5 > GPT-5.6 Sol > Kimi K3 > Claude Sonnet 5 > GPT-5.6 Terra > GPT-5.6 Luna), the entire range of mean scores remained within the bottom quarter of the scale (0.117 to 0.288 for the best single configuration). This indicates that the choice of system moved performance within a regime, but not into a fundamentally different one.
- **Limited Correlation with Cost/Effort:** The cost of exploration varied significantly (up to ninefold across systems), but this variation did not directly correlate with higher scores. The best-performing system, Claude Opus 5, had a median cost less than half of the second-placed system.
- **Nature of Agent Changes:** A detailed classification of submitted code changes revealed:
 - Of 263 submissions that made classifiable changes, 141 (53.6%) confined their modifications entirely to "run-side" aspects (e.g., training duration, hyperparameters, checkpoint saving, capacity).
 - Only 122 (46.4%) submissions touched "learning-side" aspects (e.g., loss function, supervision signal, update rule, training data).
- **Algorithmic Changes Drive Improvement:** Submissions that modified the learning procedure (learning-side changes) achieved a significantly higher average score (0.226) compared to those that only made run-side changes (0.126). This 0.100 gap indicates that the algorithmic layer is where substantial improvements are realized, yet most agents failed to reach it.
- **Reasoning Effort Buys "Nerve":** Increasing the "reasoning effort" level (a configurable knob for agents) primarily affected the *willingness* of agents to attempt algorithmic changes, rather than making the attempts themselves inherently better. The share of submissions that touched the learning algorithm increased from 8.0% at the lowest effort level to 64.0% at the highest.
 - Higher effort also led to more attempts, more lines of code edited, and higher exploration costs.
 - It also improved "completion," reducing the number of unusable or zero-scoring submissions.
 - Quantitatively, the mean score rose from 0.094 at the lowest effort to 0.196 at the highest, a doubling in score but still leaving performance modest. This suggests that effort encourages agents to engage with the impactful algorithmic layer, but the *quality* of those algorithmic changes remains limited.
- **Examples of Successful Algorithmic Interventions:** A few submissions that successfully engaged with the algorithmic layer demonstrated sophisticated problem-solving:
 - For one-shot pruning, an agent transformed it into a multi-stage training pipeline (new weight selection, distillation, fine-tuning), significantly reducing perplexity.
 - For weight averaging, an agent built a fast evaluation rig and then optimized combination coefficients using cross-entropy with Adam, outperforming simple averaging.
 - For multi-turn agentic RL, agents replaced GRPO with imitation learning from optimal solutions, achieving perfect scores by generating and fine-tuning on optimal trajectories.
 - A common thread among these successful interventions was the prior construction of measurable instruments or diagnostic tools by the agent.

In summary, AI agents in their current state primarily explore run-side adjustments or recover competent defaults. While reasoning effort pushes them towards exploring the more impactful algorithmic layer, their ability to design truly novel or significantly superior algorithms beyond the baseline remains limited.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AI4AI-Bench benchmark and its findings carry several significant implications for the future of AI research and the development of intelligent agents:

- **Pioneering Algorithmic Design Evaluation:** AI4AI-Bench is presented as the first benchmark specifically designed to isolate and evaluate an AI agent's ability to engage in algorithmic design. This contributes a novel and critical tool for measuring progress in a core area of AI self-improvement that was previously conflated with other factors. Its open-source release of tasks, evaluators, and scored submissions supports reproducibility and ongoing research.
- **Revealing a Gap in Current AI Agent Capabilities:** The results indicate a substantial gap between the current capabilities of even advanced AI agents and the desired ability to design significantly improved training algorithms. The finding that agents often default to "run-side" optimizations (e.g., hyperparameter tuning, resource allocation) rather than "learning-side" changes (e.g., loss functions, update rules) highlights a limitation in their capacity for deep algorithmic reasoning and innovation.
- **Quantifying the Value of Algorithmic Innovation:** The research empirically demonstrates that improvements at the algorithmic layer are indeed where the most significant performance gains are achieved. This reinforces the theoretical premise that algorithmic design is a crucial bottleneck and leverage point for recursive self-improvement, motivating further research into making agents more effective at this level.
- **Informing Future Agent Development:** The analysis of what agents *do* rather than just *how well they score* provides actionable insights. The observation that reasoning effort primarily increases the *willingness* to tackle algorithmic challenges, rather than immediately leading to highly effective algorithmic solutions, suggests that future agent development should focus not only on increasing reasoning depth but also on imbuing agents with better diagnostic capabilities and a more fundamental understanding of machine learning principles. The successful examples, where agents built intermediate diagnostic tools, point towards a path for improvement.
- **Guiding Research into Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI):** For the vision of RSI to materialize, AI systems must be able to improve the fundamental learning process itself. This benchmark provides a concrete measure for this ability. The current findings suggest that agents are still in the early stages of this capacity, acting more as "competent defaults" than true algorithmic designers. The benchmark offers a continuous measurement mechanism to track if and when this changes.
- **Implications for Machine Learning Research Automation:** The benchmark challenges the notion that current agents can broadly automate machine learning research at the algorithmic level. While they can perform sophisticated code modifications and experiment efficiently, their capacity for deep, mechanistic diagnosis and novel algorithmic invention appears limited. This highlights an area where human expertise remains distinct and where AI assistance can be most valuable if it can bridge this gap.

In conclusion, AI4AI-Bench establishes a foundational benchmark for a critical aspect of AI development: algorithmic design for self-improvement. Its initial findings underscore the significant remaining challenges in empowering AI agents to genuinely innovate at this level, while simultaneously providing a framework to measure future progress.
