# DITTO: Demonstration Imitation by Trajectory Transformation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Freiburg developed DITTO, a two-stage, object-centric framework that enables robots to learn complex manipulation skills from a single human RGB-D video demonstration. This approach successfully transferred demonstrated trajectories to a real Franka Panda robot in 79% of runs across 10 diverse tasks.

## Problem

- Robots often require extensive programming or large datasets to acquire new manipulation skills, hindering rapid deployment and widespread adoption.
- A significant 'embodiment gap' exists between human demonstrations and robot execution, making direct imitation challenging due to differing kinematics and morphology.
- Current imitation learning methods often depend on robot-specific demonstrations or multiple examples, which are less natural and more difficult to collect for non-experts.

## Method

- **DITTO** is a modular, two-stage pipeline: offline ==Trajectory Extraction== from human RGB-D video and online ==In-Situ Trajectory Generation== for robot execution in a new scene.
- Adopts an ==object-pose-centric representation==, defining manipulation actions as sequences of object poses relative to other objects, rather than absolute end-effector movements.
- Integrates state-of-the-art computer vision models (e.g., ==RAFT==, ==LoFTR==, ==Hands23==, ==Contact-GraspNet==) for robust object segmentation, pose estimation (via ==SVD== + ==RANSAC==), and grasp generation within the pipeline.

## Results

- DITTO achieved a **79%** success rate in warping and executing human-demonstrated trajectories on a real Franka Panda robot across **10** diverse manipulation tasks over **150 runs**.
- Offline evaluations showed RAFT yielded significantly more inliers (**84 times**) than LoFTR for intra-demonstration tracking, while LoFTR demonstrated higher precision (**79.4%** vs **56.4%**) for inter-demonstration re-detection.
- Robot execution failures were primarily attributed to kinematic constraints of the robot arm (e.g., joint limits, singularities) and challenges in precise grasping for intricate tasks, rather than issues with the core trajectory warping process.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An object-pose-centric trajectory representation effectively bridges the human-robot embodiment gap, enabling successful one-shot skill transfer from natural human demonstrations.
> - A modular architecture that leverages specialized, off-the-shelf computer vision models can achieve robust imitation learning without requiring complex end-to-end training.
> - Different correspondence methods are optimal for distinct stages: RAFT provides higher recall for intra-demonstration tracking with small movements, while LoFTR offers better precision for inter-demonstration re-detection with larger pose variations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

DITTO addresses a critical challenge in robotics: enabling robots to acquire new skills quickly and efficiently. It primarily operates within the domain of **imitation learning (IL)**, where robots learn from human or expert demonstrations rather than through extensive trial-and-error (reinforcement learning) or explicit programming.

The broader landscape of imitation learning can be characterized by several dimensions, including the source of demonstrations (robot vs. human), the number of demonstrations required (many-shot vs. few-shot/one-shot), and the representation used for actions (end-effector poses, joint trajectories, object-centric states). DITTO specifically targets **one-shot imitation from human demonstrations using RGB-D video**, positioning itself at the intersection of several challenging areas.

Historically, much of imitation learning research has focused on robot demonstrations, often collected through teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching. While effective, these methods present difficulties: teleoperation requires significant human operator training due to the embodiment gap between human input devices and robot morphology, and kinesthetic teaching can pose safety and occlusion challenges. DITTO seeks to overcome these limitations by allowing robots to **passively observe human demonstrations**, which are inherently more natural, easier to collect by non-experts, and can lead to more diverse datasets.

However, learning from human demonstrations introduces its own set of complexities, most notably the **embodiment gap**. Human kinematics, grasping strategies, and overall movement styles differ significantly from robots. Furthermore, visual observation spaces often differ (e.g., third-person human view vs. eye-in-hand robot view). DITTO tackles this by moving away from raw end-effector action representations and adopting an **object-pose-centric perspective**. This allows the demonstration data to be largely independent of the specific embodiment, making the transfer from human to robot more feasible.

Within the one-shot/few-shot imitation learning paradigm, DITTO distinguishes itself by its modular, pipeline-based approach. Other one-shot methods often rely on meta-learning, reinforcement learning, or learning generative models from large datasets of human demonstrations to infer intermediate representations. DITTO, in contrast, leverages recent advancements in various computer vision subfields—such as generic segmentation (e.g., SAM, Hands23), robust correspondence detection (e.g., RAFT, LoFTR), and off-the-shelf grasp generation (e.g., Contact-GraspNet)—to directly extract and warp trajectories. This integration of specialized, high-performance ancillary models is a key characteristic, differentiating it from end-to-end learning approaches that might struggle with the data scarcity inherent in one-shot learning. The paper's systematic evaluation of different combinations of these methods for correspondence and re-detection further highlights its contribution to understanding which tools are most effective for specific parts of the imitation pipeline.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the DITTO research is to significantly **improve the speed and convenience with which robots can acquire new skills**, thereby facilitating the broader adoption of robotic systems in diverse environments.

The specific problem DITTO aims to solve is **one-shot imitation learning from a single human demonstration**, provided as an RGB-D video recording. This goal is motivated by several practical and research-oriented considerations:

1. **Ease of Demonstration Collection:** Human demonstrations are inherently more natural and easier to collect than robot-specific demonstrations. Non-expert users can perform tasks, leading to potentially vast and diverse datasets without specialized robotic hardware or expertise. This contrasts with the tedious nature of teleoperating robots or the logistical challenges of kinesthetic teaching.
2. **Addressing the Embodiment Gap:** A major hurdle in learning from human demonstrations is the vast difference in kinematics, morphology, and sensory perception between humans and robots. DITTO aims to bridge this gap by adopting an **object-pose-centric representation** for manipulation actions. Instead of directly imitating human joint movements or end-effector trajectories, the robot's actions are defined relative to the manipulated objects or secondary objects (e.g., containers). This abstraction makes the skill transferable irrespective of the demonstrator's or robot's specific physical form.
3. **One-Shot Learning for Rapid Deployment:** The ability to learn a new skill from a *single* demonstration is crucial for applications requiring rapid deployment and adaptation to novel tasks or environments. It eliminates the need for extensive data collection campaigns or lengthy training processes common in many learning-based robotic systems.
4. **Modularity and Leveraging State-of-the-Art Vision:** DITTO is designed as a modular, two-stage pipeline. This modularity is a key objective, as it allows the system to leverage and integrate cutting-edge computer vision models for specific sub-tasks like object segmentation, pose estimation, and grasp prediction. This approach aims to build robust systems by combining the strengths of specialized models, rather than relying on a single, complex end-to-end learning architecture.
5. **Robustness and Generalizability:** By focusing on object-centric trajectories, the method implicitly aims for a degree of robustness to variations in scene setup and object initial poses, as the trajectory is warped to the live scene's specific configuration. The evaluation across a diverse range of 10 tasks, including pick-and-place, articulated object manipulation, and precise insertion, reflects the objective of demonstrating broad applicability.

In summary, DITTO's core objective is to make robotic skill acquisition highly accessible and efficient by enabling one-shot learning from natural human demonstrations, primarily by ingeniously translating human intentions (object manipulation) into robot actions through an object-pose-centric, modular, and vision-driven pipeline.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DITTO proposes a novel, modular two-stage process for one-shot imitation from single human RGB-D video demonstrations: **1) Trajectory Extraction** (offline pre-processing) and **2) In-Situ Trajectory Generation** (online for robot execution). The approach is fundamentally **object-centric**, representing manipulation actions as a sequence of poses of the manipulated object relative to itself or a secondary object (like a container).

**Stage 1: Demonstration Trajectory Extraction (Offline)**
This stage processes the human demonstration video to extract the essential object movements.
- **Object-Hand Segmentation:** The Hands23 model is used to compute segmentation masks for the manipulated object and any secondary objects (e.g., containers) in each frame. The authors acknowledge that Hands23, being a single-image processor, can sometimes yield suboptimal masks, necessitating manual verification in their experiments, highlighting an area for future improvement. If Hands23 fails to identify a secondary object, a heuristic based on SAM segmentation and point cloud distance is used.
- **Object Pose Extraction:** The core of trajectory extraction involves estimating the 3D pose of the manipulated object across successive frames. Due to small inter-frame movements and hand occlusions, the optical flow method **RAFT** is chosen for its high recall in establishing 2D correspondences between subsequent frames. These 2D correspondences are then filtered by the object mask, lifted to 3D using depth information, and a least-squares rigid transformation is computed using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), made robust to outliers by **RANSAC**. These relative transformations form the demonstration trajectory ($J_d$).
- **Hand Position Extraction:** At the moment the human demonstrator grasps the object, the 3D position of the hand (derived from the 2D hand mask center and depth) is extracted relative to the manipulated object. This "affordance" information is later used to guide robot grasp selection.

**Stage 2: In-Situ Trajectory Generation (Online)**
In this stage, the extracted demonstration trajectory is adapted and prepared for execution in a new, live robotic scene.
- **Re-Detection and Relative-Pose Estimation:** Given a live RGB-D observation of the current scene, the system first needs to locate and estimate the poses of the relevant objects.
 - The authors propose a modified version of **CNOS** (CAD-based Novel Object Segmentation), which uses DINOv2 descriptors and SAM masks, but replaces CAD model templates with cropped views from the demonstration video. This serves as a detection system to provide tight crops around the objects of interest, which is beneficial for local correspondence methods.
 - Alternatively, for cases with potentially larger rotations between the demonstration's initial object pose and the live scene's object pose, the semi-dense, global correspondence method **LoFTR** is used. LoFTR can establish correspondences without an explicit detection step, but it might have lower recall than RAFT in certain contexts. Based on their offline evaluations, RAFT is chosen for intra-demonstration tracking, and LoFTR is chosen for inter-demonstration re-detection.
 - This step estimates the 3D relative pose ($T_{d \to l}^o$) of the manipulated object (and $T_{d \to l}^s$ for the secondary object) from its initial pose in the demonstration to its current pose in the live scene.
- **Trajectory Warping:** The extracted demonstration trajectory ($J_d$) is then "warped" to the live scene.
 - If no secondary object is present, the relative pose change ($T_{d \to l}^o$) is directly applied to all transformations in $J_d$ to yield the live object trajectory ($J_l$).
 - If a secondary object is present, two potential trajectories are generated: one based on the manipulated object's live position and another based on the secondary object's live position. These two trajectories are then smoothly interpolated using **slerp** (spherical linear interpolation for rotations) and Gaussian-weighted linear interpolation for positions. This combines the relative motion with respect to both reference frames.

**Stage 3: Trajectory Execution on Robot**
Finally, the warped object trajectory is translated into robot-specific actions.
- **Grasp Generation and Selection:** The Contact-GraspNet model, an off-the-shelf system for 6-DoF grasp generation, is used to detect potential stable grasps on the manipulated object in the live scene. These grasps are filtered for reachability using inverse kinematics (IK). The final grasp is selected by minimizing the distance to the transformed 3D hand position extracted during the demonstration (affordance transformation), ensuring the robot grasps the object similarly to the human.
- **Motion Planning and Robot Control:** Using the selected grasp and the warped object trajectory, a full robot joint trajectory is computed. This involves calculating end-effector poses for the desired object states and then solving the inverse kinematics for the robot arm (using KDL kinematics and ROS MoveIt Solver in their setup). The grasp and the generated trajectory are executed separately, allowing for confirmation of the grasp. The authors note that the small relative pose changes in the warped trajectory can sometimes constrain the motion planner, potentially leading to failures.

**Experimental Setup:** The system is evaluated on a Franka Panda robot arm mounted on a mobile base, equipped with a StereoLab ZED2i RGB-D camera. Ten diverse manipulation tasks (e.g., pick-and-place, articulated object manipulation, precise insertion) are used, with five human demonstrations recorded for each task. Videos are subsampled to 11 observations for consistency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DITTO was rigorously evaluated through both offline experiments and real-world robot deployments, providing quantitative insights into its components and overall effectiveness.

**Offline Evaluation: Correspondence & Re-Detection**
This evaluation assessed the performance of different correspondence methods (RAFT, LoFTR) and the impact of the CNOS re-detection step on finding correspondences.
- **Intra-demonstration Tracking (Table Ia):** When tracking objects *within* a single demonstration (where movements are small and occlusions by the hand are common), RAFT demonstrated a significantly higher recall (84 times more inliers) than LoFTR, while maintaining comparable precision (88.2% vs. 89.4%). Using CNOS re-detection in this context *decreased* performance, as hand occlusions often led to re-detection failures. This validated the choice of RAFT for trajectory extraction.
- **Inter-demonstration Re-detection (Table Ib):** For establishing correspondences *between* the initial demonstration frame and the live scene (where objects might have strong rotations), LoFTR achieved higher precision (79.4% vs. 56.4% for RAFT) and showed better overall performance without CNOS. Interestingly, CNOS re-detection *helped* RAFT overcome its local flow limitations but *hurt* LoFTR by potentially generating incorrect mask crops. This led to the design decision of using LoFTR for re-detection in the live scene.

**Offline Evaluation: Trajectory Generation**
This experiment quantitatively measured the relative pose errors (rotation and translation) between the warped demonstration trajectory and a "pseudo ground truth" trajectory derived from another demonstration.
- **Relative Pose Errors (Table II):** All evaluated combinations of correspondence and detection methods yielded comparable errors, with rotation errors around 0.22-0.24 radians and translation errors around 0.038-0.044 meters. The authors correctly note that perfect alignment is impossible due to human variability, making these "pseudo-errors" a measure of consistency. The results broadly aligned with the inter-demonstration correspondence evaluation, indicating that the chosen methods for re-detection and warping performed as expected given the inherent challenges.

**Real Robot Evaluation**
The most critical evaluation involved deploying DITTO on a real Franka Panda robot across 10 diverse manipulation tasks and over 150 execution runs.
- **Overall Success Rate:** DITTO successfully warped the demonstration trajectory to the live scene in **79%** of the evaluation runs. This indicates a strong capability for core trajectory transfer.
- **Failure Analysis:**
 - **Trajectory Warping Failures (21%):** The primary failure mode during the warping stage was the inability to establish sufficient correspondences, often occurring when objects were heavily rotated or presented in significantly different views from the demonstration.
 - **Robot Kinematic Constraints:** For successful trajectory transfers, the majority of subsequent failures were attributed to the robot's kinematic limitations, which are more constrained than a human's. The robot frequently encountered joint limits, especially in tasks requiring specific wrist orientations (e.g., "paperroll" re-orientation task).
 - **Grasping Failures:** For precise insertion tasks (e.g., "hanoi ring," "plug charger") and pouring, grasping proved to be the most common failure point. This was largely due to small, intricate object features and potential inaccuracies in object pose estimation affecting grasp stability. Sensor noise also impacted grasp predictions on narrow handles for articulated objects.
 - **Motion Planning Issues:** For articulated object manipulation (e.g., "cabinet open," "drawer open"), in addition to grasp collisions, the inverse kinematics solver sometimes led the robot into singularities, hindering smooth execution.
- **Ablation Results:** The hand affordance-based grasp filtering did not yield a statistically significant improvement in overall success rates, though the authors suggest it might be more beneficial for tasks where grasp pose is absolutely critical. The CNOS re-detection method occasionally failed catastrophically when distractor objects similar to the target were present in the scene.

In summary, DITTO demonstrated a high success rate in transferring human-demonstrated trajectories to a robot in one shot. While effective, the real robot evaluations highlighted crucial remaining challenges, mainly revolving around the inherent differences between human and robot embodiment (kinematics, dexterity, grasping precision) and the robustness of perception components in highly varied or challenging scenes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DITTO research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic imitation learning, particularly in its focus on one-shot skill acquisition from human demonstrations. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Accessible Robot Programming:** DITTO's core achievement is demonstrating a robust method for a robot to learn complex manipulation skills from a *single, natural human demonstration*. This drastically lowers the barrier to entry for robot programming, potentially allowing non-expert users to teach robots new tasks. This accessibility is crucial for wider adoption of robots beyond highly structured industrial settings into more dynamic environments like service, healthcare, or small-scale manufacturing.

2. **Bridging the Embodiment Gap Effectively:** By adopting an object-pose-centric representation, DITTO offers an elegant solution to the long-standing embodiment gap between human demonstrators and robotic platforms. This abstraction allows the "essence" of the task (i.e., how objects move relative to each other) to be captured and transferred, rather than trying to mimic human joint movements, which are often kinematically incompatible with robots. This is a foundational step towards more generalizable imitation learning.

3. **Leveraging State-of-the-Art Computer Vision:** The modular architecture of DITTO, integrating advanced off-the-shelf computer vision models for segmentation (Hands23, SAM), correspondence (RAFT, LoFTR), and grasping (Contact-GraspNet), showcases an effective strategy for building robust robotic systems. This approach allows the system to benefit from continuous advancements in specialized vision sub-fields without requiring complete re-training of an end-to-end model.

4. **Extensive and Transparent Validation:** The paper's systematic evaluation, encompassing both detailed offline quantitative analyses of sub-components and comprehensive real-robot experiments across ten diverse tasks, lends high credibility to its findings. The commitment to open-sourcing code and data is particularly impactful, fostering reproducibility, encouraging comparisons, and accelerating future research and development in this area.

5. **Guiding Future Research:** DITTO not only provides a solution but also clearly delineates the remaining challenges. The identified failure modes—such as robot kinematic constraints, precision requirements for intricate tasks, and robust grasp planning under noisy conditions—offer valuable insights for the research community. This can direct future efforts towards improving motion planning for mobile manipulators, refining grasping strategies, and developing even more robust perception models (e.g., sequence-aware segmentation models). The suggestion for a standardized benchmark is a practical proposal to facilitate progress.

6. **Potential for Industrial and Domestic Applications:** The ability to quickly teach robots new tasks, such as assembling components, tidying spaces, or assisting in domestic chores, has immense potential. DITTO moves closer to a vision where robots can adapt to new environments and tasks with minimal human intervention, making them more versatile and cost-effective tools.

In essence, DITTO represents a significant step forward in making robots more adaptable and easier to "program" by non-experts. Its modular, vision-driven, and object-centric approach provides a powerful framework for one-shot imitation learning, setting a strong foundation for future advancements in human-robot collaboration and autonomous skill acquisition.
