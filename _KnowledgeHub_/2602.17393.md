# Contact-Anchored Proprioceptive Odometry for Legged and Wheel-Legged Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The Contact-Anchored Proprioceptive Odometry (CAPO) framework provides robust, purely proprioceptive state estimation for legged and wheel-legged robots by using contact anchoring and novel drift suppression mechanisms. It achieved a horizontal-loop error of 0.1638 m for a point-foot robot on a 200m trajectory and maintained vertical stability with less than 0.1 m error over repeated step-up/step-down cycles.

## Problem

- Exteroceptive sensors like cameras and LiDAR, while providing global feedback, are vulnerable in challenging environments (e.g., poor illumination, dust, dense vegetation) and incur high computational/installation costs.
- Purely proprioceptive methods suffer from rapid IMU drift in position and yaw, accumulation of position errors over long traverses (especially vertically), and spurious spikes in foot-end velocities from noisy joint encoders.
- Existing contact-based approaches struggle with accurately identifying contact conditions and mitigating slippage, leading to persistent position and velocity estimation issues.

## Method

- A **Contact-Anchored Proprioceptive Odometry (CAPO)** framework was developed, integrating IMU and motor measurements, that records world-frame footfall positions at touchdown and uses them as ==intermittent kinematic anchors== to suppress long-term position drift.
- A lightweight ==height clustering and time-decay correction== strategy was introduced to snap new footfall heights to previously observed support planes, preventing elevation drift over extended travel.
- An optional ==Inverse-Kinematics Cubature Kalman Filter (IKVel-CKF)== was employed to smooth noisy encoder-derived foot-end velocities, and a ==kinematics-based multi-contact geometric consistency== method was used to suppress long-horizon yaw drift.

## Results

- In Gazebo simulations, CAPO-CKE achieved a terminal position error of **0.6157 m** and a Z-axis MAE of **0.0072 m** on a flat-terrain loop, outperforming a LiDAR-based SLAM baseline's **2.078 m** error and **0.6503 m** Z-axis MAE.
- On a real point-foot quadruped, a 200m horizontal / 15m vertical mixed 3D loop was completed with a horizontal-loop error of **0.1638 m** and a vertical-loop error of **0.219 m**.
- The system demonstrated stable elevation tracking with **less than 0.1 m** height closure error over repeated step-up/step-down cycles, and the IKVel-CKF module prevented false contact events during simulated stair climbing, maintaining consistent height estimates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recording and continuously re-anchoring to world-frame contact positions during stance phases is effective at suppressing long-term horizontal and vertical position drift without external sensors.
> - A dynamic height-memory mechanism, which clusters and corrects touchdown heights to dominant support planes, significantly improves elevation consistency over varied and extended traversals.
> - Filtering noisy raw encoder velocities through an Inverse-Kinematics Cubature Kalman Filter (IKVel-CKF) greatly enhances the robustness of contact-anchored updates, particularly in dynamic and impact-prone locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reliable state estimation (pose and velocity) is a prerequisite for effective motion planning and stable locomotion control in legged robots. Existing solutions often rely on exteroceptive sensors such as depth cameras and LiDAR, coupled with Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) systems. While these methods can provide global state feedback, they exhibit vulnerabilities in challenging environments characterized by poor illumination, airborne particulates, or dense vegetation. Furthermore, their computational and installation requirements may be prohibitive for lightweight or cost-sensitive robotic platforms.

This context motivates the development of purely proprioceptive state estimation methods, which operate solely on internal sensor measurements. Early proprioceptive approaches frequently relied on IMU acceleration integration, which is susceptible to rapid drift due to biases, timing mismatches, and discrete sampling noise. More advanced methods incorporate leg kinematics to introduce velocity constraints from stance feet, leveraging the assumption that a foot in contact with the ground is stationary. A key challenge in these methods is accurately identifying valid contact conditions and mitigating slippage, which has been addressed through probabilistic contact estimation, impact detection, invariant Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) formulations, and learning-based measurement models for contact classification. However, even with improved velocity estimation, position drift can accumulate over extended periods, and foot velocities derived directly from noisy joint encoders may exhibit spikes due to quantization and numerical differentiation.

This research contributes to the field by proposing a robust, purely proprioceptive odometry system. It aims to overcome the limitations of IMU drift, long-term position drift, and noisy kinematic velocity measurements without relying on external sensors. The work provides a unified formulation applicable to a range of legged morphologies, including quadruped and wheel-legged robots, and introduces specific mechanisms for height correction and yaw drift suppression, thereby addressing several persistent challenges in proprioceptive state estimation for dynamic robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the limitations of existing state estimation techniques for legged robots. While exteroceptive sensors like cameras and LiDAR provide robust localization in ideal conditions, their performance degrades significantly in challenging real-world environments such as those with poor lighting, dust, smoke, or dense vegetation. These sensors also incur substantial computational overhead and impose specific installation requirements that may be undesirable for platforms with strict weight, power, or cost constraints. This creates a demand for reliable proprioceptive odometry systems that can operate effectively without external sensory input.

However, purely proprioceptive methods face their own set of challenges:
- **IMU Drift:** Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) are prone to accumulating errors over time, particularly in position and yaw, due to bias drift in accelerometers and gyroscopes.
- **Position Drift:** Even when velocity estimation is improved through kinematic constraints, position errors can still accumulate over extended traversal, especially in the vertical direction (elevation).
- **Noisy Kinematic Measurements:** Joint angular velocities, obtained from motor encoders, are often affected by quantization and discrete differentiation, leading to spurious spikes in derived foot-end velocities. This noise can propagate through the estimator, destabilizing contact-anchored updates.

In response to these challenges, the key objectives of this research are to:

1. **Develop a Unified Proprioceptive Odometry Framework:** Formulate a contact-anchored odometry system that relies exclusively on IMU and motor measurements (joint angles, velocities, and estimated torques). This framework is designed to be morphology-agnostic, capable of supporting various legged robots, including bipeds, quadrupeds, and wheel-legged platforms, by adapting constraints based on the currently contacting end-effectors.
2. **Implement Footfall Recording with Height Correction:** Introduce a mechanism to record world-frame contact positions at touchdown and utilize them as intermittent constraints during the stance phase to suppress long-horizon position drift. A lightweight height clustering and time-decay correction strategy is proposed to prevent elevation drift over extended traversal by snapping newly recorded footfall heights to previously observed support planes.
3. **Mitigate Encoder-Induced Velocity Spikes:** Employ an Inverse-Kinematics Cubature Kalman Filter (IKVel-CKF) as an optional module to estimate and smooth foot-end velocities directly from noisy joint angles and angular velocities, thereby providing a more stable velocity feedback for fusion and improving the robustness of contact-anchored updates.
4. **Suppress Yaw Drift with Kinematics-Based Consistency:** Reduce long-horizon yaw drift by enforcing multi-contact geometric consistency between body-frame kinematics and world-frame contact records. This mechanism is designed to continuously re-anchor heading during prolonged standing and provides a fallback yaw reference when IMU yaw constraints are degraded or disabled.
5. **Provide Robust Real-Robot Evaluation and Public Artifacts:** Validate the proposed estimator through comprehensive experiments on multiple real-world robotic platforms (quadrupeds) using long closed-loop trajectories involving both horizontal and vertical motion. Additionally, publicly release the complete implementation and representative test data to ensure reproducibility and facilitate further research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Contact-Anchored Proprioceptive Odometry (CAPO), is designed as a purely proprioceptive state estimator. It integrates measurements from an IMU and motor encoders (joint angles, velocities, and estimated torques) to estimate body pose and velocity, employing a unified formulation for various legged morphologies.

**A. Core Principle: Contact Anchoring**
The fundamental concept involves treating each reliable ground contact as a kinematic anchor. At each touchdown event, the world-frame position of the end-effector (foot or wheel) is recorded. During the subsequent stance phase, this recorded footfall position acts as an intermittent world-frame constraint, providing feedback that suppresses long-term drift in the robot's estimated position.

**B. Stance Selection and Touchdown Detection**
Contacting legs are identified through a joint-torque-based foot wrench estimation. The end-effector force is derived from estimated joint torques and the geometric Jacobian. A leg `i` is declared to be in contact if its vertical force component `f_W_i,k,z` falls below a configurable negative threshold `f_th`. A soft contact confidence `ρ_i,k` is also computed to weight auxiliary geometric cues and provide a smooth transition. A touchdown event for leg `i` is detected when it transitions from a non-contacting state to a contacting state.

**C. Footfall Recording and Body Observations**
1. **Footfall Record (`c_W_i`):** Upon detecting a touchdown, a world-frame footfall record `c_W_i` is initialized or reset to the current world-frame end-effector position `p_W_ee,i,k`. The horizontal coordinates are bootstrapped from the current body estimate, and the vertical coordinate is initialized with a ground-height prior, subsequently refined by the height-memory mechanism.
2. **Contact-Anchored Position Observation (`˜p_W_B,k`):** During stance, the footfall point `c_W_i` is assumed stationary in the world frame. The implied trunk position is calculated by subtracting the body-frame end-effector position, rotated into the world frame, from the stored footfall record: `˜p_W_B,k(i) = c_W_i - R_W_B,k p_B_ee,i,k`.
3. **Contact-Anchored Velocity Observation (`˜v_W_B,k`):** The stance velocity observation is derived from the world-frame chain Jacobian. If the contact is stationary, the body velocity implied by leg `i` is the opposite of its relative motion: `˜v_W_B,k(i) = -J_W_i,k `q_i,k`. For wheel contacts, the wheel rolling velocity `v_wheel_i,k` is added along the estimated rolling direction. The final position and velocity observations are obtained by averaging over all reliably contacting legs.

**D. Support-Plane Height Correction**
To mitigate elevation drift over long traversals, a lightweight height clustering and time-decay correction mechanism is introduced. The system maintains a set of support-plane height records, each with a height, confidence weight, and last update time. At each touchdown:
1.  Stale records are pruned.
2.  The raw touchdown height is compared to existing planes. If it falls within a configurable tolerance `∆_h`, it is associated with that plane.
3.  The matched plane's height is snapped to the new observation, and its confidence is refreshed with a time-decayed update.
4.  If no match is found, a new support plane record is created. The corrected height is then written back to the footfall record.

**E. End-Effector Radius Modeling and Wheel Contact Compensation**
1.  **Rounded Point Feet:** For point-foot robots with rounded feet, the foot-end radius is approximated as a rigid extension of the shank. An analysis quantifies the bias introduced by this approximation due to rolling contact, demonstrating that it remains small (e.g., <5mm in vertical displacement for a 0.03m radius foot over typical pitch ranges), thus justifying the simplification for computational efficiency.
2.  **Wheel-Legged Platforms:** For wheel-legged robots, the contact point propagates as the wheel rolls.
    - **Effective Rolling Angle:** The true rolling component is isolated by subtracting the apparent rotation induced by shank pitching from the raw wheel encoder increment.
    - **Slope-Aware Rolling Direction:** The rolling direction is initially projected from the body's forward axis onto the horizontal plane. When slope-aware propagation is enabled and sufficient contacts are available, a local support plane is fitted, and the rolling direction is lifted to the tangent of this plane.
    - **Contact-Point Propagation:** The world-frame contact record is propagated by integrating the effective rolling angle, wheel radius, and rolling direction, along with a lateral displacement component induced by shank roll.
    - **Velocity Augmentation:** The wheel-induced rolling velocity is added to the kinematics-derived stance velocity observation.

**F. Inverse-Kinematics Cubature Kalman Filtering (IKVel-CKF)**
To suppress spikes in hip-to-end-effector Cartesian velocities caused by encoder quantization and numerical differentiation, an optional IKVel-CKF module is employed. For each leg, it maintains a 6D state (relative position and velocity) and uses a constant-velocity process model. The measurement model maps this Cartesian state to joint angles and angular velocities via analytical inverse kinematics and differential inverse kinematics. A Cubature Kalman Filter (CKF) is used for nonlinear state estimation, providing numerically stable, Jacobian-free filtering. The output of IKVel-CKF, smoothed Cartesian velocities, can replace the raw Jacobian-derived velocities in the contact-anchored velocity observations.

**G. Kinematics-Based Yaw Estimation via Multi-Contact Geometric Consistency**
This method addresses IMU yaw drift, especially during prolonged standing. When multiple end-effectors are in stable contact, their relative geometry in the world frame is fixed. By comparing this fixed world-frame geometry (from footfall records) with the current tilt-compensated body-frame kinematics, yaw misalignment can be inferred. Pairwise relative vectors between contact points are formed and tilt-compensated using estimated roll and pitch. The yaw implied by each pair is computed from the difference in horizontal bearings, and these are aggregated using a circular mean weighted by contact confidences. This kinematics-derived yaw `ˆψ_k` is then used as a soft prior to correct the IMU yaw observation, with an adaptive gain `α_k` that increases during prolonged stable multi-contact to actively arrest IMU yaw drift. It also provides a kinematics-only fallback when IMU yaw data is unreliable.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed CAPO framework was evaluated through both physics simulations and real-robot experiments across various conditions and robot platforms.

**A. Physics Simulation in Gazebo (Unitree AlienGo)**
The simulated experiments compared CAPO, CAPO with IKVel-CKF (CAPO-CKE), and a LiDAR-based SLAM baseline on two closed-loop trajectories: a flat-ground loop with obstacle avoidance and a stair-climbing loop.

1. **Flat-Terrain Loop:**
 - Both CAPO and CAPO-CKE demonstrated robust elevation estimation and significantly reduced drift, particularly in the vertical axis, compared to the SLAM baseline.
 - Terminal position errors for CAPO-CKE and CAPO were 0.6157 m and 0.6232 m, respectively, while SLAM exhibited a terminal error of 2.078 m.
 - Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for the Z-axis was 0.0072 m (CAPO-CKE) and 0.0073 m (CAPO), significantly outperforming SLAM's 0.6503 m. This indicated that for flat-ground scenarios, the core contact-anchored position update effectively suppressed drift, with IKVel-CKF providing comparable accuracy.

2. **Stair-Climbing Loop:**
 - During stair climbing, where trunk vibration and impacts stress velocity feedback, raw encoder-derived joint rates led to pronounced velocity spikes.
 - CAPO (without IKVel-CKF) experienced a significant spike around 115.5 s, which corrupted a stance/landing decision, leading to an erroneous foot-contact update and persistent degradation in the Z-estimate.
 - CAPO-CKE, with IKVel-CKF enabled, successfully attenuated these velocity outliers, preventing the false contact event and maintaining consistent long-horizon height tracking. This demonstrated the robustness-enhancing effect of IKVel-CKF in dynamic and challenging terrains.
 - The SLAM baseline continued to drift in this low-texture environment, yielding progressively worse estimates.

**B. Real-World Experiments**
CAPO was tested on four quadruped platforms: a Unitree Go2 EDU and three Astrall robots (A: point-foot, B & C: wheel-legged) under a purely proprioceptive regime.

1. **Unitree Go2 EDU:**
 - **Flat-court Closed Loop (~120 m):** The robot completed a planar loop with a planar closure error of 2.2138 m. A mapping-only visualization (with scan matching disabled) showed a visible wall-orientation inconsistency, suggesting residual heading drift as a significant contributor to horizontal error without exteroceptive references.
 - **Repeated Step-Up/Step-Down (~8 m vertical loop):** The Go2 repeatedly traversed a low step for five cycles. The estimator showed no visually observable accumulated vertical drift, with a height closure error of less than 0.1 m, indicating stable elevation consistency under repetitive height changes.

2. **Astrall Platforms (A, B, C):**
 - **Robot A (point-foot) vs. Robot B (wheel-legged) - Mixed 3D Loop (~200 m horizontal, ~15 m vertical):**
 - Robot A achieved a horizontal-loop error of 0.1638 m and a vertical-loop error of 0.219 m.
 - Robot B yielded 0.2264 m horizontal error and 0.199 m vertical error. These results indicate good performance for both morphologies on moderately long 3D trajectories, with Astrall A (point-foot) showing slightly better horizontal consistency.
 - **Robot C (wheel-legged) - Separate Trials:**
 - **Longer Horizontal Closed Loop (~700 m):** The horizontal-loop error increased to 7.68 m. This larger drift was primarily attributed to wheel slip events and contact-geometry violations, which are more prevalent in long-range wheel-legged operation, particularly during step-down motions involving brief losses of sustained rolling contact.
 - **Vertical Loop (~20 m):** The vertical-loop error remained at 0.540 m, demonstrating continued stability in elevation tracking even for longer vertical excursions.

**C. Yaw Drift Suppression and IMU-Free Degradation:**
- Video demonstrations confirmed that the kinematics-based yaw correction mechanism effectively arrests IMU yaw drift during prolonged standing and maintains stability during short in-place rotations.
- When IMU yaw constraints were disabled, the kinematics-only heading reference degraded gracefully, with empirical errors of approximately 10 degrees per closed turn for short-step turning and 30 degrees for long-step turning. This suggests residual sensitivity to unmodeled attitude coupling (roll/pitch) and contact compliance in the absence of IMU yaw.

**D. Public Artifacts:**
- The complete implementation of CAPO and representative Unitree Go2 EDU test data (including ROS bags, CSV files, and videos) were publicly released, supporting reproducible evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in proprioceptive state estimation for legged and wheel-legged robots, offering a robust alternative to exteroceptive sensor-dependent methods. The proposed Contact-Anchored Proprioceptive Odometry (CAPO) framework has several key areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Enhanced Operational Robustness in Challenging Environments:** By eliminating reliance on cameras or LiDAR, CAPO enables legged robots to operate more reliably in environments where exteroceptive sensors typically fail or perform poorly. This includes areas with poor lighting, dust, smoke, dense vegetation, feature scarcity, or GPS-denied scenarios. Such capability is critical for applications in industrial inspection, disaster response, exploration, and military operations.

2. **Unified Framework for Diverse Legged Morphologies:** The morphology-agnostic formulation, applicable to bipeds, quadrupeds, and wheel-legged robots, streamlines the development and deployment of state estimators across a broader range of robotic platforms. This unification reduces engineering effort and promotes reusability of the estimation core.

3. **Improved Long-Term Position and Elevation Accuracy:** The core mechanism of contact-anchored footfall recording, coupled with the novel support-plane height correction strategy, effectively suppresses long-term drift in position and specifically in elevation. This addresses a critical limitation of many proprioceptive systems, which typically struggle with accumulated drift over extended traversals or repeated vertical motions, directly impacting the robot's ability to maintain a consistent understanding of its height above ground or relative to terrain features.

4. **Increased Stability and Robustness of Velocity Estimates:** The optional Inverse-Kinematics Cubature Kalman Filter (IKVel-CKF) significantly mitigates the impact of encoder quantization and discrete differentiation on derived foot-end velocities. By providing smoother and more stable velocity feedback, IKVel-CKF enhances the reliability of contact-anchored updates, particularly during dynamic maneuvers like stair climbing or traversing uneven terrain, thereby contributing to more stable locomotion control.

5. **Effective Yaw Drift Suppression:** The kinematics-based yaw estimation, leveraging multi-contact geometric consistency, offers a method to arrest IMU yaw drift during prolonged standing and provides a graceful fallback when IMU yaw constraints are compromised. Maintaining accurate heading over time is crucial for long-duration missions and for coherent map building in systems that integrate proprioceptive odometry with external sensing.

6. **Reduced Computational and Hardware Requirements:** As a purely proprioceptive system, CAPO inherently requires less computational power and simpler hardware compared to exteroceptive SLAM systems. This makes it suitable for lightweight, power-constrained, or cost-sensitive robotic platforms, expanding the accessibility and deployability of advanced legged robotics.

7. **Contribution to Reproducible Research:** The public release of the complete implementation and representative test data is a significant contribution to the robotics community. It allows other researchers to replicate results, benchmark new methods, and build upon the presented work, fostering further advancements in the field of legged robot state estimation.

While the paper identifies limitations such as the lack of explicit slip detection for wheel-legged robots and the fixed-threshold contact detection, the presented methodology lays a robust foundation. Future work addressing these limitations will further enhance the practical utility and performance of proprioceptive odometry, moving closer to fully autonomous and reliable operation of legged robots in diverse and challenging real-world environments.
