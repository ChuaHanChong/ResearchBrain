# Few-shot Sim2Real Based on High Fidelity Rendering with Force Feedback Teleoperation

> [!summary] Summary
> Proposes a teleoperation-in-simulation pipeline for few-shot sim2real pick-and-place: force-feedback leader-follower teleop collects demonstrations in MuJoCo, trains a force-conditioned ACT policy, fine-tunes on 8 real episodes, then deploys zero-shot. Finds high-fidelity PBR-style rendering, not physics accuracy, is what closes the sim2real gap.

## Problem

- Real-world teleoperation for data collection is inefficient, spatially constrained, and lacks force feedback, limiting task scope and exploration.
- Simulation-based teleoperation pipelines exist but remain unclear, and physics engines still model contact forces inaccurately, undermining sim2real transfer.
- Prior sim2real pick-and-place methods rely heavily on domain randomization, calibration, or fine-tuning, leaving open whether visual realism alone can compensate for poor physics.

## Method

- **Force Feedback Teleoperation System**: scaled ==leader-follower arm== (0.5x) with three interchangeable 1-DOF gripper controllers (trigger, roller, parallel-clip) giving linear displacement-to-width and force-to-feedback mapping.

## Results

- Stick picking real-world touch/grasp/place counts (of **20** trials) rose from **0/0/0** (only-real) to **17/13/12** (high-fidelity sim), showing rendering quality drives few-shot transfer success monotonically.
- Roller controller with force feedback cut average execution time by about **4 seconds** per task versus no feedback across **7** manipulation tasks at **3** difficulty levels.
- Trigger, roller, and parallel-clip controllers gave similar success rates and completion times despite differing operator comfort, per Table II qualitative comparison.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual rendering fidelity, not physics-engine accuracy, can be the dominant lever for zero/few-shot sim2real transfer in contact-rich pick-and-place.
> - Force feedback matters more in simulation than real teleoperation, since real-world cues like sound and visual deformation aren't simulated.
> - Conditioning imitation policies on estimated contact force gives an easy-to-learn success signal, though its benefit for fragile-object handling remains unverified.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges within robotic learning, specifically in the domains of data collection and sim-to-real transfer for robotic manipulation tasks. The broader landscape of robotic learning has seen advancements through learning-based methods, but these approaches often require extensive, high-quality datasets. Obtaining such data in real-world environments is typically time-consuming, costly, and operationally complex.

Teleoperation has emerged as a method for collecting robotic data due to its intuitive nature and flexibility. However, real-world teleoperation itself can be inefficient, may lack crucial feedback mechanisms, and presents deployment difficulties. Consequently, researchers have explored simulation-based teleoperation to mitigate these issues, offering advantages such as ease of scene randomization, simplified system deployment, and reduced spatial constraints.

A significant challenge in sim-to-real transfer is the "sim2real gap," which arises from discrepancies between simulation and reality. While modern graphics engines have achieved high levels of visual realism through techniques like Physically Based Rendering (PBR), physics engines in simulation environments often struggle to accurately model complex physical interactions, particularly contact forces critical for manipulation tasks. Existing sim-to-real techniques frequently rely on domain randomization of various parameters (forces, tactiles, images), calibration, fine-tuning with real-world data, or efforts to improve physics simulation accuracy.

This research contributes to the discourse by investigating whether advanced visual rendering capabilities can compensate for the limitations of imperfect physics engines to facilitate sim-to-real transfer, particularly in a few-shot learning context. It also explores the role of force feedback in teleoperation for enhancing data collection efficiency and quality in simulated environments. The paper positions itself within the context of reducing the reliance on large-scale real-world data by leveraging high-fidelity simulation and effective teleoperation strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance task performance in robotic manipulation while minimizing the reliance on real-world data. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives and motivations:

- **Address Efficiency and Feedback Limitations of Teleoperation:** Existing real-world teleoperation methods for data collection are constrained by efficiency, time, space, and a lack of tactile or force feedback. These limitations restrict the scope of tasks that can be effectively demonstrated and the quality of data collected. The motivation is to overcome these constraints by shifting data collection to a simulated environment.
- **Clarify the Pipeline for Simulation-Based Data Collection:** While simulation-based teleoperation offers advantages, a clear and effective pipeline for collecting robotic manipulation data in simulation and subsequently transferring policies to real-world scenarios has been underexplored, particularly regarding the role of visual fidelity and haptic feedback.
- **Explore the Impact of High-Fidelity Visual Rendering:** A key motivation is to investigate whether advanced visual rendering, approaching photorealism, can mitigate the shortcomings of inadequate physics engines in bridging the sim-to-real gap for visual-motor policies. This question arises from the observation that modern graphics engines are highly advanced, while physics engines often fall short in accurately modeling contact forces. The paper aims to quantify the benefits of enhanced visual realism in simulation for improving task performance and reducing real-world data requirements for few-shot sim-to-real transfer.
- **Integrate and Evaluate Force Feedback in Teleoperation:** The researchers aim to demonstrate that incorporating force feedback into teleoperation systems can provide human operators with more precise control and intuitive interaction, thereby improving both the success rates and efficiency of data collection, especially in simulated environments where visual and auditory cues for contact might be absent or less realistic.
- **Develop a Few-Shot Sim-to-Real Imitation Learning Framework:** The objective is to propose and validate a framework that allows a robot to learn manipulation policies primarily from simulation-collected data, requiring only a minimal number of real-world demonstrations for fine-tuning, thus achieving "few-shot" transfer. This framework aims to integrate force-enabled teleoperation, a dedicated simulation environment, and an imitation learning algorithm to enable direct deployment of trained policies on real robots.

In essence, the motivation stems from the high cost and impracticality of real-world robotic data collection, coupled with the potential of advanced simulation technologies, particularly in graphics, to provide a viable alternative for generating training data and enabling efficient policy transfer to the real world with minimal real-world intervention.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology establishes a few-shot sim-to-real imitation learning framework, comprising a teleoperation system with force feedback, a simulation environment, a force estimation method, and an imitation learning algorithm. The core approach involves collecting multimodal data in simulation, supplementing it with limited real-world data, and then training a visual-motor policy for deployment on a physical robot.

**A. Teleoperation System with Force Feedback:**
The teleoperation system utilizes a scaled leader-follower mechanism. The leader robot arm is a passive, encoded servo motor-actuated device scaled down by a factor of 0.5 relative to the slave Fanuc robotic arm. While direct joint force feedback is not provided, gravity compensation is implemented at each joint using a PID controller.

For end-effector control and gripping force feedback, three types of one-degree-of-freedom controllers were designed:
- **Trigger-based control:** A trigger mechanism for gripper width.
- **Roller-based control:** A rotatable roller for gripper width.
- **Parallel clip-based control:** A scissor-structure parallel clip for gripper width.

Each controller provides a linear mapping between its physical displacement and the robot gripper's width ($q_e = \alpha \hat{q}_e$). Crucially, the gripping force of the robot's end-effector is fed back to the operator as a haptic sensation ($\hat{F}_e = \beta F_e$), utilizing the servo motor's force control mode.

**B. Simulation Environment and Force Estimation:**
A simulation environment was created in Mujoco, replicating the Fanuc robot arm's workspace. Real-time images from four cameras are displayed to the human operator during teleoperation, though only data from the top and wrist cameras are saved for training. To improve generalization, the environment includes randomly placed visual distractors and random initial positions for the target object within a specified range (e.g., 5cm x 5cm).

The system estimates contact forces in simulation using a basic algorithm (Algorithm 1). This involves iterating through contacts in the simulation, identifying contacts with the gripper fingers ("finger left" or "finger right"). If both fingers are in contact, a linear spring model calculates the force: $F = k(q_e - q_{e0})$, where $q_e$ is the current gripper width, $q_{e0}$ is the gripper width at initial contact, and $k$ is a spring constant. This estimated force is then fed back to the human operator and also used as an input to the imitation learning policy.

**C. Imitation Learning for Few-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
The imitation learning approach is based on Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), customized for the manipulation policy.
- **Data Collection:** Demonstrations are collected via teleoperation, primarily in simulation, with a small proportion of real-world demonstrations. A demonstration $\tau_i$ comprises a trajectory of observations and actions: $\{(I_t^t, I_w^t, q_t, F_t, a_t)\}_{t=1}^T$, where $I_t^t$ and $I_w^t$ are images from the top and wrist cameras, $q_t = [q, q_e]$ is the robot and end-effector configuration, $F_t$ is the estimated contact force, and $a_t = [a, a_t]$ is the desired joint angles.
- **Policy Architecture (ACT Customization):** The policy $\pi_\theta(a_{t:t+k} | I_t^t, I_w^t, q_t, F_t)$ is designed to predict a chunk of $k$ future actions. It uses a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) structure:
 - **CVAE Encoder:** This component models the action distribution as an intermediate latent variable $z$. It takes as input the force $F_t$, robot configuration $q_t$, the $k$-step action sequence $\{a_t\}_{1}^k$, and a 1D sinusoidal positional embedding `[CLS]` token to represent temporal information. The encoder is trained to predict the mean and variance of $z$.
 - **CVAE Decoder:** This component reconstructs the $k$-chunk actions. It takes the current robot configuration $q_t$, end-effector force $F_t$, visual features encoded from the two camera images ($I_t^t, I_w^t$), and the action distribution $z$ as inputs.
- **Training:** The policy is trained using a standard VAE objective, which includes an action reconstruction loss.
- **Inference:** During inference, only the CVAE decoder is used for action generation, with the action distribution $z$ provided as input. A temporal fusion strategy is applied for robust action prediction, averaging predictions from recent time steps with exponentially decaying weights ($\hat{a}_t = \sum_{j=t-k}^t \omega_j \times a_t^j$, where $\omega_j = \frac{\exp(-0.01 \cdot (t-j))}{\Sigma \omega_j}$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments focused on two primary objectives: evaluating the effectiveness of simulation-collected data for real-world robot deployment and comparing the influence of different force feedback devices on teleoperation performance.

**A. Sim2Real Transfer Comparison Based on Visual Rendering Quality:**
The study defined three levels of visual rendering quality in the simulation environment to assess their impact on the sim-to-real gap, particularly for the "Stick Picking and Placing" task:
- **Level 1 (Low Fidelity):** Only geometric information with plain gray or colored objects, no advanced rendering.
- **Level 2 (Mid Fidelity):** Basic colors and textures added.
- **Level 3 (High Fidelity):** Detailed textures, shadows, and lighting effects added, aiming for near-realism.

For each rendering level, 47 successful simulated episodes were collected and mixed with 8 real-world demonstrations. The models were initially trained for 6000 epochs and then fine-tuned for 1000 epochs using only the 8 real-world demonstrations. The performance was evaluated based on success rates at three stages: Touch, Grasp, and Place.

**Key Findings:**
- **High-fidelity visual features significantly improved sim-to-real transfer.** In simulation (with 5x5cm random object placement), Level 3 rendering achieved a 70% success rate for the "Place" stage, compared to 40% for Level 2 and 2% for Level 1.
- **Reduced Sim2Real Gap:** When transferred to real-world scenarios, the policy trained with high-fidelity simulation data showed substantially better performance. For "Stick Picking and Placing" in the real world (1x1cm random placement), Level 3 achieved a 19% success rate at the "Place" stage, while Level 2 achieved 6%, and Level 1 yielded 0%. Even with a larger random placement range (5x5cm) in real world, Level 3 achieved 12% at the "Place" stage, significantly outperforming Level 2 (2%) and Level 1 (0%).
- **Necessity of Fine-tuning:** Models trained solely on simulation data, even high-fidelity, could not be directly deployed to the real world without fine-tuning. However, the fine-tuning process with a small amount of real-world data (8 episodes) successfully enabled real-world task completion and generalization to object position randomness.
- **Impact of Randomness:** High-fidelity visuals were particularly beneficial when the task involved randomness (e.g., target object layout, scene distractors), requiring the model to infer different output trajectories based on visual input. Low-fidelity visuals, being too divergent from reality, caused the model to exhibit repetitive actions and become stuck.

**B. Comparison of Data Collection Devices and Force Feedback:**
The performance of teleoperation was evaluated in simulation across 7 distinct tasks, categorized into three difficulty levels, using the roller controller both with and without force feedback.

**Key Findings:**
- **Force Feedback Improves Efficiency and Success:** The integration of force feedback into the teleoperation device significantly improved both the success rate and execution efficiency of human operators in simulation. For most tasks, using a roller controller with force feedback reduced the average execution time by approximately 4 seconds compared to operating without force feedback (e.g., "Stick Picking and Placing": 16s with FF, 20s without FF; "Push Door": 14s with FF, 18s without FF).
- **Operator Experience:** Without force feedback, operators required additional time to confirm successful grasping, especially when precise gripper displacement was necessary. Linear force feedback provided real-time supplementary information, enabling operators to adjust movements more effectively. Even a simple 0-1 contact feedback (indicating contact or no contact) was found to be valuable for improving efficiency.
- **Controller Usability:** While different controllers (trigger, roller, parallel clip) exhibited variations in usability, stability, and effort, operators generally performed similarly in terms of task completion time and success rate across the devices when force feedback was present.
 - **Trigger:** Pros - low effort, stable control. Cons - limited range of motion.
 - **Roller:** Pros - easy to operate, low effort. Cons - potentially unstable at travel limits (thumb tip only).
 - **Parallel Clip:** Pros - stable control. Cons - high effort, limited range of motion, mechanically complex with force feedback. The trigger was generally considered the most comfortable.

**C. Discussion and Limitations:**
- **Value of the Pipeline:** The study highlights the value of the simulation teleoperation combined with real-world fine-tuning pipeline, leveraging high-fidelity visuals and force feedback for efficient data collection without extensive hardware.
- **Force Feedback in Simulation vs. Real:** The authors suggest that force feedback might be *more* critical in simulation than in real teleoperation. In the real world, operators receive rich multimodal cues (visual deformation, contact sound) that are difficult to simulate accurately, allowing for inferring interaction states. In simulation, these cues are often absent, making haptic feedback more vital.
- **Handling Fragile Objects:** The linear force transmission model (Algorithm 1) was observed to enhance the policy's ability to handle soft and fragile objects more effectively, though systematic evaluation was limited by hardware constraints.
- **Grasping Force as Input:** Incorporating grasping force as an input feature led to segmented task characteristics. For instance, in pick-and-place, if an object wasn't grasped successfully, the model would attempt re-grasping at the current position rather than proceeding to the placement trajectory. This suggests the grasping force input provides a strong signal for interaction state.
- **Future Work:** The study acknowledges that achieving zero-shot transfer with photorealistic rendering remains an open question, as do the upper limits of visual realism's impact and generalization to diverse real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic learning by proposing and validating a framework that enhances the efficiency and scalability of data collection and policy transfer for robotic manipulation tasks. The findings suggest several significant impacts:

- **Reduced Reliance on Real-world Data:** The most prominent impact is the demonstration that high-fidelity visual rendering in simulation, combined with effective teleoperation, can substantially reduce the need for extensive real-world data. By enabling effective "few-shot sim-to-real transfer," the framework mitigates the time, cost, and logistical challenges associated with large-scale real-world data collection, making robotic learning more accessible and practical.
- **Enhanced Teleoperation Efficiency:** The integration of force feedback in teleoperation systems, particularly for simulation-based data collection, improves human operator performance. This leads to more successful and efficient demonstrations, which translates to higher quality training data. The finding that force feedback is crucial in simulation, where other natural cues are absent, highlights a specific design consideration for virtual training environments.
- **Compensation for Physics Engine Limitations:** The research provides empirical evidence that advanced visual rendering can compensate for the inaccuracies of physics engines in simulation. This suggests a paradigm where highly realistic visual fidelity can effectively bridge a significant portion of the sim-to-real gap, at least for tasks like pick-and-place, reducing the sole reliance on perfect physics modeling which remains a long-term challenge.
- **Advancement of Scalable Robotic Learning Pipelines:** By combining efficient simulation-based data collection with few-shot real-world fine-tuning, the proposed pipeline offers a path toward more scalable and data-efficient robotic learning. This could accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous robotic systems by streamlining the training process.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future research, particularly regarding the potential for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with near-photorealistic rendering, determining the upper bounds of visual realism benefits, and expanding the framework to more complex manipulation tasks and dynamic environments. The insights into the role of force feedback and visual fidelity provide a foundation for designing more effective simulation environments and teleoperation interfaces.

Overall, this research suggests a practical and effective strategy for developing more capable and autonomous robotic systems by leveraging the strengths of high-fidelity simulation environments and intuitive human-in-the-loop control, thereby lowering the barriers to entry for advanced robotic applications.
