# SpeedTuning: Speeding Up Policy Execution with Lightweight Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> SpeedTuning accelerates imitation-learned manipulation policies by adding a separate speed policy that predicts a discrete speed multiplier for each predicted action chunk, applied through linear temporal interpolation. Trained with Rainbow DQN on a combined speed-plus-task reward, it needs no new demonstrations and yields over 2.4x speed-up across six simulated and real ALOHA tasks while retaining success rates.

## Problem

- Imitation policies inherit demonstration pace, capped by teleoperation hardware and operator speed, so ALOHA tasks take 15-22 seconds where humans finish under 5.
- No established method retrofits speed onto already-trained imitation policies without new data collection or changes to the demonstration pipeline.
- Uniformly scaling execution velocity ignores stage-dependent dynamics, breaking timing-critical moments like grasping a swinging tea bag or pouring without spilling.

## Method

- ==Factorized execution policy==: a frozen ==ACT== task policy predicting action chunks, plus a separate ==speed policy== predicting a discrete speed multiplier conditioned on stacked visual and proprioceptive observations.
- ==Linear temporal interpolation== over the predicted action chunk shrinks effective chunk size from k to (k+1)/v, converting a scalar speed choice into executable accelerated joint actions.
- ==Rainbow DQN== optimizes the combined reward alpha times v^beta plus binary task reward, with ==frame skip== of 10 shortening the MDP horizon and ==frame stacking== preserving Markov structure.

## Results

- Over **2.4x** speed-up over the base ACT policy across all **six** tasks, landing as an outlier point beyond the Pareto curve traced by fixed-speed interpolation baselines.
- Almond Pouring finishes in **200** timesteps versus **500** for vanilla ACT (**2.5x**), with the policy holding high speed during lid opening and slowing through the pouring phase.
- Ablations: dropping image observations destabilizes training and lowers both success and acceleration; beta=**2** beats **0**, **1**, **3**; frame skip **10** beats **5** (accel-biased) and **25** (success-biased).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Speed is a separable action dimension: a small state-conditioned module bolted onto a frozen base policy captures speed-success structure without retraining or new demonstrations.
> - Optimal execution speed is phase-dependent, not constant, so any single-multiplier acceleration scheme is dominated wherever contact or dynamics impose timing constraints.
> - Proprioception alone cannot identify which task phases tolerate acceleration; vision is required, and the speed module can use its own encoder rather than the task policy's.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements through learned policies, particularly those acquired via imitation learning (IL). IL offers a straightforward approach for robots to acquire complex skills by observing expert demonstrations. Recent paradigms, such as those combining puppeteering setups, transformer architectures, and action chunking, have demonstrated the effectiveness of IL for rapid skill acquisition. However, a significant limitation of these methods is that the execution speed of the learned policies is inherently tied to the speed of the human operator during data collection and existing hardware constraints. This often results in suboptimal execution speeds that do not meet the demands of real-world applications where throughput and efficiency are critical. Furthermore, current IL frameworks largely lack established mechanisms to systematically accelerate policies post-hoc or to explore the empirical relationship between execution speed and task success.

Traditional approaches to achieving fast robot manipulation have typically focused on optimizing robot hardware or explicitly integrating speed objectives into the reward functions during policy training. While successful in specific dynamic scenarios like table tennis or high-throughput pick-and-place tasks, these methods are often task-specific or require specialized hardware. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an alternative by allowing agents to learn policies that maximize cumulative reward through interaction, circumventing the issues of suboptimal demonstration data. However, training complex control policies from scratch using RL in real-world robotic environments typically demands extensive interaction time, which can be prohibitive. While fine-tuning pre-trained IL policies with RL has been explored, the current work introduces a distinct approach: training a *separate* policy specifically to predict optimal action velocities, which then modulates the execution of an existing, fixed base task policy.

This paper addresses a critical gap by proposing a framework that can accelerate existing imitation-learned policies without requiring additional data collection or modifying the original base policy. It specifically aims to learn the nuanced interaction between execution speed and task success, particularly in dynamic tasks where optimal speed may vary throughout an episode. By decoupling the speed optimization from the core task learning, the proposed method offers a generalizable solution that can be retrofitted onto a wide range of pre-trained IL policies, thereby enhancing their practical applicability in real-world settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a reinforcement learning framework, termed SPEEDTUNING, designed to enhance the execution speed of learned robotic manipulation policies while simultaneously optimizing for task success. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current robotic policy learning paradigms, particularly those based on imitation learning (IL).

Firstly, policies trained via imitation learning are often constrained by the speed at which demonstrations are collected. Expert teleoperation, even with advanced hardware like ALOHA, can be significantly slower than human execution speeds for the same tasks. For example, tasks that humans perform in under 5 seconds might take a teleoperated robot 15-22 seconds. Consequently, policies learned from these demonstrations inherit suboptimal execution speeds, limiting their utility in applications where high throughput and efficiency are paramount, such as industrial assembly or service robotics.

Secondly, there is a lack of established methods for systematically accelerating existing imitation learning policies post-hoc. Naive approaches, such as applying a constant speed multiplier to all actions, are often insufficient because they fail to account for the dynamic and context-dependent nature of many manipulation tasks. Critical phases of a task, such as grasping a delicate object or precise insertion, may require slower, more controlled movements, while less critical phases, like moving to a new location, can be significantly accelerated. A uniform speed-up can compromise task success in these critical moments.

Thirdly, the empirical relationship between execution speed and task success for learned manipulation policies remains underexplored. Understanding this trade-off is crucial for developing policies that can dynamically adjust their speed to balance efficiency and reliability. The authors aim to explicitly model this relationship to enable intelligent speed adaptations.

To address these motivations, SPEEDTUNING seeks to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. **Develop a non-invasive acceleration method:** The framework should not require modifications to the original demonstration collection process or the architecture of the base imitation learning policy.
2. **Retrofit existing policies:** It should be capable of augmenting and accelerating pre-trained, existing policies to operate at faster speeds.
3. **Learn adaptive speed control:** The system must learn to predict optimal execution speeds dynamically, conditioning these predictions on the current environmental state and task phase, rather than applying a static, constant acceleration.
4. **Optimize for both speed and success:** The learned speed policy should balance the objective of maximizing execution speed with maintaining a high task success rate.
5. **Require no additional data collection:** The training of the speed policy should rely on reinforcement learning interactions in the environment, eliminating the need for further human demonstrations.

By achieving these objectives, SPEEDTUNING aims to bridge the gap between the theoretical capabilities of learned manipulation policies and their practical deployment in real-world, time-sensitive applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SPEEDTUNING framework is designed to accelerate manipulation policies by decoupling the control into two distinct components: a pre-trained base task policy and a newly introduced speed policy.

**Policy Decomposition:**
1. **Task Policy (π_θ):** This component is a base policy, typically pre-trained using imitation learning (IL) from expert demonstrations. The paper uses Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), which models the policy distribution as a transformer-based Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE). The ACT policy predicts a chunk of future joint position actions, denoted as A_t = a_t:t+kc. Input observations for the task policy include proprioceptive data (joint positions and speeds) and camera image embeddings obtained via a pre-trained image encoder. This task policy remains fixed and unmodified during the SPEEDTUNING training process.
2. **Speed Policy (π_φ):** This is a new policy trained via reinforcement learning (RL). It takes an observation history (a stack of k_stack recent observations) as input and outputs a discrete speed multiplier, v_t, for the subsequent action chunk predicted by the task policy. The possible speed multipliers are restricted to a predefined discrete set V = {v1, v2..., vK} to simplify optimization.

**Action Execution Modulation:**
The core mechanism for accelerating actions involves modulating the execution of the action chunk A_t generated by the task policy using the speed multiplier v_t predicted by the speed policy. This is achieved through **linear temporal interpolation**. For an action chunk A = a_0:k, the interpolated action a(A,v)i at speed v for step i is calculated using the formula:
`a(A,v)i = interp_f(A)a,v (i)`
where `f(A)a(i) = a(A)i` and `interp_f,v (t) = f(⌊vt⌋) + (vt − ⌊vt⌋) * (f(⌊vt+1⌋) − f(⌊vt⌋)) / v`.
This interpolation effectively accelerates the execution of the action chunk by a factor of v, reducing the effective duration of the chunk to approximately (k+1)/v.

**Reinforcement Learning Objective:**
The speed policy π_φ is trained to optimize a combined reward function that balances execution speed and task success. The objective function J(φ) is defined as the expected cumulative reward:
`J(φ) = E[Σ(α · r_speed(v_t) + r_task(s_t, a_t))]`
- **Task Reward (r_task):** A binary reward (1 for success, 0 otherwise) indicating whether the task was successfully completed within a given transition.
- **Speed Reward (r_speed):** A reward component directly related to the chosen speed multiplier, formulated as `r_speed(v) = v^β`. The hyperparameter β allows for scaling the incentive for speed.
- **Weighting Term (α):** A crucial hyperparameter that controls the trade-off between prioritizing execution speed (higher α) and maintaining task success (lower α).

**RL Algorithm and Design Choices:**
The paper employs **Rainbow DQN** for training the speed policy. Rainbow DQN is a value-based off-policy algorithm suitable for discrete action spaces and incorporates several advancements: double Q-learning (to reduce overestimation bias), prioritized experience replay (to improve sample efficiency), dueling network architecture (to enhance performance), and distributional Q-learning (to capture reward uncertainty).
Additional design choices for the speed network include:
- **Frame Skip (k_skip):** The speed policy is queried only once every k_skip steps, meaning the predicted speed v_t is applied for k_skip subsequent frames. This effectively shortens the horizon of the Markov Decision Process (MDP) for the speed policy.
- **Frame Stack (k_stack):** To maintain Markovian properties, the input to the speed policy is a stack of k_stack most recent observations.
- **Image Observations:** The speed policy utilizes both proprioceptive and visual observations (image embeddings) to make informed speed predictions.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted on an ALOHA bimanual robotic setup, operating at 50Hz, with a 14-dimensional action space. Observations included proprioception (joint position and speed) and data from four RGB cameras. The evaluation spanned six fine-grained manipulation tasks, evenly split between simulated and real-world environments: Simulated Cube Transfer, Simulated Peg Insertion, Simulated Tea Bag Transfer, real-world Tea Bag Disposal, Food Preparation, and Pouring Almond. Comparisons were made against the original ACT task policy and a baseline that applies a constant speed multiplier across the entire task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SPEEDTUNING across various simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks yielded several key findings:

**Significant Speed-up:**
SPEEDTUNING consistently achieved substantial improvements in execution speed across all six tasks. The framework demonstrated an average speed-up exceeding 2.4 times compared to the original ACT task policy. Specifically, tasks requiring less precise manipulation, such as Simulated Cube Transfer and Simulated Tea Bag Transfer, tended to exhibit higher speed-up factors. This indicates a general capability of the system to accelerate policies, with the degree of acceleration correlating inversely with the task's inherent precision requirements.

**Preservation of Task Success Rate:**
A critical finding was SPEEDTUNING's ability to maintain a high task success rate even at significantly increased execution speeds. When compared to a baseline that applies a uniform constant speed-up via linear interpolation, SPEEDTUNING consistently outperformed it, operating beyond the Pareto curve defined by the baseline. This implies that the adaptive speed control learned by SPEEDTUNING allows for a more effective trade-off between speed and success than non-adaptive methods. This advantage was particularly evident in dynamic tasks, such as Simulated Tea Bag Transfer, real-world Tea Bag Disposal, and Almond Pouring, where naive acceleration methods often led to considerable drops in success rates due to a lack of task-specific dynamic understanding.

**Task-Adaptive Speed Adjustment:**
The analysis of the learned speed policies revealed that SPEEDTUNING developed an intelligent understanding of task phases, dynamically adjusting execution speed based on the perceived criticality of actions. For instance, in the Almond Pouring task, the policy learned to maintain high speeds during less critical movements like opening the container lid or initially picking up the can. However, it deliberately slowed down during the delicate pouring phase to prevent spills, before accelerating again to place the can back. This behavior, illustrated in task trajectory comparisons, indicates that the reinforcement learning process successfully enabled the speed policy to identify and adapt to contact-rich and dynamic phases of the task, ensuring precision when required and speed when feasible.

**Ablation Study Results (on Simulated Tea Bag Transfer):**
Ablation studies provided insights into the impact of various design choices:
- **Choice of Task Policy:** Replacing the learned ACT task policy with an open-loop, scripted policy resulted in lower acceleration, although it maintained a slightly higher success rate. This suggested that the generalization capabilities of a learned base policy are beneficial for achieving robust acceleration across varying conditions.
- **Image Observations:** Removing image observations from the speed policy’s input led to less stable training, lower success rates, and reduced acceleration. This confirmed that visual information is crucial for the speed policy to effectively represent the environment state and make appropriate speed predictions, beyond what proprioceptive data alone can provide.
- **Degree of Speed Rewards (β):** The exponent β in `r_speed(v) = v^β` significantly influenced performance. High β values (e.g., β=3) overly incentivized speed, leading to lower success rates, while β=0 (no direct speed reward) resulted in unstable training. An intermediate value (β=2) was identified as providing the optimal balance between acceleration and success.
- **Frame Skip (k_skip):** The value of k_skip impacted the trade-off. Smaller frame skips (e.g., 5) provided finer granularity in speed adjustments but led to suboptimal speed policies due to extended effective horizons. Larger frame skips (e.g., 25) improved short-horizon properties but sacrificed control precision. A value of k_skip=10 was found to offer a balanced approach.
- **Image Encoder:** Different image encoders, including a randomly initialized one and a pretrained ResNet-18, achieved similar final acceleration. However, the pretrained ResNet-18 yielded the highest final success rate, suggesting that a high-quality visual representation is important for the speed policy, even if it doesn't necessarily reuse the exact encoder from the task policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPEEDTUNING framework presents several significant contributions and holds notable potential impact within the field of robotic manipulation.

Firstly, the most direct impact is the **enhancement of robotic throughput and efficiency**. By demonstrating over 2.4x speed-ups across a diverse set of manipulation tasks, SPEEDTUNING offers a concrete method to increase the operational speed of robots. This is critical for industrial applications (e.g., assembly lines, logistics, manufacturing) and service robotics, where faster task completion directly translates to higher productivity, reduced operational costs, and improved user experience.

Secondly, the work significantly addresses a major limitation of **imitation learning (IL) policies in real-world deployment**. While IL is effective for skill acquisition, the inherent speed constraints of human demonstrations often render learned policies too slow for practical use. SPEEDTUNING provides a crucial "retrofit" mechanism, allowing existing, slow IL policies to be accelerated without requiring extensive re-training or additional, faster human demonstrations. This broadens the applicability of a vast body of existing IL research and pre-trained models.

Thirdly, the framework's ability to **learn adaptive, state-conditioned speed adjustments** is a key advancement. Unlike naive uniform acceleration, SPEEDTUNING can identify critical task phases (e.g., grasping, precise pouring) and dynamically reduce speed to ensure success, while accelerating during less demanding segments. This intelligent, context-aware speed control is essential for complex, dynamic, and contact-rich manipulation tasks, enabling robots to perform delicate operations reliably at high overall speeds. This capability aligns robot behavior more closely with human-like efficiency, where critical moments are handled with care, and routine actions are expedited.

Fourthly, this research provides **empirical insights into the trade-off between execution speed and task success**. By systematically exploring this Pareto frontier, the authors offer a deeper understanding of how speed impacts performance in various robotic tasks. The fact that SPEEDTUNING operates beyond the baseline's Pareto curve indicates that the learned adaptive control achieves a superior balance, which is valuable for future policy design.

Finally, the proposed methodology, leveraging reinforcement learning to optimize a separate speed policy, offers a **generalizable and modular approach**. It is compatible with a wide range of base task policies, making it a versatile tool for roboticists. The findings also lay a foundation for future research, particularly in addressing the sample efficiency of the RL component to make the method even more practical for direct real-world application, and in further optimizing the trade-off between speed and success in more complex, long-horizon tasks. Overall, SPEEDTUNING contributes to making learned robotic manipulation policies more robust, efficient, and deployable in dynamic, real-world environments.
