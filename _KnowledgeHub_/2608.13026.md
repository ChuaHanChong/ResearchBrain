# Temporal GRPO: Beyond Trajectory-Level Credit in Vision-Language-Action Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Temporal GRPO replaces GRPO's single rollout-level advantage in vision-language-action post-training with stage-conditioned credit. A VLM proposes ordered detectable task stages, rollouts are aligned to stage-specific action intervals, and only rollouts entering the same stage are compared, with each stage advantage applied to its own interval. On RoboTwin 2.0 it reaches 75.8% macro-average success, 7.0 points above the strongest controlled baseline.

## Problem

- Outcome-driven VLA reinforcement learning broadcasts one trajectory-level advantage to every action, so correct early-stage behaviors get penalized whenever the rollout fails later.
- Binary final rewards map rollouts with very different stage progress to identical advantages, hiding where along the trajectory the outcome difference actually originated.
- Prior credit-redistribution and state-regrouping approaches fail on continuous robot rollouts, which rarely revisit identical visual states and vary in path, stage duration, and speed.

## Method

- ==Task-conditioned stage generation==: a frozen RynnBrain-4B planner proposes candidate semantic stages from instruction plus initial observation, then a ==Stage Compiler== normalizes them into ordered, detectable credit stages.
- ==Rollout-to-stage alignment==: a relation detector records the first stable completion time per stage, carving each rollout into ordered non-overlapping action intervals, with the trailing suffix assigned to the failed stage.
- ==Stage-conditioned advantage reconstruction==: ==entered-stage gating== restricts each group to rollouts satisfying prerequisites, and per-stage normalized advantages are assigned to their intervals inside one clipped **Temporal GRPO** update.

## Results

- RoboTwin 2.0 macro-average success reaches **75.8±0.7%**, versus **68.8%** for SimpleVLA-RL and **46.4%** for Trajectory-GRPO, with **6.2-8.3** point gains across every horizon group.
- LIBERO-Long ablations give **99.1±0.4%**, dropping to **96.8%** without the Stage Compiler, **92.5%** without entered-stage gating, and **90.6%** without same-stage grouping.
- Controlled stage analysis shows Trajectory-GRPO degrades shared preceding stages while Temporal GRPO keeps those changes near zero and peaks improvement at the first-difference stage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Match the comparison group to shared task progress: relative advantages are only informative among rollouts that faced the same current subproblem.
> - Adding stage rewards helps, but reshaping where advantages are assigned helps more, since scalar stage rewards still smear credit across the whole trajectory.
> - The gains assume reliable stage predicates and a linear stage order, so branching, repeated, or recovery-heavy tasks may break the alignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant development in robotic manipulation, integrating visual observations, natural language instructions, and robot actions to enable multi-task capabilities. While these models possess strong potential, their deployment often necessitates downstream adaptation due to variations in embodiments, scene distributions, and specific task objectives.

The current research landscape for VLA models includes efforts to post-train pre-trained VLAs using reinforcement learning (RL). This approach aims to enhance policy performance through direct environment interaction and task feedback, thereby reducing reliance on extensive expert demonstrations. A common paradigm within outcome-driven VLA-RL is Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where policies are updated based on group-relative advantages derived from the final success or failure of sampled trajectories.

However, a recognized limitation in traditional outcome-driven GRPO for long-horizon tasks is the assignment of a single, trajectory-level credit signal. This uniform application of advantage across an entire action sequence can lead to "trajectory-level credit aliasing." This occurs when different patterns of task progress, particularly in multi-stage tasks, are mapped to the same final outcome, resulting in a misaligned credit signal. For instance, an agent that correctly completes several early stages of a task but fails at a later stage may receive the same negative advantage as an agent that fails at the very beginning. This uniform negative reinforcement can undesirably penalize actions that contributed to early, successful progress.

The problem of sparse or delayed rewards and appropriate temporal credit assignment is a known challenge in reinforcement learning. Previous methods like Hindsight Experience Replay, RUDDER, and others have attempted to address this by relabeling goals, redistributing returns, or segmenting trajectories. Some approaches use segment-level feedback, stage-aware rewards, or local group-relative comparisons based on similar states. However, these often struggle with the continuous nature of robotic rollouts, where identical visual states are rare, and trajectories can differ significantly in motion paths, durations, and execution speeds, making raw-state-based grouping unreliable.

This paper positions itself by specifically tackling trajectory-level credit aliasing within the outcome-driven GRPO framework for VLA models. It aims to refine the units of outcome comparison and temporal credit assignment without altering the fundamental VLA architecture or introducing additional value models. By leveraging structured task progress through detectable stages, the work seeks to provide a more granular and accurate credit assignment, thereby improving sample efficiency and the stability of learned subskills in complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks. This contrasts with methods that use high-level representations like reward machines primarily for task specification, planning, or reward construction, by directly applying structured task progress to advantage estimation in group-relative VLA optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to address and mitigate the problem of "trajectory-level credit aliasing" in outcome-driven Vision-Language-Action (VLA) reinforcement learning. The paper outlines several specific motivations and objectives:

**Detailed Problem: Trajectory-Level Credit Aliasing**
In outcome-driven VLA reinforcement learning, particularly with Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the policy is typically optimized by sampling multiple complete trajectories for a given task and computing a single group-relative advantage based on their final success or failure. This scalar advantage is then uniformly applied to every action within the entire trajectory.

The paper identifies the following issues stemming from this practice:
- **Misalignment of Credit with Local Progress:** A primary issue is that a rollout which completes several valid preceding stages but ultimately fails at a later stage receives the same scalar, often negative, trajectory-level advantage as a rollout that fails much earlier. When this negative advantage is broadcast across the entire executed action sequence, it penalizes not only the actions responsible for the final failure but also earlier actions that successfully contributed to progress. For example, if a robot successfully grasps and transports an object but fails during final placement, the actions leading to the successful grasp and transport are undesirably suppressed by the negative feedback associated with the overall failure.
- **Aggregation of Distinct Stage Outcomes:** The final outcome of a long-horizon task aggregates the outcomes of multiple sequential stages. This aggregation obscures the specific points of success and failure within a trajectory, causing rollouts with substantially different intermediate task-progress patterns to be mapped to the same failure label and corresponding trajectory advantage.
- **Weakened Sample Efficiency and Subskill Stability:** The inability to distinguish and appropriately credit or penalize actions based on their contribution to specific stages leads to inefficient learning. The policy struggles to improve effectively because successful sub-behaviors are undermined, and the signal for improving problematic stages is diluted. This can degrade the stability of learned subskills necessary for complex manipulation.

**Proposed Solution and Objectives:**
The paper proposes Temporal GRPO to overcome trajectory-level credit aliasing by refining the temporal credit assignment mechanism. The key objectives are:
1. **Characterize and Formulate Trajectory-Level Credit Aliasing:** To explicitly define this problem, where rollouts with varying stage progress receive identical final-outcome advantages, leading to suppression of successful preceding actions alongside failed ones.
2. **Introduce a Stage-Conditioned Temporal Credit Assignment Framework:** To design a method that moves beyond trajectory-level credit by:
 - **Constructing Detectable Ordered Task Stages:** Breaking down a complex task into a sequence of semantically meaningful and verifiable stages.
 - **Aligning Rollouts with Stage-Specific Action Intervals:** Precisely identifying which actions correspond to which task stage, both successful and failed.
 - **Comparing Rollouts within the Same Stage:** Instead of comparing complete trajectories, comparing only those rollouts that have successfully entered and are attempting to complete a specific stage, thereby forming a more comparable local group.
 - **Computing Stage-Specific Advantages:** Deriving group-relative advantages based on the success or failure of completing each individual stage, rather than the entire task.
 - **Assigning Stage Advantages Locally:** Applying these stage-specific advantages only to the corresponding action intervals responsible for that stage's outcome, preserving credit for successful earlier stages and targeting updates to the failing stages.
3. **Demonstrate Improved Task Success and Sample Efficiency:** To experimentally validate that Temporal GRPO can achieve higher task success rates and learn more efficiently across various task horizons, particularly in long-horizon manipulation tasks.
4. **Verify Localized Policy Updates:** To provide controlled evidence that the proposed method preserves already acquired preceding behaviors and concentrates policy improvements on the specific action intervals where rollout outcomes diverge, thus ensuring more targeted and effective learning.

In essence, the motivation is to enable outcome-driven VLA-RL to learn more effectively from sparse task-success feedback by providing a more precise and temporally sensitive credit assignment mechanism that respects the sequential nature of long-horizon robotic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Temporal GRPO addresses trajectory-level credit aliasing by introducing a stage-conditioned temporal credit assignment framework. The methodology consists of three main steps: Task-Conditioned Stage Generation, Rollout-to-Stage Alignment, and Stage-Conditioned Advantage Reconstruction and Policy Optimization.

#### 3.1. Task-Conditioned Stage Generation

The first step involves decomposing a given task instruction and initial visual observation into an ordered sequence of detectable task stages, denoted as $M = (m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_K)$.
1. **Semantic Stage Proposal:** A frozen RynnBrain-4B model (a large multimodal model) is employed to generate candidate semantic stages ($\tilde{M} = (\tilde{m}_1, \ldots, \tilde{m}_{\tilde{K}})$) conditioned on the natural language task instruction ($l$) and the initial visual observation ($o_0$). These candidate stages provide a high-level semantic description of the task progression.
2. **Stage Compilation:** A "Stage Compiler" then converts these candidate semantic stages into "credit stages" ($M$). This compilation process:
 - Normalizes stage ordering and prerequisite dependencies, ensuring a linear progression ($m_1 \rightarrow m_2 \rightarrow \ldots \rightarrow m_K$).
 - Translates abstract stage descriptions into concrete, verifiable completion conditions.
 - Resolves any redundant or ambiguously bounded candidate stages.
 - The final stage ($m_K$) is explicitly matched to the overall task-success condition.
This process generates the stage sequence once per task instance, which is then reused across all subsequent rollouts.

#### 3.2. Rollout-to-Stage Alignment

After the credit stages are defined, each complete rollout generated by the policy is aligned with these stages to identify the specific action intervals corresponding to stage progress.
1. **Stage Completion Detection:** A relation detector evaluates the completion conditions of each stage along the rollout. This detection for stage $m_k$ only begins after stage $m_{k-1}$ has been confirmed as completed. For successful stages, the first time $T_{i,k}$ at which stage $m_k$ is stably completed in rollout $i$ is recorded. Stability criteria are used to prevent false positives from transient events.
2. **Action Interval Definition:**
 - For a successfully completed stage $m_k$, the corresponding action interval $B_{i,k}$ is defined as $(T_{i,k-1}, T_{i,k}]$, where $T_{i,0} = 0$. This interval encompasses the actions that transitioned the rollout from the completion of $m_{k-1}$ to $m_k$.
 - If rollout $i$ completes $m_{k-1}$ but fails to complete $m_k$ before termination, the remaining action suffix forms the failed interval for stage $m_k$, $B_{i,k} = (T_{i,k-1}, T_i]$, where $T_i$ is the rollout termination time. Subsequent stages beyond this failed stage are considered unreached and are not included in further comparisons.

#### 3.3. Stage-Conditioned Advantage Reconstruction and Policy Optimization

This is the core mechanism for refining credit assignment.
1. **Stage Participation Gating ($V_{i,k}$):** For any given stage $m_k$, only rollouts that have successfully completed its prerequisite stage ($m_{k-1}$) are considered to have "entered" that stage. Such rollouts have $V_{i,k}=1$ and participate in the group-relative comparison for $m_k$. Rollouts that have not met this prerequisite ($V_{i,k}=0$) are excluded from the comparison for $m_k$ and are not treated as failures for that specific stage.
2. **Binary Stage Outcome ($R_{i,k}$):** For participating rollouts ($V_{i,k}=1$), a binary outcome $R_{i,k}$ is defined: 1 if stage $m_k$ is completed, and 0 if it fails to be completed within that rollout.
3. **Stage-Conditioned Group-Relative Advantage ($\hat{A}_{i,k}$):** The group-relative advantage for stage $m_k$ is computed using the $R_{i,k}$ values of only the valid rollouts ($V_{i,k}=1$). This involves normalizing the stage outcomes within this specific group, similar to standard GRPO: $\hat{A}_{i,k} = (R_{i,k} - \mu_k) / (\sigma_k + \epsilon)$, where $\mu_k$ and $\sigma_k$ are the mean and standard deviation of stage outcomes among valid rollouts for stage $m_k$. If a stage has no valid rollouts or all valid rollouts have the same outcome, its comparison is skipped for that update.
4. **Temporal Advantage Reconstruction ($\hat{A}_{i,t}$):** The uniform trajectory-level advantage is replaced by a temporally varying advantage. Each action $a_t$ in rollout $i$ is assigned the advantage of the stage interval $B_{i,k}$ it belongs to: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{k=1}^K I[t \in B_{i,k}] \hat{A}_{i,k}$. Since stage intervals are ordered and non-overlapping, each action or action chunk receives at most one stage-specific advantage.
5. **Policy Optimization:** The policy $\pi_\theta$ is then optimized using a modified GRPO objective. The key change is replacing the uniformly broadcast trajectory-level advantage with the reconstructed stage-conditioned temporal advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ in the PPO-style clipped objective function. This allows for joint optimization of all stage intervals within the same training batch, updating a single VLA policy without training independent sub-policies.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Benchmarks:** RoboTwin 2.0 (for overall task performance and sample efficiency across different task horizons) and LIBERO-Long (for controlled analysis of credit assignment and ablation studies with clear stage dependencies).
- **Baselines:** Included VLA models ($\pi_0$, RDT-1B), SFT checkpoints (OpenVLA-OFT SFT), and VLA-RL methods (SimpleVLA-RL, TGRPO). For controlled RL comparisons, custom baselines were created: Trajectory-GRPO (standard GRPO, assigns final outcome to entire trajectory) and Stage-Reward GRPO (uses detected stage progress for scalar rewards but retains trajectory-level advantage assignment).
- **Initialization:** All controlled RL methods were initialized from the same task-specific OpenVLA-OFT SFT checkpoint.
- **Budgets:** All controlled RL methods used identical training scenarios, rollout budgets, environment-interaction budgets, and policy-update budgets.
- **Evaluation:** Primary metric was task success rate, with sample efficiency measured by success rate under fixed interaction budget. Controlled credit assignment analyzed changes in stage-conditional completion probabilities ($\Delta p_k$).
- **Privileged Information:** Stage predicates and related modules, including privileged simulator states for detection, were used only during RL post-training for credit construction and not during policy evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents empirical evaluations demonstrating the efficacy of Temporal GRPO across various metrics and experimental setups.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Sample Efficiency on RoboTwin 2.0

- **Task Success Rates:** Temporal GRPO achieved the highest task success rates across all evaluated task-horizon groups (Short, Medium, Long & Extra-Long) on the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark. Its macro-average success rate was 75.8% (±0.7%), outperforming the strongest controlled baseline, SimpleVLA-RL (68.8% ±0.9%), by 7.0 percentage points. Compared to Trajectory-GRPO (46.4% ±1.3%), Temporal GRPO demonstrated a substantial improvement of 29.4 percentage points. The gains were consistent across horizons, ranging from 6.2 to 8.3 percentage points compared to the next best method in each category. This indicated that stage-conditioned credit assignment is particularly beneficial for long-horizon manipulation tasks involving sequential dependencies.
- **Sample Efficiency:** The performance curves showed that Temporal GRPO maintained higher task success rates than Trajectory-GRPO throughout the reinforcement learning post-training phase. This was observed for both all RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and specifically for the Long and Extra-Long subset. This suggested that Temporal GRPO extracts more effective training signals from the same environment-interaction budget, leading to faster learning and higher final task success.

#### 4.2. Controlled Credit Assignment on LIBERO-Long

To analyze the localized effect of credit assignment, experiments on LIBERO-Long tasks were conducted, aligning tasks by the "first-difference stage" ($m_d$) where rollout outcomes diverge.
- **Trajectory-GRPO Behavior:** This baseline exhibited negative changes in conditional stage success probabilities ($\Delta p_k$) on the shared preceding stages (e.g., $m_{d-3}$ to $m_{d-1}$). This indicated that the negative trajectory-level advantage, when broadcast uniformly, propagated backward and undesirably suppressed actions that had previously contributed to successful completion of early stages.
- **Temporal GRPO Behavior:** In contrast, Temporal GRPO showed changes in $\Delta p_k$ close to zero on the shared preceding stages. This implies that it successfully preserved the acquired preceding behaviors. Simultaneously, Temporal GRPO produced the largest positive improvement at the first-difference stage ($m_d$), where rollouts started to diverge. This result supported the hypothesis that Temporal GRPO effectively concentrates policy updates on the specific action intervals responsible for observed outcome differences, rather than penalizing successful earlier steps.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies on LIBERO-Long

Ablation studies were performed to evaluate the contribution of key components of Temporal GRPO.
- **Impact of "entered-stage gating":** Removing the mechanism that ensures only rollouts having completed prerequisite stages participate in a stage's comparison led to a clear performance degradation. This supported the importance of comparing rollouts under shared prerequisite progress.
- **Impact of "same-stage grouping":** Disabling the grouping of rollouts by the stage they entered, and thus failing to compute stage-specific advantages, resulted in the largest component-level degradation. This finding highlighted that forming reliable relative comparison groups within the same stage is crucial for effective credit assignment.
- **Impact of "Stage Compiler":** Degradation was observed when the Stage Compiler, responsible for normalizing stage ordering and ensuring detectable completion conditions, was removed. This indicated that well-defined and verifiable stages are essential for reliable rollout-to-stage alignment.
- **Comparison with Baselines and Ablations:**
 - Stage-Reward GRPO (which converts stage progress into scalar rewards but retains trajectory-level advantage assignment) consistently outperformed Trajectory-GRPO, confirming the utility of incorporating stage progress information.
 - Temporal GRPO, however, achieved the best performance among all ablated variants and baselines. This demonstrated that the combined approach of stage-conditioned comparison and the assignment of advantages specifically to their corresponding action intervals is critical for maximizing performance.

In summary, the results consistently indicate that Temporal GRPO improves task success, enhances sample efficiency, and enables more precise credit assignment by localizing policy updates to relevant stages.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Temporal GRPO represents a methodological advancement in the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) reinforcement learning, particularly for training robotic manipulation policies from outcome-driven feedback. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several aspects:

- **Addressing a Core Challenge in Long-Horizon RL:** The paper effectively identifies and addresses "trajectory-level credit aliasing," a fundamental issue in outcome-driven reinforcement learning where a single, terminal reward signal is used for long-horizon tasks. This problem is exacerbated in robotic manipulation due to the sequential nature of complex tasks and the difficulty of sparse rewards. By providing a more granular, stage-conditioned credit assignment, Temporal GRPO offers a more accurate learning signal.
- **Improved Performance and Sample Efficiency:** The empirical results on RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrate substantial improvements in task success rates across various task horizons, especially for long and extra-long tasks. The enhanced sample efficiency implies that policies can learn more effectively with fewer environment interactions, which is crucial in robotics where real-world data collection can be costly and time-consuming. This directly contributes to more practical and scalable VLA policy post-training.
- **Enhanced Interpretability and Subskill Learning:** The controlled credit assignment experiments on LIBERO-Long reveal that Temporal GRPO can preserve already acquired successful sub-behaviors (e.g., preceding stages) while focusing policy updates on the specific stages where failures occur. This localized learning mechanism not only prevents the degradation of functional subskills but also potentially leads to more stable and interpretable learning, as the policy effectively isolates and resolves specific points of failure.
- **Compatibility with Existing VLA Frameworks:** Temporal GRPO is designed to integrate with existing GRPO-based VLA optimization schemes without requiring modifications to the VLA architecture or the introduction of additional value models. This makes it a readily adoptable improvement for current and future outcome-driven VLA-RL systems.
- **Foundation for More Complex Task Structures:** While the current implementation relies on reliable stage predicates and a linear stage order, the conceptual framework of stage-conditioned temporal credit assignment lays a foundation for future extensions. The explicit acknowledgement of limitations, such as handling ambiguous stage boundaries, branching task progress, or recovery through earlier stages, suggests clear avenues for future research into uncertainty-aware stage detection and dynamic stage graphs. Such advancements could further broaden the applicability of this approach to even more intricate and flexible robotic tasks.
- **Reduced Dependence on Expert Demonstrations:** By improving the efficacy of reinforcement learning from sparse task-success feedback, Temporal GRPO contributes to reducing the overall reliance on extensive expert demonstrations for VLA models. This aligns with the broader goal of enabling robots to acquire general manipulation capabilities through scalable interaction and self-improvement.

In conclusion, Temporal GRPO makes a significant contribution by refining the credit assignment problem in VLA-RL, leading to more robust, efficient, and targeted policy learning for complex robotic manipulation tasks. Its principles could influence the design of future reinforcement learning algorithms for embodied AI that operate in multi-stage environments.
