# Self-Refining Vision Language Model for Robotic Failure Detection and Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> ARMOR, a Vision-Language Model (VLM) based system, addresses robotic failure detection and open-ended reasoning by leveraging a multi-task self-refinement process. It achieved superior performance in both detection accuracy and reasoning quality across diverse robotic datasets while effectively handling heterogeneous supervision, leading to more reliable and interpretable robotic operations.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic failure detection struggles with the complexity and diversity of real-world failures, often relying on closed-set classification that is inadequate for subtle and combinatorial faults.
- Acquiring detailed natural language reasoning annotations for diverse failure modes is costly and time-consuming, creating a data heterogeneity challenge where large-scale sparse labels exist alongside limited dense reasoning data.
- Existing VLM-based approaches for robotic failure reasoning often reduce the problem to classification, lose fine-grained visual cues, or lack effective fine-tuning for open-ended generation and iterative refinement.

## Method

- ARMOR formulates failure detection and reasoning as a multi-task self-refinement process, using a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL VLM backbone with separate prediction heads for binary detection and natural language reasoning, plus custom prompt engineering for task-specific conditioning.
- It utilizes a two-phase training procedure, including offline imitation (warm-up and expert-conditioned training) and online refinement, to effectively learn from heterogeneous datasets (sparse binary and dense reasoning labels).
- During inference, ARMOR generates multiple stochastic refinement trajectories and uses an entropy-based self-certainty metric to select the most confident final detection and reasoning output.

## Results

- ARMOR consistently achieved the highest scores in both binary failure detection and reasoning quality across four diverse robotic failure datasets (RLBench-Fail, Maniskill-Fail, Sparrow-Fail, ARMBench).
- The system demonstrated robustness to distribution shifts, outperforming baseline fine-tuning by **+25.6%** in detection and maintaining high reasoning quality in scenarios with small shifts (R→M), and by **+13.3%** in detection and **+14.6%** in reasoning for large shifts (S→A).
- Iterative refinement improved reasoning by up to **+42.4%** from Round 0 to Round 1 (with further gains and reduced variance in later rounds), and ARMOR stayed robust with up to a 10x sparse/dense annotation imbalance, scaling effectively to the 32B VLM version.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Iterative self-refinement, guided by an entropy-based confidence metric, significantly enhances both binary failure detection accuracy and the quality of open-ended natural language reasoning, effectively reducing uncertainty and improving prediction consistency across multiple refinement rounds.
> - A multi-task architecture with dedicated heads for detection and reasoning allows for effective optimization of distinct objectives (classification vs. generation), especially under heterogeneous supervision.
> - Combining large-scale sparse binary labels with smaller-scale dense reasoning annotations through a self-refinement process is crucial for developing practical and robust robotic failure analysis systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of reliable and trustworthy robotic systems necessitates the ability to reason about task execution and diagnose failures. Traditional approaches to robotic failure detection often treat the problem as a closed-set classification task or rely on extensive human annotations for a predefined set of failure modes. However, real-world robotic failures are frequently subtle, combinatorial, and difficult to enumerate comprehensively, rendering closed-set classification inadequate. Furthermore, acquiring detailed human annotations for diverse reasoning labels is costly and time-consuming.

Recent advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened avenues for leveraging broad visual knowledge and open-ended natural language generation for more sophisticated failure reasoning. Prior VLM-based approaches have either focused on abstracting visual input into symbolic representations, potentially losing fine-grained visual cues (e.g., Liu et al., 2023b; Zhou et al., 2025), or utilized off-the-shelf VLMs without fine-tuning, which limits performance (e.g., Skreta et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024). Some methods fine-tune VLMs but still assume a fixed set of failure modes, reducing the task to classification (e.g., Duan et al., 2025; Du et al., 2023b). A notable prior work, AHA (Duan et al., 2025), applied supervised instruction fine-tuning to simulated failures with complete reasoning annotations but suffered from data inefficiency in settings with heterogeneous data and relied on hand-crafted regular expressions for evaluation, which can be sensitive to formatting.

Separately, the field of foundation models has explored enhancing reasoning capabilities through iterative refinement, demonstrating success in domains such as mathematics and coding (e.g., Madaan et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024). These methods often involve sequential refinement, thought verbalization, or self-critique mechanisms. While some approaches utilize self-correction, they may require a robust pre-existing reasoning model or leverage external reward models for optimization.

This work integrates these two lines of research by proposing a novel VLM-based approach that extends iterative reasoning to multi-modal robotic failure detection and open-ended natural language reasoning from high-dimensional video input. It addresses the challenge of heterogeneous supervision, where large-scale sparse binary labels are available alongside smaller-scale dense reasoning annotations, a common practical scenario in robotics. By formulating detection and reasoning as a multi-task self-refinement process, the paper aims to overcome the limitations of prior work in handling nuanced failures, data sparsity, and coupled task optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic systems to perform sophisticated failure detection and provide detailed, open-ended explanations for these failures. This capability is presented as crucial for building reliable and trustworthy autonomous robots, allowing for human intervention, continuous learning, and downstream applications such as reinforcement learning reward shaping and corrective task planning.

The motivation for this work arises from several challenges inherent in robotic failure reasoning:

1. **Complexity and Diversity of Real-World Failures:** Failures in real-world robotic operations are often subtle, combinatorial, and cannot be exhaustively enumerated into a fixed set of categories. This characteristic limits the effectiveness of traditional closed-set classification approaches. The authors argue for the need for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) that can leverage broad visual knowledge to reason about novel failure modes in open-ended natural language.
2. **Data Heterogeneity and Annotation Cost:** Training effective failure reasoning models is complicated by data availability. While binary success/failure labels can often be automatically derived from system logs or sensor data at scale, obtaining detailed natural language reasoning labels requires expert human annotation, making them expensive and time-consuming to acquire. This disparity creates a heterogeneous data regime that prior approaches, such as AHA, which often assume dense supervision for both tasks, are not optimized to handle.
3. **Limitations of Existing VLM-based Approaches:** Current VLM methods applied to robotic failure reasoning often reduce the problem to classification of predefined failure modes. Additionally, treating failure detection and reasoning as a single free-form text generation task can lead to optimization difficulties, reduced classification accuracy, and inconsistent outputs, as the two tasks have distinct objectives and evaluation criteria. Prior methods also typically do not leverage iterative refinement to improve prediction quality.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines the following key objectives:

- To propose a novel approach, ARMOR, capable of handling heterogeneous datasets (large-scale sparse binary labels and small-scale rich reasoning annotations) for open-ended, iterative failure detection and reasoning in robotics.
- To be the first to apply iterative reasoning with VLMs on multi-modal video input for robotic failure detection and reasoning.
- To develop a model that achieves state-of-the-art performance in both failure detection rate and reasoning quality, demonstrating robustness to heterogeneous supervision and the ability to reason beyond predefined failure modes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named ARMOR (Adaptive Round-based Multi-task mOdel for Robotic failure detection and reasoning), formulates failure detection and reasoning as a multi-task self-refinement process. This involves an iterative prediction mechanism where the model refines its detection outcomes and natural language reasoning conditioned on its past outputs.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup as a Multi-task MDP

The problem is formalized as a multi-task Markov Decision Process (MDP) M = (S, A, R, T).
- **State Space (S):** A state `s_t` at round `t` includes the input video `x`, prior detection and reasoning predictions (`l_t-1`, `e_t-1`), and an auxiliary prompt `p`.
- **Action Space (A):** An action `a_t` comprises the current multi-task predictions (`l_t`, `e_t`), which are jointly produced.
- **Reward Functions (R):** `R_detect` and `R_reason` define rewards for detection and reasoning, respectively, based on the correctness of predictions compared to ground truth.
- **Horizon (T):** The number of refinement rounds.
The goal is to fine-tune a VLM, `π_θ`, to produce human-like reasoning and achieve high failure-detection accuracy after `T` refinement rounds.

#### 3.2. Heterogeneous Supervision Setting

ARMOR operates under a heterogeneous supervision setting, utilizing two types of datasets:
- `D_sparse = {(x_i, l_i)}`: A large-scale dataset containing input videos `x_i` and sparse binary labels `l_i` (success/failure). These are typically easier to acquire.
- `D_dense = {(x_i, l_i, e_i)}`: A smaller, richer dataset with `x_i`, binary labels `l_i`, and detailed natural language reasoning `e_i`. These require expert human annotation.

#### 3.3. Multi-task Prediction Architecture

Instead of treating detection and reasoning as a single free-form text generation task, ARMOR employs a multi-task architecture with separate prediction heads.
- **VLM Backbone:** Qwen2.5-VL (Bai et al., 2025) is used as the foundational VLM, comprising a vision encoder and a language model decoder.
- **Detection Head:** A lightweight classification head is attached to an intermediate decoder representation (specifically, from the [CLS] token). This head is trained with a binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss to produce a binary detection outcome (`l`).
- **Reasoning Head:** The original language model head is retained for generating natural language reasoning (`e`), trained with a next-token prediction (NTP) loss.
Both heads share the vision encoder and language decoder parameters, promoting consistent representations while allowing task-specific optimization. Task-specific input prompts are used for each head.

#### 3.4. Training VLMs on Multi-task Sequential Prediction Paths (Algorithm 1)

The training procedure consists of two phases to fine-tune the VLM:
- **Phase I: Offline Imitation:**
 - **Warm-up:** The model is initially trained to predict detection and reasoning without conditioning on previous rounds, i.e., `(l_1, e_1) ~ π_θ(· | [x, ∅, ∅, ∅])`. This establishes a baseline.
 - **Expert-conditioned:** Leveraging `D_dense`, the model is further trained to predict `(l_1, e_1)` conditioned on the *ground-truth* expert outputs `(l, e)`. This stage encourages the model to maintain consistency across tasks and preserve correct prior predictions. During this, one of the ground truth inputs (`l` or `e`) is randomly masked to prevent direct copying and encourage learning from cross-task and visual information.
- **Phase II: Online Refinement:**
 - To address potential discrepancies between expert demonstrations and the model's own behavior, the model is fine-tuned using online rollouts. For each training example, the policy is rolled out for `T` rounds.
 - At each round `t`, the model predicts `(l_t, e_t)` conditioned on its *own* previous predictions `(l_t-1, e_t-1)`.
 - Losses are applied based on available supervision: BCE for detection (on both `D_sparse` and `D_dense`) and NTP for reasoning (on `D_dense` only). Reasoning on `D_sparse` is generated but not penalized. This process enables the model to refine its predictions over its own generated trajectories while still being anchored by ground truth where available.

#### 3.5. Iterative Self-refinement for Inference (Algorithm 2)

During inference, ARMOR generates multiple stochastic refinement trajectories and employs an entropy-based self-certainty metric (Kang et al., 2025) to select the most confident prediction.
- **Trajectory Generation:** Starting from empty initial predictions, the model iteratively predicts `(l_t^(m), e_t^(m))` for each trajectory `m` and round `t`, conditioned on the previous round's outputs.
- **Confidence Metric:** A combined entropy score `C^(m) = H_det^(m) + λ H_exp^(m)` is computed. `H_det` is the detection entropy, and `H_exp` is the mean token entropy for reasoning. A weight `λ` (set to 0.1) prioritizes detection entropy.
- **Termination and Selection:** Refinement continues until the best trajectory's uncertainty (lowest `C^(m)`) no longer decreases significantly (beyond a tolerance `ϵ`). The final output is chosen from the trajectory with the lowest entropy score at termination. Entropy thus guides both the stopping criterion and the selection of the most confident prediction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated ARMOR across four diverse failure datasets, covering both simulated environments (RLBench-Fail, Maniskill-Fail) and real-world warehouse operations (Sparrow-Fail, ARMBench). The evaluation metrics included binary success rate for failure detection, and LLM Fuzzy Match score and ROUGE-L for reasoning quality.

#### 4.1. Failure Detection and Reasoning Performance

- **Superior Performance:** ARMOR consistently achieved the highest scores in both binary failure detection and reasoning quality across all four evaluation datasets (Table 1).
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - **Fine-tuning Approaches:** Supervised fine-tuning on dense data only (SFT-D) yielded suboptimal results. Fine-tuning on both sparse and dense data (SFT-S+D) often led to overfitting on sparse data and failure to produce meaningful reasoning, resulting in inferior performance compared to ARMOR.
 - **Open-source VLMs:** Generic instruction-tuned VLMs like Qwen2.5-VL and Cosmos-Reasoning performed close to random guessing (50% accuracy) for detection, indicating a need for specific alignment for failure reasoning tasks. LLaVA-NeXT also showed limited performance.
 - **Proprietary VLMs:** Claude-3.7, especially with few-shot prompting, achieved stronger overall performance than open-source VLMs but generally performed below ARMOR in most tasks.
- **Robustness to Heterogeneous Supervision and Distribution Shifts:**
 - **Small Distribution Shift (R→M):** When trained with sparse data from RLBench and dense data from Maniskill (differing in embodiment and workspace), ARMOR improved detection by +25.6% over SFT-D and maintained high reasoning quality. SFT-S+D's reasoning quality substantially deteriorated, illustrating the challenges of naively mixing heterogeneous data.
 - **Large Distribution Shift (S→A):** In a more challenging setting with sparse data from Sparrow and dense data from ARMBench (differing in workspace and failure reasoning), ARMOR outperformed SFT-D by +13.3% in detection and +14.6% in reasoning, and significantly surpassed SFT-S+D. These results indicate ARMOR's robustness and generalization capabilities in complex data scenarios.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Component Contribution (Table 2):**
 - **Multitask Prediction (architecture only):** Achieved strong detection accuracy (0.897) but poor reasoning quality (0.460), suggesting that architectural separation alone is insufficient for robust reasoning.
 - **Refinement Only (no multi-task conditional training):** Showed a slight improvement in reasoning (0.488) but a reduction in detection accuracy (0.803), highlighting the necessity of multi-task conditional training.
 - **Offline Imitation / Online Imitation Only:** Both improved reasoning quality over the previous variants, with online data contributing more significantly to reasoning.
 - **Full ARMOR:** Combining all components (multi-task design, refinement, offline, and online training) yielded the best overall performance, affirming the synergistic benefits of the proposed methodology.
- **Number of Refinement Rounds (Table 3, Figure 3a):**
 - Reasoning performance, measured by LLM Fuzzy score, consistently improved with each additional refinement round, while the variance decreased. For instance, Round 1 showed a +42.4% improvement over Round 0, with subsequent smaller gains.
 - Entropy for both detection and reasoning steadily decreased and stabilized across rounds. The combined entropy metric also decreased, correlating with improvements in both detection accuracy and reasoning quality. This demonstrates that ARMOR's iterative refinement process reduces uncertainty and enhances prediction quality.
- **Effect of Sparse-Dense Data Ratio (Table 7):** ARMOR demonstrated robustness to up to a 10x imbalance between sparse and dense annotations. While reasoning performance started to deteriorate at a 30x imbalance, ARMOR scaled effectively with increasing dense data.
- **Effect of Model Size (Table 8):** The 32B version of Qwen2.5-VL integrated with ARMOR showed consistent performance gains compared to the 7B version, indicating that ARMOR's approach scales effectively with larger model capacities.

#### 4.3. Refinement Process Qualitative Analysis

- A qualitative example (Figure 3b) illustrated ARMOR's ability to iteratively refine predictions. An initially correct detection but incorrect reasoning was first adjusted for consistency and then refined into an accurate account of the failure. This indicates that multi-round refinement can reduce inconsistencies and enhance reasoning quality, often by correcting reasoning based on more reliable detection results.
- An example of refinement failure (Appendix C.2) showed that while binary detection remained correct, the reasoning drifted through alternative incorrect explanations, failing to converge on the true cause of failure. This suggests future improvements could involve structured failure attributes.

#### 4.4. Inference Cost

- ARMOR introduced minimal computational overhead. Each additional refinement round increased wall-clock time by approximately 1 second and caused a modest increase in memory usage per GPU (from 3.48GB for Round 0 to 6.31GB for Round 3). This suggests a practical trade-off can be made between refinement rounds and computational efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents ARMOR as a novel approach for robotic failure detection and reasoning, offering several points of significance and potential impact:

- **Advancement in Robotic Reliability:** By enabling robots to not only detect failures but also provide open-ended natural language explanations, ARMOR contributes to building more reliable and trustworthy autonomous systems. This capability can facilitate human understanding of robotic malfunctions, allowing for more effective intervention and supervision.
- **Addressing Real-World Complexity:** The method moves beyond the limitations of predefined failure modes, allowing for reasoning about subtle, combinatorial, and novel failures common in real-world robotic environments. This generalization capability is crucial for deploying robots in diverse and unstructured settings.
- **Efficient Use of Heterogeneous Data:** ARMOR's ability to effectively leverage heterogeneous supervision—combining readily available large-scale sparse binary labels with expensive, small-scale dense reasoning annotations—addresses a practical data acquisition challenge in robotics. This can reduce the cost and effort associated with manual data annotation for training sophisticated reasoning models.
- **Enhanced Interpretability and Learning:** The natural language reasoning provided by ARMOR offers interpretable feedback. This feedback can be used for various downstream applications, such as generating shaped rewards for reinforcement learning to improve robot policies, or informing the creation of corrective task plans, thereby fostering continuous learning and reducing the reliance on expert supervision during deployment.
- **Extension of Iterative Refinement:** The work extends the concept of iterative refinement, which has shown success in other foundation model applications (e.g., math, coding), to the multi-modal domain of robotic failure detection and reasoning with high-dimensional video inputs. This demonstrates a novel application of these advanced reasoning techniques.
- **Improved VLM Fine-tuning:** ARMOR's multi-task architecture and training approach, which includes both offline imitation and online refinement, provides a framework for effectively fine-tuning generative VLMs for combined classification and open-ended generation tasks, particularly under mixed supervision conditions.

Future research directions suggested by the authors include integrating richer training signals like task rewards and human preferences, extending ARMOR to synergistic tasks such as predicting safety violations and recovery strategies, and incorporating additional modalities like force-torque sensing and proprioception to capture a broader range of real-world failures. The broader impact aims to enhance safety, transparency, and reliability in autonomous systems, while also acknowledging the ethical consideration of avoiding over-reliance on imperfect reasoning models in safety-critical contexts, emphasizing the continued need for rigorous monitoring and human oversight.
