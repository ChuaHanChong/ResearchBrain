# Graph-Structured Visual Imitation

> [!summary] Summary
> Visual Entity Graphs (VEGs) cast visual imitation as visual correspondence: nodes are objects, points, and hand/gripper keypoints detected without annotation; edges encode relative 3D spatial arrangements. Mismatch between demonstrator and imitator graphs forms a reward for PILQR trajectory optimization, letting a Baxter robot imitate pushing, stacking, and pouring from one human video within minutes.

## Problem

- Prior visual imitation relies on environment instrumentation such as AR tags or dense articulated trackers to read off object and hand 3D poses during demonstrations.
- Full-frame CNN embedding methods need many videos of both humans and the robot executing the task, which defeats the purpose of learning purely from observation.
- Off-the-shelf detectors trained on ImageNet or COCO miss demonstration-specific objects, and pixel-intensity matching breaks under viewpoint, embodiment, and illumination differences.

## Method

- **VEG** (==Visual Entity Graphs==): hierarchical what-where video encoding with object, point, and hand/robot nodes, plus edges scoring differences of relative 3D coordinate offsets across demonstrator and imitator.
- Three annotation-free detectors: ==Mask R-CNN== finetuned on synthetically augmented background-subtracted masks, ==ResNet-34== point descriptors trained by cross-view ==contrastive loss==, and off-the-shelf hand keypoint detection.
- Motion-saliency binary attention adds or drops edges over time around an anchor moving object; the resulting cost drives ==PILQR== trajectory optimization over a 4-dimensional end-effector action space.

## Results

- Straight-line pushing with randomized start configurations within a 6cm-diameter ball succeeded in **5/5** runs on the trained octagon and **4/5** on an unseen smaller square, within **1cm** of target.
- Grasped-pushing and 90-degree direction-change pushing converged after **8** trajectory-optimization iterations, and pouring after **10**; the time-contrastive baseline failed both pushing variants and pouring rotation.
- The baseline, trained on **10** videos including **5** robot executions, produced non-discriminative cost curves and failed grasp-based stacking, succeeding only when the object was already grasped.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Factorizing scenes into persistent entities plus changing spatial relations yields reward curves discriminative enough for policy search, where holistic frame embeddings stay flat.
> - Reusing pretrained vision modules as detectors, rather than learning similarity end-to-end, removes the need for robot-execution data and enables single-demonstration imitation.
> - Structured entity rewards transfer to objects with novel geometry and tolerate clutter and partial occlusion, but sustained full occlusion and model-free exploration remain unaddressed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The work situates itself within the field of visual imitation learning, a sub-area of artificial intelligence and robotics focused on enabling agents to acquire skills by observing human demonstrations. This approach aims to provide an intuitive method for programming robotic agents, contrasting with traditional hard-coding of behaviors. Visual imitation, particularly third-person imitation where the demonstrator and imitator operate in different environments, presents significant challenges. A primary difficulty lies in the "correspondence problem," which involves understanding the demonstrator's scene and its dynamic changes, then mapping these observations to the imitator's environment to cause a similar change. This requires a robust visual similarity function that can identify relevant visual cues while ignoring irrelevant variations.

Previous approaches to visual imitation often involve instrumentalizing the environment with markers (e.g., AR tags) to obtain precise 3D object and hand poses, or using hand-designed reward detectors that are effective only in constrained scenarios. More recent methods attempt to learn visual similarity by training and matching full-image feature embeddings using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). These CNN-based approaches often rely on extensive datasets, including both human video demonstrations and robot executions of the task, to learn embeddings robust to visual discrepancies between human and robot bodies, viewpoints, and environments. However, requiring robot execution data for training diminishes the utility of visual imitation as a quick and intuitive programming method, moving it closer to kinesthetic teaching. Furthermore, direct pixel matching is generally ineffective due to sensitivity to illumination, viewpoint changes, and human-robot morphological differences.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a departure from unstructured, full-frame CNN encodings. Instead, it advocates for a factorized, hierarchical visual representation that explicitly disentangles "what" to imitate (visual entities) from "where" they are located (spatial arrangements). This approach aims to address the limitations of prior work by achieving successful imitation from a single human demonstration, without requiring prior robot interactions, extensive random exploration, or environment instrumentation, thereby improving generalization across diverse environments and objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and generalized framework for visual imitation learning that enables a robotic agent to acquire manipulation skills from a single human video demonstration. The core motivation stems from the observed limitations of existing visual imitation techniques, particularly their inability to generalize effectively across varied environments and objects, and their often substantial data requirements.

Specifically, the authors identify several key areas of motivation:

1. **Addressing the Visual Correspondence Problem:** Visual imitation fundamentally relies on establishing correspondences between the demonstrator's visual scene and the imitator's scene. Traditional methods often fail when faced with differences in viewpoint, background clutter, object instances, or the inherent visual disparity between a human demonstrator and a robot imitator. The objective is to develop a method that can reliably detect and correspond relevant visual entities despite these variations.
2. **Reducing Data Requirements:** Many deep learning-based imitation methods necessitate large datasets, including robot interaction data, to train robust visual representations. This requirement contradicts the goal of rapid, intuitive skill acquisition. A key objective is to enable "few-shot" or "single-demonstration" learning, making imitation more practical and scalable for complex tasks.
3. **Enhancing Generalization:** Previous methods employing full-frame image encodings often struggle with generalization to novel objects or environmental configurations not explicitly seen during training. The research aims to achieve stronger generalization by decomposing the scene into constituent visual entities and their relationships, which are hypothesized to be more invariant than raw pixel data.
4. **Factorizing Visual Representations:** The motivation behind the proposed Visual Entity Graphs (VEGs) is to disentangle the identity ("what") of visual entities from their spatial configurations ("where"). This factorization is hypothesized to provide a more robust and interpretable representation for imitation, allowing the robot to focus on imitating the functional changes in spatial arrangements rather than superficial visual appearance.
5. **Minimizing Environmental Instrumentation:** Many successful robotic imitation systems rely on external sensors or markers (e.g., AR tags, motion capture) to obtain precise 3D information about objects and hands. A significant objective is to remove this dependency, making the imitation process less intrusive and more adaptable to real-world, unconstrained environments.
6. **Leveraging Computer Vision Advances:** The work is motivated by recent advancements in computer vision, such as robust human keypoint detectors, and self-supervised methods for learning object and point detectors. The objective is to integrate these techniques to automatically acquire fine-grained visual understanding without human annotations or extensive robot interactions.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to advance visual imitation learning towards more practical, generalized, and data-efficient robotic skill acquisition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology frames visual imitation as a visual correspondence problem, where a robotic agent is rewarded for actions that lead to a better match of relative spatial configurations between corresponding visual entities in its workspace and the demonstration.

#### 3.1. Visual Entity Graphs (VEGs)

The core of the approach is the use of hierarchical graph video representations, termed Visual Entity Graphs (VEGs). For each timestep, two VEGs are constructed: one for the demonstrator ($G_D^t$) and one for the imitator ($G_I^t$).
- **Nodes ($V^t_i$):** Represent visual entities such as objects, object parts, or salient points. Each node is associated with its 3D (X, Y, Z) world coordinate.
- **Edges ($E^t_{(i,j)}$):** Represent 3D spatial relations between node entities. These relationships, rather than individual appearances, are preserved during imitation.
- **Hierarchy:** The graphs are hierarchical, distinguishing between object nodes ($V_o$), point nodes ($V_p$), and hand/robot nodes ($V_h$). Point nodes are connected only to the object node they belong to, not directly to other point nodes.
- **Correspondence:** All nodes in the demonstrator's graph are in one-to-one correspondence with nodes in the imitator's graph. Entities can dynamically appear or disappear.
- **Disentanglement:** VEGs disentangle the "what" (identity of entities, constant over time) from the "where" (spatial arrangements, which change over time), based on the observation that the appearance of abstract entities remains constant, while their spatial configurations evolve according to physical laws.

#### 3.2. Cost Function

The imitation reward function is formulated as a cost function $C(G_D^t, G_I^t)$ that measures visual dissimilarity between the demonstrator's and imitator's graphs at each timestep $t$. It is defined as:

$C(G_D^t, G_I^t) = \sum_{i,j,i<j} w(E^t_{(i,j)}) \cdot att(E^t_{(i,j)}) \cdot ||(x^t_{D,i} - x^t_{D,j}) - (x^t_{I,i} - x^t_{I,j})||$

Where:
- $x^t_{D,i}$ and $x^t_{I,i}$ are the 3D world coordinates of corresponding entity $i$ in the demonstrator's and imitator's environments, respectively.
- $att(E^t_{(i,j)}) \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary attention function indicating whether an edge is considered at that timestep, based on motion saliency.
- $w(E^t_{(i,j)}) \in \mathbb{R}$ are edge weights, empirically set hyper-parameters tied across edges of the same type (object-hand, object-object, object-point). The Euclidean distance between the relative spatial configurations of corresponding entity pairs is minimized.

#### 3.3. Detecting Visual Entities

The system employs scene-specific self-supervised detectors to establish correspondences without human annotations or robot interactions.
- **Human Hand Keypoint Detectors:** State-of-the-art hand detectors (Simon et al.) are used to detect 3D locations of human finger joints via an RGB-D camera. These are then mapped to the robot's gripper (e.g., Baxter's parallel-jaw gripper tips to human thumb and index finger tips). Grasp/release actions are inferred by thresholding the distance between human finger tips.
- **Point Feature Detectors from Cross-View Correspondence:** To handle fine-grained object pose and deformation, self-supervised point feature detectors are used. An RGB-D camera attached to the robot's end-effector moves along random trajectories, collecting multiview image sequences of the workspace. Using robot kinematics and hand-eye calibration, accurate 3D scene reconstruction and pixel correspondences across viewpoints are obtained. These self-generated correspondences supervise the learning of deep feature descriptors (e.g., using a ResNet-34 backbone) via a pixel-wise contrastive loss. This produces dense per-point descriptors robust to pose and viewpoint changes, enabling matching between imitator and demonstrator scenes, and generalization to novel objects.
- **Synthetic Data Augmentation for Object Detectors:** For objects not found in large-scale datasets, a scene-specific object detector is trained. Background subtraction extracts 2D segmentation masks from the demonstration video. A large synthetic dataset is then generated by applying translation, scaling, rotation, and pixel intensity changes to these masks, creating images where objects may partially overlap to promote amodal detection. A Mask R-CNN, pre-trained on MS-COCO, is fine-tuned on this synthetic data to predict object bounding boxes and masks. In cases of full occlusion, the last known location of the object is used.

#### 3.4. Motion Saliency for Dynamic Graph Construction

The `att(E^t_{(i,j)})` function dynamically determines which edges are active in the cost function. An "anchor object" is defined as any object in motion. If no objects are moving, the closest-in-the-future moving object becomes the anchor. Edges are then considered between the anchor object node, all other corresponding object nodes, the hand/robot node, and all point nodes belonging to the anchor object. This heuristic is inspired by human attention mechanisms during imitation.

#### 3.5. Policy Learning with Visual Entity Graphs

The imitation task is formulated as a reinforcement learning problem where the cost at each timestep is derived from the VEG-based dissimilarity (Eq. 1). The policy is learned using PILQR (Path Integral Linear Quadratic Regulator), a trajectory optimization method that combines model-based LQR with path integral policy search.
- **Action Space:** The robot's end-effector 3D position and orientation about the vertical axis (4-dimensional).
- **State Representation:** Concatenation of joint angles, end-effector position, and the graph configuration of the scene (including averaged pairwise distances of point features).
- **Gripper Control:** Opening and closing of the robot gripper are inferred via behavioral cloning, by thresholding the distance between the human index finger and thumb during the demonstration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed graph-structured visual imitation framework was evaluated on a Baxter robot across several manipulation tasks, comparing it against a Time-Contrastive Network (TCN) baseline.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks:** Pushing (straight-line, grasped, direction-change), Stacking, and Pouring.
- **Detector Training:** Object detectors via synthetic data augmentation and point features via multi-view self-supervised learning were fully automated and did not require human demonstrations or robot interactions.
- **Baseline:** TCN, an Inception-v3 based image embedding network trained using a triplet ranking loss on 10 video sequences (5 human, 5 robot executions) for each task. The TCN reward function was based on the L2 distance between embedding vectors. The data collection for TCN training was given comparable time effort to the automated data collection for VEG detectors.
- **Policy Learning:** PILQR was used for both methods, minimizing their respective cost functions.

#### 4.2. Reward Shaping Analysis

Figure 3c illustrates the behavior of the cost functions. The VEG-based cost function exhibited high discriminability for imitation quality. It consistently identified correct robot imitations, even with significant background clutter, and accurately signaled incorrect imitation segments. In contrast, the TCN baseline's cost curves were non-discriminative, failing to clearly distinguish between successful and unsuccessful imitations. This discriminative power of the VEG cost function was identified as crucial for effective policy learning.

#### 4.3. Task - Pushing

- **Straight-line pushing:** The robot needed to push an object (yellow octagon) along a straight path. The VEG method achieved a 100% success rate (5/5 runs) when perturbing the starting configurations within a 6cm diameter norm ball. It also demonstrated strong generalization by successfully pushing a novel object (smaller orange square) in 4 out of 5 runs, despite differences in geometry. The TCN baseline failed in almost all runs, including the basic straight-line pushing, even with more iterations.
- **Straight-line-grasped:** The robot had to push the object while grasping it, requiring a different trajectory than the human demonstrator. The VEG method successfully solved this task within 8 iterations of trajectory optimization.
- **Direction-change:** The robot pushed the yellow octagon along a 90-degree trajectory, necessitating a change in contact point. The VEG method succeeded within 8 iterations, with an increased hand edge weight for stronger guidance.
- **Overall Pushing Performance:** The VEG method demonstrated robustness to spatial configuration variations and strong generalization across objects with novel geometries. TCN consistently failed in tasks requiring precise contact or grasping.

#### 4.4. Task - Stacking

- **Task Description:** The robot grasped an object (yellow octagon) and placed it on another (purple ring).
- **Performance:** The VEG method enabled robust policy learning for the yellow octagon and generalized to the orange square (a flatter, harder-to-grasp object), successfully learning the skill in most trials.
- **TCN Performance:** The TCN baseline was unable to learn a successful grasping action, which demands precise end-effector movements. When tested on a simpler variant (stacking an already-grasped yellow octagon, "simple-stacking"), TCN performed reasonably well, indicating its ability to imitate smooth trajectories but not fine-grained actions.

#### 4.5. Task - Pouring

- **Task Description:** The robot translated and rotated a can to pour liquid into a mug.
- **Performance:** The VEG method converged within 10 iterations and solved the task for all tested objects, including a novel object with a different shape and texture, demonstrating generalizability.
- **TCN Performance:** The TCN baseline could move the can along the trajectory but failed to perform the necessary rotation for successful pouring.

#### 4.6. Discussion and Limitations

The findings indicate that imitating the human hand trajectory significantly improves performance in reaching and grasping motions. While the method exhibits robustness to partial occlusions, tracking fully occluded objects over prolonged periods remains a limitation. Future work could explore temporal visual memory or active vision to address this. The absence of prior model information is another limitation, suggesting that learning a model over visual entity motion could accelerate policy search.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in visual imitation learning by introducing a graph-structured visual encoding that factorizes visual representations into entities and their spatial arrangements. The proposed Visual Entity Graphs (VEGs) provide a method for the robotic agent to learn complex manipulation skills from a single human video demonstration, which is a notable departure from approaches requiring extensive robot interaction data or environmental instrumentation.

The key contributions and their potential impacts include:

1. **Robust and Generalizable Visual Correspondence:** By explicitly modeling visual entities (objects, parts, points) and their 3D relative spatial arrangements, the method demonstrates enhanced robustness to variations in viewpoint, background clutter, and visual discrepancies between human demonstrators and robot imitators. This capability for fine-grained visual understanding, without relying on specific object categories or labeled datasets, improves generalization to novel object instances and varied environments.
2. **Data Efficiency:** The ability to learn from a single demonstration, without requiring robot random exploration or prior robot execution data, significantly reduces the data overhead and time commitment for teaching robots new skills. This makes visual imitation a more practical and accessible method for quickly programming agents in diverse real-world scenarios.
3. **Self-Supervised Visual Entity Detection:** The use of scene-specific self-supervised methods for detecting objects and points, combined with state-of-the-art human hand keypoint detectors, eliminates the need for manual annotations. This automated approach to visual parsing is critical for scaling visual imitation to new tasks and environments without expert human labeling.
4. **Discriminative Reward Function:** The VEG-based cost function provides a highly discriminative measure of imitation quality, which is crucial for effective policy learning in reinforcement learning frameworks. This contrasts with less structured full-frame image encodings that often yield non-discriminative reward signals.
5. **Hybrid Approach to Learning:** The work embodies a philosophy that integrates "hand-engineering" (structured graph representations, specific detector choices) with "end-to-end learning" (deep feature learning, policy optimization). This hybrid approach, leveraging inductive biases regarding object fixation and motion saliency, demonstrates a pathway to overcome the limitations of purely end-to-end or purely engineered systems, as advocated by contemporary research.

The potential impact of this work is in advancing the capabilities of robots to learn new manipulation tasks quickly and intuitively, making them more adaptable to unstructured environments. By providing a framework for robust visual imitation with minimal supervision and high generalization, it contributes towards the development of more intelligent and autonomous robotic systems that can acquire a broader range of skills through observation. Future work on handling prolonged occlusions and incorporating prior physics models could further enhance the method's capabilities.
