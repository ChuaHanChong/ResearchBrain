# Feedback-MPPI: Fast Sampling-Based MPC via Rollout Differentiation -- Adios low-level controllers

> [!summary] Summary
> Feedback-MPPI (F-MPPI) introduces a method to compute local linear feedback gains within the Model Predictive Path Integral framework, enabling high-frequency closed-loop control without full re-optimization. This approach demonstrated improved trajectory tracking, enhanced stability, and smoother control actions, reducing Root Mean Square Error by up to 64.6% in quadrotor real-world experiments.

## Problem

- The high computational load of Model Predictive Control (MPC), especially Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), limits its real-time applicability for fast, highly dynamic robotic systems requiring high control frequencies.
- Standard MPPI's control frequency is directly coupled with its solution computation rate, often necessitating simpler, separate low-level controllers for high-frequency operation.
- While gradient-based MPC benefits from Riccati-based feedback for high-frequency control, a similar systematic approach for sampling-based MPPI was lacking, restricting its responsiveness.

## Method

- Developed **Feedback-==MPPI==** (F-MPPI), which augments standard MPPI by incorporating local linear feedback gains derived from the sensitivity of the optimal MPPI action to initial state variations.
- Utilizes rollout differentiation, leveraging JAX's automatic differentiation capabilities on GPUs, to efficiently compute the gain matrix in parallel with trajectory evaluations.
- These computed gains enable a high-frequency inner control loop to apply rapid, local state corrections around a setpoint, without requiring a full re-optimization of the MPPI problem at every high-frequency step.

## Results

- F-MPPI gains for an inverted pendulum converged to LQR gains under comparable conditions, validating the gain computation methodology.
- In simulation, F-MPPI (50 Hz outer loop, 500 Hz inner loop) achieved competitive velocity tracking performance on a quadruped robot compared to standard MPPI running at 100 Hz, with similar computational cost to 80 Hz MPPI.
- Real-world quadrotor experiments showed F-MPPI (50 Hz outer, 200 Hz inner loop) significantly reduced Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) by **64.6%** (x-component) and **28.9%** (z-component) compared to standard MPPI (**66.7** Hz), while generating smoother control commands.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local linear feedback gains can be effectively derived from a sampling-based optimal control algorithm (MPPI) by differentiating its optimal control actions with respect to the initial state.
> - Integrating these 'MPPI gains' allows sampling-based controllers to achieve high-frequency closed-loop regulation, bridging a gap between the robustness of MPPI and the responsiveness of gradient-based feedback methods.
> - The proposed method provides a mechanism for local state stabilization and disturbance rejection in highly nonlinear and non-convex problems, where explicit linearization for feedback might be difficult or lead to poor approximations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model Predictive Control (MPC) has gained increased adoption in robotics due to advancements in computational hardware, allowing its application to systems with faster dynamics than traditional process industries. Despite these advancements, the computational time required to solve optimal control problems online at each control step remains a significant bottleneck, particularly for fast-paced and highly nonlinear robotic systems involved in tasks like agile motion generation and dynamic physical interaction. This challenge necessitates ongoing research into methods for accelerating MPC.

Existing approaches to address this computational bottleneck include:
- **Simplifying internal models**: Using reduced-order or template dynamics.
- **Framework modifications**: Employing distributed optimization or transformer-based constraint handling, which often require substantial alterations or specialized hardware.
- **Policy approximation**: Training neural networks to imitate MPC policies for faster execution.
- **Gradient-based methods**: These achieve efficiency by linearizing dynamics and constraints, notably through Real-Time Iteration schemes. A specific strategy within this category involves using equivalent feedback gains, such as Riccati gains derived from Differential Dynamic Programming (DDP) solvers or NLP sensitivities, to locally approximate the MPC solution. These gains enable high-frequency inner-loop control, effectively bridging the gap between slower optimal planning and fast low-level control. This concept has been applied to humanoid robots, quadruped locomotion, and has been extended to parameter-adaptive schemes and approximation methods with closed-loop guarantees.

An alternative to traditional gradient-based MPC is Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control. MPPI is a stochastic, sampling-based technique capable of handling highly nonlinear and non-convex problems without explicit gradient computations. It has been successfully applied in aggressive autonomous driving, agile quadrotor maneuvers, and contact-rich quadruped locomotion. However, MPPI faces its own set of challenges, including high computational load and actions that can exhibit noise, potentially leading to oscillations. Prior work on MPPI has focused on GPU acceleration, low-pass filtering, and adaptive sampling to improve its real-time applicability and stability.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by identifying a gap: while gradient-based MPC benefits from Riccati-based feedback for high-frequency control, a similar approach has not been systematically integrated into sampling-based MPPI. The proposed Feedback-MPPI (F-MPPI) aims to bridge this gap by introducing a method to compute local linear feedback gains within the MPPI framework. This integration intends to enhance responsiveness, smooth control actions, and enable high-frequency feedback control for sampling-based MPC, thereby making it more suitable for demanding real-time robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the computational limitations of Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control in real-time, high-frequency robotic scenarios. While MPPI is recognized for its flexibility in handling nonlinear dynamics and non-convex costs, its applicability is restricted by the computational demands required to solve the optimal control problem at each timestep. The control frequency of standard MPPI is directly coupled with the rate at which its solution can be computed, which is often insufficient for rapidly evolving, highly dynamic robotic systems. This limitation necessitates the use of additional, often simpler, low-level controllers for high-frequency operation, introducing complexity and potential performance compromises.

To address this challenge, the key objectives of this work are as follows:
- **Introduce a novel framework for computing local linear feedback gains within MPPI**: The central objective is to develop a method, termed Feedback-MPPI (F-MPPI), that augments standard MPPI by incorporating local linear feedback gains. These gains are derived from sensitivity analysis, drawing inspiration from Riccati-based feedback strategies commonly used in gradient-based MPC.
- **Enable rapid closed-loop corrections without full re-optimization**: The aim is for these locally derived gains to facilitate quick, closed-loop adjustments around the current state. This allows for high-frequency control updates without the need to perform a full re-optimization of the MPPI problem at every single control cycle, thereby improving responsiveness.
- **Extend Riccati-based feedback to sampling-based controllers**: By computing these local gains for MPPI, the research aims to extend the established paradigm of high-frequency feedback (historically associated with gradient-based methods) to the domain of stochastic sampling-based controllers.
- **Demonstrate improved control performance and stability**: A core objective is to validate that incorporating local feedback significantly enhances the overall control performance and stability of the system. This includes improvements in accuracy and smoothness of control actions.
- **Enable robust, high-frequency operation on complex robotic systems**: The ultimate goal is to enable MPPI to operate robustly at high frequencies, making it a more viable solution for complex robotic platforms, such as quadrupedal robots performing dynamic locomotion on uneven terrain and quadrotors executing aggressive maneuvers with onboard computation.
- **Bridge the gap between sampling-based and gradient-based MPC**: The work is motivated by the desire to integrate beneficial aspects of both classes of MPC, demonstrating that local feedback can effectively enhance sampling-based methods.

These objectives collectively aim to enhance the practical utility and real-time applicability of MPPI for demanding robotic control tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Feedback-MPPI (F-MPPI) methodology augments the standard Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control by integrating local linear feedback gains. This approach is designed to provide high-frequency control corrections around the current state without requiring a full re-optimization of the MPPI problem at every control step.

**Background on Model Predictive Path Integral Control (MPPI)**
MPPI is a sampling-based stochastic optimization technique used for optimal control problems with nonlinear dynamics, non-convex cost functions, and potentially non-differentiable constraints. It operates by:
1. **Problem Formulation**: Defining an optimal control problem (Equation 1) that minimizes a cost function over a prediction horizon N, subject to system dynamics `x_i+1 = f(x_i, u_i)` and a given initial state `x_0`. Control inputs `u_i` are parameterized by `π(θ, x_i, t_i)`, where `θ` are decision variables.
2. **Sampling**: Drawing `K` parameter samples `θ_k` from a Gaussian distribution centered at an initial guess `¯θ`.
3. **Rollout Generation**: For each sample `θ_k`, propagating the system dynamics forward in time to generate a candidate trajectory (rollout).
4. **Cost Evaluation**: Computing a total cost `J_k` for each trajectory (Equation 2).
5. **Optimal Parameter Update**: Calculating an optimal parameter update `θ*` as a weighted average of parameter perturbations `Δθ_k`, where weights `ω_k` are exponentially derived from trajectory costs `J_k` (Equations 3 and 4). A tuning parameter `λ` balances exploration and exploitation.
6. **Control Action**: The optimal parameters `θ*` directly yield the commanded control action `u*` (Equation 5), which is typically held constant over the sampling interval in standard MPPI.
7. **Parallelization**: MPPI commonly utilizes GPU acceleration for parallel evaluation of trajectories to reduce computation time.

**Feedback MPPI (F-MPPI) Enhancement**
The core innovation of F-MPPI is the computation of a first-order approximation of the MPPI action, allowing for higher control bandwidth than the base MPPI frequency.
1. **Sensitivity Derivation**: The method derives the sensitivity of the optimal inputs `u*` (from Equation 5) with respect to variations in the current initial state `x̂`. This sensitivity is represented by a feedback gain matrix `F ∈ ℝ^(n_u × n_x)`.
2. **Feedback Control Law**: The gain `F` is used to compute a new control action `u = u* + F(x̂ - x_sp)` (Equation 6), where `x_sp` is a local setpoint for the inner loop, continuously updated by linearly interpolating between the current state `x_0` and the next predicted state `x_1`.
3. **Gain Matrix Calculation**:
 - The derivation begins by applying the chain rule to differentiate `u*` with respect to `x_0` (Equation 7). This involves terms `∂π/∂θ`, `∂θ*/∂x_0`, and `∂π/∂x_0`.
 - The term `∂θ*/∂x_0` requires differentiating the weighted average of parameter perturbations. This involves differentiating the weights `ω_k` with respect to `x_0` (Equations 8, 9, 10), which in turn depends on the gradients of the trajectory costs `∂J_k/∂x_0`.
 - Combining these steps, the full expression for the MPPI gain matrix `F` is derived (Equation 12). For direct control sampling, this simplifies to Equation 13.

**Implementation Details**
- **Computational Efficiency**: The computation of cost gradients `∂J_k/∂x_0` is performed in parallel for each rollout, leveraging the same parallelization capabilities as standard MPPI.
- **Software Framework**: The F-MPPI algorithm is implemented in an open-source Python library utilizing JAX for JIT (Just-In-Time) compilation on CUDA-compatible GPUs. JAX's automatic differentiation capabilities are employed to compute cost gradients in parallel with rollouts, enhancing computational efficiency.

**Methodological Considerations and Limitations**
- **Differentiability**: The gain computation relies on the local differentiability of dynamics and costs within the rollouts. While MPPI can optimize over discontinuous problems, the computed gains will only approximate local behavior away from discontinuities.
- **Constraints**: Generic inequality constraints typically handled by indicator functions (adding large constant costs for violations) do not influence gain computation, as the cost term is locally constant. To account for constraints in the feedback, smooth barrier functions or explicit input clipping (which the gains can naturally account for) are required.

**Illustrative Example: Inverted Pendulum**
To validate the gain computation, F-MPPI was applied to an inverted pendulum system. The computed gains were compared to those obtained from an infinite horizon discrete-time Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) applied to the linearized system. Three MPPI scenarios were considered: using linearized dynamics, using nonlinear dynamics, and using nonlinear dynamics with input saturation. The objective was to observe if MPPI gains converged to LQR gains under similar conditions and how saturation affects them.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of Feedback-MPPI (F-MPPI) was evaluated through an illustrative example, simulations on a quadrupedal robot, and real-world experiments on a quadrotor, alongside further analysis on hybrid systems and non-convex constraints.

**Illustrative Example: Inverted Pendulum**
The inverted pendulum system served as a quantitative example to assess the behavior of the computed MPPI gains.
- **Comparison with LQR**: F-MPPI gains were compared against LQR gains for the linearized system. In scenarios without input constraints (both linear and nonlinear MPPI rollouts), the F-MPPI gains demonstrated average similarity to the optimal LQR gains.
- **Impact of Samples**: The variance of the MPPI gains decreased significantly as the number of samples (K) increased, indicating a convergence towards a stable value.
- **Effect of Input Saturation**: When input bounds were introduced, causing saturation of the optimal solution, the MPPI gains converged to zero. This outcome aligns with expectations, as no additional feedback action can be applied when inputs are already at their limits.

**Quadruped Motion Control (Simulation)**
The F-MPPI method was tested on a simulated Aliengo quadruped robot in MuJoCo, tracking linear velocity references on randomly generated uneven terrain while subjected to random disturbances.
- **Setup**: F-MPPI operated at 50 Hz, with its computed gains feeding a low-level controller at 500 Hz. This was compared against standard MPPI running at 50 Hz, 80 Hz, and 100 Hz, to evaluate performance against different computational budgets. All controllers used 5000 samples. The F-MPPI incurred a computational cost of 3 ms per run, while standard MPPI took 2 ms.
- **Performance**: F-MPPI, despite running its outer loop at 50 Hz, demonstrated competitive performance against standard MPPI running at 100 Hz and outperformed standard MPPI operating at 80 Hz (which represents a similar computational cost). The Mean Absolute Errors over 50 trials indicated the performance advantage of incorporating the high-frequency feedback.

**Quadrotor Motion Control (Real-World Experiments)**
The method was deployed on a MikroKopter quadrotor, with the F-MPPI controller executing onboard an Nvidia Jetson Orin NX.
- **Setup**: F-MPPI operated at 50 Hz, and its gain matrix was used to update control actions at 200 Hz for the low-level control module. This was compared to standard MPPI running at its maximum feasible frequency of 66.7 Hz on the same hardware. Both used 800 samples. The task involved reaching a goal position (1, 0.5) m in the x-z plane.
- **Performance**: F-MPPI achieved the target more quickly and with higher precision. The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for F-MPPI in the x-component was 0.017 m (compared to 0.048 m for standard MPPI), representing a 64.6% reduction. In the z-component, F-MPPI achieved an RMSE of 0.027 m (compared to 0.038 m for standard MPPI), a 28.9% reduction.
- **Control Smoothness**: F-MPPI generated significantly smoother control commands (rotor speeds), which can contribute to reduced actuator wear and lower power consumption.
- **Computation Time Analysis**: On the Jetson Orin NX, the gain calculation in F-MPPI increased the overall runtime by approximately 40%–70% compared to standard MPPI. This overhead was observed to increase with the number of samples and the prediction horizon, particularly when the number of rollouts exceeded available GPU cores.

**Application to Hybrid Systems and Non-Convex Constraints**
- **1D Hopper (Simulation)**: Applied to a 1D Hopper robot with hybrid dynamics (bouncing motion), subject to Gaussian noise. F-MPPI (50 Hz outer loop, 500 Hz inner loop) was able to regulate the system across its hybrid modes, successfully maintaining the desired height. Without the inner-loop correction, disturbances caused the robot to become stuck in a local minimum. This demonstrated the ability of MPPI gains to provide local regulation even for discontinuous problems.
- **Quadrotor Navigation Among Obstacles (Simulation)**: In a scenario involving navigation through circular obstacles, F-MPPI successfully guided the quadrotor to its goal. Obstacle avoidance was handled by incorporating a barrier term into the cost function. This illustrated the method's capacity to handle non-convex optimization problems and navigate complex environments, even though the gains do not explicitly account for indicator-function-based constraints.

In summary, the results consistently indicate that incorporating local linear feedback gains into MPPI significantly enhances control performance, stability, and trajectory tracking, even when accounting for the increased computational cost and operating the outer MPPI loop at a lower frequency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Feedback-MPPI (F-MPPI), a novel methodology that significantly extends the capabilities of Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control. The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to bridge a performance gap between sampling-based and gradient-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) approaches.

**Key Contributions and Advancements:**
- **Extension of Feedback Control Paradigm to MPPI**: By deriving a method for computing local linear feedback gains from sensitivity analysis within the stochastic, sampling-based MPPI framework, the authors effectively extend the Riccati-based feedback control paradigm, traditionally associated with gradient-based MPC, to MPPI. This represents a methodological advancement by combining strengths from both families of MPC.
- **Enhanced Real-Time Performance and High-Frequency Operation**: The introduction of these feedback gains enables rapid, high-frequency closed-loop corrections around the current state without requiring full re-optimization of the MPPI problem at every single control cycle. This directly addresses one of the primary limitations of standard MPPI, namely its computational demand limiting real-time applicability for fast, dynamic robotic systems.
- **Improved Control Performance and Stability**: The empirical results demonstrate that F-MPPI leads to notable improvements in control performance and stability. For the quadruped robot, it provided competitive performance even when its outer loop operated at a lower frequency compared to a faster standard MPPI. For the quadrotor, F-MPPI achieved significantly lower Root Mean Square Errors (RMSE) and faster goal acquisition in real-world experiments.
- **Smoother Control Actions**: The method generates smoother control commands, as evidenced in the quadrotor experiments. This is a practical benefit, as smoother actions can contribute to reduced actuator wear, lower power consumption, and improved overall system longevity.
- **Applicability to Complex Systems**: The validation across diverse robotic platforms (quadrupedal robot, quadrotor) and scenarios (dynamic locomotion, aggressive maneuvers, hybrid systems, navigation with non-convex constraints) suggests broad applicability for complex, highly nonlinear robotic tasks where MPPI's flexibility is valuable.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Wider Adoption of MPPI**: By mitigating the computational bottleneck and improving responsiveness, F-MPPI makes sampling-based predictive control a more viable and competitive option for real-time applications requiring high control bandwidth, particularly in agile robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The framework establishes a foundation for several future research directions. The authors specifically highlight its potential integration into:
 - **Sensitivity-based robust MPC**: To develop controllers that are more resilient to parametric uncertainties.
 - **Safe reinforcement learning**: To enhance safety guarantees in learning-based control policies.
 - **Data augmentation for sample-efficient learning**: To generate more effective training data for learned controllers.
- **Computational Optimization**: The work also points towards continued efforts in optimizing the computational aspects of gain calculation, such as leveraging advanced backpropagation techniques and further parallelization, which could lead to even greater efficiency.
- **Constraint Handling**: Future research into explicitly incorporating inequality constraints into the gain calculation could further enhance the method's robustness and safety guarantees.

In conclusion, F-MPPI represents a significant step towards enhancing the practical utility and robustness of sampling-based MPC for demanding robotic applications. By combining the strengths of path integral control with the benefits of local feedback, this work contributes to the development of more capable and efficient control strategies for advanced robotic systems.
