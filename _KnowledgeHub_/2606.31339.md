# Verification-Gated Agentic Mission-State Governance for Intelligent Industrial Multi-Robot Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications developed a verification-gated framework for intelligent industrial multi-robot systems, ensuring autonomous agent proposals adhere to critical operational constraints. This framework achieved 100% safety-audited mission completion with zero violations in industrial factory and remote-construction benchmarks, outperforming existing methods that generated hundreds of inconsistencies.

## Problem

- Autonomous agents (e.g., LLMs, optimizers) generating task proposals often violate critical real-world constraints like robot capabilities, resource exclusivity, or safety boundaries in dynamic environments.
- Existing multi-robot task allocation and planning methods lack a unified, synchronized state management for persistent task hierarchy, online commitments, temporary constraints, and cross-branch couplings.
- Global replanning after disturbances is computationally expensive and can disrupt unaffected or protected work regions, while simple blackboards may allow inconsistent state updates.

## Method

- A runtime governance framework featuring a dual-object canonical mission state: an ==Evolving Task Forest== for hierarchical task decomposition and a ==Governed Blackboard== for online execution records, resource locks, and temporary constraints.
- A ==Derived Execution Coupling Topology (ECT)== that dynamically projects execution couplings among task subtrees, exposing "hard edges" from persistent contracts and verified-hard blackboard records.
- A **Verification-Gated Commit** process where all agentic proposals undergo ==deterministic verification== against hard constraints, and **Coupling-Aware Bounded Repair** localizes recovery operations to only hard-coupled affected regions using the ECT.

## Results

- The proposed framework achieved **100%** raw and safety-audited mission completion with **zero** invalid commitments, lock conflicts, duplicate assignments, or disruption events in an industrial factory scenario and remote-construction benchmarks.
- Compared to baselines (DART-DAG, LiP-LP, Flat-BB, Global-Repair), the framework drastically reduced governance violations; for instance, DART-DAG exhibited **200.4** invalid commitments and **222.0** lock violations per **100** tasks, leading to significantly lower safety-audited completion rates.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of the verifier and structural repair, with global repair causing high "disruption node events" (**152.3 ± 135.7** in the Medium scenario), underscoring the benefits of the bounded repair approach.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Strict separation of proposal generation from verified commitment is crucial for safely integrating agentic AI into industrial multi-robot systems, preventing constraint violations and maintaining mission-state integrity.
> - A dual-object state representation with an evolving task hierarchy and a governed execution ledger provides a robust and consistent foundation for managing complex, long-horizon multi-robot missions.
> - Localized, coupling-aware repair, guided by a dynamically derived execution topology, significantly improves system robustness by preventing disruptive global replanning and preserving unaffected mission segments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of intelligent industrial systems, multi-robot coordination, and advanced artificial intelligence, particularly agentic AI. It addresses a challenge arising from the increasing use of autonomous agents—such as those powered by large language models (LLMs), optimization algorithms, or heuristics—to decompose tasks, propose robot actions, and adapt execution plans in dynamic cyber-physical environments.

Existing multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) methods provide tools for assigning tasks under various constraints, including capabilities, time, and energy. However, these methods often do not comprehensively account for long-duration factors such as persistent task hierarchy, online execution commitments, temporary operational constraints, complex cross-branch execution couplings, or the precise boundaries required for localized repair. Similarly, symbolic and hierarchical planning approaches, while capable of long-sequence reasoning and explicit model encoding (e.g., PDDL, HTN), typically assume that the underlying domain model, task instance, and execution state are consistently maintained. They do not intrinsically solve the *runtime maintenance* problem of ensuring consistency and synchronization of these evolving states.

Recent advancements in LLM and multimodal foundation models have demonstrated their utility in task interpretation, decomposition, and high-level robot coordination. Systems like SMART-LLM, DART-LLM, COHERENT, LiP-LLM, and EmboTeam leverage these models for semantic decomposition and high-level planning. However, the paper notes that proposals generated by these models, while semantically plausible, may still violate critical real-world constraints such as robot capabilities, residual energy, temporal orders, resource exclusivity, or safety boundaries. Benchmarks indicate that LLMs can sometimes fail at systematic planning and reasoning about change, necessitating additional grounding mechanisms, precondition checks, or simulator feedback in practical robot systems. This highlights a need to treat LLMs and similar agentic modules as *proposal sources* rather than ultimate execution authorities.

Feedback-driven planning and online repair methods are crucial for addressing real-world uncertainties, delays, and partial failures. Classical blackboard architectures provide a precedent for coordinating diverse reasoning sources through shared state. Nevertheless, existing feedback loops and blackboard implementations often function as communication logs or state caches without enforcing the rigorous coupling and governance needed across persistent task hierarchy, online commitments, temporary constraints, derived dependencies, and bounded repair. Such systems may permit inconsistent updates or disrupt protected work outside the immediate affected region.

This work draws upon principles from systems engineering, including runtime governance, supervisory control, runtime assurance, and model-based system representation. These disciplines emphasize explicit models and verification gates to ensure system safety and adherence to constraints. The paper extends these concepts by proposing a specific architectural framework for maintaining a dynamic, multi-robot mission state that integrates evolving task hierarchy, temporary constraints, resource locks, and verified execution in a synchronized manner. By explicitly separating proposal generation from verified commitment and introducing a mechanism for bounded, coupling-aware repair, this research aims to bridge the gap between the flexibility of agentic AI and the safety and reliability requirements of industrial multi-robot systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a recognized challenge in deploying intelligent industrial multi-robot systems: while agentic artificial intelligence offers substantial capabilities for task decomposition and action generation, it lacks intrinsic mechanisms to guarantee adherence to critical operational constraints and safety protocols during long-horizon execution. Autonomous proposal generation, whether from LLMs, optimization modules, or heuristics, does not inherently ensure that multi-robot systems preserve task dependencies, resource ownership, safety holds, or defined repair boundaries within dynamic cyber-physical environments.

The paper identifies specific limitations of current approaches that motivate the development of a novel governance framework:
- **Constraint Violations by Agentic Proposals**: A plausible action proposed by an agentic module may inadvertently violate a workstation mutex, residual energy bound, precedence relation, spatial access condition, temporary protective hold, or an already committed robot trace. Industrial operations demand that generated proposals are subject to a rigorous validation process before they translate into executable mission state.
- **Lack of Unified State Management**: Existing multi-robot allocation, symbolic planning, behavior-tree, and feedback-driven execution methods tend to address only parts of the overall problem. They rarely maintain a synchronized, comprehensive record that encompasses persistent task hierarchy, online execution commitments, temporary operational constraints, cross-branch execution couplings, and clearly defined repair boundaries.
- **Limitations of Static Task Graphs**: Static task graphs can lead to "frontier starvation" when unforeseen circumstances, such as delayed observations, expose new work or invalidate existing plans, preventing the system from progressing.
- **Inconsistencies in Flat Blackboard Memories**: While blackboards facilitate communication, they may record events without inherently governing which updates remain consistent with the overall mission state and its constraints. This can lead to issues like duplicate assignments or conflicting resource locks.
- **Disruptions from Global Replanning**: Global replanning, used to recover feasibility after disturbances, can be computationally expensive and may undesirably rewrite or disrupt protected work regions that are outside the immediate scope of the failure, leading to inefficiency and loss of progress.

Given these motivations, the research establishes four main objectives, presented as contributions:
1. **Agentic Mission-State Governance**: To design and implement a runtime layer that facilitates the generation of proposals by autonomous agents while safeguarding the integrity of industrial multi-robot mission state through a process of deterministic verification and atomic commitment.
2. **Task-Forest Blackboard Representation**: To develop a dual-object state model composed of an "evolving task forest" and a "governed blackboard." This model aims to logically separate persistent task hierarchy, decomposition, and contracts from online execution records, robot traces, resource locks, world beliefs, and temporary constraints, ensuring synchronized and consistent state management.
3. **Coupling-Aware Bounded Repair**: To create a mechanism for localized and bounded repair that utilizes a derived "execution coupling topology" (ECT). This mechanism is intended to restrict repair operations to only the hard-coupled affected neighborhood, thereby preventing unintended modifications to protected or unaffected parts of the mission.
4. **Industrial Validation and Stress Testing**: To rigorously evaluate the proposed framework in realistic industrial scenarios, including an indoor factory setting and remote-construction benchmarks. This objective involves stress testing, structural ablations, and scalability probes to demonstrate the framework's effectiveness in improving verified and safety-audited mission-state progress under modeled mission predicates.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a "verification-gated agentic mission-state governance framework" for intelligent industrial multi-robot systems. This framework operates through a structured control loop involving mission inputs, canonical state representation, a derived coupling view, and a governed transition mechanism.

#### 3.1. Canonical Mission State: Task Forest and Governed Blackboard

The core of the framework’s state management relies on two synchronized canonical state objects:
- **Evolving Task Forest ($\text{F}_t$)**: This object stores the hierarchical decomposition of the mission, representing tasks as nodes with associated contracts. Node contracts encapsulate preconditions, expected outputs, resource requirements, candidate robots or teams, temporal anchors, spatial zones, and verification summaries. The forest allows for "delayed grounding," meaning task attributes or descendants can remain unresolved until sufficient observations are acquired, thus adapting to unfamiliar environments. Node states (e.g., open, ready, committed, running, blocked, completed, failed, frozen, abandoned) are transitions governed by the framework. The "executable frontier" is dynamically determined, restricted to grounded nodes whose predecessors and resources are feasible.
- **Governed Blackboard ($\text{B}_t$)**: This object maintains all online, execution-relevant records. These include task-state projections, current robot states, world belief, resource locks, committed execution traces, candidate proposals, verification records, and scene-temporary constraints. The blackboard is "governed" in three key aspects:
 1. **Node-Anchored**: Execution-relevant state information is explicitly bound to specific nodes within the task forest.
 2. **Write-Restricted**: Heuristic, optimization, rule-based, or semantic modules can generate and propose changes, but they are restricted to writing *candidate* states or proposals, not directly committing changes to the mission state.
 3. **Atomically Committed**: Any update to the mission state (resulting from a verified proposal) modifies both the task forest and the blackboard as a single, synchronized, atomic transition to maintain consistency.
 Scene-temporary constraints are a crucial component of the blackboard, originating from dynamic events like world-belief updates or operator input. These constraints can be categorized as candidate, protective, verified-soft, verified-hard, rejected, or expired, with only verified-hard constraints influencing the "hard" couplings in the derived Execution Coupling Topology (ECT).

#### 3.2. Derived Execution Coupling Topology (ECT)

The ECT ($\text{G}_t$) is a decision-time projection that exposes dynamic execution couplings among semantic subtree units within the task forest. It is *derived* from the current snapshot of $\text{F}_t$ and $\text{B}_t$, rather than being a persistently stored state object.
- **Units ($\text{U}_t$)**: The ECT represents execution units selected from an antichain of task-forest subtrees. This design ensures that a branch and its descendant are not simultaneously treated as independent global execution units, allowing the verifier to reason at an appropriate semantic level for current decisions.
- **Typed Edge Set ($\text{R}_t$)**: The edges in the ECT represent various types of dependencies and constraints, including precedence, enabling-flow, resource-mutex, synchronization, spatial-coupling, and soft-preference edges. Crucially, "hard edges" are induced exclusively by persistent node contracts in the task forest and by verified-hard records on the blackboard. Candidate constraints and soft edges do not contribute to hard repair closure, and protective holds are enforced by the verifier as guards, not as persistent topological features.

#### 3.3. Verification-Gated Commit and Bounded Repair

The framework treats all proposed changes—assignments, deferrals, reorderings, diagnostic actions, repairs, and constraint updates—as typed proposals ($\text{p}$). These proposals can be generated by various modules (e.g., heuristics, optimization, rule-based systems, LLM/VLM reasoning), but their ability to modify the committed mission state is strictly conditional on passing a deterministic verification process.
- **Deterministic Verification ($\Omega$)**: A proposal is deemed valid only if it satisfies a conjunction of hard-constraint predicates. These predicates check for:
 - **Dependency consistency ($\Omega_{dep}$)**: Ensures adherence to forest hierarchy, ECT precedence, and flow edges.
 - **Robot capability feasibility ($\Omega_{cap}$)**: Verifies that assigned robots or coalitions possess necessary capabilities.
 - **Residual energy feasibility ($\Omega_{eng}$)**: Confirms that robot energy levels meet requirements after accounting for traversal and execution.
 - **Temporal consistency ($\Omega_{temp}$)**: Checks for adherence to predicted intervals, robot traces, temporal anchors, and synchronization constraints.
 - **Resource exclusivity ($\Omega_{res}$)**: Prevents conflicts over non-shareable resources and ensures adherence to resource locks.
 - **Repair boundary preservation ($\Omega_{bd}$)**: This is critical during repair, ensuring modifications do not cross protected boundaries or bypass safety-critical holds.
- **Atomic Commit**: If a proposal satisfies all verification predicates (i.e., $\Omega=1$), it is selected based on an associated cost function ($\text{J}(\text{p})$) and then atomically committed. This atomic operation ensures that both $\text{F}_t$ and $\text{B}_t$ are updated synchronously, preserving the modeled consistency invariant. If no valid proposal exists, no high-level commitment is made in that decision cycle.
- **Bounded Repair**: Upon detection of a disturbance, the framework localizes the affected region rather than initiating a global replan. Disturbed forest nodes are identified, leading to a set of "disturbed units" in the ECT. This set is then expanded via hard-edge closure to determine all "affected units." Repairs are then strictly restricted to the subforest corresponding to these affected units and their projected blackboard context. A "boundary context" (including completed work, protected commitments, resource locks, temporal anchors, safety-critical holds, and hard-coupled protected boundary units) is rigorously preserved. If no verified repair proposal is found, a deterministic fallback optimization is attempted within the same localized scope. If still no valid option, the affected branch is marked as blocked or deferred, rather than committing an unverified update. Safety-critical holds are never bypassed.

The framework's theoretical underpinnings are supported by two propositions: Proposition 1 states that under an assumption of sound projected state, verified atomic commitments preserve the hard constraints checked by the verifier. Proposition 2 asserts that ECT-aware bounded repair, when satisfying the boundary predicate, ensures the invariance of completed nodes, protected commitments, and unaffected traces outside the defined affected scope.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation assessed the verification-gated mission-state governance framework across an industrial factory scenario and remote-construction benchmarks, comparing it against various baselines and structural ablations.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

The evaluation used heterogeneous robot teams, capability-typed tasks, residual-energy checks, terrain/path costs, non-shareable resource locks, delayed grounding, and scheduled execution feedback. Scenarios included:
- **Indoor Factory**: 12 robots, 72 executable nodes, shared workstations, aisle-like resource locks, inspection/rework loops, and cross-tree production dependencies. This tested safety and coordination under industrial constraints.
- **Remote-Construction Benchmarks (Small, Medium, Large, XL probe)**: Varied in robot count (6-24), executable nodes (76-704), and uncertainty (25-70% unknown information). These introduced challenges like sparse prior information, delayed grounding, high traversal costs, temporary spatial constraints, and expensive repair, often with scheduled faults and resource pressure.

Baselines included classical allocators (Greedy, Random), simulator reproductions of DART-DAG and LiP-LP, and internal variants of the proposed framework: "flat-BB" (removing the governed ledger) and "global-repair" (replanning over all non-frozen tasks instead of bounded repair). All methods operated under a shared baseline loop that performed coarse dependency, capability, energy, passability, duplicate, and lock checks to ensure a common minimum standard of execution.

#### 4.2. Primary Industrial Factory Validation

In the factory scenario (72 executable nodes), the framework demonstrated its primary strengths:
- **Static Graph**: Failed significantly, stalling due to "frontier starvation" because delayed grounding and structural repair mechanisms were absent.
- **Baseline Methods (DART-DAG, LiP-LP)**: While achieving high raw completion, these methods incurred substantial "invalid commitments" and "lock burdens." For example, DART-DAG exhibited 200.4 invalid commitments and 222.0 lock violations per 100 executable tasks.
- **Flat-BB Variant**: Showed issues with duplicate assignments and lock conflicts (5.0 duplicate and 5.4 lock violations per 100 tasks).
- **Global-Repair Variant**: Reduced some direct violations but introduced a high number of "disruption node events" (42.2 per 100 tasks) by frequently rewriting committed regions of work.
- **Proposed Framework ("Ours")**: Achieved 100% raw *and* safety-audited completion across five seeds, with *zero recorded invalid commitments, lock conflicts, duplicate assignments, or disruption events*. This pattern held consistently under increasing disturbance and resource contention in stress sweeps. This result highlighted the framework's ability to maintain high mission-state quality alongside operational completeness.

#### 4.3. Remote-Construction Stress Comparison

Across 30-seed Medium and Large remote-construction benchmarks:
- **Proposed Framework ("Ours")**: Maintained high raw completion (e.g., 0.921 in Medium, 0.898 in Large) and, critically, identical "safety-audited completion" with *zero invalid commitments, lock violations, duplicate assignments, abandoned executable nodes, and disruption node events*. This demonstrated robustness under significant stress.
- **Other Methods**: While some baselines achieved comparable *raw completion* rates in certain scenarios, they consistently generated substantial governance violations. For example, DART-DAG and LiP-LP in the Large scenario showed safety-audited completion rates around 0.25-0.27, significantly lower than raw completion rates (0.59-0.60), due to hundreds of invalid commitments and abandoned nodes (e.g., DART-DAG: 865.2 invalid commitments, 118.1 abandoned nodes per 100 tasks). The Flat-BB variant also accumulated violations, while the Global-Repair variant showed very few invalid commitments but significant disruption events (2.0 per 100 tasks in Large). The findings indicated that high raw completion alone is an insufficient metric; the framework's ability to maintain *audited mission-state consistency* was a differentiator.

#### 4.4. Ablation, Repair, and Runtime Diagnostics

Ablation studies on the Medium scenario further clarified the contribution of each framework component:
- **Static Graphs**: Confirmed frontier starvation, yielding minimal completion.
- **No Verifier**: Allowed proposals to inflate raw task counts but resulted in a high number of invalid commitments (26.7 ± 51.4), underscoring the necessity of verification.
- **No Structural Repair**: Led to a significant number of "abandoned affected work" nodes (42.5 ± 51.0), demonstrating the importance of structural repair.
- **Global Repair**: Increased "disruption events" (152.3 ± 135.7), supporting the bounded repair approach.
- **Proposed Framework**: Was the sole variant that combined high safety-audited progress with zero invalid commitments and zero disruption node events.

Runtime analysis revealed that the proposed framework is computationally heavier than simpler baselines. Verification was identified as the dominant internal cost, particularly as the executable frontier expanded and the number of candidate proposals increased (e.g., Large scenario: 149.78 seconds for verification out of 153.56 total seconds). However, bounded repair remained localized, contributing less to overall runtime compared to what global replanning might entail.

Overall, the results consistently supported the claim that a verification-gated mission-state governance layer significantly improves audited progress and reduces mission-state violations compared to methods lacking such a rigorous governance mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that holds significance for the development and deployment of intelligent multi-robot systems in industrial and safety-critical applications. Its potential impact lies in enhancing the reliability, safety, and robustness of autonomous operations, particularly in dynamic and uncertain environments.

#### 5.1. Enabling Safe Integration of Agentic AI

The primary significance is the provision of a practical methodology for safely integrating advanced agentic AI technologies (such as LLMs, complex optimizers, and heuristics) into multi-robot control architectures. By clearly separating the *generation of proposals* from their *verified commitment*, the framework mitigates the risks associated with unchecked autonomous decision-making. This addresses a critical concern in industrial settings where agentic modules might generate semantically plausible but physically unsafe or inconsistent actions. This approach allows industries to leverage the flexibility and reasoning capabilities of agentic AI while maintaining strict control over mission-critical state transitions.

#### 5.2. Improved Mission-State Quality and Audited Progress

The experimental results consistently demonstrate that the verification-gated approach leads to superior mission-state quality. By reducing or eliminating invalid commitments, resource lock conflicts, duplicate assignments, abandoned nodes, and disruptive repairs, the framework ensures that mission progress is not merely achieved but is *audited and verified* against defined hard constraints. This is crucial for applications where errors can have significant financial, operational, or safety consequences, providing a higher degree of assurance for long-horizon tasks.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Robustness and Localized Repair

The dual-object state representation (task forest and governed blackboard) combined with the derived Execution Coupling Topology (ECT) provides a robust foundation for managing mission complexity. The ability to localize disturbances and restrict repair operations to a hard-coupled affected neighborhood is a significant advancement. This "bounded repair" mechanism prevents cascading failures and preserves unaffected, already committed, or protected segments of the mission, contrasting favorably with global replanning approaches that can be disruptive and inefficient. This enhances the system's resilience to unforeseen events and partial failures.

#### 5.4. Inspectability and Trustworthiness

The deterministic verification process makes the framework's decision-making transparent and inspectable. Each proposed state transition must pass a set of explicit, modeled predicates, providing a clear audit trail. This inspectability is invaluable for regulatory compliance, post-incident analysis, and building trust in autonomous systems, particularly in highly regulated industrial sectors. By establishing clear rules for what constitutes a "valid" commitment, the framework fosters greater confidence in the system's operational integrity.

#### 5.5. Blueprint for Future Industrial Autonomy

The framework provides an architectural blueprint for future intelligent industrial systems, including smart factories, automated logistics, and remote construction. It shifts the paradigm from purely reactive or globally planned systems to a more governed, adaptive, and safe execution model for multi-robot teams. This paves the way for more complex, long-duration missions with higher degrees of autonomy.

#### 5.6. Acknowledged Limitations and Future Directions

While significant, the framework's guarantees are bounded by the represented mission-state predicates and the assumption of conservative projected states from lower-level systems. It does not inherently guarantee physical safety, global optimality, or task success under unmodeled low-level failures (e.g., actuator faults, localization drift, unmodeled physics). The reliance on conservative inputs from perception, motion planning, and control modules highlights areas for future research, including:
- Connecting the verifier to low-level motion and sensing certificates for more comprehensive safety guarantees.
- Investigating decentralized blackboard substrates for distributed control.
- Conducting evaluations with real industrial data and hardware-in-the-loop experiments to bridge the gap between simulation and physical deployment, addressing a broader range of real-world uncertainties and failures.

In conclusion, this research offers a foundational solution for governing the behavior of intelligent industrial multi-robot systems, enabling a safer and more reliable pathway for the deployment of agentic AI in complex, real-world environments.
