# NeuroMesh: A Unified Neural Inference Framework for Decentralized Multi-Robot Collaboration

> [!summary] Summary
> A unified framework for deploying learning-based multi-robot algorithms on heterogeneous platforms in real-world settings, NeuroMesh integrates decentralized neural inference for collaborative perception, control, and task assignment. It demonstrates efficacy with improved depth estimation accuracy, real-time control execution, and near-optimal task assignment performance, while effectively managing communication and computation in diverse network topologies.

## Problem

- Transitioning learning-based multi-robot algorithms from theoretical demonstrations to real-world applications faces challenges with hardware heterogeneity and communication constraints.
- Existing multi-robot collaboration frameworks are often specialized for specific tasks, platforms, or communication settings, lacking a unified deployment stack.
- There is a need for a cohesive execution stack that supports diverse compute resources and inference engines, while maintaining decentralization and flexibility for various task-dependent models.

## Method

- **NeuroMesh** was developed as a unified, ==multi-domain, cross-platform, and modular decentralized neural inference framework==, implemented in C++ for real-time efficiency.
- A ==dual-aggregation paradigm== was introduced to unify ==dense perception problems== and ==compact-message inference problems== within a single architectural formulation.
- A ==parallel three-stage pipeline== (encoder, message passing/aggregation, decoder) was implemented to enhance throughput by decoupling the system's output cycle time from total end-to-end latency.

## Results

- In collaborative depth estimation, NeuroMesh achieved an Absolute Relative Error of **5.29** and an Inlier Ratio of **61.32**, demonstrating accuracy comparable to centralized setups and an improvement over single-robot estimation.
- For multi-robot task assignment, the framework achieved near-optimal results with a **128 B** message size (**93.92%** success rate, **0.05%** higher total cost than expert), demonstrating scalability up to **10 robots** at **200 Hz** throughput.
- Computation studies showed TensorRT on GPU significantly outperformed ONNX on CPU for complex perception tasks (**2.9565 ms** vs. **14.8209 ms** total parallel time), while ONNX on CPU was faster for lightweight control MLPs due to GPU transfer overhead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A dual-aggregation paradigm can effectively unify collaborative perception (requiring relational information) and control/task assignment (using compact messages) within a single architectural design.
> - Parallelizing the neural inference pipeline across encoding, communication/aggregation, and decoding stages significantly improves system throughput in real-time multi-robot operations.
> - Zenoh, particularly in peer mode over mesh radios, provides superior performance for inter-robot communication in terms of latency, jitter, and packet loss, especially for high-bandwidth perception data, compared to other ROS 2 protocols.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has experienced a shift towards multi-robot systems, driven by the demand for enhanced resilience, robustness, efficiency, and performance in complex operational environments. Learning-based multi-robot collaboration methods, which enable robots to share neural representations and computational resources, have emerged as a significant area of research. These methods aim to achieve objectives that surpass the capabilities of individual robots, offering benefits such as improved robustness to single-point failures, increased efficiency through task parallelization, and enhanced performance via collective intelligence.

Applications of learning-based multi-robot collaboration are diverse, encompassing collaborative perception (e.g., sharing sensor information for improved scene understanding), cooperative control (e.g., coordinating actions for complex maneuvers), and planning and task assignment (e.g., decentralized coordination for resource allocation and mission execution). While these applications demonstrate promise in simulations and datasets, their transition to real-world deployment on physical robot teams presents challenges.

Existing frameworks for multi-robot collaboration typically address specific aspects of deployment, often focusing on a single task family, platform, or communication setting. For instance, ModGNN emphasizes architectural modularity for multi-agent control but lacks a complete deployment stack for inter-robot communication, hardware-accelerated inference, or ROS 2 microservices for heterogeneous robots in real-time. Similarly, a ROS 2 framework for decentralized control, while demonstrating real-robot capabilities, is Python-based and specialized for control tasks and platform-specific payloads, with a computation/communication stack not optimized for perception-heavy or mixed-domain workloads. This creates a gap between algorithmic development and the availability of reusable infrastructure for deploying learning-based multi-robot algorithms on heterogeneous teams in real-world conditions. NeuroMesh aims to address this deployment gap by providing a unified, multi-domain, cross-platform, and modular decentralized neural inference framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust and flexible framework that facilitates the deployment of learned multi-robot models onto heterogeneous robotic platforms in real-world scenarios. The motivation stems from several challenges encountered when attempting to transition learning-based multi-robot algorithms from theoretical demonstrations to practical applications:

- **Hardware Heterogeneity:** Robotic teams often consist of diverse platforms with varying computational capabilities (e.g., GPUs, CPUs) and hardware specifications. Existing solutions frequently lack the flexibility to accommodate such heterogeneity.
- **Communication Constraints:** Real-world multi-robot systems operate under significant communication limitations, including bandwidth restrictions, latency, jitter, and potential message drops, particularly in decentralized network topologies. A unified execution stack must robustly handle these constraints.
- **Lack of a Unified Execution Stack:** Integrating perception, control, and decision-making into a single, cohesive execution stack that supports diverse compute resources and inference engines, while maintaining decentralization and flexibility for task-dependent models, remains an unresolved problem.

To address these challenges, NeuroMesh was designed with several key principles and contributions:

- **Generality (Multi-domain):** The framework aims for compatibility across multiple task domains (perception, control, task assignment). This is achieved through a dual-aggregation paradigm that unifies dense perception and compact-message decentralized neural inference problems within a single formulation, eliminating the need for architectural redesign for different task types.
- **Cross-Platform:** NeuroMesh is designed to be effective across heterogeneous robotic platforms. Its software modules are capable of instantiating inference tasks on either CPU or GPU, adapting to specific algorithmic requirements and hardware limitations.
- **Modularity:** The framework employs a plug-and-play microservice architecture. This allows task-dependent components (encoders, aggregators, decoders) to be easily interchanged, ranging from simple identity mappings to complex deep networks, all while utilizing the same parallel execution pipeline.
- **Efficiency:** A high-performance C++ implementation is provided, optimized for real-time operation. This includes an efficient network protocol implementation and an accelerated network inference engine, crucial for managing computational latency. The framework also introduces a parallelized architecture that decouples the system's output cycle time from the total end-to-end latency, improving throughput.

Ultimately, the motivation is to bridge the gap between algorithmic research in learning-based multi-robot collaboration and its practical, real-world deployment by providing a standardized, flexible, and efficient infrastructure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NeuroMesh conceptualizes the decentralized multi-robot neural inference problem as a four-stage process: observation encoding, message passing, aggregation, and task output decoding. This formulation is designed to be flexible, allowing for task-dependent modules for each stage.

- **Problem Formulation:**
 - **Observation Encoding:** Each robot *i* transforms its local observations (*xᵢ*, e.g., image, point cloud, poses) into a feature tensor (*fᵢ*).
 - **Message Passing:** Robots exchange these encoded feature tensors with neighboring robots within their communication range. *N(i)* denotes the set of neighbors for robot *i*.
 - **Aggregation:** Each robot aggregates the received information from its neighbors with its own feature tensor (*hᵢ⁽ˡ⁾*) to produce an updated feature tensor (*hᵢ⁽ˡ⁺¹⁾*). This stage can be executed for *L* communication rounds.
 - **Task Output Decoding:** Finally, each robot decodes its aggregated feature tensor (*hᵢ⁽ᴸ⁾*) to generate the task-specific output (*yᵢ*, e.g., depth maps, semantic segmentation, control commands).
 The encoder/decoder components can range from deep vision stacks for perception to lightweight learned mappings for control or task assignment.

- **Dual-Aggregation Paradigm:** A central methodological contribution is the dual-aggregation formulation, which unifies various collaboration domains.
 - **Reduction Paradigm:** This approach, typical in consensus-like control tasks, reduces the self-feature (*fᵢ*) and neighbor features (*{fⱼ: j ∈ N(i)}*) into a single aggregated feature (*hᵢ*) (e.g., by averaging or max-pooling). This method may discard pairwise relational information.
 - **Broadcast Paradigm:** Introduced to support dense perception tasks that require preserving relational information. For *M* neighbors, the self-feature (*fᵢ*) is replicated and concatenated with each neighbor feature (*fⱼ*). A network then processes these pairs in parallel to produce an aggregated tensor (*hᵢ*) with *M* channels, thereby maintaining pairwise relational data.
 The framework can adapt to intermittent communication or message drops by pausing operations, transitioning to partial-neighborhood processing, or falling back to single-robot inference, depending on the task's tolerance for incomplete information.

- **Parallel Three-Stage Pipeline:** To mitigate the accumulation of computational latency in real-world deployments, NeuroMesh employs a parallelized pipeline architecture. This pipeline consists of three concurrently running components: the encoder module, the message passing and aggregation module, and the decoder module.
 - The system's output cycle time (*Tₚᵢₚₑₗᵢₙₑ*) is determined by the duration of the slowest stage: *Tₚᵢₚₑₗᵢₙₑ = max(Tₑ, Tₘ, Td)*, where *Tₑ, Tₘ, Td* are computation times for encoder, message passing, and decoder, respectively.
 - The total end-to-end latency (*T𝚍𝚎𝚕𝚊𝚢*) is the sum of all stages: *T𝚍𝚎𝚕𝚊𝚢 = Tₑ + Tₘ + Td*. This parallel design improves throughput by decoupling the processing rate from the sum of individual stage delays.

- **Software Implementation:**
 - NeuroMesh is implemented in C++ to minimize serialization overhead and maximize inference throughput.
 - It utilizes ROS 2 as the underlying communication middleware for intra-robot communication and as a microservice-based architecture, where each pipeline stage is a ROS 2 node.
 - For neural network inference, the framework supports NVIDIA's TensorRT for GPU-accelerated platforms and ONNX Runtime for non-NVIDIA or CPU-based inference, providing cross-platform compatibility.
 - The framework is model-agnostic; users can integrate task-specific neural networks as plug-and-play modules.

- **Communication Protocol:**
 - The framework is designed to be agnostic to centralized or decentralized network topologies.
 - For inter-robot communication, NeuroMesh integrates Zenoh, a protocol that combines publish/subscribe primitives with geo-distributed storage and querying. Zenoh can operate in peer mode (direct robot-to-robot exchange with link-state routing) or router mode (centralized forwarding).
 - Each inter-robot message includes a timestamp and sequence index, enabling robots to maintain keep-latest buffers, discard out-of-order packets, and remove old messages. Aggregation can be configured as blocking (waiting for all messages) or best-effort (using available messages).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

NeuroMesh's performance was evaluated through case studies in collaborative perception, control, and task assignment, and detailed communication and computation ablation studies.

**Case Studies:**
- **Multi-Robot Collaborative Perception:**
 - **Setup:** Two aerial robots and one ground robot, equipped with NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX (aerial) and Mini-ITX with Intel i7 CPU/NVIDIA 1050 Ti GPU (ground), using Doodlelabs mesh radios.
 - **Collaborative Depth Estimation:** A decentralized DUSt3R model was deployed. Qualitative results (Fig. 6a vs. 6b) indicated that collaboration reduced uncertainty and improved depth accuracy compared to single-robot monocular estimation. Quantitatively, both centralized and NeuroMesh-enabled decentralized setups achieved an Absolute Relative Error of 5.29 and an Inlier Ratio of 61.32, suggesting performance equivalence for depth accuracy.
 - **Collaborative Semantic Segmentation:** A GNN-based method was used. Qualitative results (Fig. 6c) showed that collaboration enhanced object recognition (e.g., chairs) from different perspectives.
- **Multi-Robot Collaborative Control:**
 - **Setup:** Three ground robots with non-holonomic dynamic constraints.
 - **Task:** RL-based point-to-point navigation with collision avoidance. A GNN-based policy from, adapted for a unicycle motion model, was trained.
 - **Inputs:** Compact state features (pose, heading, goal) from onboard localization, encoded by a 4-layer MLP. Aggregation processed neighbor differences.
 - **Results:** While simulation achieved an 80% success rate, real-world deployment resulted in a 60% success rate, attributed to sim-to-real transfer challenges, particularly in angular velocity control. The framework enabled real-time distributed execution of these policies (Fig. 7).
- **Multi-Robot Collaborative Task Assignment:**
 - **Setup:** Five robots performing goal assignment to minimize global cost, avoiding conflicts.
 - **Task:** Supervised learning with a GNN, trained using the Hungarian algorithm as an expert, to predict assignment labels. Input was a compact cost vector for possible assignments.
 - **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) and Total Cost Performance (TCP) relative to optimal.
 - **Results (Table I):** The framework's scalability concerning communication message size was evaluated. Larger message sizes generally improved the success rate (e.g., 40 B: 87.12% SR; 128 B: 93.92% SR). A message size of 128 B was found sufficient for near-optimal results, with a TCP of at most 0.05% higher than the expert. Increasing the message size to 256 B yielded no further performance gain.

**Performance Analysis:**
- **Communication Ablation Study (Table II, Table III, Fig. 8):**
 - **Decentralized Mesh (Doodlelabs radios):** For 128 B control payloads, Zenoh peer mode exhibited the lowest latency (4.8 ms), jitter (0.6 ms), and packet loss (0.3%), achieving a stable 200 Hz throughput, outperforming Fast RTPS, CycloneDDS, and Zenoh router mode which showed fluctuating throughput. For the high-bandwidth 3.15 MB perception payload, only Zenoh peer mode succeeded, albeit at a lower rate (approx. 0.5 Hz) with higher latency (2712.2 ms) and jitter (540.5 ms), demonstrating its robustness for large tensor transfers over mesh networks where other protocols failed.
 - **Centralized Wi-Fi:** All four protocols achieved similar throughputs for control payloads, with Zenoh peer mode showing the highest stability. However, the 3.15 MB perception payload could not be sustained due to bandwidth saturation at the central node. Zenoh router mode over centralized Wi-Fi was identified as a viable option for control-class workloads without mesh radios.
 - **Emulated Scalability (Table III):** Zenoh peer mode, under a simulated 6 MB/s per-node bandwidth limit, maintained full 200 Hz throughput for 128 B control messages up to 10 robots. At 50 robots, the per-link rate remained above 95 msgs/s, indicating reasonable scalability.
- **Computation Ablation Study (Table IV):**
 - **Inference Engine Comparison:** For perception tasks involving resource-intensive transformer architectures, TensorRT (GPU) significantly outperformed ONNX (CPU) (e.g., total parallel 2.9565 ms vs. 14.8209 ms). Conversely, control experiments using small MLPs were faster on CPU with ONNX (total parallel 18.1 ms vs. 23.4 ms for TRT GPU) due to CPU-GPU data transfer overhead. For task assignment, TensorRT on GPU was more efficient for the attention mechanism in aggregation, making the overall parallel execution faster on GPU (0.2939 ms vs. 0.3851 ms for ONNX CPU).
 - **Parallel Pipeline Validation:** The parallel pipeline consistently demonstrated lower total computation time (output cycle time) compared to sequential computation for all tasks, confirming its benefit in improving system throughput for real-time applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

NeuroMesh presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-robot systems by providing a unified, multi-domain, cross-platform, and modular framework for decentralized neural inference. Its ability to operate across diverse tasks—perception, control, and decision-making—and on heterogeneous robotic platforms addresses key challenges in real-world deployment of learning-based multi-robot algorithms.

The framework's dual-aggregation paradigm is notable for its capacity to handle both dense perception tasks requiring relational information and compact-message control tasks within a single architectural design. The parallelized execution pipeline optimizes throughput for real-time applications by decoupling cycle time from total latency. The detailed communication and computation ablation studies provide empirical evidence supporting the robust performance of Zenoh as an inter-robot communication protocol in decentralized mesh networks and offer guidance on selecting appropriate inference engines (GPU vs. CPU) based on task complexity.

Beyond learning-based applications, NeuroMesh's modular design allows it to accommodate classical coordination algorithms, such as consensus or flocking, by reusing its encoding, aggregation, and decoding modules. This broad applicability positions NeuroMesh as a versatile platform for deploying a wide range of analytical and learning-based algorithms in collaborative robotics. The planned open-source release is expected to facilitate community contributions and accelerate future advancements in multi-robot research, potentially enabling the deployment of larger physical teams, operations in more diverse RF environments, and integration of heterogeneous sensing modalities.
