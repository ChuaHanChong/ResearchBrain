# Differentiable MPC for End-to-end Planning and Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Amos et al. present an efficient method for differentiating through Model Predictive Control (MPC), allowing its parameters to be learned end-to-end from task-specific losses in continuous control settings. The framework achieves greater data efficiency for structured policies in imitation learning compared to neural networks and outperforms traditional system identification when expert dynamics are not perfectly modeled.

## Problem

- Traditional Model Predictive Control (MPC) lacks differentiability, preventing its parameters (cost functions, dynamics models) from being learned end-to-end from task-specific losses.
- Existing methods for differentiating through optimal control algorithms often rely on computationally and memory-intensive "unrolling" of optimization steps.
- Learning accurate system dynamics via traditional system identification (SysId) does not always translate to optimal performance for complex, task-specific objectives, especially when expert dynamics are not perfectly captured by the model.

## Method

- The method differentiates through ==Model Predictive Control== (**MPC**) by applying implicit differentiation to the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions of the convex approximation at the solver's fixed point.
- It leverages the property that the backward pass for gradients can be computed by solving a modified LQR problem, reusing forward pass factorizations for efficiency.
- The framework extends to MPC with box constraints, handling active inequality constraints by effectively solving a zero-constrained quadratic program in the backward pass.

## Results

- The fixed-point differentiation (FP) method achieved substantially higher computational efficiency in the backward pass compared to explicit unrolling, especially when many optimization iterations are required.
- Differentiable MPC policies demonstrated superior data efficiency and consistently outperformed generic neural network policies (LSTMs) in continuous control imitation learning tasks.
- When expert dynamics were outside the learner's model class, directly optimizing the imitation loss via differentiable MPC yielded better task performance than traditional system identification, which focused solely on dynamics accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit differentiation through the KKT conditions of the final convex approximation at an MPC solver's fixed point enables efficient and accurate gradient computation.
> - The backward pass computation for differentiable MPC can be formulated as solving a modified LQR problem, reusing forward pass computations for significant efficiency gains.
> - Optimizing dynamics and cost parameters directly against a task-specific loss can yield superior policy performance compared to optimizing for dynamics accuracy alone, especially when the expert model is complex or non-realizable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) broadly encompasses two main paradigms: model-free and model-based approaches. Model-free methods, particularly those leveraging deep neural networks, have demonstrated successes in complex domains by directly learning policies that map observations to actions. However, these methods often exhibit high sample complexity, poor generalization, and result in "black-box" policies that lack interpretability.

In contrast, model-based methods aim to learn a model of the environment and then use this model for planning and control. Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a prominent model-based technique that solves an optimization problem online over a receding horizon. Model-based approaches are typically more sample-efficient and offer greater interpretability due to explicit dynamics models. However, they often require manually specified or tuned cost functions, and the learned models may not always accurately capture task-relevant aspects of the state space. Furthermore, traditional MPC policies are generally not differentiable, which hinders their integration into end-to-end learning systems prevalent in deep reinforcement learning.

Recent research has explored combining the advantages of both paradigms. One direction involves using model-based priors to accelerate model-free learning, though often keeping the model separate from the learned policy. Another direction, more relevant to this work, integrates planning modules directly into deep network architectures, allowing for end-to-end learning. This includes methods that unroll simple optimization procedures or approximate value iteration within neural networks, primarily in discrete state and action spaces. For continuous control, approaches have emerged that learn goal-conditioned value functions or abstract environmental models for planning, some interpretable as "distilled optimal controllers."

This paper specifically contributes to the area of *differentiable optimal control* and *differentiable planning*. Previous works in this domain have attempted to differentiate through optimal control algorithms by explicitly "unrolling" the optimization process. While conceptually sound, this unrolling approach can be computationally and memory-intensives, with the complexity of the backward pass scaling linearly with the number of optimization iterations. The current work addresses this limitation by proposing a more efficient method for differentiating through MPC, specifically for continuous state and action spaces, thereby enabling its seamless integration into end-to-end learning pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a foundational framework for utilizing Model Predictive Control (MPC) as a differentiable policy class within the context of reinforcement learning, particularly for systems with continuous state and action spaces. This framework aims to facilitate end-to-end learning, where the parameters of the MPC policy (such as the cost function and dynamics model) can be learned directly from a task-specific loss.

The motivations behind this objective are multifaceted:

1. **Bridging Model-Free and Model-Based Advantages**: The authors seek to combine the strengths of both model-free and model-based approaches. Model-free methods offer the ability to optimize directly for a task loss without explicit knowledge of the system, but often suffer from data inefficiency and lack of interpretability. Model-based methods, like MPC, are known for their sample efficiency, robust control, and interpretability through explicit system models, but traditionally lack a mechanism for end-to-end optimization of their internal parameters based on a task loss. By making MPC differentiable, the authors aim to leverage the benefits of both.
2. **Enabling End-to-End Learning for MPC**: Conventional MPC implementations typically involve designing the cost function and dynamics model separately, often requiring domain expertise and manual tuning. The ability to differentiate through the MPC optimization allows the controller's internal parameters (cost and dynamics) to be learned directly from a task objective, such as an imitation loss. This allows the controller to "specialize" its parameters for a given task, potentially leading to improved performance compared to components learned in isolation.
3. **Improving Computational Efficiency of Differentiable Planning**: Prior methods for differentiating through complex optimization procedures, including some for iLQR, often rely on explicitly unrolling the iterative optimization algorithm. This results in a backward pass whose computational and memory cost scales linearly with the number of iterations in the forward pass. This can be prohibitive for algorithms like MPC that may require many iterations to converge. A key motivation for this paper is to develop a more efficient differentiation strategy that avoids this linear scaling, specifically by leveraging the fixed-point properties of the MPC solver.
4. **Addressing Limitations of Traditional System Identification**: In many practical scenarios, directly identifying system dynamics (system identification or SysId) is performed independently of the ultimate control task. The authors suggest that SysId often serves as a surrogate for a more sophisticated task, like imitation or policy learning. They are motivated to demonstrate that by directly optimizing the task's loss function with respect to the dynamics, better task performance can be achieved, especially when the true expert dynamics are outside the model class being learned.

In essence, the work aims to transform MPC from a standalone control algorithm into a learnable, differentiable policy class that can be seamlessly integrated into modern deep reinforcement learning architectures, unlocking new possibilities for intelligent control system design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology presented in this paper involves differentiating through Model Predictive Control (MPC) by leveraging the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions of the convex approximation at a fixed point of the iterative controller. This approach provides an efficient analytical method for computing gradients, in contrast to explicitly unrolling the optimization procedure.

#### 3.1. Differentiable Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)

The paper first establishes the groundwork by explaining how to differentiate through a discrete-time, finite-horizon Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem. LQR minimizes a convex quadratic objective subject to affine state-transition dynamics.
The LQR problem is formulated as:
$argmin_{\tau_{1:T}} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{1}{2} \tau_t^T C_t \tau_t + c_t^T \tau_t$
subject to $x_1 = x_{init}$, $x_{t+1} = F_t \tau_t + f_t$, where $\tau_t = [x_t, u_t]^T$.

- **KKT Conditions**: The optimal trajectory $\tau^*$ and dual variables $\lambda^*$ satisfy the KKT conditions. The authors highlight that the LQR solution procedure, typically based on dynamic Riccati recursion, implicitly solves a large linear system derived from these KKT conditions.
- **Gradient Computation**: To compute the derivatives of a loss function $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to the LQR parameters $\theta = \{C, c, F, f, x_{init}\}$, the authors apply the implicit differentiation technique previously introduced by Amos and Kolter (2017). This method involves solving a linear system for the derivatives of the optimal trajectory $d\tau^*$ and dual variables $d\lambda^*$.
- **Efficiency**: The critical insight is that this linear system for the derivatives ($d\tau^*, d\lambda^*$) has the *same structure* as the KKT system for the original LQR problem. This allows the backward pass to be computed efficiently by solving *another LQR problem*, where the cost and dynamics terms are modified based on the gradients of the loss function. Importantly, this backward LQR step can reuse the factorizations computed during the forward pass, leading to significant computational savings.

#### 3.2. Differentiable Model Predictive Control (MPC)

Building upon the differentiable LQR, the methodology is extended to handle general MPC problems, which typically involve non-linear costs, non-linear dynamics, and box constraints on control actions. The general MPC problem is defined as:
$argmin_{\tau_{1:T}} \sum_t C_{\theta,t}(\tau_t)$
subject to $x_1 = x_{init}$, $x_{t+1} = f_{\theta}(\tau_t)$, $u \le u \le u$.

- **Iterative Convex Approximation**: Since the general MPC problem is non-convex, it is typically solved iteratively by forming and optimizing a convex (quadratic) approximation around a current nominal trajectory $\tau^i$. This involves second-order Taylor approximations for the cost and first-order Taylor approximations for the dynamics.
- **Fixed-Point Differentiation**: The crucial assumption is that the iterative solver converges to a *fixed point*. At this fixed point, the final convex approximation (quadratic cost, affine dynamics) accurately represents the local optimum of the non-convex problem. Therefore, differentiating the non-convex MPC problem can be achieved by differentiating through this final convex approximation.
- **Differentiating Box-Constrained Quadratic Programs (QPs)**: The paper extends the KKT-based differentiation to include box constraints on control inputs. At the optimum of a box-constrained QP, the active inequality constraints (where control inputs hit their bounds) become equality constraints. The KKT system for such a problem can then be implicitly differentiated. The resulting system for the derivatives ($dx^*, d\lambda^*$) is equivalent to solving a *zero-constrained QP*, where the gradients of control variables that were clamped at their bounds are forced to be zero.
- **MPC with Box Constraints**: By combining these concepts, the derivatives for the MPC problem at a fixed point are obtained by solving a modified LQR problem (a "zero-constrained LQR"). This backward problem uses the final quadratic cost (Hessian) and linear dynamics (Jacobian) approximations from the forward pass, with the additional condition that the gradients of control actions that hit their bounds are set to zero. The box-DDP heuristic (Tassa et al., 2014) is used for the forward MPC pass, and a customized LQR solver handles the zero-constrained LQR in the backward pass.

#### 3.3. Drawbacks

The primary limitation of this approach is its reliance on the assumption that the iterative MPC solver reaches a fixed point. If the solver does not converge to a fixed point, particularly when using neural networks to approximate dynamics (which can introduce non-smoothness or non-stationarity), the gradients computed by this method might not be accurate. In such scenarios, explicitly unrolling the optimization might provide surrogate gradients, though at a higher computational cost.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental section of the paper presents several results demonstrating the performance and capabilities of the differentiable MPC framework, comparing it against neural network policies and traditional system identification (SysId) in various imitation learning settings.

#### 4.1. MPC Solver Performance

- **Efficiency Comparison**: The authors compared their fixed-point differentiation (FP) approach to an alternative method that explicitly unrolls each box-constrained iLQR iteration for gradient computation (Unroll).
- **Runtime Results**: Figure 2 illustrates that the FP method is marginally more computationally efficient in the forward pass because it avoids creating and maintaining backward pass variables. Critically, it is *significantly more efficient* in the backward pass, especially when a large number of iLQR iterations are required for convergence. The backward pass in the FP approach is described as "essentially free" because it reuses factorizations from the forward pass and does not require multiple iterations itself. The Unroll method’s backward pass scales linearly with the number of iLQR steps in the forward pass, incurring a substantial overhead. The FP method also demonstrates improved memory efficiency in the forward pass for similar reasons.

#### 4.2. Imitation Learning: Linear-Dynamics Quadratic-Cost (LQR)

- **Setup**: An imitation learning task was designed where both the expert and learner are LQR controllers. They share a quadratic cost function, control bounds, and horizon, but the learner's linear system dynamics ($A, B$) are initialized randomly and trained to mimic the expert. The imitation loss is defined as the mean squared error (MSE) between the expert's and learner's nominal action sequences.
- **Results**: Figure 3 shows the training dynamics of the model loss (MSE between true and learned dynamics parameters) and the imitation loss. In approximately half of the trials, the learner's dynamics converged to the true expert parameters, resulting in a perfect imitation loss. However, in other trials, the optimization got stuck in local minima, causing the learned model to diverge from the true model, even as the imitation loss decreased to a locally optimal value. This finding indicates that optimizing the imitation loss with respect to controller parameters is a non-convex problem with potential for local minima.

#### 4.3. Imitation Learning: Non-Convex Continuous Control (Pendulum and Cartpole)

- **Setup**: The method was evaluated on the standard pendulum and cartpole environments, using MPC experts and learners. The goal was to optimize the imitation loss based solely on observed control actions, without state information. Several methods were compared:
 - `nn`: An LSTM neural network policy trained to directly predict action sequences.
 - `sysid`: Assumes known cost and approximates dynamics parameters by optimizing next-state transitions (traditional system identification).
 - `mpc.dx`: Our method, assuming known cost, approximates dynamics parameters by directly optimizing the imitation loss.
 - `mpc.cost`: Our method, assuming known dynamics, approximates cost function parameters by directly optimizing the imitation loss.
 - `mpc.cost.dx`: Our method, approximates both cost and dynamics parameters by directly optimizing the imitation loss.
- **Results**: Figure 4 summarizes the imitation loss across different training data sizes (10, 50, 100 trajectories).
 - **Superiority over Neural Networks**: Differentiable MPC methods (`mpc.dx`, `mpc.cost`, `mpc.cost.dx`) consistently and significantly outperformed the generic `nn` (LSTM policy) across both environments and all data sizes. This suggests that the inherent structure of MPC policies allows them to learn with lower sample complexity.
 - **Cost and Dynamics Recovery**: In many instances, the differentiable MPC approach was able to recover the true cost function and dynamics of the expert.
 - **Comparison to SysId**: `mpc.dx` achieved performance comparable to `sysid` (which uses state transition information for dynamics learning) while using only control information for its loss. This highlights the ability of the proposed method to learn dynamics parameters effectively from an action-based task loss.

#### 4.4. Imitation Learning: SysId with a Non-Realizable Expert

- **Setup**: This experiment explored a scenario where the expert's dynamics fall outside the model class being learned by the learner. An expert pendulum environment included additional damping and a "wind" force, while the learner's dynamics model was a standard pendulum without these terms. The comparison was between vanilla `SysId` (optimizing next-state transitions) and `mpc.dx` (directly optimizing the imitation loss).
- **Results**: Figure 5 indicates that while vanilla `SysId` achieved a slightly lower "SysID Loss" (better fit to next-state transitions), it resulted in a higher "Imitation Loss" compared to `mpc.dx`. This implies that `SysId`, by focusing solely on state prediction, can find an inferior model for the actual imitation task when the expert's dynamics are complex or outside the learner's model class. The `mpc.dx` method, by directly optimizing the imitation loss, leveraged the task-specific loss signal to guide dynamics learning, leading to a better policy for imitation despite learning an inherently imperfect dynamics model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper introduces a significant methodological advance for integrating Model Predictive Control (MPC) into end-to-end learning frameworks, particularly for continuous control tasks.

**Foundational Contribution and Efficiency**: The primary significance lies in establishing a general and efficient framework for differentiating through MPC. By leveraging the KKT conditions at the fixed point of the iterative optimization, the authors have developed a method where the backward pass for gradient computation requires only a single, modified LQR solve. This is a considerable improvement over previous "unrolling" approaches, which scale linearly with the number of optimization iterations and incur higher computational and memory costs. This efficiency makes it practical to embed powerful MPC policies as differentiable modules within larger deep learning architectures.

**Bridging Model-Based and Model-Free RL**: The differentiable MPC acts as a bridge between the model-based and model-free reinforcement learning paradigms. It offers the sample efficiency, interpretability, and robust control characteristics of MPC while enabling the end-to-end learning capabilities typically associated with model-free deep RL. This integration allows for learning explicit cost functions and dynamics models directly from task-specific objectives, which can lead to policies that are both effective and understandable.

**Improved Learning for Structured Policies**: The experimental results demonstrate that differentiable MPC policies achieve significantly higher data efficiency compared to generic neural network policies in imitation learning scenarios. This is attributed to the inherent structure of MPC, which provides a well-defined notion of dynamics and cost, enabling it to learn effectively with less data.

**Advantage over Traditional System Identification**: The research highlights a crucial advantage over traditional system identification (SysId). While SysId focuses on accurately modeling system dynamics, the differentiable MPC approach can directly optimize the dynamics and/or cost parameters for the ultimate task at hand (e.g., imitation). The experiment with a non-realizable expert demonstrates that directly optimizing the task loss can yield superior task performance, even if the learned dynamics model is not the most accurate representation of the environment. This suggests that the task loss provides valuable guidance for learning task-relevant model parameters, moving SysId from an isolated step to an integral part of an end-to-end learning pipeline.

**Potential Impact and Future Directions**:
The ability to efficiently integrate MPC into deep neural networks has broad potential impacts:

- **Adaptive Control**: Facilitating the learning of adaptive controllers whose dynamics and cost parameters can be tuned in real-time based on task performance.
- **Complex Robotics and Autonomous Systems**: Enabling the deployment of robust and learnable control policies in systems requiring explicit planning and safety guarantees, such as autonomous vehicles or manipulators, where interpretability and sample efficiency are paramount.
- **Joint Model-Based and Policy-Based Learning**: Opening avenues for hybrid learning approaches that combine the strengths of both explicit planning and direct policy optimization.
- **Stochastic Control**: The authors note the potential for extension to stochastic control, which would further broaden the applicability of this framework to uncertain environments.

Overall, this work provides a robust foundation for incorporating powerful, interpretable, and data-efficient control policies into modern machine learning systems, paving the way for more sophisticated and generalized intelligent agents.
