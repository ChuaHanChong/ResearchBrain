# When Instructions Retrieve Trajectories: Diagnosing and Mitigating Generalization Failures in VLA Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Diagnoses instruction-action binding in VLAs: instructions retrieve familiar demonstrated trajectory families while vision only adjusts execution, so policies fail counterfactual changes needing new actions. Behavioral probes and internal-state interventions on π0.5 and GR00T-N1.7 confirm it. Equivariant Counterfactual Training pairs same-instruction demonstrations in distinguishable scenes requiring different actions, trained in one update; it lifts LIBERO-PRO swap success 36% to 59% and real UR5e unseen-position success 8% to 88%.

## Problem

- VLA robustness benchmarks aggregate nuisance and counterfactual perturbations, so high scores hide failures where a changed instruction or scene demands a genuinely different action.
- Task-specific demonstrations give each instruction a narrow trajectory family, so an instruction-keyed lookup fits the imitation loss as well as grounded, scene-dependent action selection does.
- Prior diagnoses blame ignored language or vision shortcuts, and inference-time fixes like counterfactual action guidance leave the missing scene-dependent supervision in the training data unaddressed.

## Method

- ==Instruction-action binding framework==: separates instruction-key change from required-action change, sorting LIBERO-PRO perturbations into nuisance (Semantic, Object) and counterfactual (Swap, Task) cells, tested with behavioral probes and ==KV-cache interventions==.
- **ECT data**: action-valid counterparts built by mirroring or shifting scenes, transforming end-effector paths, and replaying them in simulation, keeping only successful demonstrations that stay visually distinguishable.
- **ECT loss**: samples matched counterpart pairs and sums both standard ==flow-matching== imitation losses in the same update, sharing noise and flow time, with no auxiliary objective added.

## Results

- Frozen-LM π0.5 on LIBERO-PRO: ECT raises mean Swap success from **36% to 59%** and Task from **21% to 28%**; ECT data drives most gains, ECT loss adds **2-5 points**.
- CALVIN ABC→D: the ECT loss alone, applied to naturally occurring counterparts, raises five-task completion from **57.6% to 75.6%** and average length from **3.63 to 4.28** without new demonstrations.
- Real UR5e with a fixed 400-demonstration budget: unseen-position success rises from **3/40 to 35/40**; across 381 probes, retrieval-like failures drop from **69% to 47%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Report nuisance and counterfactual robustness separately; a policy that survives paraphrases and appearance changes may still replay familiar trajectories when the required action changes.
> - More demonstration repetitions cannot fix this shortcut; supervision must contain same-instruction alternatives whose required actions differ, otherwise instruction-keyed lookup stays an optimal solution.
> - Counterfactual pairs must be visually distinguishable: front-back mirrors changed actions without changing image cues and collapsed in-distribution success from 94% to 24%.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot can make a smooth, visually competent movement and still do the wrong job. When asked to put a plate on a cabinet rather than a bowl, a fine-tuned policy may complete the familiar bowl placement. Success on paraphrases or changed appearances does not tell a user whether a robot will choose a different action when the instruction or scene requires one. The authors' remedy changes the training examples. They add valid examples where the same instruction calls for different actions in distinguishable scenes, then train each example with its counterpart in the same update. In a controlled comparison, mean Swap success rises from 36% to 59%.

### 2. A fluent failure

The distinction is whether a change in the input also changes the required action. Nuisance changes, such as paraphrase or appearance changes, can leave the required action unchanged. Counterfactual changes, such as position swaps or a new requested goal, require a different action.

The authors call the resulting gap **instruction-action binding**. In this pattern, instructions can cue a familiar trajectory family while visual feedback adjusts its execution, without the policy combining instruction and scene to select the action the task requires. This is the authors' diagnosis of a behavioral pattern, not proof of one unique retrieval algorithm.

Human annotators, rather than the simulator, judged the evidence. Across 381 targeted π₀.₅ rollouts, 69.3% were categorized as retrieval-like: source-task, other-task, or hybrid familiar behavior. Another 26.5% were correct or near-correct following, and 4.2% were collapse without a coherent trajectory. "Retrieval-like" labels resemblance to demonstrated behavior. It does not show that a model literally retrieves a stored trajectory.

One probe illustrates the pattern. In all nine combined Object probes, an object was moved 10 cm. The policy tracked the object that occupied the source-bound slot, rather than the object named in the unchanged instruction. This shows retained tracking in that probe. It does not show that vision is the only remaining cause of the failure.

### 3. Teach the scene to choose

In the studied training data, an instruction often comes with a narrow family of trajectories. More repetitions can increase the example count without adding an action alternative under the same instruction. ECT aims to supply that missing distinction, rather than more samples. It has two parts.

**ECT data** create a counterpart with the same instruction, a changed scene, and an action that is valid in that scene. In LIBERO, the authors transform the scene and the corresponding end-effector path. They replay the path with a controller, record the resulting action, and retain successful replays. They do not assume that transforming raw action commands alone produces a valid demonstration.

**ECT loss** trains the original and its counterpart with the standard per-example imitation loss in the same parameter update. It adds no auxiliary objective.

The changed scene must make the appropriate action distinguishable. In a diagnostic Object construction, front–back mirroring changed the required action but left dominant landmarks in similar image regions. ID success fell from 94% to 24%. Left–right mirroring or adding a shift separated the relevant landmarks.

### 4. What the tests establish

The headline comparison uses fine-tuned π₀.₅ on LIBERO-PRO. Swap means exchanging positions so the same instruction now requires a different action. Success is whether the simulator's task predicate is true at episode termination. The mean averages the four LIBERO-PRO suites, with 500 trials per suite and cell.

Under a matched Frozen-LM recipe, the language model stays fixed while the vision encoder and action expert are trained. Steps, batch size, and trainable parameters match, and results average three seeds. With this recipe, full ECT raises mean Swap success from 36% to 59%, and the ECT data explain most of the gain. Task results are more mixed. On Spatial Task, the matched comparison is 22.3% versus 22.1%.

The second comparison tests the paired loss itself. A same-pool ablation compares independent sampling against training matched counterparts together. All rows use 64 examples per update, matched steps and batch size, and three seeds. Relative to ECT data alone, the ECT loss changes Swap success by +2.3 points on Spatial, −0.3 on Object, +3.7 on Goal, and +5.2 on Long. This supports a selective additional benefit from paired training, not a universal improvement. It also does not isolate a single pairing mechanism. The sampler changes counterpart co-presentation and the original/counterpart balance, and π₀.₅ pairs share flow-matching noise and time.

### 5. The boundary

A familiar, fluent movement is not evidence that a robot selected the action demanded by the current instruction and scene. ECT puts that choice into the demonstrations. It holds the instruction fixed, changes the scene and action, and makes the difference visible. Swap and Task success remain below in-distribution success in every suite after ECT. The results cover task-specific fine-tuning, not generalist use, navigation, mobile manipulation, or contact-rich manipulation.
