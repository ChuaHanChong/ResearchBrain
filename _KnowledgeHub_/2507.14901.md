# Learning Nonlinear Causal Reductions to Explain Reinforcement Learning Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Max Planck Institute developed Nonlinear Targeted Causal Reduction (nTCR), a method for explaining complex Reinforcement Learning policies by learning interpretable high-level causal models from low-level interactions. It provides theoretical guarantees for exact causal reductions and identifies policy biases and failure modes, such as a surprising clockwise swing preference in a pendulum task and critical ball trajectory dependencies in robot table tennis.

## Problem

- The black-box nature of complex Reinforcement Learning policies makes their decision-making opaque to humans, hindering debugging and trust in real-world applications.
- Existing Causal Model Reduction methods are limited by their reliance on linear mappings and Gaussian approximations, failing to capture the inherently nonlinear relationships prevalent in real-world RL systems.
- Standard performance metrics for RL agents offer limited insight into *why* specific actions or sequences of actions lead to success or failure, complicating policy improvement.

## Method

- Formulates RL policy explanation as a **Causal Model Reduction** (CMR) problem using ==Structural Causal Models== (SCMs) and identifying causal influences through shift interventions during policy execution.
- Introduces Nonlinear Targeted Causal Reduction (nTCR) which extends the TCR framework to nonlinear settings by incorporating a normality regularization term to ensure interpretable, Gaussian high-level causal representations.
- Employs a structured and interpretable nonlinear function class, based on Gaussian kernel basis functions over time-series features, to map low-level observations and interventions to high-level causes.

## Results

- nTCR achieved near-zero consistency loss and accurately identified ground-truth causal maps on synthetic data, validating its theoretical properties and implementation.
- In a pendulum swing-up task, nTCR uncovered a non-monotonic policy bias (a preference for clockwise motion) and specific failure modes, which linear causal reduction methods failed to detect.
- For a robot table tennis task, the method revealed detailed, state-dependent insights into critical robot joint movements and specific ball trajectories that posed greater challenges (e.g., balls closer to the net).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Enforcing normality (Gaussian distribution) on the learned high-level causes in nTCR is crucial for both theoretical consistency and practical interpretability, especially in high-noise environments.
> - The use of a structured, nonlinear function class allows nTCR to capture complex, non-monotonic causal relationships in RL policies while maintaining the ability to pinpoint influential features and time steps.
> - Theoretical guarantees are provided for the existence and uniqueness of exact nonlinear causal transformations for a specific class of additive noise SCMs, offering a rigorous foundation for the learned explanations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of Explainable Reinforcement Learning (XRL), an area focused on making the decision-making processes of learning agents understandable to humans. As reinforcement learning (RL) systems are increasingly deployed in consequential real-world applications such as autonomous systems, robotics, and resource management, understanding their behavior—why they succeed or fail—becomes critical for ensuring reliability, safety, and trust.

Traditional XRL approaches are often categorized into three types: feature importance (FI) methods, which explain the immediate context of single actions; learning process methods (LPM), which reveal influential experiences during training; and policy-level (PL) methods, which summarize the agent's overall behavior. This research falls into the policy-level category, specifically focusing on methods that extract abstract states by reducing the dimensionality of states, actions, and reward spaces into high-level variables.

The paper builds upon existing theoretical frameworks for causal abstraction and causal model reduction (CMR), particularly the Targeted Causal Reduction (TCR) framework. Previous work in causal abstraction has established formal conditions for valid abstractions, but few have addressed the challenge of learning such abstractions from complex, low-level systems. Linear TCR, for instance, offered a principled approach but was limited by its reliance on linear mappings and Gaussian approximations, which may not adequately capture the inherently nonlinear relationships prevalent in real-world RL scenarios. Other causal XRL approaches, such as action influence models, typically require predefined causal graph structures and focus on explaining individual actions rather than broader, high-level causal patterns.

The primary gap addressed by this work is the need for a principled, nonlinear framework for learning causal reductions that can accurately represent complex systems while maintaining interpretability and offering theoretical guarantees. By extending TCR to a nonlinear setting (nTCR), the authors aim to overcome the limitations of linear models and provide more expressive and robust explanations for the behavior of trained RL policies, particularly in scenarios involving intricate, non-linear agent-environment interactions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to provide a principled method for explaining the behavior of reinforcement learning policies, particularly why they succeed or fail. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Complexity of RL Policies:** Trained RL policies, especially those based on deep neural networks, often operate in high-dimensional observation spaces and make decisions through mechanisms that are opaque and not easily interpretable by humans. This "black-box" nature hinders understanding and debugging.
2. **Lack of Insight from Performance Metrics:** Standard performance metrics, such as cumulative reward, offer limited insight into the specific reasons behind an agent's success or failure. They indicate *what* happened but not *why*.
3. **Credit Assignment Problem:** A fundamental challenge in RL is determining which specific actions or sequences of actions contribute most significantly to eventual outcomes. This makes it difficult to pinpoint the causal factors influencing policy performance.
4. **Necessity for Trust and Safety:** As RL systems are increasingly deployed in real-world, high-stakes applications (e.g., autonomous systems, medical applications), understanding and explaining their behavior is crucial for ensuring reliability, safety, and building user trust. Practitioners require robust explanations to diagnose unexpected behaviors and guide improvements.
5. **Guiding Training and Development:** Identifying specific failure modes and biases in trained policies can inform more efficient training regimes, inspire improvements to policy architectures, or suggest modifications to learning algorithms.

To address these challenges, the authors propose a causal perspective, formulating the problem as a Causal Model Reduction (CMR). Their specific objectives include:

- **Formulating RL Policy Explanation as CMR:** To translate the complex agent-environment interactions into a low-level causal model and then learn a simplified high-level causal model that explains variations in cumulative reward.
- **Developing a Nonlinear TCR Framework (nTCR):** To extend the existing Targeted Causal Reduction framework to handle nonlinear relationships, enabling more expressive explanations for complex systems while retaining the focus on explaining a specific target phenomenon.
- **Providing Theoretical Guarantees:** To establish uniqueness and existence guarantees for exact causal reductions within a broad class of nonlinear causal models, ensuring that learned explanations are unambiguous and meaningful despite the inherent identifiability challenges of nonlinear systems.
- **Introducing Interpretable Nonlinear Reduction Functions:** To design a class of nonlinear functions (e.g., based on Gaussian kernels) that are expressive enough to capture complex patterns yet constrained to maintain interpretability, allowing identification of which specific features, at which time steps, are most influential.
- **Demonstrating Practical Utility:** To experimentally validate that the nTCR approach can uncover important behavioral patterns, biases, and failure modes in trained RL policies across diverse tasks, ranging from synthetic models to practical control problems like pendulum swing-up and robot table tennis.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on adapting the Causal Model Reduction (CMR) framework, specifically a nonlinear extension of Targeted Causal Reduction (TCR), to explain Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies.

#### 3.1. Causal Perspective on RL and Intervention Strategy

The authors view RL episodes as inherent causal chains, where observations lead to actions, which in turn cause environmental changes, new states, and rewards. This sequence is naturally mapped to a Structural Causal Model (SCM). To identify causal influences, the method introduces **shift interventions** during policy execution. Each action $A_t$ selected by the policy is perturbed by a random shift $\delta A_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_t)$ before execution. This allows observation of how deviations from the policy's intended actions impact subsequent states and the final cumulative reward.

For the TCR formulation, the low-level endogenous variables $X$ consist of states and actions ($X_{\pi(1)} = (A_0, \ldots, A_{T-1}, S_0, \ldots, S_T)^\intercal$) and rewards ($X_{\pi(0)} = R = (R_1, \ldots, R_T)^\intercal$). The interventions $I_{\pi(1)}$ are applied only to actions ($\delta A_0, \ldots, \delta A_{T-1}$), while states are not directly intervened upon. The target variable $Y$ is defined as the cumulative reward over an episode, $Y = \sum_{t=1}^T R_t$.

#### 3.2. Targeted Causal Reduction (TCR) Framework

TCR aims to learn transformations between a complex low-level SCM (L) and a simplified high-level SCM (H) while preserving causal properties, particularly under interventions.

- **Low- and High-level SCMs:** A low-level SCM (L) describes the detailed system with endogenous variables $X$ and exogenous noise variables $U$. A high-level SCM (H) describes a lower-dimensional system with endogenous variables $Z$ and exogenous noise variables $W$.
- **Interventional Consistency:** The core criterion for a good reduction is that the high-level model should respond to interventions in a way analogous to the original low-level model. This is formalized by requiring that the push-forward distribution of the low-level model under intervention $i$, $\tau_\# [P_L^{(i)}]$, matches the interventional distribution of the high-level model under a corresponding high-level intervention $\omega(i)$, $P_H^{(\omega(i))}$.
- **General TCR Framework (Keki´c et al., 2024):**
 - **Target:** The high-level model $H$ has two nodes: a predefined scalar target variable $Y = \tau_0(X)$ (cumulative reward) and a learned high-level cause $Z = \tau_1(X)$.
 - **Parameterized High-level Models:** The high-level SCM is constrained to be a linear additive Gaussian noise model, meaning $Z \to Y$ is linear with a causal coefficient $\alpha$ and additive noise $W_0$, and exogenous variables are factorized Gaussian.
 - **Constructive Transformations:** The reduction maps $\tau$ and $\omega$ are constructive, meaning $\tau_0$ depends on $X_{\pi(0)}$, $\tau_1$ depends on $X_{\pi(1)}$, and $\omega_1$ depends on $I_{\pi(1)}$. The high-level intervention on $Y$ ($\omega_0$) is fixed to zero, forcing changes in $Y$ to be explained through $Z$.
 - **Approximate Consistency Objective:** The objective is to minimize a consistency loss $L_{\text{cons}}(\tau, \omega, \gamma) = \mathbb{E}_{i \sim P_I}[D(\tau_\# [P_L^{(i)}(Y,Z)] \| P_H^{(\omega(i))}(Y,Z))]$, where $D$ is a divergence measure and $P_I$ is a prior over interventions. Linear TCR uses a Gaussian KL-divergence approximation (effectively matching means and variances).

#### 3.3. Nonlinear Targeted Causal Reduction (nTCR)

To overcome the limitations of linear mappings, nTCR extends TCR to a nonlinear setting while aiming to retain interpretability and theoretical guarantees.

- **Normality Regularization:** The original linear TCR's objective, based on Gaussian KL-divergence approximation, does not enforce distributions to be truly Gaussian. For nTCR, a **normality regularization term** $L_{\text{norm}}$ is introduced. This term encourages the learned high-level cause $Z = \tau_1(X)$ to have a Gaussian distribution by minimizing the 1-Wasserstein distance between its standardized push-forward distribution and a standard normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. This regularization aids interpretability (by enforcing a simple, unimodal cause distribution) and helps in achieving more faithful interventional consistency. The total optimization objective is $L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{cons}} + \eta_{\text{norm}}L_{\text{norm}}$. For practical optimization, a differentiable approximation of the Wasserstein distance using smooth CDFs is employed.
- **Theoretical Guarantees:**
 - **Uniqueness (Proposition 4.1):** Under assumptions that the low-level model is an additive noise SCM, the exogenous noise has a probability density with a non-vanishing Fourier transform, and the high-level model has a non-zero causal effect, an exact constructive transformation (if it exists) is unique up to multiplicative and additive constants.
 - **Existence (Proposition 4.2):** A family of low-level additive SCMs with jointly Gaussian noise and specific functional forms for $f_0$ and $f_1$ (relating low-level cause and effect to an intermediate matrix B) is constructed, for which exact nonlinear transformations $\bar{\tau}_1$ and $\bar{\omega}_1$ can be derived analytically. These propositions provide a theoretical foundation for the approach, particularly for validating the algorithm on synthetic data.
- **Interpretable Nonlinear Function Class:** To ensure interpretability despite nonlinearity, a structured function class for $\tau_1$ and $\omega_1$ is designed. This class leverages the temporal structure of RL episodes. Each feature's trajectory $X_j$ (e.g., angular velocity over time) is mapped via a sum of Gaussian kernel basis functions:
 $\tau_1(X) = \sum_{j=1}^d \tau_{1}^j(X_j) = \sum_{j=1}^d \sum_{t=1}^T w_{j,t} \cdot \phi_{j,t}(X_{j,t})$, where $\phi_{j,t}(x) = \exp(-(x - \mu_{j,t})^2 / 2\sigma_{j,t}^2)$.
 The weights $w_{j,t}$ are learned, and kernel centers $\mu_{j,t}$ and widths $\sigma_{j,t}$ are fixed to span the range of observed values. This allows identification of which features, at which time steps and values, contribute most significantly to the high-level cause. The function $\omega_1$ is defined analogously for interventions.

#### 3.4. Experimental Validation

The approach is validated on:
- **Synthetic data:** Generated from models adhering to Proposition 4.2, to verify theoretical guarantees (consistency loss, identification of ground truth $\tau$ and $\omega$).
- **RL tasks:**
 - **Pendulum:** A classical swing-up control task, state includes angle, angular velocity; action is torque.
 - **Robot Table Tennis:** A simulation of a pneumatic robot arm returning balls, with state including robot joint states and ball position/velocity; actions are changes in desired muscle pressures.
 For RL tasks, policies are trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Interventions are applied for a portion of the episode, and the cumulative reward is the target variable.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated that nTCR can successfully learn causal reductions and provide interpretable explanations for RL policies across synthetic and practical tasks.

#### 4.1. Experiments on Synthetic Data

- **Validation of Theoretical Properties:** nTCR consistently achieved near-zero consistency loss on synthetic low-level causal models constructed according to Proposition 4.2.
- **Ground-Truth Identification:** The learned $\tau$ and $\omega$ maps closely matched the theoretical ground-truth solutions (as quantified by a normalized L2 identification loss), demonstrating that the algorithm successfully converges to the correct causal reduction. This confirmed the theoretical uniqueness and existence guarantees for the tested model class and validated the implementation.
- **Effect of Normality Regularization:** An ablation study showed that normality regularization (L_norm) significantly improved the identification of ground-truth solutions, particularly in high-noise regimes (where intervention variance was low relative to exogenous noise). Without regularization, the nonlinear functions could exploit flexibility to achieve low consistency loss but result in highly non-Gaussian, multi-modal high-level cause distributions, which are undesirable for interpretability. This indicates that normality regularization acts as an important inductive bias, preventing overfitting and ensuring interpretability, especially when causal signals are weak.

#### 4.2. Pendulum Task

- **Policy A (Bias Discovery):** nTCR successfully identified two distinct trajectory groups with significant reward variations: pendulums starting in the bottom-right corner and swinging clockwise (associated with higher rewards) versus those starting in the bottom-left and swinging counterclockwise (associated with lower rewards). This revealed a surprising bias in the policy's performance, as the environment is mirror-symmetric along the axis of gravitation and initial conditions are uniformly sampled. A linear TCR model failed to capture this nuanced, non-monotonic behavioral pattern.
- **Policy B (Failure Mode Identification):** For Policy B, the learned $\omega$ map indicated that applying more negative torque towards the end of the episode would improve performance. Further analysis confirmed that this policy frequently failed to stabilize the pendulum in the upright position, allowing it to tip over in the positive $\theta$ direction before initiating a full-rotation recovery. nTCR correctly identified that increased negative torque at the top position could prevent this instability. Linear TCR also captured this observation, suggesting that this particular failure mode exhibited a somewhat linear relationship between intervention and outcome.

#### 4.3. Table Tennis Task

- **Robot Arm Movement Patterns:** The learned $\tau$ maps for the robot's joint positions provided insights into critical movements. For Joint 0 (the main axis for swinging the arm), nTCR highlighted two critical state regions: (A) the arm swinging back (negative angle) before ball arrival and then forward upon ball arrival (leading to high rewards), and (B) the arm swinging forward too early (leading to low rewards). This indicates precise timing and motion sequencing are crucial.
- **Ball Trajectory Dependencies:** The $\tau$ reductions for ball position revealed clear trends in task difficulty:
 - Balls traveling further toward the outside edge of the table were harder to hit.
 - Balls bouncing further from the robot (closer to the net) presented greater challenges.
 - These findings were validated by analyzing missed balls, which showed a disproportionate number missing toward the top and left (corresponding to the outside edge of the table and positions further from the robot's base).
- **Comparative Analysis with Linear TCR:** While linear TCR identified that the periods around ball contact were most crucial, it lacked the granularity to reveal the specific state-value dependencies uncovered by nTCR. For instance, linear TCR could not distinguish how different ball positions (e.g., closer to the net versus further) impacted success, demonstrating its limitation in capturing complex, state-dependent relationships.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of Explainable Reinforcement Learning by introducing nonlinear Targeted Causal Reduction (nTCR). Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Explanations for Complex RL Policies:** nTCR provides a method for generating policy-level explanations that can uncover complex, non-linear behavioral patterns, biases, and failure modes in trained RL agents. This is crucial for understanding policies that operate in high-dimensional, intricate environments where linear models are insufficient.
- **Theoretical Foundations for Nonlinear Causal Reduction:** The work offers theoretical guarantees for the existence and uniqueness of exact nonlinear causal transformations for a specific class of additive noise SCMs. These guarantees are important for establishing the robustness and reliability of the learned explanations, addressing challenges related to identifiability in nonlinear systems.
- **Improved Interpretability through Structured Function Classes:** By introducing an interpretable nonlinear function class based on Gaussian kernels, the method balances the need for expressivity with the requirement for human understanding. This allows for pinpointing which features, at specific time steps and values, contribute most to the learned high-level causal explanations.
- **Practical Utility in RL Development:** The empirical results on Pendulum and robot table tennis demonstrate nTCR's ability to:
 - **Identify Policy Biases:** As shown in the Pendulum task, uncovering unexpected biases (e.g., preference for clockwise motion) can inform data augmentation strategies or architectural changes to improve robustness.
 - **Diagnose Failure Modes:** Pinpointing specific conditions or actions leading to policy failure (e.g., incorrect torque application in Pendulum, challenging ball trajectories in table tennis) can guide targeted debugging, reward function adjustments, or policy retraining.
 - **Inform System Design:** Understanding critical interaction points and state dependencies in complex robotic systems can inform hardware design or control strategies.
- **Advancement of Causal AI:** The work contributes to the broader field of Causal AI by extending causal model reduction techniques to nonlinear settings and demonstrating their application in a high-impact domain like RL.

**Potential Impact:**
The ability of nTCR to provide detailed, interpretable explanations has several potential impacts:

- **Increased Trust and Adoption of RL:** By making RL policies more transparent, nTCR can help foster trust among stakeholders, regulators, and end-users, facilitating the adoption of RL in safety-critical and high-stakes applications.
- **More Efficient RL Development Cycles:** Developers can leverage nTCR's explanations to rapidly identify weaknesses, biases, or unexpected behaviors in their RL agents, leading to more efficient debugging, accelerated policy improvement, and targeted optimization efforts.
- **Enhanced Human-AI Collaboration:** With clearer explanations of "why" an agent acted in a certain way, humans can better collaborate with and oversee autonomous systems.

**Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several avenues for future work, including extending theoretical guarantees to broader classes of nonlinear models, exploring alternative high-level model structures beyond linear and Gaussian assumptions (though this might introduce new interpretability challenges), adapting to more complex or less structured input data (e.g., image-based observations in RL), and extending the intervention framework to discrete action spaces. These directions suggest a continued commitment to refining and expanding the applicability of nTCR.
