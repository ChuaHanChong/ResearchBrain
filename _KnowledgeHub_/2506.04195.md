# MACS: Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Optimization of Crystal Structures

> [!summary] Summary
> A multi-agent reinforcement learning approach named MACS optimizes periodic crystal structures by modeling each atom as an independent agent in a partially observable Markov game. This method achieves geometry optimization that is 34% faster and requires 28% fewer energy calculations than established baselines, while maintaining a low 0.36% failure rate and demonstrating zero-shot transferability to new chemical compositions.

## Problem

- Geometry optimization is a fundamental, computationally intensive bottleneck in materials science, especially for crystal structure prediction workflows.
- Traditional optimization methods (e.g., BFGS, FIRE) are often inefficient for large structures or high-throughput applications, hindering new materials discovery.
- Existing machine learning-based optimizers for atomic structures have not adequately addressed the unique complexities and periodicity of crystal structures using multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL).

## Method

- Models periodic crystal structure optimization as a ==Partially Observable Markov Game==, where each atom within the unit cell acts as an independent agent.
- Utilizes an Independent Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm for decentralized policy learning, employing a shared neural network architecture for all atom-agents.
- Employs CHGNet, a fast and accurate machine learning interatomic potential, for efficient energy and force calculations needed for training and optimization.

## Results

- MACS performs geometry optimization approximately **34%** faster and requires **28%** fewer energy calculations on average compared to the strongest traditional baseline (BFGSLS).
- It achieves a very low failure rate of **0.36%**, comparable to the most robust baselines, ensuring high reliability in converging to stable local minima.
- The method demonstrates strong zero-shot transferability to diverse, unseen chemical compositions and maintains efficiency with increasing structure size, suggesting broad applicability without retraining.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating crystal structure optimization as a MARL problem, where atoms are agents, effectively captures the localized yet interdependent nature of atomic interactions in periodic systems.
> - A comprehensive observation space incorporating local atomic features (e.g., gradient, history, local environment) and an action space with an adaptive gradient-dependent scaling factor are crucial for stable and sample-efficient learning.
> - The learned decentralized policy demonstrates strong zero-shot transferability to unseen chemical compositions and scalability to larger structures, highlighting its potential as a general-purpose optimizer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Geometry optimization, the process of finding stable atomic arrangements corresponding to local energy minima, is a fundamental and frequently performed task in computational chemistry and materials science. This process is particularly critical in crystal structure prediction (CSP) workflows, where thousands of candidate structures often require local optimization to determine their stability and properties. Traditional optimization methods, such as Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS), Conjugate Gradient (CG), and Fast Inertial Relaxation Engine (FIRE), although established, can be computationally intensive, especially for large structures or in applications requiring numerous optimization runs. The time consumed by these optimizations represents a bottleneck in materials discovery efforts.

In response to these computational challenges, the "learning to optimize" (L2O) paradigm has emerged, leveraging machine learning to develop more efficient optimization algorithms tailored to specific problem domains. L2O has been applied to various classical optimization problems, including black-box optimization and solving partial differential equations. Within atomic structure optimization, L2O has been explored for finite, non-periodic atomic clusters and molecular optimization tasks, utilizing methods like graph-based reinforcement learning (RL) to achieve lower energies or faster convergence. However, the application of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) specifically to the optimization of *periodic crystal structures* remains an underexplored area. Prior work on molecular geometry optimization using MARL, such as MolOpt, has shown performance comparable to or surpassing some baselines, but has not addressed the unique complexities of periodicity in crystal structures.

This work contributes to the L2O landscape by introducing MACS, a MARL method designed for periodic crystal structure optimization. By formulating the problem as a partially observable Markov game where individual atoms act as agents, the research addresses the inherent interdependencies and local environmental influences within crystals. It extends the application of MARL to a class of chemical systems (periodic crystals) not previously covered in this context, aiming to improve efficiency, scalability, and transferability compared to existing optimization techniques. The integration of a fast and accurate machine learning interatomic potential model, CHGNet, for energy and force calculations, positions this work at the intersection of machine learning, computational chemistry, and materials design.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and reliable method for the geometry optimization of periodic crystal structures. The authors define geometry optimization as the autonomous adjustment of atomic positions within a fixed unit cell to locally minimize all atomic forces below a specified threshold (0.05 eV/Å) as quickly as possible, within a maximum of 1000 steps.

The motivation for this objective stems from several factors:

- **Computational Bottleneck in Materials Design:** Geometry optimization is a ubiquitous and computationally demanding task in computational chemistry, particularly within crystal structure prediction (CSP) workflows. The exploration of potential energy surfaces (PES) in CSP often necessitates thousands of local optimization runs on candidate structures. The time required for these optimizations, using conventional methods, constitutes a significant bottleneck, hindering the pace of new materials discovery.
- **Limitations of Existing Methods:** Traditional first- and second-order optimization methods (e.g., BFGS, CG, FIRE) can require a large number of steps for extensive structures or involve computationally expensive calculations at each step. While effective, their efficiency can be suboptimal for high-throughput applications.
- **Inherent Properties of Crystal Structures:** The stability of a crystal structure depends on the collective forces acting on its constituent atoms. These forces, and thus the local energy minima, are primarily determined by an atom's chemical identity and its immediate local environment. Importantly, similar chemical environments often recur within an optimized crystal structure, suggesting that individual atoms could learn to make coordinated local adjustments.
- **Leveraging the "Learning to Optimize" Paradigm:** The authors hypothesize that by adopting the "learning to optimize" (L2O) paradigm, a machine learning model can learn an adaptive optimization policy that surpasses the efficiency of hand-designed algorithms.
- **Novelty of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL):** Recognizing the collective nature of structural stability, the authors propose a novel formulation wherein each atom is treated as an independent agent in a multi-agent system. This approach aims to learn decentralized policies that enable atoms to simultaneously and cooperatively adjust their positions, collectively converging to a stable, locally optimized structure. This is presented as the first application of MARL to periodic crystal structure optimization.
- **Scalability and Transferability:** A crucial goal is to develop an optimizer that not only performs well on known systems but also exhibits strong scalability to larger structures and zero-shot transferability to unseen chemical compositions, without requiring re-training.
- **Reducing Computational Cost:** By focusing on reducing the number of optimization steps and, critically, the number of energy calculations (which are computationally expensive, even with machine learning potentials), the objective is to accelerate the overall optimization process.

In essence, the research aims to address a critical computational challenge in materials science by introducing a novel MARL-based approach that exploits the local nature of atomic interactions in periodic crystals to achieve faster, more reliable, and transferable geometry optimization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the MACS methodology involves modeling the geometry optimization of periodic crystal structures as a Partially Observable Markov Game (POMG), where each atom within a periodic unit cell is treated as an individual agent. All agents act independently and simultaneously, aiming to collectively discover a local minimum energy configuration by maximizing their individual rewards. The energy and force calculations necessary for this process are performed using CHGNet, a machine learning interatomic potential model trained on DFT data, selected for its speed and accuracy.

The POMG is defined by the following components:

- **Agents (A):** Each atom within the periodic unit cell of a crystal structure is considered an individual agent.
- **State Space (S):** The global state encompasses the positions of all atoms in the structure.
- **Observation Space (O):** Each agent (atom a_i) has a private local observation (o_t_a_i) of the global state at time step t. This observation is designed to provide information about the agent itself and its local environment.
 - **Atom Feature Vector (f_t_a_i):** For each agent a_i, a feature vector is constructed by concatenating:
 - **Covalent radius (r_i):** A static chemical property distinguishing elements.
 - **Action scaling factor (c_t_i):** A dynamic factor (explained in Actions) influencing displacement magnitude.
 - **Log-norm of the gradient vector (log(|g_t_i|)):** Captures the magnitude of the force acting on the atom, directly indicating stability.
 - **Gradient vector (g_t_i):** The force vector, scaled to prevent excessively large values, providing directionality for optimization.
 - **Previous displacement (d_t-1_i):** History feature reflecting past movement.
 - **Change in gradient vector (g_t_i - g_t-1_i):** History feature indicating the effectiveness of the previous step.
 - **Full Observation (o_t_a_i):** The agent's full observation combines its own feature vector with the feature vectors and relative positions of its *k nearest neighbors*. These neighbors can be within its unit cell or periodic images from adjacent cells. The k nearest neighbors are determined using the `average-minimum-distance` package. In this study, k is set to 12, resulting in an observation vector of length 204. The use of relative positions and the avoidance of absolute atomic coordinates facilitate smooth crossing of unit cell boundaries and handling of periodic images.
- **Action Space (U):** The action (u_t_a_i) for each agent is a 3-dimensional vector in the range [-1, 1]^3. This action is transformed into an atomic displacement (d_t_i) using a scaling factor:
 - **Scaling Factor (c_t_i):** c_t_i = min(|g_t_i|, c_max), where c_max is a tunable hyperparameter (0.4 in this study). This factor ensures that displacements are proportional to the magnitude of the force, preventing excessive movements, particularly when forces are high. The final displacement is d_t_i = c_t_i * u_t_a_i.
- **Reward Function (R_i):** The individual reward for agent a_i at time step t is designed to encourage force reduction:
 - R_t_a_i = log(|g_t_i|) - log(|g_t+1_i|). This logarithmic form helps to balance short-term gains, especially in early optimization stages where force magnitudes can vary significantly. The discount factor (γ = 0.995) encourages faster convergence to low-force states.
- **Transition Function (T):** The transition function is deterministic. Taking a joint action (u) leads to a new state (s') determined by the updated atomic positions after displacement.

**Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:**

- **Independent Soft Actor-Critic (SAC):** MACS utilizes independent SAC, a decentralized approach where each atom-agent learns its policy by treating all other agents as part of the environment. SAC is chosen for its sample efficiency and entropy regularization, which supports exploration and avoids premature convergence to suboptimal policies.
- **Architecture:** The policy and twin Q-networks are two-layered MLPs with ReLU activation functions, shared among all agents for efficient training. The policy network outputs parameters (mean, std) for the action vector, which are then squashed by tanh to fit the action space.
- **Training Workflow:**
 1. Given a structure, CHGNet estimates forces (gradients).
 2. Gradient vectors are scaled.
 3. Local observation vectors are constructed and normalized.
 4. Observations are fed to the policy network, which outputs actions.
 5. Actions are scaled and applied as atomic displacements to update the structure.
 6. The updated structure's energy and forces are calculated by CHGNet to determine the next state and individual rewards.
 7. Transitions (<o_t_a_i, u_t_a_i, o_t+1_a_i, R_t_a_i>) are stored in a replay buffer.

**Training and Testing Data Generation:**

- **Training Compositions:** Six diverse chemical compositions were selected: Y2O3, Cu28S16, SrTiO3, Ca3Ti2O7, Ca3Al2Si3O12, and K3Fe5F15. These vary in the number of elements (2-4) and atoms (5-80 for experimental structures).
- **Structure Generation:** Initial pseudo-random structures are generated using the Ab Initio Random Structure Searching package (AIRSS). During training, structures are generated on the fly, with approximately 40 atoms and reasonable volumes for chosen compositions.
- **Test Sets:** For each training composition, three test sets of 300 structures each are generated, containing K, 1.5K, and 2K atoms, where K is the training structure size.
- **Transferability Test Sets:** To evaluate zero-shot transferability, test sets for three *unseen* compositions (Sr2TiO4, Sr3Ti2O7, Sr4Ti3O10, related to a training composition SrTiO3) are created, each with training-size and twice-training-size structures.
- **Termination Conditions:** Optimization terminates upon achieving maximum atomic force below 0.05 eV/Å (success) or reaching 1000 steps (failure).

**Ablation Studies:**

The methodology also included ablation studies to evaluate the design choices for the observation space (feature representation variants), reward function (fixed penalty, partial reward sharing), and action space (direct displacement without scaling factor). These studies assess the impact of different components on sample efficiency, training stability, and overall optimization performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that MACS exhibits favorable performance compared to established geometry optimization methods across various metrics, showcasing its efficiency, scalability, and transferability.

#### 4.1. Performance Benchmark Against Baselines

MACS was benchmarked against six state-of-the-art methods: BFGS, BFGSLS (BFGS with line search), FIRE, MDMin, CG (Conjugate Gradient), and FIRE+BFGSLS (a hybrid approach). Performance was evaluated using mean optimization time (T_mean), mean number of energy calculations (C_mean), mean number of steps (N_mean) for successful optimizations, and failure rate (P_F).

- **Overall Efficiency:** MACS consistently outperforms baselines in terms of T_mean and C_mean. On average across all test sets, MACS is approximately 34% faster and requires 28% fewer energy calculations than BFGSLS, which is identified as the strongest baseline in these metrics.
- **Failure Rate:** MACS achieves the lowest failure rate (P_F = 0.36%), performing comparably to BFGSLS (0.36%) and FIRE+BFGSLS (0.82%), and significantly outperforming others like MDMin (46.19%) and CG (18.22%). This indicates MACS's reliability in converging to a stable structure.
- **Number of Steps (N_mean):** BFGSLS shows a slightly better N_mean than MACS, requiring 5% fewer steps on average. However, BFGSLS and CG often involve multiple energy calculations per step, leading to higher C_mean and T_mean despite fewer nominal steps. MACS performs comparably to CG in terms of N_mean but with a significantly lower failure rate.
- **Energy Distribution and Evolution:** Analysis of the energy distributions for local minima obtained by MACS and baselines indicates that MACS samples from the same distribution of stable structures. Energy evolution plots show that MACS decreases energy faster than most baselines or performs comparably to the best among them, reaching a low-energy state efficiently.

#### 4.2. Scalability and Zero-Shot Transferability

- **Scalability:** MACS maintains competitive performance (superior T_mean and C_mean) as the structure size increases, demonstrating its scalability across different atom counts within the trained compositions.
- **Zero-Shot Transferability:** A notable finding is MACS's strong zero-shot transferability. When applied to optimize structures of compositions not encountered during training (Sr2TiO4, Sr3Ti2O7, Sr4Ti3O10), MACS still outperforms all baselines in T_mean and C_mean, even for structures twice the size of those used in training. This suggests that the learned policy generalizes across different, yet chemically related, compositions.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of specific design choices in MACS:

- **Observation Space Features:**
 - Excluding "force-related" features (gradient vector and its log-norm) or "history" features (previous displacement and gradient change) significantly degrades performance, leading to higher episode lengths and lower rewards during training, and poorer T_mean and N_mean in testing.
 - The full feature set of MACS, incorporating covalent radius, action scaling factor, log(|gradient|), gradient vector, gradient change, and previous displacement, is shown to be crucial for its competitive performance. Removing either `g_t_i - g_t-1_i` (feat.9) or `d_t-1_i` (feat.8) leads to a degradation in N_mean, with feat.9 requiring 7.7% more steps than MACS on average.
- **Reward Function:**
 - Adding a fixed penalty (rew.10) or incorporating partial reward sharing (rew.11) to the original logarithmic reward function did not yield superior performance. MACS's original logarithmic reward (R_t_a_i = log(|g_t_i|) - log(|g_t+1_i|)) resulted in better T_mean and N_mean on the largest test structures compared to these alternatives.
- **Action Space Design (Scaling Factor):**
 - Directly using the action vector as displacement (without the gradient-dependent scaling factor c_t_i) makes training significantly less sample-efficient and stable. This variant also converges to higher average episode lengths, confirming the importance of the adaptive scaling factor in MACS for efficient and stable learning.

#### 4.4. Composition-Targeted Training

For the Ca3Al2Si3O12 composition, where general MACS performed comparably to BFGS, a MACS policy specifically trained only on this composition demonstrated superior performance to both the general MACS and BFGS across all metrics (T_mean, N_mean, P_F). This suggests that for particularly complex compositions or those with many elemental species, longer training or adjusted training set proportions might further enhance performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of MACS represents a notable advancement in the field of computational materials science, particularly for crystal structure prediction and general geometry optimization. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Novelty in Problem Formulation:** MACS introduces a novel approach by formulating periodic crystal structure optimization as a multi-agent coordination problem using MARL. This is an underexplored direction that effectively leverages the localized yet interdependent nature of atomic interactions in crystals. The treatment of individual atoms as agents, each learning a decentralized policy, is a conceptual shift from traditional centralized optimization methods.
- **Enhanced Efficiency:** The demonstrated ability of MACS to optimize crystal structures significantly faster (34% faster on average) and with fewer energy calculations (28% fewer on average) than current state-of-the-art methods is a direct contribution to addressing a major bottleneck in materials design. Reducing computational cost per optimization run can accelerate high-throughput screening and discovery of new materials with desirable properties.
- **High Reliability:** Achieving one of the lowest failure rates (0.36%) among benchmarked methods underscores MACS's reliability in successfully converging to a stable local minimum. This characteristic is crucial for applications like CSP where incomplete or failed optimizations can lead to wasted computational resources and inaccurate results.
- **Scalability and Zero-Shot Transferability:** MACS's confirmed scalability to larger structures and, more significantly, its zero-shot transferability to unseen chemical compositions, indicates its potential as a general-purpose optimizer. This implies that a single trained policy could be applied to a wide range of materials without requiring extensive re-training, which is a substantial advantage for broad applications in materials science.
- **Foundation for Future Development:** The work establishes a robust foundation for further research at the intersection of MARL and materials science.
 - **Improved Atomic Features:** Future integration of a broader range of atomic features beyond covalent radius, potentially utilizing advanced descriptor implementations, could enhance the model's understanding of chemical diversity.
 - **Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs):** The identified importance of history features in ablation studies suggests that incorporating recurrent neural networks could further improve MACS's efficiency by allowing agents to better leverage temporal dependencies in the optimization process.
 - **Unit Cell Optimization:** Expanding the MARL framework to include optimization of the unit cell vectors, by treating them as additional agents, could extend MACS's capabilities beyond fixed-cell geometry optimization.
 - **DFT Integration:** The proposed "train-with-CHGNet-run-with-DFT" workflow is a promising direction. Training with a fast machine learning potential (like CHGNet) and then applying the learned policy with a high-accuracy but computationally intensive method (like DFT) could offer an optimal balance of speed and accuracy for practical applications.

In conclusion, MACS represents a significant step towards developing a potentially universal and highly efficient geometric optimizer for periodic crystal structures, with direct applicability to accelerating materials discovery and computational chemistry research.
