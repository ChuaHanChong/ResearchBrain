# PhyX: Does Your Model Have the "Wits" for Physical Reasoning?

> [!summary] Summary
> A collaborative research team introduces PHYX, a large-scale multimodal benchmark featuring 3,000 university-level physics questions grounded in visual scenarios. The benchmark reveals a performance gap exceeding 29% between state-of-the-art AI models and human experts in complex physical reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Existing AI benchmarks primarily evaluate disciplinary knowledge recall or abstract mathematical problem-solving, lacking a robust assessment of 'physical reasoning' capabilities in multimodal contexts.
- Physical problem-solving requires models to decode implicit conditions from realistic visual scenarios, maintain physical consistency, and ground abstract formulas in real-world contexts, which prior benchmarks do not adequately capture.
- Current physics-specific benchmarks often rely on text-based problems or simplified, schematic visuals, limiting their capacity to evaluate the integration of visual perception with implicit physical constraints.

## Method

- The authors introduce **PHYX**, a large-scale multimodal benchmark with 3,000 visually-grounded university-level physics questions spanning six core domains (Mechanics, Electromagnetism, Thermodynamics, Wave/Acoustics, Optics, Modern Physics).
- Each question is available in multiple-choice and open-ended formats, and in three text variations (Full-Text, Text-DeRedundancy, Text-Minimal) to assess model reliance on explicit textual information versus visual cues.
- Questions are categorized into six distinct physical reasoning types, such as Physical Model Grounding and Implicit Condition Reasoning, to enable fine-grained diagnostic evaluation of model strengths and weaknesses.

## Results

- The best-performing MLLM (GPT-o4-mini) achieved **45.8%** accuracy on open-ended questions with de-redundant text, trailing behind human experts (**75.6%**) by over 29 percentage points.
- Error analysis of GPT-4o revealed a high prevalence of visual reasoning errors (**39.6%**) and a lack of deep domain knowledge (**38.5%**), indicating models struggle with interpreting visual contexts and applying fundamental physical principles.
- LLMs augmented with high-quality image captions demonstrated competitive performance with many MLLMs, suggesting current MLLMs face limitations in effectively processing raw visual signals for physical reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physical reasoning, particularly when requiring visual interpretation and inference of implicit conditions, presents a more substantial challenge for current AI models than purely mathematical or general reasoning tasks.
> - State-of-the-art MLLMs exhibit significant over-reliance on detailed textual descriptions, struggling to leverage raw visual information effectively when textual cues are reduced or absent.
> - Dedicated reasoning-oriented models outperform general-purpose MLLMs, but a considerable performance gap of over 29% persists between the best AI models and human experts on physical reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of sophisticated artificial intelligence models, particularly large language models (LLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs), has led to significant advancements in various reasoning tasks. Recent benchmarks have demonstrated that state-of-the-art models can achieve human-competitive accuracy on complex mathematical problems, such as those found in AIME, GPQA, MATH-500, and OlympiadBench. MLLMs, exemplified by models like GPT-4o and Claude-3.7-Sonnet, further integrate visual understanding into their reasoning capabilities.

The existing research landscape includes benchmarks designed to assess disciplinary knowledge and mathematical problem-solving. Examples include MMMU for broad disciplinary reasoning and MathVista, MathVerse, and MATH-V for mathematical reasoning in visual contexts. However, the authors identify a critical gap in these evaluations: the assessment of *physical reasoning*. Prior science benchmarks are often critiqued for relying on memorization or basic understanding, while mathematical benchmarks primarily focus on logical deduction with explicit conditions. Physics-specific benchmarks, such as PHYBench, UG-Physics, and OlympiadBench, tend to prioritize text-based problems or simplified, schematic visuals, thereby limiting their capacity to evaluate the integration of visual perception with implicit physical constraints.

This work addresses this identified gap by introducing PHYX, a benchmark specifically designed to assess physical reasoning. Physical reasoning is defined as the integrated ability to combine domain knowledge, symbolic reasoning, and understanding of real-world constraints. The authors contend that physical problem-solving fundamentally differs from pure mathematical reasoning or science knowledge recall because it requires models to: (1) decode implicit conditions from realistic scenarios (e.g., interpreting "smooth surface" as zero friction), (2) maintain physical consistency throughout reasoning chains, and (3) ground abstract physical formulas in real-world visual contexts. This involves a tight integration of visual perception, material properties, and dynamic simulations, which existing benchmarks reportedly do not fully capture. PHYX aims to fill this void by providing a rigorous, multimodal evaluation framework centered on visually-grounded, university-level physics problems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce PHYX, a large-scale benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the capacity of foundation models for physics-grounded reasoning in multimodal, visual scenarios.

The motivation for developing PHYX stems from the observation that existing benchmarks for LLMs and MLLMs do not adequately capture the complexities of "physical reasoning." The authors argue that physical reasoning is a crucial aspect of intelligence that involves a multifaceted integration of disciplinary knowledge, symbolic operations, and the comprehension of real-world constraints. Current models, despite their performance in other domains, reportedly exhibit limitations in this area.

The specific motivations for creating PHYX include:
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** Previous benchmarks for science and mathematics primarily focus on factual recall, abstract mathematical logic, or text-based reasoning. They often overlook the requirement for models to interpret visual cues, infer implicit physical conditions (e.g., "starts from rest," "smooth surface"), and apply physical laws within dynamic, real-world contexts.
- **Highlighting the Unique Nature of Physical Problem-Solving:** The authors emphasize that solving physics problems is distinct. It necessitates the ability to translate observed scenarios into appropriate physical models, account for material properties, and simulate dynamic interactions. This demands a deeper level of understanding beyond mere formula application or logical deduction.
- **Evaluating Multimodal Integration:** Many advanced MLLMs combine visual and textual understanding, but their ability to genuinely integrate these modalities for complex physical reasoning, where visual information is often crucial for problem setup and constraint identification, remains under-assessed. PHYX aims to provide scenarios where models must concurrently leverage visual perception and abstract knowledge.
- **Providing a Diagnostic Tool:** By identifying specific weaknesses in current models' physical reasoning capabilities, the benchmark is intended to serve as a diagnostic instrument. It aims to reveal whether models primarily rely on memorized knowledge, surface-level pattern matching, or excessively on mathematical formulations, rather than exhibiting a robust physical understanding.
- **Guiding Future AI Development:** The findings from PHYX are expected to provide insights and a roadmap for the development of future AI systems. This includes inspiring research into models that can more effectively integrate abstract scientific concepts with real-world knowledge and visual input, ultimately leading to more physically grounded AI.

Through its design, PHYX seeks to provide a comprehensive and challenging evaluation, exposing critical shortcomings in current multimodal reasoning frameworks and guiding the advancement of more intelligent, scientifically capable AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the design, curation, and evaluation of the PHYX benchmark, followed by a detailed analysis of model performance and error types.

#### 3.1. PHYX Benchmark Design

PHYX is introduced as a large-scale, multimodal benchmark consisting of 3,000 meticulously curated questions. These questions are visually-grounded, meaning they require the interpretation of an accompanying image or figure for problem-solving. Each problem is designed to represent realistic physical scenarios.

- **Coverage:** The benchmark spans six core physics domains: Mechanics (550 questions), Electromagnetism (550 questions), Thermodynamics (500 questions), Wave/Acoustics (500 questions), Optics (500 questions), and Modern Physics (400 questions). These domains are further broken down into 25 sub-domains (e.g., Kinematics, Electrostatics, Laws of Thermodynamics).
- **Question Formats:** The 3,000 unique questions are available in two formats: 3,000 multiple-choice (MC) questions and 3,000 open-ended (OE) questions, resulting in 6,000 total instances.
- **Reasoning Types:** Each question is categorized into one or two of six distinct physical reasoning types to enable fine-grained assessment:
 - **Physical Model Grounding Reasoning:** Connecting problem details to fundamental physical concepts and idealized models.
 - **Spatial Relation Reasoning:** Understanding and manipulating geometric and directional aspects.
 - **Multi-Formula Reasoning:** Requiring the use of multiple interrelated physics formulas.
 - **Implicit Condition Reasoning:** Recognizing information not explicitly stated but implied by context or keywords.
 - **Numerical Reasoning:** Requiring advanced mathematical methods (e.g., calculus) beyond basic algebra.
 - **Predictive Reasoning:** Forecasting future states or behaviors based on physical laws.
- **Information Variations:** For each question, three parallel versions are created to assess model dependence on textual detail:
 - **Full-Text:** The original question as it appears in textbooks, often containing redundant information also present in the image.
 - **Text-DeRedundancy:** A concise version where textual information duplicated by the image is removed. This aims to force models to leverage both modalities.
 - **Text-Minimal:** A question-only version, retaining only the core question text.

#### 3.2. Data Curation Process

A four-stage process was implemented to ensure high-quality, scientifically accurate, and challenging data:
1. **Survey and Definition:** Core physics disciplines, subfields, and reasoning types were identified.
2. **Expert Annotation:** Graduate students in STEM fields were recruited as expert annotators. They were instructed to avoid copyright violations, select questions not easily found online to mitigate data contamination, and create both open-ended and multiple-choice versions for each problem.
3. **Parallel Version Creation:** Annotators constructed the Full-Text, Text-DeRedundancy, and Text-Minimal versions for each question.
4. **LLM Augmentation for LLMs:** GPT-4o was used to generate descriptive captions for each image, enabling the evaluation of text-only LLMs on these visually-grounded tasks by providing textual representations of the visual content.
This process initially yielded 3,300 questions.

#### 3.3. Data Quality Control

A three-stage data cleaning process was applied:
1. **Duplicate Detection:** Lexical overlap analysis was used to identify potential duplicates, followed by manual review and removal by physics Ph.D. students.
2. **Length Filtering:** The shortest 10% of questions based on textual length were filtered out to maintain quality and difficulty.
This rigorous process aimed to ensure scientific accuracy and eliminate dataset bias.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Test Subset:** A smaller `testmini` subset was created for efficient evaluation, comprising 1,000 problems (6,000 instances) sampled proportionally across physics domains. All quantitative evaluations were performed on this subset.
- **Baselines:**
 - Random chance.
 - Human expert performance: 15 undergraduate and graduate physics students, divided into three groups, each completing 18 questions, providing "worst," "medium," and "best" expert baselines.
- **Models Evaluated:** 16 foundation models were tested, including:
 - **Reasoning MLLMs:** GPT-o4-mini, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, MiniCPM-o.
 - **General MLLMs:** GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Claude-3.5-Haiku, InternVL3 (78B, 14B, 8B), Yi-VL-34B, DeepSeek-VL2-4.5B, Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct-2.8B.
 - **Large Language Models (LLMs):** GPT-o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-8B (augmented with GPT-4o image captions).

#### 3.5. Evaluation Protocols

A unified three-step evaluation pipeline, leveraging Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, was designed for both LLMs and MLLMs to account for varying instruction-following capabilities:
1. **Prediction Generation:** Models were prompted with the input query (problem description, question, and image/caption) using CoT prompting to generate a detailed response.
2. **Answer Extraction:** Rule-based strategies were applied to extract the definitive answer from the raw model predictions, filtering out reasoning steps or conversational filler for both MC and OE formats.
3. **LLM Judge:** For open-ended questions, and for MC questions where direct option letter matching failed, a powerful LLM (DeepSeek-V3) was employed as an "answer judge." This judge compared the extracted answer against the ground truth, feeding it multiple times to ensure robust assessment of correctness, particularly for varied answer expressions. A preliminary study indicated over 99% accuracy for the judge.

This comprehensive methodology aims to provide a robust and fair assessment of physical reasoning capabilities across a diverse set of foundation models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 16 foundation models on the PHYX benchmark revealed several key findings regarding their physical reasoning capabilities:

- **Challenging Nature of PHYX:** The benchmark proved significantly challenging for all tested models. The worst-performing human experts (physics undergraduates and graduates) achieved an accuracy of 75.6% on open-ended questions with de-redundant text. In contrast, the best-performing MLLM, GPT-o4-mini, scored only 45.8%, and the best LLM, DeepSeek-R1, achieved 51.2% (with image captions). This indicates a substantial performance gap exceeding 29% between human experts and state-of-the-art AI models, underscoring the difficulty inherent in physical reasoning tasks as assessed by PHYX.

- **Impact of Multiple-Choice Format:** The multiple-choice (MC) format generally narrowed the performance gap across models compared to open-ended (OE) questions. Models achieved higher scores on MC questions (e.g., GPT-o4-mini: 86.9% MC vs. 45.8% OE on Text-DeRedundancy). This suggests that MC questions allow models to rely on surface-level cues or partial understanding, whereas OE questions demand a more profound reasoning ability and precise answer generation, providing greater discriminative power.

- **Model Performance Across Different Domains:** Performance varied across the six physics domains. Models tended to achieve higher accuracy in domains such as Waves/Acoustics (e.g., GPT-o4-mini: 52.7%) and Mechanics (e.g., GPT-o4-mini: 52.3%), which often involve more natural images and problems requiring less complex multi-step reasoning. Conversely, performance was notably lower in domains like Thermodynamics (e.g., GPT-o4-mini: 41.8%) and Modern Physics (e.g., GPT-o4-mini: 40.6%), which frequently demand intricate visual perception and multi-step reasoning, as shown in Figure 1 and Table 3.

- **Superiority of Reasoning-Oriented Models:** Models specifically optimized for reasoning tasks, such as GPT-o4-mini and DeepSeek-R1, generally outperformed more general-purpose models. For instance, on open-ended Text-DeRedundancy questions, GPT-o4-mini achieved 45.8%, and DeepSeek-R1 (an LLM with captions) achieved 51.2%, significantly higher than GPT-4o (32.5%) and Claude3.7-Sonnet (42.2%). This highlights the influence of architectural and training strategies on multimodal reasoning capabilities.

- **Competitive Performance of LLMs with Captions:** Despite lacking direct visual input, LLMs like DeepSeek-R1 (51.2%) and GPT-o3-mini (31.5%) demonstrated competitive performance with many MLLMs when augmented with GPT-4o-generated image captions. This suggests that in many scenarios, high-quality textual descriptions of visual content can provide sufficient context for LLMs to perform physical reasoning, and it also indicates current MLLMs' limitations in effectively leveraging raw visual signals compared to text.

- **MLLMs' Over-Reliance on Textual Descriptions:** A clear performance gradient was observed across the three input variations (Full-Text, Text-DeRedundancy, and Text-Minimal). Model accuracy consistently decreased from Full-Text to Text-DeRedundancy to Text-Minimal. For example, Claude3.7-Sonnet's accuracy on open-ended questions dropped from 44.4% (Full-Text) to 42.2% (Text-DeRedundancy) to 17.2% (Text-Minimal). This pattern indicates that MLLMs heavily rely on detailed textual descriptions and struggle to reason effectively when visual context alone or minimal text is provided, failing to fully leverage visual input for physical reasoning.

- **Physical Reasoning is More Challenging Than Mathematical Reasoning:** Comparing GPT-4o's performance on PHYX (32.5% on open-ended Text-DeRedundancy questions) to its reported scores on existing mathematical reasoning benchmarks like MathVista (63.8%) and MATH-V (63.8%), a significant drop in accuracy for physical reasoning tasks was observed. This finding suggests that physical reasoning requires a deeper integration of abstract concepts with real-world knowledge and visual context, presenting a more substantial challenge for current models than purely mathematical problems.

- **Detailed Error Analysis (GPT-4o):** An in-depth analysis of 96 randomly sampled incorrect predictions from GPT-4o revealed a distribution of error types:
 - **Visual Reasoning Errors (39.6%):** Models frequently misinterpreted visual context, spatial relationships, or failed to extract correct information from the realistic physical scenarios depicted in images.
 - **Lack of Knowledge (38.5%):** Models exhibited an incomplete understanding of specific domain knowledge or fundamental physical principles required to solve the problem (e.g., ignoring wave speed differences across media in optics problems).
 - **Text Reasoning Errors (13.5%):** Models struggled with correctly processing or interpreting textual content, including overlooking implicit conditions (e.g., ignoring "ignore friction" instruction) or misinterpreting logical relationships.
 - **Calculation Errors (8.3%):** Models made mistakes in arithmetic operations, formula application, or unit conversions, despite having potentially understood the physical context.

These findings collectively highlight critical limitations in current AI models' ability to perform sophisticated physical reasoning, particularly in visually-grounded, real-world contexts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of PHYX contributes significantly to the field of artificial intelligence by addressing a recognized gap in the evaluation of foundation models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Novel Benchmark for Physical Reasoning:** PHYX is presented as the first large-scale benchmark specifically designed to assess physics-grounded reasoning in multimodal, visually-driven scenarios. This fills a critical void in the evaluation landscape, moving beyond mere disciplinary knowledge recall or abstract mathematical problem-solving to test a more integrated form of intelligence that combines domain knowledge, symbolic reasoning, and real-world constraints.
- **Revealing Critical Model Limitations:** The comprehensive evaluation using PHYX exposes significant shortcomings in state-of-the-art MLLMs and LLMs. The substantial performance gap between human experts and models, coupled with detailed error analysis, reveals that current AI often relies on memorized disciplinary knowledge, excessive dependence on mathematical formulations, and surface-level visual pattern matching rather than genuine physical understanding. This diagnostic capability is crucial for identifying precise areas for improvement.
- **Guiding Future AI Model Development:** By pinpointing specific weaknesses, such as visual reasoning errors, lack of deep physical knowledge, and over-reliance on explicit textual cues, PHYX provides a roadmap for future research and development. It suggests that future models need to improve their ability to:
 - Better interpret complex visual information in realistic physical contexts.
 - Ground abstract physical principles in real-world scenarios more effectively.
 - Infer and utilize implicit conditions from visual and textual cues.
 - Integrate information across modalities (visual and text) more robustly, especially when textual redundancy is removed.
 This direction encourages the design of AI systems that truly understand and reason about the physical world.
- **Advancing AI for Scientific Discovery and Education:** The development of AI models with enhanced physical reasoning capabilities holds potential for various applications. In education, such models could serve as more effective tutors, assisting learners in grasping complex physical concepts by interpreting visual aids and providing contextual explanations. In scientific research, physically-grounded AI could accelerate automated scientific discovery by simulating complex systems, interpreting experimental data, and even generating hypotheses based on physical laws.
- **Promoting Robust Multimodal Integration:** The benchmark's design, particularly the Text-DeRedundancy setting, forces models to integrate information from both visual and textual modalities. This emphasis helps drive progress towards truly multimodal AI systems that can robustly process and reason across different forms of input, which is a fundamental requirement for general-purpose AI.
- **Reproducibility and Community Support:** The implementation of an evaluation protocol based on widely-used toolkits like VLMEvalKit ensures reproducibility, enabling other researchers to easily benchmark their models and contribute to the collective advancement in this area.

The authors acknowledge certain limitations, including the benchmark's exclusive focus on English language prompts and annotations, which may limit generalizability to other languages, and the use of schematic/textbook-style images rather than real-world photographs, which might not fully capture the complexity of perception in natural environments. Despite these, PHYX represents a significant step towards developing AI that possesses a deeper, more genuine understanding of the physical world.
