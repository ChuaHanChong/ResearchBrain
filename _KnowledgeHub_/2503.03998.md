# Robotic Compliant Object Prying Using Diffusion Policy Guided by Vision and Force Observations

> [!summary] Summary
> A robotic system for compliant object prying utilizes a diffusion policy guided by vision and force observations, integrated through a novel cross-attention architecture and force data augmentation. This approach demonstrated a 96% success rate in prying tasks, including zero-shot transfer to unseen battery types and configurations.

## Problem

- Automating robotic disassembly for product recycling, particularly for components combining compliant and rigid parts, presents challenges due to unknown assembly states and high precision requirements.
- Traditional offline programming and vision-only imitation learning methods struggle with real-world variability, tight tolerances, and the critical need for precise force control in contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- Effectively integrating low-dimensional contact force information with high-dimensional image data within diffusion policies is difficult, as force signals can be diluted or underutilized due to dimensionality disparity.

## Method

- A diffusion policy framework is developed that conditions action prediction on a joint feature embedding from synchronized vision and force data, along with the robot's end-effector state.
- A novel cross-attention architecture is employed, where projected force features query encoded image features, to learn relational information between heterogeneous sensor modalities and prevent force signal dilution.
- Force data augmentation techniques, including random scaling and Gaussian noise, are applied during training to improve the policy's generalizability and robustness to varying force requirements and out-of-distribution objects.

## Results

- The proposed Diffusion Policy with Cross-Attention (DP-CA) achieved an overall success rate of **96%** in compliant object prying tasks across diverse scenarios, encompassing both seen and unseen objects.
- DP-CA demonstrated a **57%** improvement over the vision-only baseline (**39%** success rate) and outperformed naive force integration methods, exhibiting strong zero-shot transfer capabilities to unseen battery types.
- The model maintained high success rates in edge-case scenarios, achieving **95%** for batteries in series and **90%** for batteries with varying colors, while robot-applied force magnitudes and trends closely matched human demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating force data at each time step allows the robot to maintain necessary contact and traction, thereby significantly enhancing robustness in complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks.
> - Learning relational features between image and force through cross-attention improves the diffusion policy's generalization capacity to unseen object sizes, configurations, and battery types.
> - The enhanced relational understanding from cross-attention enables the model to make more informed decisions regarding critical mode transitions (e.g., when to initiate prying), preventing common failure modes seen in vision-only baselines.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The escalating global adoption of battery-powered devices across electric vehicles and consumer electronics necessitates robust recycling solutions. A critical step in these solutions is robotic disassembly, particularly for products that integrate both compliant and rigid components. Traditional robotic disassembly approaches, often reliant on offline programming, encounter limitations when confronted with the unknown assembly states, worn components, and high precision requirements characteristic of real-world disassembly tasks. Human operators typically address these complexities by integrating multiple sensory modalities, such as vision and force.

Within this context, imitation learning has emerged as a strategy for robots to acquire skills from human demonstrations. While effective for less complex tasks, traditional imitation learning methods face challenges in scenarios exhibiting multi-modality, where multiple valid action sequences can achieve the same objective. Diffusion models have recently been recognized for their capacity to handle such multi-modal action distributions effectively, making them a promising avenue for learning low-level robotic skills.

The specific task of compliant object prying, exemplified by battery disassembly, introduces further complexities. It requires a robot to execute precise tilting motions while applying controlled contact force to deform a component for separation. Challenges include tight tolerances, variability in tool approach, tilt, and insertion, which are influenced by object size and assembly compliance. Prior work has explored rule-based methods for battery prying, demonstrating effectiveness in controlled environments with precise state information and high tolerances. However, these methods often lack the generalizability required for diverse scenarios involving multiple battery types and configurations.

The present research positions itself within the intersection of advanced imitation learning, sensor fusion, and robotic manipulation for disassembly. While diffusion policies have shown promise in visuomotor policy learning, effectively integrating low-dimensional contact force information with high-dimensional image data remains an open challenge. Previous attempts at multi-sensory policy learning have explored vision with audio or tactile feedback. However, a specific gap exists in developing robust methods for combining force data with images within diffusion policies, particularly for contact-rich tasks where precise force control is paramount. This paper aims to address this gap by proposing a method that enhances the integration of force and vision data to improve the robustness and generalization capabilities of diffusion policies in compliant object prying tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to advance robotic capabilities for scalable product recycling by enabling autonomous disassembly of compliant components, specifically through compliant object prying. The authors are motivated by the challenges inherent in automating disassembly tasks, which often require human-like precision and multi-sensory feedback.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing the need for robotic disassembly in recycling:** The growing volume of battery-powered products necessitates automated recycling solutions. Robotic disassembly is identified as a critical enabling step, particularly for products containing a mix of compliant and rigid components. This automation requires robots to perform complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- **Overcoming limitations of traditional and vision-only methods:** Traditional offline programming for disassembly often lacks the real-time feedback and adaptability required for tasks with unknown states, worn parts, or tight tolerances. While imitation learning and diffusion policies have shown promise for visuomotor tasks, relying solely on vision can be insufficient for contact-rich operations where physical interaction forces are crucial.
- **Integrating force feedback effectively into diffusion policies:** The paper identifies a specific technical challenge: when combining low-dimensional force data with high-dimensional image data in diffusion policies, the force information can become diluted or underutilized due to the disparity in dimensionality. A primary motivation is to develop a method that ensures force signals significantly influence policy output, providing essential contact-rich feedback.
- **Enhancing generalizability to diverse and unseen objects:** Rule-based methods for tasks like battery prying often struggle with variations in object size, compliance, and configuration. The research aims to develop a learning-based approach that can generalize to a wide range of battery types and product casings, including those not encountered during training, thereby increasing the system's adaptability and robustness.
- **Improving robustness in multi-step, high-precision tasks:** Compliant object prying involves a sequence of precise actions, including insertion, tilting, and lifting, all while maintaining appropriate contact force. The motivation is to create a policy that can reliably execute these multi-step processes, adapting to real-time interactions and transitions between different task phases.
- **Specific Contributions:** The paper explicitly outlines three contributions that collectively address these objectives:
 1. **A novel cross-attention architecture:** To effectively integrate force into the observation space for diffusion policy action prediction, ensuring that the low-dimensional force signal is not overshadowed by high-dimensional visual features.
 2. **Force signal augmentation techniques:** To account for variability in force requirements and potential noise during inference, thereby improving the policy's robustness when encountering out-of-distribution objects.
 3. **Successful application to compliant object prying:** To demonstrate the proposed method's efficacy by achieving a high success rate (96%) across diverse scenarios, including both seen and unseen objects and battery types, showcasing zero-shot transfer capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research aims to learn a policy, π, for compliant object prying, conditioned on an observation O_t at each time step t. The observation O_t comprises a joint feature embedding Γ(I, F) derived from an image (I) and three-axis Cartesian force (F) from the robot's end-effector, along with the 6-DoF robot end-effector state (S). The policy predicts a sequence of actions A_t, conditioned on the current and n past observations, with a focus on ensuring effective incorporation of force information within the joint embedding Γ(I, F).

**Overview of Approach:**
The methodology involves several interconnected components: synchronizing observation modalities, augmenting force data to enhance generalizability, and employing a cross-attention mechanism to learn relational features between vision and force data.

**Diffusion Policy Formulation:**
The policy learning framework utilizes a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM), a generative model that learns to reverse a noise injection process through iterative denoising. The action sequence A_t is treated as the data to be generated. The reverse diffusion process is formulated to predict the action sequence by progressively denormalizing a noisy input, guided by a noise prediction network ε_θ. The loss function minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the actual noise and the predicted noise. The diffusion policy predicts a sequence of 6-DoF *delta actions*, representing the relative difference between the current and next target poses over an action horizon (T_a), from which a subset (T_e) is executed.

**Processing Force for Enhanced Generalizability:**
1. **Synchronizing Observation Modalities:** To ensure accurate inference, force, robot state, and image data are sampled simultaneously at each action execution step, achieving sub-half-second sampling precision. This minimizes latency disparities between modalities, though for dynamic tasks, more precise synchronization or interpolation techniques might be considered.
2. **Force Data Augmentation:** To improve the model's generalization to objects requiring varied force levels, the force data undergoes augmentation during training. This involves applying random scaling (uniformly sampled from [0.9, 1.2]) and adding Gaussian noise N(0, 0.005). The choice of a higher upper bound for scaling (1.2) is intended to bias the model towards applying sufficient force, as insufficient force often leads to task failure.

**Using Cross-Attention to Learn Relational Features between Image and Force:**
A core aspect of the methodology addresses the dimensional mismatch between low-dimensional force data and high-dimensional image features, which can lead to force information dilution.
1. **Problem Identification:** Simple concatenation of Cartesian force components with image features, or even linear projection to match dimensions, was found to be insufficient for effective conditioning.
2. **Cross-Attention Mechanism:** A cross-attention architecture is employed to learn Γ(I, F). This mechanism is chosen for its ability to capture complex relationships and dependencies by allowing one input (query) to attend to relevant parts of another (key and value).
 - **Image Encoding:** A ResNet-18 model is used to encode RGB image data into a feature representation I_encoded ∈ ℝ^(N×d), where N is the number of spatial features and d is the feature dimension. The input image is cropped to 98x98 pixels.
 - **Force Processing:** The 3-axis Cartesian force is separated into its normalized magnitude and three directional components, forming a four-parameter input (|F|, ˆF). This input is then expanded using a linear projection layer to F_projected ∈ ℝ^(4×d), matching the dimension of the image features.
 - **Cross-Attention Application:** In the cross-attention mechanism, the projected force feature (F_projected) serves as the *query*, while the encoded image features (I_encoded) act as the *key* and *value*. Projection matrices (W_K, W_V, W_Q) transform these inputs. The attention output is computed as A = softmax((Q · K^T) / √d)V, where d is the dimension of key/query vectors. The architecture uses a hidden dimension of 512 and 4 attention heads. The final output is refined by a two-layer MLP. This setup allows the model to dynamically integrate complementary information from the image based on the force context.
 - **Integration into Diffusion Policy:** The joint embedding vector (A) resulting from the cross-attention is concatenated with the robot's end-effector pose (S). This combined vector is then used as conditioning for the noise prediction network (U-Net architecture) within the diffusion framework, specifically via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM).

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Hardware:** A bi-manual setup with a KUKA IIWA 14 robot for battery extraction (where the diffusion policy is applied) and an ABB IRB120 robot as a support arm.
- **Objects:** Experiments involved various consumer products powered by AAA, AA, C, and D batteries. These varied in size, casing angles, colors, tolerances, and battery depths.
- **Task Initialization:** Each episode begins by positioning the robot end-effector near one end of the battery using Aruco markers or object detection, within an imaginary 1.0cm × 1.0cm × 2.0cm area to account for initial positioning variability.
- **Data Collection:** Human expert demonstrations were recorded via hand-guiding the robot at 3Hz. These trajectories were replayed to collect synchronized force, robot state, action, and RGB image data. A RealSense D415 camera on the robot's wrist and the KUKA IIWA14's built-in force/torque sensor were used.
- **Training:** The model was trained on 419 episodes using AAA and D batteries (with multiple casing types). Action representation used 6D continuous rotation and delta actions. A ResNet-18 vision backbone was trained end-to-end, coupled with global average pooling and spatial softmax pooling. Group Normalization replaced Batch Normalization, and Exponential Moving Average (EMA) was incorporated. Key hyperparameters included a learning rate of 2 × 10^-4, weight decay of 1 × 10^-3, and 100 diffusion steps. An observation count of 2 and an action horizon of 16 (with 6 executed actions for continuous replanning) were used. Image augmentation included color jitter and random cropping. Training consisted of 2000 epochs with a batch size of 72, performed on an Nvidia RTX 3080 GPU for approximately 14 hours.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation aimed to demonstrate the performance and generalizability of the proposed cross-attention-based sensor fusion method for compliant object prying.

**A. Success Rate Test**
The proposed method, DP-CA (Diffusion Policy with Cross-Attention between Image and Force), was benchmarked against three variants:
1. **DP-B (Baseline Diffusion Policy):** A vision-only diffusion policy.
2. **DP-LF (Diffusion Policy with Low-Dimensional Force):** Image conditioning concatenated with a four-parameter low-dimensional force vector.
3. **DP-PF (Diffusion Policy with Projected Force):** Image and force features up-scaled using linear projection to match image dimensions before concatenation.

The evaluation involved 12 different objects (9 unseen, 3 in-distribution during training) and 10 inference iterations per object, totaling 120 experiments per model. Success was defined as fully prying out and lifting the battery from its casing.

- **Overall Performance:** DP-CA achieved an overall success rate of 96%. This marked a 57% improvement over the vision-only baseline (DP-B, 39% success rate).
- **Comparison to Force-Incorporated Baselines:** DP-CA also outperformed DP-LF (48% success rate) by 48% and DP-PF (57% success rate) by 39%.
- **Generalizability:** The significant performance difference was particularly evident with unseen objects and battery types (AA and C-type batteries were out-of-distribution during training). DP-CA demonstrated superior generalization, achieving a 100% success rate on these unseen battery types, whereas baseline methods struggled. Naive force integration (DP-LF and DP-PF) provided modest improvements over vision-only (9-18%) but showed limited generalization to unseen objects.
- **Failure Mode Analysis:** For DP-CA, failures predominantly occurred with AAA batteries, attributed to tight tolerances and slight misalignment of the prying tool, which reduced contact area. One failure with an AA battery involved insufficient lifting before retraction. In contrast, baseline methods exhibited more varied failure modes, including failure to insert the tool, premature prying, insufficient force, and loss of contact during lifting.

**B. Performance under Edge Cases**
The model's robustness was further evaluated in two specific edge-case scenarios not seen during training:
- **Batteries in Series:** Prying tasks involving two or three C or AA batteries connected in series within white casings. The model achieved a 95% success rate.
- **Varying Colors:** Prying tasks with batteries of six different colors (brown, black, dark orange, bright orange, metallic, grey) across four battery types. The model achieved a 90% success rate.
These results indicate that the model maintains a high success rate comparable to the main evaluation, demonstrating its ability to generalize to diverse and complex unseen scenarios.

**C. Comparison in Time Taken between Human Demonstration and Robot Inference**
A comparison of the average time taken to complete the prying task was made between human hand-guiding and robotic inference.
- **Execution Time:** Robotic inference generally took slightly longer than human demonstrations. This was attributed to occasional idle actions at the beginning of inference and the approximate 1-second duration of each inference step.
- **Potential for Optimization:** The authors suggest that this time difference could be reduced by increasing demonstration speed, raising manipulator velocity, and tuning impedance values, though these were avoided in the study to prioritize safety.

**D. Comparison of Force Applied to the Battery during Human Demonstration and Robot Inference**
To validate the robot's action consistency with human demonstrations, the maximum force applied to the battery, particularly the z-component, was compared.
- **Force Magnitude and Trend:** The maximum force applied by the robot during inference was comparable to that observed during human demonstrations (Fig. 7). The force trend throughout the prying task also closely aligned with human expert patterns (Fig. 8).
- **Role of Force Feedback:** Maintaining appropriate force was identified as critical; insufficient force led to loss of contact and failure, while excessive force risked tool or battery damage. The force modality also assisted the robot in detecting state changes, guiding transitions between action modes (e.g., insertion to prying), and preventing premature actions, which were common failure modes in vision-only baselines (Fig. 9).

**E. Key Findings**
The research synthesized several key findings:
- **Force enhances robustness:** Force data at each time step enables the robot to maintain necessary contact and traction, improving robustness in contact-rich tasks.
- **Relational features improve generalization:** The learned relational features between image and force, facilitated by cross-attention, significantly improve the diffusion policy's generalization capacity, especially for out-of-distribution object sizes and configurations (e.g., different battery types). Force serves as a consistent, in-distribution modality across varying products.
- **Cross-attention supports mode transitions:** The enhanced relational insight provided by cross-attention enables the model to make more informed decisions regarding mode transitions (e.g., when to initiate prying), preventing incorrect or premature actions that were observed in baseline methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing practical challenges in automated disassembly and by advancing the capabilities of learning-based robotic policies. The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

- **Enabling Scalable Recycling Solutions:** By demonstrating a robust and generalizable robotic prying solution for compliant objects like batteries, the work directly contributes to the development of higher levels of automation needed for scalable and efficient recycling processes. This has implications for material recovery and reducing environmental impact from electronic waste.
- **Advancement in Multi-Modal Sensor Fusion for Robotics:** The proposed cross-attention architecture for integrating low-dimensional force data with high-dimensional visual information in diffusion policies represents a methodological advancement. It addresses a known challenge in multi-modal learning, where disparity in data dimensionality can dilute the influence of crucial sensory inputs. This technique could be adopted for other contact-rich robotic tasks requiring precise control and state awareness.
- **Improved Robustness and Generalization in Complex Manipulation:** Achieving a 96% success rate in compliant object prying, including zero-shot transfer to unseen battery types, object colors, and configurations, highlights the practical utility and robustness of the developed policy. This level of performance and generalization capability is crucial for deploying robots in varied and unpredictable real-world environments, reducing the need for extensive retraining.
- **Foundation for Future Contact-Rich Tasks:** While validated on battery prying, the framework provides a generalizable approach for other contact-rich manipulation tasks that benefit from combined visual and force feedback. This could include assembly, material handling, or delicate interaction with deformable objects in manufacturing and service robotics.
- **Enhanced Learning from Demonstration:** The work demonstrates how learning from human demonstrations can be effectively channeled through diffusion policies to acquire complex, multi-step skills. The alignment of robot-applied force and execution time with human demonstrations suggests that the robot has effectively learned the underlying task dynamics and strategies.
- **Guidance for Policy Design:** The findings regarding the benefits of relational feature learning (via cross-attention) over simpler concatenation or projection methods for multi-modal inputs provide valuable insights for future policy design in robotics, particularly when combining heterogeneous sensor data.
- **Practical Implications for Manufacturing:** The ability to perform high-precision prying with adaptability to variations in products has potential applications beyond recycling, extending to manufacturing processes that involve disassembly, repair, or quality control where components might need to be carefully separated.

In summary, this research offers a practical and methodologically sound approach to a challenging robotic manipulation problem, with broad implications for automation in industry and a significant contribution to the field of learning-based control and sensor fusion in robotics.
