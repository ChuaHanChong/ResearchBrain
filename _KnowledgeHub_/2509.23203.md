# CE-Nav: Flow-Guided Reinforcement Refinement for Cross-Embodiment Local Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> AMAP, Alibaba Group's CE-Nav framework enables robust local navigation policies transferable across diverse robot embodiments by combining an offline-learned multi-modal geometric expert with an online reinforcement learning refiner. This dual-stage approach achieves high navigation performance on five distinct robots, including bipedal and quadrupedal platforms, while reducing training time by 8x compared to state-of-the-art end-to-end RL methods and demonstrating strong real-world transferability.

## Problem

- Existing learning-based navigation policies often rely on embodiment-specific expert data, incurring high costs and limiting generalization to new robotic platforms.
- Traditional deterministic learning methods for navigation struggle with multi-modal decision-making, leading to 'disastrous averaging' and suboptimal actions when multiple valid paths exist.
- End-to-end policies tightly couple high-level planning with low-level robot dynamics, making them brittle during transfer, while hierarchical methods often assume ideal low-level controllers, creating a sim-to-real gap.

## Method

- A two-stage framework: first, an ==embodiment-agnostic== geometric expert policy is trained offline using imitation learning with VelFlow, a conditional normalizing flow model, to capture multi-modal navigation principles from a 2D simulated rigid body.
- Second, an online reinforcement learning (PPO) stage trains a dynamics-aware refiner module to adapt the general expert's abstract plans to the specific dynamics and imperfect low-level control of a target robot embodiment.
- The refiner is guided by a hybrid loss function combining PPO's objective with an auxiliary guidance loss from the frozen expert, and a curriculum learning strategy is used to dynamically anneal the guidance strength.

## Results

- CE-Nav achieved consistently high success rates (mSR **0.7450**-**0.8600**) and path efficiencies (mSPL) across five diverse robot embodiments (quadrupeds, biped, quadrotor) with various non-ideal low-level controllers.
- The framework demonstrated an 8-fold reduction in training time (6 hours vs. 50 hours) compared to state-of-the-art end-to-end RL methods like NavRL, while also outperforming them in navigation performance.
- Real-world deployments on physical Unitree Go2 and MagicDog robots confirmed strong sim-to-real transferability, outperforming a carefully tuned DWA planner and NavRL in complex environments at an inference rate over **10 Hz**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling universal geometric reasoning from embodiment-specific dynamic adaptation significantly improves policy transferability and reduces adaptation costs for new robots.
> - Leveraging a conditional normalizing flow model (VelFlow) to learn multi-modal action distributions as an expert prior effectively overcomes the 'disastrous averaging' problem in navigation tasks.
> - A curriculum-based guidance strategy during reinforcement learning allows the agent to efficiently adopt expert knowledge while still enabling sufficient exploration to discover robot-specific optimal behaviors and compensate for low-level controller imperfections.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of navigation policies that can generalize across diverse robot morphologies presents a significant challenge in mobile robotics. Existing learning-based strategies generally fall into two categories: end-to-end (E2E) policies and hierarchical methods.

E2E policies, such as those mapping observations directly to low-level joint commands, can be powerful but often deeply entangle high-level planning with a robot's specific dynamics. This entanglement renders policies brittle when deployed on new platforms and necessitates extensive embodiment randomization during training. Hierarchical methods, conversely, decouple planning and control by first generating a path or a series of waypoints [2–6]. While this separation is beneficial, it often introduces a critical gap: the high-level planner typically operates on a simplified or idealized model of the low-level controller, leading to difficulties in compensating for unmodeled dynamic effects or imperfect tracking performance.

Hierarchical velocity planning, as a "middle ground" [7–11], aims to balance these approaches by decoupling high-level geometric reasoning from low-level motor control while providing a reactive command interface capable of learning to compensate for underlying system dynamics. However, this approach faces two primary limitations addressed by the CE-Nav framework:
1. **Reliance on embodiment-specific expert data:** Sourcing data from costly real-world trajectories or physics-based simulations often introduces a strong bias, limiting generalization and scalability.
2. **Deterministic learning paradigm:** Framing navigation as a deterministic regression task fails to capture the inherent multi-modality of decisions (e.g., multiple valid paths around an obstacle), leading to the "disastrous averaging" problem where the model outputs an average of conflicting optimal actions.

In the context of modeling multi-modality in robotic learning, most deep learning methods have traditionally treated navigation as a deterministic regression problem. Recent works have explored generative models, such as diffusion policies and flow models, which show promise in capturing the full distribution of expert actions. However, their application has largely been confined to pure imitation learning scenarios.

CE-Nav contributes to this landscape by integrating a flow-based generative model (VelFlow) to specifically address multi-modal expertise in its initial stage, then leveraging this expertise as a guiding prior for online reinforcement learning in a subsequent stage. This novel two-stage, IL-then-RL architecture aims to allow the policy to retain "common sense" multi-modal navigation principles while dynamically refining actions to adapt to the specific dynamics of unseen robot embodiments. This dual approach systematically addresses the identified limitations of hierarchical velocity planning by decoupling universal geometric reasoning from embodiment-specific dynamic adaptation, thereby offering a solution for low-cost transferability and high performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce CE-Nav, a novel framework designed for Cross-Embodiment Local Navigation, with the aim of achieving both cost-effective transferability and high performance across a diverse range of robot morphologies. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives, each motivated by identified limitations in current navigation policy development:

- **Objective 1: Eliminate embodiment-specific data bias and cost.**
 - **Motivation:** Current learning-based navigation policies often require extensive and expensive data collection from specific robots in real-world or physics-based simulations. This data is typically biased towards the robot it was collected from, hindering generalization and scalability to new platforms. The motivation is to create a system that can learn fundamental navigation principles without relying on such costly and biased data.

- **Objective 2: Address the "disastrous averaging" problem in multi-modal decision-making.**
 - **Motivation:** Navigation tasks frequently present situations with multiple equally valid actions (e.g., turning left or right around an obstacle). Traditional deterministic learning models, which treat navigation as a regression problem, tend to average these optimal choices, leading to suboptimal, indecisive, or unsafe behaviors. The motivation is to develop a policy capable of capturing and utilizing the full distribution of kinematically-sound actions, thereby enabling more nuanced and effective decision-making.

- **Objective 3: Systematically decouple universal geometric reasoning from embodiment-specific dynamic adaptation.**
 - **Motivation:** End-to-end policies tightly couple high-level planning with low-level robot dynamics, making them difficult to transfer. While some hierarchical methods decouple planning from control, they often assume ideal low-level controllers, leading to performance gaps in real-world deployments. The motivation is to create an architecture that clearly separates the abstract principles of navigation (e.g., obstacle avoidance) from the physical realities and imperfections of a specific robot's locomotion system.

- **Objective 4: Enable rapid and stable adaptation to new robotic platforms with minimal interaction.**
 - **Motivation:** Deploying navigation capabilities on a new robot typically involves significant retraining or fine-tuning efforts, which are resource-intensive and time-consuming. The motivation is to develop an efficient adaptation mechanism that allows a new robot to quickly learn to compensate for its unique dynamics and controller imperfections through limited interaction with its environment.

- **Objective 5: Achieve state-of-the-art navigation performance in challenging environments.**
 - **Motivation:** While generalization is critical, the resulting navigation policies must also be highly effective in complex and cluttered settings, ensuring safety and efficiency. The motivation is to demonstrate that the proposed framework can surpass existing methods in performance metrics like success rate and path efficiency.

- **Objective 6: Validate real-world applicability and sim-to-real transferability.**
 - **Motivation:** Simulation-trained policies often struggle with deployment on physical hardware due to the sim-to-real gap. The motivation is to provide empirical evidence through real-world deployments that the framework is robust enough to transfer effectively from simulation to diverse physical robots, providing a practical solution for real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CE-Nav employs a two-stage, IL-then-RL framework structured around a hierarchical control architecture. This architecture decouples the high-level velocity planning (π_high) from the low-level locomotion control (π_low) into a universal action space of body velocity commands (v_x, v_y, v_yaw). This abstraction is intended to facilitate the learning of a transferable navigation core. The framework does not assume an ideal low-level controller; instead, a refiner module in Stage 2 is explicitly trained to compensate for the specific dynamic characteristics and execution imperfections of the robot's π_low.

**Stage 1: Offline Imitation Learning of an Embodiment-Agnostic General Expert**
The goal of this initial stage is to train a universal kinematic expert policy (π_expert) that comprehends general planning principles, such as obstacle avoidance, purely from a geometric perspective. This approach aims to avoid reliance on real robot data and to resolve the multi-modality inherent in navigation decisions.

- **Expert Experience Construction:**
 - To prevent high costs and embodiment bias, the expert dataset is generated in a 2D simulation environment where the agent is modeled as a circular rigid body. This simplification reduces the planning problem to 2D geometric reasoning.
 - The Dynamic Window Approach (DWA), a classical local planner, is used to synthesize the dataset. Crucially, DWA is configured with general dynamic constraints (see Table 5 in the Appendix) rather than robot-specific models, ensuring the expert's decisions are embodiment-agnostic.
 - Data is collected from tens of thousands of procedurally generated "obstacle forest" environments (Fig. 2) with random obstacle layouts.
 - To capture multi-modality, multiple high-scoring candidate actions are saved if their objective function scores are within a δ threshold (δ=0.1) of the optimal score.
 - The final dataset comprises 10 million state-action pairs, filtered to include only successful, geometrically sound demonstrations.

- **Network Architecture:**
 - **State Encoder:** The observation (s) consists of a 2D LiDAR scan and a 7-dimensional robot state vector. The LiDAR scan (N_ray = 144 rays, 4-meter range) is processed by a three-layer CNN. Its feature map is concatenated with the robot state (normalized goal direction (3D), current linear velocity (2D), current angular velocity (1D), and Euclidean distance to goal (1D)). This combined representation is passed through a two-layer MLP to yield a 256-dimensional state embedding, which serves as the conditional input.
 - **VelFlow Design (Conditional Normalizing Flow Model - CNFM):** To overcome the "disastrous averaging" problem, VelFlow learns the complete conditional probability distribution p(x|s) of expert actions. Built on the Real-NVP architecture with 12 coupling layers and 512 hidden dimensions, it maps a simple base distribution (e.g., a standard Gaussian) to the complex expert velocity distribution. Training involves minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of expert demonstrations (Equation 1). For inference, diverse reference velocities (v_ref) are generated by sampling from the base distribution and transforming them via the learned VelFlow network.

**Stage 2: Online Reinforcement Learning of the Dynamics-Aware Refiner**
This stage grounds the General Expert's abstract plans in the physical reality of a specific robot through a guided reinforcement learning (RL) process.

- **Reinforcement Learning Formulation:**
 - **Observation Space (S):** The initial observation (360-degree raycast and 7D robot state) is processed by the State Encoder. This embedding is then concatenated with a reference velocity (v_ref) sampled from the frozen General Expert, forming a "guided state" (s_guided) that is fed to the actor and critic networks.
 - **Action Space (A):** The policy outputs a normalized velocity vector (v_norm), which is then scaled by predefined, embodiment-specific velocity limits (V_lim) to produce the final velocity command (v_final) (Equation 2).
 - **Reward Function (R):** A dense reward function (Table 6 in Appendix 6.2) encourages efficient, smooth, and safe navigation. Rewards are based on the robot's actual achieved trajectory, forcing the refiner to compensate for π_low's latencies or tracking errors. Components include efficiency (R_distance, R_checkpoint, R_heading, R_goal), movement smoothness/stability (P_linear_smooth, P_yaw_smooth, P_stability), and safety (R_safety, P_collision).

- **Refiner Design and Guided Training:**
 - During the RL phase, the state observation is processed in parallel: one stream feeds the frozen General Expert to generate v_ref, and another passes through the refiner's state encoder. The outputs are concatenated to form s_guided for the refiner’s actor and critic networks. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training.
 - **Hybrid Loss Function ("Principled Deviation"):** A hybrid loss balances imitation and exploration: L_total = L_PPO + λ · L_guide (Equation 4). L_guide is an auxiliary guidance loss, L_guide = ||π_refiner(s_guided) - scale · v_ref||^2 (Equation 3), where v_ref is a single command sampled from f_VelFlow, and 'scale' is an auto-computed, embodiment-specific hyperparameter (Appendix 6.3) to map v_ref to the target robot’s velocity range. This term acts as an inductive bias, anchoring the refiner's behavior.
 - **Curriculum Learning for Guidance Strength (λ):** The guidance strength λ is dynamically annealed:
 - **Initial Phase (e.g., 0-1k steps, λ = 0.5):** Strong guidance for rapid adoption of expert logic.
 - **Mid-Phase (e.g., 1k-5k steps, λ: 0.5 → 0.05):** Exponential decay, granting autonomy for exploration and fine-tuning.
 - **Final Phase (e.g., >5k steps, λ = 0.05):** Weak guidance as a regularizer to prevent catastrophic forgetting or policy drift.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of CE-Nav was conducted using the Isaac Sim physics simulator in procedurally generated "obstacle forest" environments. Performance was assessed across five distinct robot embodiments: three quadrupeds (Unitree Go2, MagicDog, Spot), one biped (Unitree H1), and one quadrotor (Hummingbird), all utilizing various non-ideal low-level locomotion controllers (Appendix 6.4, Table 7). The key metrics were Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), and Extra Training Time (ETT).

**Ablation Studies:**
Ablation studies on the Unitree Go2 platform demonstrated the necessity of CE-Nav's core components:
- **Role of VelFlow and Expert Guidance:**
 - Policies trained with only the General Expert (GE-Only_velflow and GE-Only_regr) exhibited very low SRs (Table 1), indicating that direct imitation learning struggles with the covariate shift problem and that the online refiner is essential for robust performance.
 - CE-Nav_pure-rl, an agent trained without expert guidance, required approximately 9 times more training time (52 hours vs. 6 hours for CE-Nav) and achieved significantly lower SRs and SPLs across varying obstacle densities. This highlights the challenges of pure exploration in complex environments.
 - CE-Nav_regr-rl, using a regression-based MLP instead of VelFlow for guidance, performed worse than even the pure-RL baseline. This outcome suggests that a suboptimal, unimodal action prior, which averages multi-modal decisions, can actively mislead the RL agent, underscoring the importance of VelFlow's ability to capture multi-modal distributions (visualized in Fig. 4).
 - CE-Nav_dp-rl, using a diffusion policy, showed improvement over pure-RL and regr-rl but still performed below VelFlow-guided CE-Nav and was significantly more computationally expensive during inference.
 - These results collectively affirmed that a high-quality, multi-modal guidance model like VelFlow is fundamental to CE-Nav's effectiveness.
- **Effect of Curriculum-based Guidance Loss:**
 - Comparing the curriculum annealing strategy against a static strong guidance (CE-Nav_λ=0.5) showed that while static guidance was better than no guidance, it was inferior to the curriculum-based approach (Table 1). This indicates that continuous strong adherence to the expert can stifle the agent's exploration and ability to discover more optimal, robot-specific policies. The curriculum strategy effectively balances imitation and exploration, leading to superior overall performance.

**Comparisons with State-of-the-Art Methods:**
CE-Nav was compared against DWA, Behavioral Cloning (BC), Diffusion Policy (DP), and NavRL on the Unitree Go2 (Table 2).
- CE-Nav achieved higher average SR and SPL (0.8575 mSR, 0.8190 mSPL) compared to all baselines.
- DWA, a classical local planner, showed limited performance (0.6400 mSR), consistent with its typical struggles in long-horizon tasks.
- IL methods (BC and DP) demonstrated poor generalization (0.0275 mSR and 0.0725 mSR, respectively).
- NavRL, a state-of-the-art end-to-end RL method, performed reasonably (0.6925 mSR, 0.6460 mSPL), but CE-Nav surpassed it in both performance and efficiency, requiring 8 times less training time (6 hours vs. 50 hours). This indicates the efficacy of CE-Nav's guided, two-stage methodology.

**Cross-Embodiment Generalization:**
The framework's ability to transfer to new robot embodiments was evaluated by deploying the same pre-trained General Expert across all five robot platforms, with only the brief RL adaptation stage being performed for each (Table 3).
- CE-Nav consistently achieved high navigation performance (mSR ranging from 0.7450 to 0.8600) across all platforms, including quadrupeds, a biped (Unitree H1), and a quadrotor (Hummingbird).
- This outcome demonstrated strong generalization across diverse morphologies, dynamics, and varying tracking fidelities of underlying low-level controllers.

**Real-World Deployment:**
CE-Nav was deployed on physical Unitree Go2 and MagicDog robots in real-world scenarios, including an indoor obstacle maze, office corridor, and outdoor path (Fig. 5). The pipeline achieved an inference rate exceeding 10 Hz on a Jetson Orin NX.
- CE-Nav significantly outperformed both a carefully tuned DWA planner and the open-source NavRL implementation in real-world SR and SPL (Table 4).
- DWA frequently failed in complex indoor environments, becoming trapped. NavRL, despite its generalized design, showed frequent collisions and unstable gaits in direct real-world deployment.
- This performance gap highlights the critical role of CE-Nav's dynamics-aware refiner in adapting the general policy to specific physical characteristics.
- Identified limitations stemmed mainly from sensor limitations (e.g., transparent glass walls, lack of RGB perception) rather than planning or control errors, suggesting robustness of the core navigation framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CE-Nav represents a notable advancement in the field of mobile robotics by addressing critical challenges in the development and deployment of generalizable navigation policies. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling True Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** By systematically decoupling universal geometric reasoning from embodiment-specific dynamic adaptation, CE-Nav offers a robust solution for transferring navigation capabilities across a wide array of robot morphologies, from legged robots to quadrotors. This capability streamlines the deployment of new robotic platforms without extensive, platform-specific re-engineering.

- **Significant Reduction in Development Costs and Time:** The framework's ability to learn an embodiment-agnostic expert offline without relying on expensive, biased real-world robot data, combined with a rapid online reinforcement learning adaptation stage, drastically reduces the financial and temporal overhead associated with endowing new robots with navigation intelligence. The demonstrated 8-fold reduction in training time compared to state-of-the-art end-to-end RL methods underscores this efficiency.

- **Robustness to Imperfect Low-Level Control:** Unlike methods that assume idealized low-level controllers, CE-Nav's dynamics-aware refiner is specifically designed to learn compensatory behaviors for a target robot's specific dynamic characteristics and controller imperfections. This makes the deployed policies more robust and reliable in real-world scenarios where ideal tracking is rarely achieved.

- **Addressing Multi-Modal Decision-Making:** The introduction of VelFlow, a conditional normalizing flow model, to capture the full distribution of kinematically-sound actions directly tackles the "disastrous averaging" problem prevalent in deterministic imitation learning. This enables more nuanced, intelligent, and context-aware navigation decisions, such as effectively choosing between multiple valid paths around an obstacle.

- **Modular and Scalable Architecture:** The two-stage, "plug-and-play" design allows for a single, pre-trained General Expert to serve as a foundation for numerous robot types. Only a lightweight, embodiment-specific refiner needs to be trained for each new platform, ensuring scalability as robot ecosystems expand.

- **Foundation for Future Hierarchical AI Systems:** CE-Nav's robust local navigation capability positions it as an effective "fast system" component within larger, hierarchical navigation architectures. Its potential for integration with high-level planners, such as Vision Language Models (VLMs), for complex, long-horizon tasks (e.g., navigating to fetch coffee), opens avenues for more sophisticated and intelligent robotic agents.

- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transferability:** The successful real-world deployments on diverse robot platforms, outperforming both classical planners and purely generalized learning-based methods, validate the framework's strong sim-to-real transferability. This is crucial for bridging the gap between simulated research and practical applications.

In summary, CE-Nav offers a comprehensive, efficient, and robust solution for local navigation across diverse robot embodiments, addressing key limitations of existing approaches. Its impact is expected in accelerating the development and deployment of autonomous mobile robots in complex and dynamic environments, laying groundwork for more advanced, generalizable robotic intelligence.
