# Knowledge or Reasoning? A Close Look at How LLMs Think Across Domains

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Santa Cruz, Stanford University, and Tongji University developed a fine-grained evaluation framework to dissect Large Language Model reasoning into factual knowledge and logical inference components. Their analysis shows that while Supervised Fine-Tuning improves knowledge, Reinforcement Learning enhances reasoning by refining knowledge application across medical and mathematical domains.

## Problem

- Existing LLM evaluation methods largely focus on final-answer accuracy, obscuring the intricate interplay between factual knowledge and logical inference in their step-by-step reasoning processes.
- Earlier fine-grained reasoning metrics (e.g., ROSCOE) can be unreliable because LLMs often generate reasoning from internal knowledge, not just by rephrasing input, leading to potentially misleading similarity scores.
- A lack of transparent understanding of LLM 'thinking trajectories' makes it difficult to discern whether correct answers stem from robust factual recall, sound logical inference, or a fortuitous combination.

## Method

- Developed a novel, fine-grained ==three-stage evaluation framework== that decomposes LLM responses into explicit, successive logical steps.
- Introduced the =='Information Gain (InfoGain)' metric== to quantify reasoning efficiency by measuring how much each step reduces uncertainty towards the correct solution using step-wise perplexity.
- Established the =='Knowledge Index (KI)' metric== to evaluate the factual correctness of knowledge within each reasoning step by extracting knowledge points and judging their consistency against external ground truth databases.

## Results

- Trained Qwen-Base models consistently outperformed DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B in the medical domain, indicating that pre-training optimized for reasoning (like math/code) does not effectively transfer to knowledge-intensive domains.
- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) increased average Knowledge Index (KI) by **6.2%** on medical tasks but resulted in a significant average **38.9%** reduction in Information Gain (reasoning efficiency) compared to untrained models.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) consistently enhanced Information Gain across both domains and boosted Knowledge Index (average **12.4 points** gain for Qwen-Base in medicine) primarily by guiding models to discard incorrect or irrelevant knowledge in reasoning paths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Knowledge and reasoning are distinct evaluation dimensions that should be measured independently, as models can have similar overall accuracy but differing strengths in factual correctness (Knowledge Index) and reasoning efficiency (Information Gain).
> - Domain demands vary, with medical tasks being more knowledge-intensive (Knowledge Index strongly correlates with accuracy), while mathematical tasks are more reasoning-intensive.
> - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) primarily boosts factual knowledge but can compromise reasoning clarity, whereas Reinforcement Learning (RL) enhances reasoning quality by guiding models to prune inaccurate or irrelevant knowledge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to remarkable performance improvements across a myriad of tasks, including complex reasoning benchmarks in mathematics and general knowledge. However, the prevailing method for evaluating LLMs has largely centered on final-answer accuracy or aggregate performance metrics. This approach, while convenient, obscures the intricate, step-by-step internal processes by which LLMs arrive at their conclusions. It provides limited insight into the crucial interplay between factual knowledge and logical inference, which are both fundamental to true reasoning.

Earlier attempts to delve deeper into LLM reasoning, such as the ROSCOE suite of metrics, have evaluated reasoning based on embedding similarity to the original question or source information. However, as this paper points out, such metrics can be unreliable because LLMs frequently generate reasoning based on their internal knowledge or prior deductions, rather than merely rephrasing input. This means a high similarity score might not accurately reflect the correctness of the knowledge invoked or the logical soundness of the reasoning steps. Appendix A of the paper, showing similar ROSCOE scores across models with differing capacities, empirically supports this limitation.

This paper addresses a critical gap by moving beyond black-box final answer evaluations and attempting to open the "thinking trajectory" of LLMs. It proposes a fine-grained evaluation framework that explicitly decomposes the reasoning process into two distinct components: the factual *knowledge* utilized and the logical *reasoning* operation performed. This decomposition is particularly salient when comparing domains with varying knowledge and reasoning demands, such as mathematics (emphasizing symbolic manipulation and internal consistency) versus medicine (requiring extensive domain-specific knowledge grounded in external facts). By differentiating these aspects, the research aims to provide a more nuanced and transparent understanding of LLM capabilities, which is crucial for building more reliable, interpretable, and domain-adaptive AI systems, especially in high-stakes applications. It contributes to the ongoing discourse about LLM interpretability and the underlying mechanisms of their "intelligence."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to gain a deeper, more transparent understanding of the internal reasoning processes of Large Language Models. Moving beyond mere final-answer accuracy, the authors aim to investigate the step-by-step reasoning trajectories, explicitly dissecting them into two fundamental components: factual *knowledge* and logical *reasoning*.

The core motivation stems from the limitations of existing LLM evaluation paradigms. While state-of-the-art LLMs have shown impressive performance on complex tasks, the "how" behind their success remains largely opaque. Current evaluation metrics are often insufficient to discern whether a correct answer is due to robust factual recall, sound logical inference, or a fortuitous combination of both. This lack of transparency is particularly problematic in critical domains like medicine, where incorrect or inefficient reasoning, even if leading to a "correct" final answer by chance, can have severe implications.

To address this, the paper poses a fundamental question: **"What are the respective roles of knowledge and reasoning in the thinking process of LLMs, and how do they interact across different domains?"** By answering this, the authors aim to:
- Develop a fine-grained evaluation framework that can independently measure the correctness of knowledge used and the quality of reasoning.
- Characterize the distinct competencies models require across different domains (e.g., knowledge-intensive vs. reasoning-intensive tasks).
- Understand how different training strategies, specifically Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), impact these two distinct aspects of LLM performance.
- Provide empirical insights that can guide the development of more effective and targeted training strategies for building truly reliable and domain-adaptive LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper introduces a novel, fine-grained evaluation framework designed to dissect LLM reasoning into knowledge and reasoning components. This framework is applied to analyze Qwen models trained with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) across both medical and mathematical domains.

#### 3.1. Models and Training

- **Base Models:** The study uses two 7B-parameter base models from the Qwen family: `Qwen2.5-7B` (a universal base model) and `DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B` (a variant specifically distilled from DeepSeek-R1, known for strong performance in math and coding). The choice is justified by their strong generalization, open-source nature, and shared backbone for fair comparison.
- **Training Strategies:**
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Models are trained on high-quality human-annotated data. For the medical domain, `medical-o1-reasoning-SFT` (∼40,000 physician-level question-answer pairs with chain-of-thought) is used. For math, SFT data from `RLHFlow` is employed.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used. For the medical domain, `medical-o1-verifiable-problem` (∼40,000 clinical cases with only question and ground truth answer, without chain-of-thought) provides sparse, sign-based rewards (+1 for correct, 0 for incorrect). For math, RL training leverages `RLHFlow` data. This "SFT then RL" or "RL alone" paradigm is crucial for comparing their effects.

#### 3.2. Evaluation Domains and Datasets

The study evaluates models across two distinct domains to highlight differing demands:
- **Medical Domain (Knowledge-intensive):** Benchmarks include MedMCQA, MedQA-USMLE, PubMedQA, MMLU-Pro (Medical), and MedXpertQA. These cover multiple-choice questions, question answering, and expert-level free-response scenarios.
- **Mathematical Domain (Reasoning-intensive):** Benchmarks include AIME 2024, MATH500, AMC (10 & 12), Minerva-Math, and USAMO 2025 / Olympiad. These range from multiple-choice to proof-based questions, emphasizing symbolic manipulation and logical consistency.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Framework

The core of the methodology lies in its three-stage evaluation pipeline:
1. **Response Decomposition (Figure 2a, Figure 3):**
 - Given an LLM's full response (reasoning process + answer), `gpt-4o` is used to break it down into a sequence of explicit, successive logical steps (e.g., `s_1, s_2..., s_t`). Separate prompts are designed for medical (requiring `id`, `planning`, `action`, `step_text`) and math domains (simpler `id`, `step_text`).
2. **Information Gain (InfoGain) (Figure 2b):**
 - **Purpose:** Measures how much a reasoning step reduces uncertainty toward the final answer, reflecting reasoning efficiency.
 - **Calculation:** For each step `s_i`, the probability `P_i` assigned to the *correct solution* (`A*`) is computed by concatenating the question `Q` with all preceding steps `s_1:i`. This leverages step-wise perplexity (PPL).
 - **Metric:** `∆PPL_i = PPL_{i-1} - PPL_i`. The final InfoGain (`∆I`) is the average `∆PPL` across all steps. A higher InfoGain means a more informative and efficient reasoning path. The untrained `Qwen2.5-7B` is used for PPL calculation to avoid self-evaluation biases.
3. **Knowledge Index (KI) (Figure 2c):**
 - **Purpose:** Evaluates the factual correctness of the knowledge presented in each reasoning step.
 - **Calculation:**
 - **Knowledge Extraction:** `gpt-4o` extracts specific knowledge points (`k_i`) from each step `s_i` and reformats them into a query.
 - **Knowledge Retrieval:** Using this query, external medical databases (e.g., medical textbooks mentioned as) are queried to retrieve ground truth knowledge (`k*_i`). For math, the knowledge base of math itself serves as ground truth.
 - **Knowledge Judgment:** `gpt-4o` assesses whether the extracted `k_i` is consistent with the retrieved ground truth `k*_i`. This yields a Boolean `consistency_i` (True/False).
 - **Metric:** The overall KI is the average `consistency_i` across all steps. A higher KI indicates more accurate knowledge incorporation.

This two-pronged approach allows for a granular analysis of LLM behavior, distinguishing between logical flow and factual accuracy, which are often conflated in traditional evaluations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yields several intriguing findings, shedding light on the distinct roles of knowledge and reasoning in LLMs and how different training paradigms affect them across domains:

1. **Domain Transfer and Model Base Performance:**
 - **Trained Qwen-Base outperforms its R1-distilled counterpart in the medical domain.** Qwen-Base consistently shows superior accuracy, InfoGain, and KI compared to `DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B` (Qwen-R1), regardless of whether SFT or SFT+RL is applied. For instance, Qwen-Base with SFT achieved a 14.7% higher average accuracy than Qwen-R1 with SFT (52.79% vs. 38.07%).
 - **Reasoning-centric pre-training (like DeepSeek-R1's math/code focus) does not transfer effectively to knowledge-intensive domains like medicine.** The authors hypothesize that the pre-trained `DeepSeek-R1`'s internal representations, optimized for math and code, may conflict with the medical domain knowledge introduced during post-training, undermining its performance. This suggests that domain-specific knowledge gaps are critical and cannot be easily overcome by general reasoning abilities alone.

2. **Knowledge vs. Reasoning as Distinct Evaluation Aspects:**
 - The study empirically validates that knowledge and reasoning should be treated as independent evaluation dimensions. Even when overall performance (accuracy) is similar, models can exhibit different strengths in KI and InfoGain. For example, while RL-enhanced Qwen-R1 showed minor overall improvement, its KI dropped by 2.2 points compared to its SFT-only version, despite similar reasoning abilities. This underscores the necessity of granular metrics.

3. **Domain-Specific Demands and Correlations with Accuracy:**
 - The correlation analysis (Figure 5) reveals that different benchmarks impose varying demands on knowledge and reasoning.
 - **Medical tasks are generally more knowledge-intensive:** For most medical benchmarks, the Knowledge Index (KI) correlates more strongly with final accuracy than Information Gain (InfoGain). For instance, in PubMedQA, KI correlated at 0.897 with accuracy, whereas InfoGain was 0.465. The average correlation across all medical tasks showed KI at a striking 0.998 correlation with accuracy, 0.3 higher than reasoning. This indicates that access to correct factual knowledge is often the primary driver of success in medical problems.
 - **Mathematical tasks are more reasoning-intensive:** (Though not explicitly detailed with correlations in Figure 5, this is inferred from the SFT vs. RL findings).

4. **Impact of Training Strategies (SFT vs. RL):**
 - **SFT usually provides knowledge but compromises reasoning quality:** SFT consistently boosts domain-specific knowledge (higher KI, e.g., 6.2% average KI increase on medical tasks for Qwen-Base), leading to higher final accuracy in knowledge-intensive domains like medicine. However, SFT often results in more verbose or suboptimal reasoning paths, causing a significant *drop* in InfoGain (average 38.9% reduction compared to untrained models). This suggests SFT helps the model "know more" but not necessarily "think more efficiently or concisely."
 - **RL enhances medical reasoning and improves knowledge correctness by pruning inaccuracies:** RL consistently enhances reasoning (higher InfoGain) across both medical and math domains. Crucially, in the medical domain, RL also boosts the Knowledge Index (average 12.4 points gain over RL-free baselines for Qwen-Base), primarily by guiding the model to *discard* reasoning paths containing incorrect or irrelevant knowledge, rather than introducing new facts. This "enhanced knowledge correctness" leads to more accurate and reliable reasoning trajectories (Figure 9 illustrates this by showing how SFT+RL avoids a factual error made by SFT-only).

In summary, the findings highlight a crucial trade-off: SFT prioritizes factual recall, sometimes at the expense of reasoning efficiency, while RL refines the reasoning process by ensuring the *correct application* of knowledge. The domain's inherent nature (knowledge-heavy vs. reasoning-heavy) dictates which training strategy offers greater benefit to overall accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in understanding the internal mechanisms of Large Language Models, moving beyond the traditional black-box evaluation of final answer accuracy. By introducing and applying the fine-grained `Knowledge Index (KI)` and `Information Gain (InfoGain)` metrics, the authors provide a transparent lens into how LLMs utilize factual knowledge and perform logical reasoning.

**Key Significance:**
1. **Deeper Interpretability:** The framework offers a more granular and interpretable view of LLM decision-making processes. Understanding *why* an LLM makes a certain decision (whether due to strong knowledge or sound reasoning) is crucial for building trust, especially in high-stakes applications.
2. **Guidance for Targeted Training:** The findings provide empirical evidence for the distinct effects of SFT and RL on LLM capabilities. The observation that SFT boosts knowledge but can compromise reasoning clarity, while RL enhances reasoning by refining knowledge application, is invaluable. This insight can guide developers to select and combine training strategies more effectively based on the specific demands of a target domain (e.g., emphasizing SFT for knowledge acquisition in medicine, and RL for logical coherence in mathematics).
3. **Domain-Adaptive Model Development:** The demonstration that medical tasks are more knowledge-intensive while math problems benefit more from enhanced reasoning underscores the need for domain-adaptive model design and training. This moves away from a "one-size-fits-all" approach to LLM development.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Reliable AI in High-Stakes Domains:** In fields like medicine, where factual accuracy and safe reasoning are paramount, this framework allows for a more thorough audit of LLM behavior, identifying potential "hallucinations" or logical fallacies within the reasoning trajectory itself, not just the final outcome. This can lead to the development of more trustworthy AI doctors or legal assistants.
2. **Improved Model Debugging and Auditing:** By pinpointing whether an error originates from incorrect knowledge or flawed reasoning, developers can more effectively debug and improve LLM architectures and training data.
3. **Broader Applicability:** The authors suggest the framework's extensibility to other structured reasoning domains. For instance:
 - **Legal Tasks:** Decomposing legal reasoning into "Issue, Rule (knowledge), Application (reasoning), Conclusion" (IRAC) can allow for assessment of factual legal recall and logical application of laws.
 - **Financial and Economic Tasks:** Analyzing how LLMs summarize trends (knowledge), apply economic models (reasoning), and predict outcomes could enhance transparency in financial forecasting.
4. **Advancing AI Research:** The study paves the way for further research into neural symbolic reasoning, hybrid AI systems that combine statistical patterns with explicit knowledge graphs, and new reward mechanisms for RL that optimize not just accuracy, but also reasoning quality and factual grounding.

**Limitations Acknowledged:** The authors transparently acknowledge that the study was conducted on the Qwen family of 7B-parameter models. While these models are robust and generalize well, extending the findings to a broader range of models (larger or from different families) would be necessary to confirm the generality of their conclusions. Additionally, while the focus on medicine and mathematics is deliberate (representing high-stakes, knowledge-intensive vs. conventional reasoning testbeds), future work could explore even more diverse domains.

Despite these limitations, this research provides a robust framework and compelling empirical evidence that significantly advances our understanding of how LLMs "think," offering crucial insights for developing more capable, transparent, and reliable AI systems.
