# Learning Deployable Locomotion Control via Differentiable Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> An analytically smoothed contact model within a differentiable simulation framework enabled the first reported zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a legged locomotion policy to a real ANYmal quadruped, showing an order of magnitude improvement in sample efficiency.

## Problem

- Applying differentiable simulation to contact-rich tasks like legged locomotion faces challenges due to the non-smooth and discontinuous nature of contact mechanics.
- Existing differentiable contact models either compromise physical accuracy (soft contact), hindering real-world transfer, or yield uninformative gradients (hard contact), making first-order gradient (FoG) optimization difficult.
- Achieving zero-shot transfer of locomotion policies learned purely within a differentiable simulator, leveraging its analytic gradients, to real physical hardware has remained an unsolved problem.

## Method

- A ==differentiable rigid-body simulation== framework was developed using NVIDIA Warp, incorporating ==Moreau's time stepping scheme== for stable dynamics advancement.
- A novel **analytically smoothed contact model** was introduced, which scales contact forces with a ==sigmoid function== to provide informative and unbiased FoGs while maintaining physical fidelity akin to hard contact.
- The ==Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC)== algorithm was employed for robust optimization, alongside a fully differentiable reward formulation, a simplified PD-fitted actuator model, and domain randomization to facilitate successful sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The analytically smoothed contact model produced policies with smoother, more natural gaits and higher performance compared to hard and soft contact models in simulation, successfully transferring to hard contact settings.
- The SHAC algorithm demonstrated an order of magnitude improvement in sample efficiency over PPO, achieving comparable asymptotic performance despite longer wall-clock times per iteration due to backpropagation.
- A locomotion policy trained entirely within the differentiable simulator with the proposed methods successfully transferred zero-shot to a real ANYmal quadruped, exhibiting closely matched real-world and simulated trajectories and robustness to external perturbations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Analytically smoothing hard contact dynamics effectively mitigates discontinuities, yielding informative first-order gradients that are crucial for optimization and physical accuracy, outperforming both traditional soft and hard contact models.
> - Combining short-horizon backpropagation in SHAC with a carefully designed differentiable contact model allows for stable and efficient learning in complex, contact-rich environments where full backpropagation through time is prone to instability.
> - Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is achievable with a fully differentiable simulator by strategically balancing physical fidelity with gradient informativeness and integrating robust learning practices like domain randomization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control has significantly benefited from reinforcement learning (RL), particularly for complex tasks like legged locomotion. Traditional RL algorithms, often relying on zeroth-order gradient (ZoG) estimation through stochastic sampling of objectives, have demonstrated success even with non-differentiable system dynamics present in conventional physics simulators.

A recent development in this landscape is the emergence of differentiable simulation frameworks. These frameworks offer the capability to compute analytic first-order gradients (FoGs) of system dynamics efficiently. The theoretical advantage of analytic gradients, due to their lower variance compared to ZoG estimates, is expected to lead to improved sample efficiency and potentially enhanced asymptotic performance in RL.

However, a primary challenge in applying differentiable simulation to contact-rich robotic tasks, such as legged locomotion, lies in the inherent non-smoothness and discontinuities of contact mechanics. This characteristic complicates the optimization landscape and impedes effective FoG-based optimization. Existing approaches to address this issue typically fall into two categories:
1. **Soft contact models:** These models introduce smoothness and continuous gradients, making them amenable to gradient-based optimization. However, they often compromise physical accuracy, which tends to limit their applicability to purely simulated environments and hinders direct transfer of learned policies to real-world robots (sim-to-real transfer).
2. **Hard contact models:** These models offer high physical fidelity but generate discontinuous and less informative gradient signals, making FoG-based optimization difficult.

Consequently, learning robust locomotion policies entirely within a differentiable simulator using its analytic gradients and then successfully transferring these policies zero-shot to real hardware has remained an open problem. While some works have shown promise in simulation using soft contact models, their real-world applicability has been limited. Other successful sim-to-real efforts have often circumvented the direct differentiable simulation challenge by using hybrid approaches, such as employing a non-differentiable simulator for forward dynamics and a simplified model for gradient computation. This work specifically aims to bridge the gap by developing a single, fully differentiable simulation framework capable of both physically accurate contact modeling and effective gradient computation for real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable the effective use of analytic gradients derived from differentiable simulators for learning contact-rich robotic control policies, specifically for legged locomotion, with the crucial capability of zero-shot transfer to real physical hardware.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and opportunities in robot learning:

1. **Harnessing Analytic Gradients for Sample Efficiency:** Differentiable simulators provide analytic first-order gradients (FoGs), which theoretically offer significant advantages in sample efficiency and optimization performance compared to zeroth-order gradients (ZoGs) commonly used in RL. The authors are motivated to realize these benefits for complex robotic tasks.

2. **Addressing Contact Discontinuities in Differentiable Simulation:** The inherently non-smooth and discontinuous nature of contact dynamics presents a major obstacle to applying FoG-based optimization in contact-rich tasks like legged locomotion. Existing differentiable contact models often face a trade-off:
 - **Soft contact models:** Provide smooth gradients but lack the physical accuracy required for real-world deployment, confining learned policies to simulation.
 - **Hard contact models:** Offer high physical fidelity but yield uninformative or misleading gradients due to discontinuities, making direct FoG-based learning challenging.
 The authors are motivated to develop a contact model that overcomes this trade-off.

3. **Enabling Sim-to-Real Transfer with Differentiable Simulation:** A significant limitation of prior work in differentiable simulation for locomotion has been the inability to achieve successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The ultimate goal of learning robotic control is deployment on physical systems. Therefore, the researchers are motivated to demonstrate that policies learned purely within a differentiable simulator, leveraging its analytic gradients, can function effectively on real robots. This would validate the practical utility of differentiable simulation for real-world robotics.

4. **Improving Locomotion Policy Learning:** The research aims to develop a method that not only enables sim-to-real transfer but also results in robust and efficient locomotion policies, avoiding the unnatural or erratic behaviors sometimes observed when training with less informative gradients or inaccurate models.

In summary, the core motivation is to unlock the full potential of differentiable simulation for real-world robot learning by developing a physically accurate yet differentiable contact model and demonstrating its efficacy through successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a learned legged locomotion policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology addresses the challenges of differentiable contact-rich simulation for real-world robot deployment through a multi-faceted approach, combining a novel differentiable contact model with a robust learning framework and considerations for sim-to-real transfer.

**Differentiable Rigid-Body Simulation Framework:**
The simulation is implemented within NVIDIA Warp, a Python-based framework leveraging GPU parallelization and automatic differentiation (AD) to generate adjoint kernels for gradient computation. The system dynamics are advanced using generalized coordinates based on Moreau’s time stepping scheme, known for its stability. This scheme involves an explicit Euler half-step, evaluation of Equation of Motion (EoM) terms (via Recursive Newton-Euler Algorithm for inverse dynamics and Composite-Rigid-Body Algorithm for inertia matrix), and a contact solver to determine contact impulses. The generalized velocities and positions are then updated using a midpoint step and trapezoidal rule, respectively.

**Analytic Smoothing of Contact Dynamics:**
The core innovation lies in a differentiable contact model designed to provide informative gradients while maintaining physical fidelity.
1. **Problem with Discontinuities:** Hard contact models introduce discontinuities in dynamics, leading to biased and uninformative first-order gradients (FoGs), even if stochasticity might smooth the objective for zeroth-order gradient (ZoG) methods. Discrete-time contact resolution can also alter gradient directions.
2. **Proposed Solution:** The authors analytically smooth the hard contact model by scaling the contact forces with a sigmoid function: $\text{sigmoid}(d, \kappa) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-d\kappa}}$, where $d$ is the penetration depth and $\kappa$ controls stiffness. This approach is inspired by stochastic smoothing but aims to provide unbiased FoGs.
3. **Integration into Gauss-Seidel Solver:** The sigmoid scaling is incorporated into a modified Gauss-Seidel scheme for contact resolution (Algorithm 1 in the paper) in two places:
 - **Inter-contact Influence:** During each solver iteration, the impulse contribution of other contacts ($k$) on the current contact ($j$) is scaled by $\text{sigmoid}(d_k, \kappa)$, ensuring that the influence of other contacts is weighted by their expected impulse magnitude based on penetration depth.
 - **Final Impulse Scaling:** After the solver iterations are complete, the final computed impulses $p$ are scaled by $\text{sigmoid}(d, \kappa)$. This introduces "forces at a distance," meaning contact forces are exerted even before full penetration, providing useful gradients to guide optimization towards contact.
4. **Stiffness Parameter $\kappa$:** The parameter $\kappa$ can be adjusted to control the level of smoothing, allowing the model to range from a very soft contact to an approximation of hard contact as $\kappa$ increases. The authors note that scheduling $\kappa$ was not necessary for the studied task.
5. **Validation (Toy Example):** A toy example of a falling mass demonstrated that analytic smoothing effectively mitigates discontinuities in position and velocity, similar to stochastic smoothing, but crucially provides informative and unbiased FoGs, unlike stochastic FoGs.

**Considerations for Successful Learning and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**

1. **Optimization Algorithm (SHAC):** To address the issue of diverging and noisy gradients when differentiating over long task horizons (Backpropagation Through Time – BPTT) in contact-rich scenarios, the Short-Horizon Actor-Critic (SHAC) algorithm is employed. SHAC is a mixed-order method that uses FoGs over a limited short horizon for local policy updates and approximates the long-term return with a value function trained using ZoGs, thus balancing efficiency with stability.
2. **Differentiable Reward Formulation:** The reward function for the velocity-tracking task is made fully differentiable. For instance, the non-differentiable "feet air time" reward (common in locomotion tasks) is replaced with a differentiable reward that encourages feet to follow a target height trajectory.
3. **Actuator Modeling:** To achieve accurate sim-to-real transfer without unduly complicating the differentiation graph for FoGs, a simplified Proportional-Derivative (PD) law is fitted to real-world actuator data. This provides a reasonable approximation of actuator dynamics while maintaining computational tractability for differentiation.
4. **Policy Robustness (Domain Randomization):** To account for unmodeled real-world effects (e.g., sensor noise, force disturbances) and bridge the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization is applied during training. This includes randomizing observation noise, robot mass, friction coefficients, and introducing random base velocity disturbances. This also contributes to stochastically smoothing the optimization objective.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across comparisons of contact models, RL algorithms, and real-world deployment for quadrupedal locomotion.

**Comparison between Contact Models:**
Experiments using a simple quadruped locomotion task demonstrated the following:
- **Soft Contact Model:** Policies trained with soft contact achieved high performance in their training environment. However, they required smaller simulation time steps (reducing speed by a factor of 2) and, critically, failed to transfer effectively to a physically accurate hard contact simulation (sim-to-sim transfer). This indicates a lack of physical fidelity sufficient for real-world relevance. Increasing stiffness destabilized gradients and prevented learning.
- **Hard Contact Model:** Policies trained with the hard contact model also achieved high returns in their native environment. Interestingly, SHAC successfully optimized despite the inherent discontinuities. However, the resulting locomotion behaviors were described as unnatural, exhibiting suboptimal footholds and erratic movements, which the authors attributed to misleading gradients during training.
- **Analytically Smoothed Contact Model:** This model yielded the highest performance metrics when evaluated in its own training environment and demonstrated successful transfer to the hard contact setting. Policies trained with this model produced significantly smoother, more natural, and efficient gaits compared to those trained with hard contact. This suggests that the analytic smoothing approach effectively captures the underlying hard contact dynamics while providing informative gradients conducive to robust learning.

**Comparison between RL Algorithms:**
A comparison between SHAC (FoG-based) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO, a ZoG-based state-of-the-art RL algorithm) on the quadruped locomotion task revealed:
- **Similar Final Performance:** Both algorithms achieved nearly identical final reward values, suggesting comparable asymptotic performance.
- **Superior Sample Efficiency for SHAC:** SHAC exhibited an improvement in sample efficiency by over an order of magnitude compared to PPO. This is attributed to the reduced variance of analytic gradients used by SHAC.
- **Higher Wall-Clock Time for SHAC:** Despite its sample efficiency, SHAC demonstrated a longer computational time per training iteration (0.33 s) compared to PPO (0.18 s). This difference is due to SHAC's need for backpropagation through the entire simulation rollout during its learning phase, whereas PPO's learning phase is faster. The authors suggest that the wall-clock time advantage of FoG-based optimization becomes more apparent in higher-dimensional problems where sample generation is a bottleneck.

**Training a Velocity-Tracking Policy for Real-World Deployment:**
- **ANYmal Robot Challenges:** Moving from a simpler quadruped to the ANYmal robot, with its distinct mass distribution, inertia, and higher actuator torque, presented new challenges. The ANYmal exhibited less smooth dynamics, leading to exponential increases in gradient norm and instability when backpropagating FoGs over more than 16 steps (Figure 5). A short horizon length of 12 steps was chosen to facilitate stable optimization.
- **Successful Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A locomotion policy for velocity tracking, trained entirely within the differentiable simulator using the proposed analytically smoothed contact model, successfully transferred zero-shot to a real ANYmal quadrupedal robot.
- **High Fidelity Matching:** The measured velocity trajectory on the real robot closely matched the trajectory recorded in simulation (Figure 6), validating the accuracy of the differentiable simulator and the transfer capabilities of the learned policy.
- **Robustness:** The learned control policy demonstrated robustness against external force perturbations during real-world execution, as evidenced in the supplementary video.
- **Joint Trajectory Analysis:** Joint position trajectories for the ANYmal robot showed a close match between real-world execution and simulations using both smoothed and hard contact (Figure 8), further confirming the successful sim-to-real transfer and that the smoothed contact model captures the essential dynamics of hard contact. The smooth and periodic nature of the trajectories indicated a stable learned gait.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and differentiable simulation, particularly for contact-rich tasks like legged locomotion.

The primary significance of this work lies in achieving the **first reported successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a legged locomotion policy learned entirely within a differentiable simulator leveraging its analytic gradients**. This outcome addresses a long-standing challenge in the application of differentiable simulation to real-world robotics.

The potential impacts are multifaceted:

1. **Validation of FoG-based Optimization for Real-World Robotics:** By demonstrating successful real-world deployment, this research validates the feasibility and practical utility of using first-order gradient (FoG)-based optimization for complex, contact-rich robotic tasks. This moves differentiable simulation beyond theoretical benefits and simulated-only results into tangible hardware application.

2. **Enabling Sample-Efficient Robot Learning:** The demonstrated superior sample efficiency of SHAC (an FoG-based method) over PPO (a ZoG-based method) suggests a pathway towards significantly reducing the amount of data required to train complex robotic control policies. This is particularly impactful for real-world scenarios where data collection is often expensive, time-consuming, or even dangerous.

3. **Advancement in Differentiable Contact Modeling:** The proposed analytically smoothed contact model is a key enabling technology. It effectively resolves the trade-off between gradient informativeness (for optimization) and physical fidelity (for real-world transfer) that has hindered previous differentiable simulators. This model could be adopted in other differentiable simulation frameworks to enhance their capabilities for contact-rich interactions.

4. **Broader Applicability to High-Dimensional Systems:** The paper highlights that the benefits of FoG-based optimization, particularly in terms of sample efficiency, are expected to be even more pronounced for systems with high-dimensional action spaces (e.g., humanoids) or observation spaces (e.g., learning from vision). This work establishes a foundation for scaling differentiable learning to these more complex and currently challenging robotics domains.

5. **Simplified Development Cycle:** By enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, this approach could streamline the development cycle for robotic behaviors, reducing the need for extensive real-world fine-tuning or sophisticated domain adaptation techniques typically required with non-differentiable simulators.

In conclusion, this research establishes a robust methodology for leveraging the power of differentiable simulation to learn physically realistic and deployable locomotion policies, paving the way for more efficient and effective development of advanced robotic capabilities.
