# Cloak: Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Manipulation by Masking the End-Effector from the VLA

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University introduce Cloak, a training recipe that enables zero-shot cross-embodiment manipulation for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by masking the end-effector from wrist camera views. This method facilitates direct transfer from a single source parallel-jaw gripper to diverse, unseen end-effectors, including multi-fingered hands, while maintaining high task progression rates.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation policies trained on specific hardware (e.g., a parallel-jaw gripper) experience significant performance degradation when deployed on different embodiments.
- The high cost and labor involved in collecting new training data for each novel robot system impede the scalability and rapid deployment of robotic learning.
- The visual appearance gap of the end-effector in wrist-mounted camera views is a critical barrier to zero-shot transfer, especially with the emergence of complex multi-fingered hands.

## Method

- The **Cloak** method dynamically ==masks the end-effector== in wrist camera inputs using real-time geometric rendering based on the robot's proprioceptive state and camera parameters.
- During training, mask augmentations (capsule attachment, disk removal) are applied to the generated mask to expose the VLA to a broader distribution of end-effector shapes, promoting generalization.
- A masked attention mechanism in the VLA's vision encoder prevents masked patches from contributing to attention computations, ensuring embodiment-agnostic visual reasoning, coupled with tip-pose retargeting for kinematic alignment.

## Results

- Cloak-VLA achieved an average task progression rate of **81.8%** on the five-fingered Sharpa hand, a complex unseen embodiment, significantly outperforming baselines which degraded sharply to **54.4**-**60.2%**.
- The method maintained high performance across other unseen embodiments like the UMI gripper (**85.1%**) and the YAM arm and gripper (**86.3%**), closely matching its source embodiment rate (**88.0%**).
- Ablations demonstrated that mask augmentation was essential, with Cloak-VLA outperforming an unaugmented version (Cloak-VLA-NoAug) by **16.7%** and a version overlaying known target masks (Cloak-VLA-Overlap) by **9.7%** on the Sharpa hand.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly addressing the visual gap in wrist camera views by masking the end-effector is a critical factor for achieving robust zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, outperforming methods focused solely on action interface alignment.
> - Mask augmentation during training is crucial for the policy to generalize to novel, unseen end-effector silhouettes, enabling the model to learn truly embodiment-agnostic features.
> - Masking the end-effector from the wrist view does not significantly degrade performance on the source embodiment, suggesting that the masked regions typically contain little information essential for scene understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is characterized by a diverse range of end-effectors, from simple parallel-jaw grippers to complex multi-fingered hands. Traditionally, models trained on demonstrations collected with a specific robot embodiment exhibit strong performance on that particular hardware but experience significant performance degradation when deployed on different embodiments. This challenge, known as cross-embodiment transfer, is further complicated by the high cost and labor associated with collecting new training data for each novel robotic system. The research addresses this issue, particularly as the community transitions from extensive gripper-centric datasets to the emerging domain of dexterous and humanoid hands.

Prior efforts in developing cross-embodiment policies typically involve scaling data across numerous robots and incorporating representations designed to accommodate their heterogeneity. Examples include methods that combine demonstrations from multiple robot embodiments, such as Open X-Embodiment, OpenVLA, and $\pi_0$. Other approaches employ explicit mechanisms for learning cross-embodiment representations, such as identity embeddings, soft prompts (e.g., GR00T, X-VLA), per-embodiment tokenizers (Octo, CrossFormer, HPT), or padded action vectors with robot-specific slots (RDT-1B, $\pi_0$). While these methods leverage diverse datasets, the resulting policies often remain fundamentally linked to the embodiments present during training, necessitating new data or learned modules for unseen robots.

Some recent works claim zero-shot transfer to novel embodiments. For instance, RDT2 demonstrated transfer across robot arms but maintained the same parallel-jaw gripper as in training. LAP achieved transfer across grippers, but typically between parallel-jaw types with minimal visual variation. These methods often do not fundamentally address the visual appearance gap of the end-effector in wrist-mounted camera views, which the authors identify as a critical barrier to true zero-shot transfer.

A complementary line of research has focused on addressing the visual gap directly by modifying the robot's appearance in images. Approaches include repainting techniques, where generative models replace an unseen target robot with a render of a source robot (Mirage), or synthesize alternative robots. However, these methods can suffer from issues such as hallucination, realism discrepancies, and limitations in real-time control. Other strategies involve removing the robot's view entirely. Shadow, for example, overlays a composite silhouette of source and target robots during training, but requires prior knowledge of the target embodiment. ARRO and RoboEngine utilize learned segmentation for masking, which can be unreliable, mislabel objects, or degrade on thin structures.

Research specifically on cross-embodiment dexterous manipulation has largely concentrated on control and morphology. Techniques like fingertip and keypoint retargeting (e.g., DexPilot, AnyTeleop) enable teleoperation across different hands. Other works learn policies that transfer across morphologies using shared action spaces or canonical representations (e.g., CrossDex, One-Hand-to-Rule-Them-All, DexFormer, One-Policy-Fits-All). While these address kinematic and control gaps, they often leave the visual gap unaddressed. DexUMI tackles the dexterous visual gap by removing human hands from wrist video and compositing robot hands, but this is for data collection and involves segmentation, inpainting, and physical replay.

The current work, Cloak, distinguishes itself by directly tackling the visual gap of wrist-mounted cameras through a geometry-based masking approach. It aims to enable zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer from a single source gripper to diverse, unseen end-effectors, including multi-fingered hands, without relying on generative models or prior knowledge of the target's silhouette.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that allows a robotic manipulation policy, trained on a single robot embodiment, to transfer zero-shot to new, previously unseen end-effectors and robot arms. This transfer should occur without requiring new data collection or retraining on the target embodiments.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges in current robotic manipulation research:
- **Hardware Fragmentation:** The diverse and rapidly evolving landscape of robotic end-effectors (from grippers to dexterous hands) creates a fragmentation of manipulation capabilities. Policies trained on one type of hardware often do not generalize to others.
- **Cost of Data Collection:** Acquiring large datasets of demonstrations for each new robot embodiment is a time-consuming and expensive process. This cost impedes the rapid deployment of new hardware and the scalability of robotic learning.
- **Visual and Kinematic Gaps:** When transferring a policy from a source embodiment to a target embodiment, two primary discrepancies arise:
 - **Kinematic Mismatch:** Differences in joint configurations, link geometries, and degrees of freedom. The authors note this can often be addressed through structured geometric approaches like inverse kinematics (IK).
 - **Visual Gap:** Disparities in the appearance of the end-effector within its own wrist-mounted camera view. This is identified as the more significant and "binding constraint" on zero-shot transfer. A policy trained on a specific end-effector's appearance in its wrist camera becomes accustomed to those visual features. Even minor deviations in a target end-effector's appearance can cause observations to fall out of the training distribution, leading to performance degradation. This problem is particularly pronounced with wrist cameras, where the end-effector consistently occupies a substantial portion of the frame, and is exacerbated by the increasing complexity and articulation of multi-fingered hands compared to simpler grippers.
- **Limitations of Existing Solutions:** Previous attempts to close the visual gap, such as generative models for image repainting, introduce issues like hallucination and are unsuitable for real-time control. Other masking approaches either require prior knowledge of the target embodiment or rely on learned segmentation which can be unreliable.

The research aims to overcome these limitations by proposing a method that decouples visual reasoning from the specific appearance of the end-effector. By effectively "cloaking" the end-effector from the robot's own wrist camera, the policy is intended to learn embodiment-agnostic visual features, thereby enabling robust zero-shot transfer. The focus is specifically on addressing observation gaps, rather than complex morphology-specific dexterous skills, by employing simple manipulation tasks achievable by placing two tips.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Cloak method is a training recipe designed to facilitate zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. It operates by "cloaking" the end-effector from its wrist camera view, thereby promoting embodiment-agnostic visual reasoning. The approach is demonstrated with Cloak-VLA, a VLA finetuned from a $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint on a single parallel-jaw gripper dataset. The methodology integrates geometric masking with mask augmentations during training and tip-pose retargeting for kinematic alignment.

#### 3.1. Embodiment Masking

The core of Cloak involves dynamically masking the end-effector in the wrist camera's visual input. This process consists of construction, augmentation, and image filling.

- **Mask Construction:**
 - A geometric mask of the end-effector is rendered in real-time. This is achieved by using the robot's known proprioceptive state (joint positions), the wrist camera's intrinsic parameters, and the robot's description file (which contains 3D link meshes).
 - The wrist camera's pose in the end-effector frame ($T_{ec}$) is refined per episode using a "Silhouette Calibration" procedure. This involves a zeroth-order optimization (Nelder–Mead) to minimize the inverse Intersection over Union (IoU) between a rendered mask and a pseudo-ground-truth mask. The pseudo-ground-truth mask is derived from gripper-open frames by identifying pixels that are both consistently dark (low temporal median intensity) and rigid (low temporal standard deviation).
 - Forward kinematics (FK) is used to determine the world pose of each link mesh based on the robot's proprioceptive state. These meshes are then projected into the camera frame and rasterized to create a binary mask.
 - A small morphological dilation is applied to the generated mask to account for minor residual calibration errors.

- **Mask Augmentation (Training Only):**
 - To enable the policy to generalize to end-effector silhouettes unseen during training, the constructed mask is augmented. This involves two steps:
 - **Capsule Attachment:** Randomly sized and oriented capsules are added to the contour of the mask. This distorts the source embodiment's silhouette, exposing the model to a broader distribution of potential end-effector shapes.
 - **Disk Removal:** Randomly sized disks are subtracted from the interior of the augmented mask. This further alters the silhouette and ensures the VLA encounters patch positional embeddings that might otherwise be consistently covered.

- **Filling Body Pixels:**
 - To prevent the visual encoder from seeing the original end-effector pixels in regions that are unmasked due to augmentation or partial patch coverage, the masked area in the wrist image is filled. Instead of using a solid color, random noise, or a generative model, the authors simply inpaint a rolled copy of the same image onto the masked region. This maintains the local pixel distribution and avoids introducing out-of-distribution artifacts.

#### 3.2. Masked Attention in the Vision Encoder

The augmented wrist image and its corresponding mask are fed into the VLA's vision encoder (a ViT-style transformer).
- The image-resolution mask is downsampled to the patch grid resolution of the vision encoder (e.g., 224x224 image to 16x16 patches for a 14x14 pixel patch size) by majority vote.
- In the self-attention layers of the ViT, attention scores for patches identified as masked are biased to negative infinity. This ensures that no query can "read" information from a masked patch, effectively cloaking the end-effector.
- The masked patches retain their sequence slots but do not contribute to the attention computation for other tokens. This mask is also propagated to the VLA backbone to gate masked patch tokens out of downstream cross-modal attention with language and state.
- The external camera view is not masked, as it is considered to contribute minimally to the visual embodiment gap.

#### 3.3. Tip-Pose Retargeting

Since Cloak-VLA's inputs and outputs are designed to remain in the source robot's joint space, a mechanism is required to translate between the source and target embodiments' kinematic configurations. This is achieved through tip-pose retargeting, which leverages forward kinematics (FK) and inverse kinematics (IK).
- **Action Output Translation (src2trg):** When the VLA outputs a source joint action (e.g., 7 DoF arm + 1 DoF gripper), FK is used to compute the 6-DoF poses of two designated "tips" on the source end-effector. Inverse kinematics is then employed to find the corresponding target embodiment joint actions that achieve these same tip poses. For multi-fingered hands, the gripper signal from the source policy is used to linearly interpolate between predefined open and closed hand configurations.
- **State Input Translation (trg2src):** To provide the VLA with a state input in the source embodiment's joint space, FK is first applied to the target robot's current joint state to determine its tip poses. IK is then used to find a source embodiment joint configuration that matches these target tip poses. The source's gripper opening is derived analytically from the distance between the target tip positions.
- **IK Regularization:** Inverse kinematics objectives include regularizing terms (e.g., for wrist orientation, damping, and posture) to prevent instability in null-space solutions and guide the solver towards stable arm configurations, while allowing flexibility in finger movement.

#### 3.4. Training

Cloak-VLA is trained by finetuning a pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint. The training data consists of tuples including wrist images, external camera views, proprioceptive state, language instructions, and action chunks, all in the source robot's native joint space (8 DoF for the DROID dataset). The embodiment mask is pre-computed offline, and the mask augmentation and body pixel filling steps are applied online during each training step. Training is performed on a filtered version of the DROID dataset, encompassing approximately 70,000 successful episodes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate Cloak-VLA's zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer capabilities and to ascertain the role of the masking strategy and augmentations. Evaluation was conducted on four common manipulation tasks: "Pick and Place," "Remove," "Move," and "Fold/Unfold." Four embodiments were used for deployment: the source Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, an unseen UMI gripper, an unseen YAM arm and gripper, and an unseen Sharpa five-fingered humanoid hand. The primary metric was task progression rate, with success rate also reported.

#### 4.1. Source Embodiment Performance (Q1)

On the source Robotiq gripper, Cloak-VLA exhibited comparable average progression rates to the $\pi_{0.5}$-droid baseline (88.0% vs. 89.3%). This indicates that masking the end-effector from the wrist view does not significantly degrade performance on the embodiment it was trained on. The masked regions typically contain little information critical to scene understanding. However, an ablation with excessive masking (Cloak-VLA-Overlap) resulted in a slight performance drop (81.6%), suggesting that over-masking can lead to a loss of useful scene information.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Transfer to Unseen Embodiments (Q2, Q3)

Cloak-VLA demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer across all three unseen embodiments:
- **UMI Gripper:** Cloak-VLA achieved an average progression rate of 85.1%, maintaining performance close to its source embodiment rate (88.0%). In contrast, $\pi_{0.5}$-droid + TP retargeting (69.0%) and LAP-VLA (60.9%) experienced significant performance drops.
- **YAM Arm and Gripper:** Cloak-VLA achieved 86.3% progression, again close to source performance. Baselines $\pi_{0.5}$-droid + TP retargeting (69.5%) and LAP-VLA (68.5%) performed considerably worse. This result is notable as the YAM arm represents a change in the entire robot platform, not just the end-effector.
- **Sharpa Hand:** The most pronounced difference was observed with the five-fingered Sharpa hand. Cloak-VLA maintained a high progression rate of 81.8%, while $\pi_{0.5}$-droid + TP retargeting degraded sharply to 54.4%, and LAP-VLA to 60.2%.

These results indicate that Cloak effectively enables zero-shot transfer to a variety of unseen embodiments, including a significantly different multi-fingered hand. The substantial performance gap between Cloak-VLA and baselines that either lack wrist masking ($\pi_{0.5}$-droid) or align embodiments at the language layer (LAP-VLA) suggests that directly addressing the visual gap in the wrist camera view is a critical factor for achieving robust zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer.

#### 4.3. Mask Augmentation Ablation (Q4)

To assess the importance of the mask augmentation strategy:
- **Cloak-VLA (with augmentation)** consistently outperformed **Cloak-VLA-NoAug** (trained on unaugmented source mask) and **Cloak-VLA-Overlap** (overlays known target masks during training).
- For example, on the Sharpa hand, Cloak-VLA achieved 81.8% progression, compared to 65.1% for Cloak-VLA-NoAug and 72.1% for Cloak-VLA-Overlap.
- This indicates that mask augmentation is necessary for generalization and that augmenting the source silhouette provides better transfer capabilities than merely overlaying a fixed, known target mask. The strategy of exposing the model to a distribution of end-effector silhouettes during training appears to enhance its robustness to novel, unseen shapes.

#### 4.4. Success Rate

The success rate metric, which provides no partial credit for incomplete subtasks, generally mirrored the trends observed with the progression rate. The performance gap between Cloak-VLA and the baselines was often more pronounced in success rate, reinforcing Cloak's effectiveness in achieving task completion on unseen embodiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Cloak method represents a contribution towards achieving zero-shot cross-embodiment manipulation. By strategically masking the end-effector from the wrist-mounted camera view, the approach enables a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model to develop visual and spatial reasoning capabilities that are largely independent of the specific robot hardware.

### 6. Significance

- **Decoupling Skill from Hardware:** The work demonstrates a practical method for decoupling manipulation skill from the specific physical body executing it. This suggests a pathway for creating more generalist robotic policies that are less tethered to a particular embodiment's visual appearance.
- **Enhanced Data Longevity and Reusability:** A significant implication is that manipulation data collected on one end-effector can be more effectively repurposed for entirely different, future hardware. This mitigates the problem of data obsolescence in a rapidly evolving hardware landscape, potentially reducing the need for extensive, costly data collection campaigns for every new robot design.
- **Strong Initialization for New Embodiments:** The resulting body-agnostic model can serve as a robust initial policy. For scenarios where some target-specific data can be collected, this provides a strong starting point for finetuning or continual learning, potentially requiring fewer new demonstrations to achieve high performance on a novel embodiment.
- **Addressing the Visual Gap:** The research provides empirical evidence that directly addressing the visual appearance gap of the end-effector in wrist camera views is a critical enabler for zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, particularly in comparison to methods focusing solely on action interface alignment.

**Potential Impact:**
- The methodology could accelerate the development and deployment of new robotic hardware by reducing the associated software development costs.
- It may foster the creation of more versatile and adaptive robotic systems, capable of performing tasks across a wider array of platforms without extensive re-engineering.
- The approach's reliance on geometric rendering rather than generative models offers advantages in terms of real-time performance and reduced risk of visual hallucination, which are crucial for reliable control applications.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Skill Repertoire:** The current approach primarily covers manipulation tasks expressible through two fingertips, such as picking, placing, and simple folding. It does not extend to more complex dexterous skills requiring rich contact interactions or in-hand reorientation. The learned behaviors are constrained by the source model and the gripper-style tabletop data used (DROID dataset).
- **Tip-Pose Retargeting Tradeoffs:** While effective, the tip-pose retargeting mechanism, particularly the inverse kinematics (IK) regularization objectives, can suppress certain desirable tilting behaviors in the robot arm to ensure stability in null-space solutions. This represents a tradeoff that might limit generalization for certain tasks or require per-embodiment tuning to relax.
- **Residual Gap:** Despite the strong zero-shot performance, transfer is not entirely lossless. The authors suggest that closing this residual performance gap with a small number of target-specific demonstrations from the embodiment-agnostic checkpoint is a logical next step for future research.
- Further work could explore how to expand the range of transferable skills, improve the robustness and flexibility of the kinematic retargeting, and integrate few-shot learning strategies to refine performance on novel embodiments.
