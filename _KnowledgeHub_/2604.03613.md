# Human-Robot Copilot for Data-Efficient Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC San Diego developed a Human-Robot Copilot framework that enables data-efficient imitation learning by balancing dexterous teleoperation with broad robot platform compatibility. The system integrates a low-cost, heterogeneous teleoperation device with human-in-the-loop policy fine-tuning, achieving up to an 86% success rate in precision tasks like Tower of Hanoi Insertion and significantly reducing data collection time compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Imitation learning policies frequently fail when encountering 'out-of-distribution' (OOD) states during deployment, leading to poor generalization.
- Existing human-in-the-loop (HIL) robot learning systems struggle to balance fine-grained control dexterity with broad compatibility across diverse robot manipulators.
- Collecting high-quality human demonstrations for robot tasks is often time-consuming and labor-intensive, especially for addressing specific failure modes.

## Method

- A heterogeneous teleoperation system utilizes an inverse kinematics (IK) solver to decouple the kinematics of a compact leader device from the follower robot, enabling cross-embodiment control.
- A bidirectional control logic ensures continuous synchronization between the leader and follower arms, maintaining the human operator's proprioceptive awareness during transitions between autonomous and teleoperated modes.
- An adjustable control scaling factor allows operators to switch between large-scale movements and high-precision corrective actions, enhancing control for delicate task segments, combined with clip-based batch fine-tuning for data efficiency.

## Results

- The system achieved a **86%** success rate in the real-world Tower of Hanoi Insertion task (50 trajectories), outperforming the base policy (**60%**) and other HIL interfaces (e.g., VR **74%**).
- Data collection time for corrective interventions was substantially reduced, for instance, requiring **252.9** seconds for Cube Sorting compared to **334.7** seconds with a keyboard interface.
- A smaller control scaling factor (α=**0.5**) reduced end-effector RMS deviations from **6.5** mm (lateral) and **4.3** mm (forward) to **2.4** mm in both directions during precision alignment in the Tower of Hanoi task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Targeted, clip-based corrective human interventions are more efficient for improving policy robustness and addressing OOD states than collecting full trajectories.
> - Bidirectional synchronization between a heterogeneous teleoperation device and the robot maintains human proprioceptive awareness, enabling intuitive and seamless control transitions.
> - Adjustable control scaling for teleoperation significantly enhances precision for delicate tasks, leading to more accurate human corrections and better training data for policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of task-specific skills by robots often relies on human demonstrations collected through teleoperation. While effective for initial policy bootstrapping, imitation learning policies frequently encounter "out-of-distribution" (OOD) states during deployment due to compounding errors or environmental variability. These OOD states lead to policy failure as the robot struggles to generalize appropriate actions from limited initial data.

Current research efforts to address this limitation often involve human-in-the-loop (HIL) or interactive imitation learning paradigms, commonly following a Human-Gated DAgger (HG-DAgger) approach. In this paradigm, a human intervenes during policy execution to provide corrective demonstrations when the robot fails, which are then used to augment the training dataset.

Existing HIL systems exhibit limitations in balancing dexterity (fine-grained control) and generality (compatibility with diverse robot platforms). Some approaches, like Sirius, employ general-purpose input devices such as space mice or VR controllers. These devices offer cross-embodiment control by applying uniform translational or rotational adjustments, but their delta-control nature can be unintuitive for complex, precise refinements, lacking a fixed spatial reference and often leading to higher variance and jerk in demonstrations. Conversely, methods like Robo-Copilot utilize dual-robot setups with joint-space mirroring, offering intuitive one-to-one control. However, these systems demand isomorphic or proportionally scaled kinematics between the leader and follower robots, significantly restricting their applicability to new robot platforms and necessitating hardware redesign for different manipulators.

This tension between achieving fine-grained control and maintaining broad compatibility across heterogeneous robot platforms represents a significant challenge in the field. The presented work aims to bridge this gap by developing a framework that facilitates dexterous teleoperation while remaining compatible with a wide range of manipulators, thereby improving data efficiency and robustness in human-robot collaboration for imitation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and data-efficient human-robot copilot framework for imitation learning that overcomes the limitations of existing human-in-the-loop (HIL) systems, particularly concerning the trade-off between dexterity and generality.

The key motivations driving this work include:

- **Addressing Out-of-Distribution (OOD) State Failures:** Standard imitation learning policies are prone to failure when encountering states not well-represented in the initial, limited demonstration dataset. The authors aim to enable targeted human intervention to collect corrective data specifically for these failure modes.
- **Balancing Dexterity and Generality:** Current HIL methods struggle to simultaneously provide fine-grained control for complex tasks and maintain compatibility with a broad array of robot manipulators. The motivation is to design a system that supports dexterous, high-precision teleoperation without requiring kinematic similarity between the human interface and the robot arm.
- **Improving Data Efficiency:** Collecting full, high-quality human demonstrations can be time-consuming and labor-intensive. The research is motivated by the desire to reduce the total data collection time by focusing human input on critical failure cases and leveraging clip-based data augmentation.
- **Facilitating Intuitive Control:** Existing cross-embodiment teleoperation devices often use unintuitive delta control, making precision tasks challenging. The authors seek to create an interface that provides a more intuitive control experience, akin to direct mapping, while still allowing for kinematic heterogeneity.
- **Enabling Seamless Human-Robot Collaboration:** The framework aims to allow fluid transitions between autonomous policy execution and human teleoperation, ensuring that the human operator can intervene effectively without losing proprioceptive awareness of the robot's state.
- **Promoting Accessibility and Reproducibility:** A motivation is to provide an integrated, low-cost, and open-source solution that can be easily adopted and reproduced by other researchers and practitioners, thereby accelerating progress in robot learning.

To achieve these objectives, the researchers propose a framework that integrates a novel heterogeneous teleoperation system with a human-in-the-loop imitation learning pipeline, emphasizing adjustable control scaling and targeted data collection.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Human-Robot Copilot framework is composed of two primary components: an interactive heterogeneous teleoperation system and a human-in-the-loop imitation learning pipeline.

**A. Teleoperation System for Human-in-the-loop**

The teleoperation system is designed to enable interactive data collection across heterogeneous robot embodiments and at varying control scales.

- **Cross-Embodiment Design:** The system leverages an inverse-kinematics (IK) solver to translate the end-effector pose of a "leader" device into joint positions for a "follower" robot arm. This approach decouples the kinematics of the human interface from the robot, allowing for compatibility with diverse manipulators. The setup requires loading the follower robot's URDF and specifying the end-effector link, with no task-specific calibration beyond defining overlapping reachable workspaces.
- **Leader Device:** The leader device is a compact, low-cost tabletop unit constructed from commercially available components and 3D-printed parts, with an estimated total cost of approximately $1,000. It provides 3-DoF translational force feedback to maximize mechanical transparency and maintain low inertia for dexterous movements. The 3-DoF rotational tracking is achieved purely via an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) without active torque feedback, keeping orientation control lightweight.
- **Bidirectional Control Logic:** The system operates in two main modes:
 - **Initial Data Collection:** The leader arm's motor encoders and IMU signals are read. Forward kinematics (FK) are applied to obtain the end-effector position, combined with IMU rotation for the full pose. This pose is sent to the follower arm's IK solver, and the resulting joint commands are executed. Follower states are treated as observations.
 - **Human-in-the-Loop Fine-tuning:** Teleoperation and policy execution run as parallel channels. The operator can switch between these channels instantly. A critical feature is the bidirectional synchronization: even when the follower executes policy commands, its joint positions are continuously fed back to the leader arm. The follower's joint positions are converted via FK to a Cartesian end-effector pose, then solved through IK to update the leader arm's joint positions. This ensures the leader arm remains aligned with the follower's workspace, maintaining proprioceptive awareness for the human operator and facilitating intuitive control switching and corrective interventions.
- **Control Stabilization and Communication:** Both leader and follower arms employ gravity and friction compensation, driven by low-gain PD controllers in teleoperation mode for compliance. In policy mode, the leader's friction compensation is disabled, and its PD gains are increased for real-time synchronization. Communication between the leader device and the control computer is via USB, while the follower arm uses a wired Ethernet connection (libfranka FCI interface). Local, high-frequency inter-process communication via LCM ensures minimal synchronization delay.

**B. Control Scale Adjustment**

The heterogeneous design allows for flexible adjustment of the teleoperation control scale, catering to both large-scale movements and precision-demanding tasks.

- **Scaling Factor (α):** The positional mapping between the follower end-effector position (x_f) and leader end-effector position (x_l) is defined by the equation: x_f = α(x_l - c_l) + c_t, where c_l is the center of the leader's workspace and c_t is the center of the task workspace.
- **Application:** A larger scaling factor (e.g., α = 2.0) is used for tasks requiring broad movements, like object transfer. A smaller scaling factor (e.g., α = 0.5) is adopted for precision-demanding tasks, such as object insertion.
- **Operator Preference:** The scaling factor is an operator-level preference and can be adjusted without retraining the policy. Fixed scaling factors are used during a task to maintain absolute pose mapping stability during bidirectional mode switching.

**C. Human-in-the-Loop Imitation Learning**

The framework incorporates a human-in-the-loop approach for continuous policy improvement.

- **Workflow:** An initial base policy is trained from a set of human demonstrations. During deployment, the robot executes this policy, but the human operator can intervene via teleoperation when failures occur. Unlike collecting full trajectories, only segments after human takeover, termed "data clips," are recorded. These clips provide targeted supervision for specific failure modes.
- **Policy Architecture:** The Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) is utilized due to its capability for fast iterations, but the network size is reduced (using ResNet-18 as a vision backbone instead of larger alternatives) to enable rapid fine-tuning. The overall pipeline is policy-agnostic, and ACT is chosen for its incremental training support.
- **Fine-tuning Procedure:** New data clips are combined with the original demonstration dataset to fine-tune the policy, preventing catastrophic forgetting of previously learned skills. The smaller network size allows base policy training in approximately 40 minutes and fine-tuning in less than 10 minutes, supporting rapid redeployment and iterative refinement. Algorithm 1 formally describes this clip-based batch fine-tuning process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in both simulation (Robosuite benchmarks) and real-world scenarios, evaluating the framework's ability to identify failure conditions, collect targeted fine-grained demonstrations, and demonstrate data efficiency.

**A. Task Performance**

- **Simulation Results (Table I):** The proposed Human-Robot Copilot consistently achieved higher success rates compared to the base policy on both the Can Pick & Place and Nut Assembly tasks. For instance, in Nut Assembly, the proposed framework achieved an overall success rate of 56% with 40 trajectories, compared to 46% for the base policy using the same number of trajectories.
- **Real-World Results (Table II):** The proposed system generally outperformed the base policy and alternative human-in-the-loop interfaces (Keyboard and VR controllers). For the Cube Sorting task (50 trajectories), the proposed system achieved an overall success rate of 64%, compared to 40% (Base), 48% (Keyboard), and 62% (VR). In the Tower of Hanoi Insertion task (50 trajectories), the proposed system achieved an overall success rate of 86%, significantly higher than 60% (Base), 66% (Keyboard), and 74% (VR). The performance gap was more pronounced in the precision-demanding Tower of Hanoi task, suggesting the advantages of bidirectional leader-follower teleoperation for fine-grained corrections.

**B. Data Collection Efficiency**

- **Reduced Collection Time (Table III):** The proposed framework demonstrated substantially lower data collection times for corrective interventions compared to acquiring full trajectories for the base policy. For instance, in Can Pick-and-Place (20 added trajectories), the proposed system required 189.8 seconds, while the base policy took 501.7 seconds for a comparable number of trajectories. Among human-in-the-loop interfaces, the proposed system achieved the lowest collection times in both real-world tasks (e.g., 252.9 seconds for Cube Sorting vs. 334.7s for Keyboard and 266.9s for VR). This indicates that the bidirectional leader-follower setup enabled more efficient corrective interventions.

**C. Locating Failure Conditions**

The human-in-the-loop deployment process effectively identified specific failure conditions and OOD states where the base policy struggled.
- For **Can Pick & Place**, failures in Stage 2 (placement) were attributed to imprecise grasps in Stage 1, causing object slippage.
- For **Cube Sorting**, the wide randomization of object positions frequently led to OOD states where the robot struggled to grasp or place objects in seldom-visited areas.
- For **Nut Assembly** and **Tower of Hanoi Insertion**, unexpected failures were observed during the picking phase rather than the assembly/insertion phase, despite the latter requiring high precision. For Nut Assembly, the nut rotation procedure sometimes caused unintended shifts, leading to OOD states. For Tower of Hanoi, the disk's near-cylindrical geometry required precise grasping below its widest diameter to prevent slippage. These findings suggest that targeted data augmentation guided by observed failures is more effective than blind data collection.

**D. Fine-grained Correction with Scaling Factors**

The adjustable scaling factor was shown to facilitate precise corrective actions.
- **Tower of Hanoi Insertion (Fig. 6):** In this precision-demanding task, human teleoperators initially achieved only a ~40% success rate with the default scaling factor. When a smaller scaling factor (α = 0.5) was adopted, end-effector trajectories during the alignment phase demonstrated significantly improved precision. With α = 2.0, RMS deviations were 6.5 mm laterally and 4.3 mm forward, characterized by overshooting and oscillations. In contrast, with α = 0.5, a smoother, monotonic approach was observed, resulting in RMS deviations of only 2.4 mm in both lateral and forward directions.
- **Impact on Efficiency:** The use of a smaller scaling factor (α=0.5) led to higher success rates for human corrective demonstrations and fewer attempts, consequently reducing collection time and yielding more concise, effective training data for the policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Human-Robot Copilot framework presents a significant contribution to the field of robot imitation learning, addressing critical challenges in data efficiency, control dexterity, and platform generality.

- **Resolution of Dexterity-Generality Trade-off:** The framework successfully navigates the long-standing challenge of balancing fine-grained control with broad compatibility across diverse robot manipulators. By leveraging inverse kinematics for cross-embodiment control and introducing an adjustable scaling factor, it allows for dexterous teleoperation suitable for high-precision tasks, while maintaining compatibility with a wide range of industrial and research robots.
- **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Robustness:** The human-in-the-loop data augmentation methodology, which involves targeted, clip-based corrective demonstrations, significantly improves policy performance with the same number of trajectories but substantially less data collection time. This approach allows the system to identify and address specific out-of-distribution (OOD) states, leading to more robust policies that can handle unforeseen scenarios during deployment.
- **Practicality for Real-World Deployment:** The integrated design, featuring a low-cost leader device (~$1,000) and an open-source system, enhances the practicality and accessibility of the framework. The fast fine-tuning cycle (under 10 minutes) enables rapid iterative improvement of policies, making it suitable for real-world applications where continuous adaptation is required.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** The bidirectional control logic ensures continuous synchronization between the leader and follower arms, maintaining the human operator's proprioceptive awareness and making seamless switching between autonomous and teleoperated modes intuitive. This enhances the effectiveness and reduces the cognitive load on human supervisors.
- **Broader Adoption in Research and Industry:** The open-source nature and low hardware cost have the potential to democratize access to advanced human-in-the-loop robot learning systems, fostering broader adoption in research laboratories and potentially streamlining the deployment of robot skills in various industrial settings.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge that, similar to other HG-DAgger-style approaches, the strategy for intervention and correction remains a limitation. Current corrections primarily refine existing policy behaviors rather than introducing entirely new execution modes, given the constraints of policy fine-tuning and ACT's multimodal handling limitations. Future work could explore training auxiliary policies on correction data or adopting base policies capable of handling multimodality. Additionally, while fixed scaling factors ensure stability, exploring adaptive scaling mechanisms—such as adjusting the scale based on task phases or force feedback—is identified as a promising direction, provided that workspace drift and mapping offsets can be effectively compensated.
