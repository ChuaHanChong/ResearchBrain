# Beyond Visual Quality: A Study of Test-Time Planning with World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> World action models emit actions alongside predicted visual futures, so an agent can sample several actions from one state and execute the one whose imagined outcome looks best. This study measures that planning potential empirically: an outcome oracle lifts success from 68.9% to 79.2%, while visual-quality, physical-consistency, task-progress and learned-value selectors recover little of it, exposing a recognition gap rather than a generation gap.

## Problem

- World action model predictions are judged by video-quality benchmarks measuring perceptual fidelity and physical plausibility, which never test whether a prediction helps choose the better action.
- Test-time verifiers report gains from reranking sampled actions, but never measure how much selection opportunity a candidate set actually holds, leaving reported gains uninterpretable.
- It is unclear whether actions sampled from one state even reach different task outcomes, so whether reranking generated futures can help at all is unknown.

## Method

- **Best-of-K selection testbed**: a frozen Cosmos Policy samples K paired ==visual futures== and ==action chunks== per state, a scoring rule ranks them, the top action executes, then replans.
- **Outcome oracle** and **local selection headroom**: candidates replay from restored simulator states under a shared continuation policy, so realised terminal success defines an upper bound matched across every selector.
- **Diagnostic decomposition** into ==task consequence diversity==, ==outcome observability== and ==ranking alignment==, probed via counterfactual branching, farthest-point action-spread subsets, and within-state score-versus-outcome AUC on identical candidate sets.

## Results

- Same-state oracle reaches **79.2%** success at K=16 versus **68.9%** uniform selection; trajectory-level pass@K rises from **64.3%** to **77.4%** over **11,520** complete runs.
- Online, learned value reaches **66.8%** and background consistency **66.7%** against a **65.9%** single-sample reference, with task-resampled ranges spanning both negative and positive changes.
- Full-chunk panel gives oracle **59.4%** versus uniform **50.2%** but learned value only **51.6%**; just **6 of 48** early decisions were mixed-outcome, carrying all headroom.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Video-quality metrics are not control metrics; evaluate generated futures by the decisions they enable, and report available opportunity alongside the gain a selector recovers.
> - Wider action spread does not create better alternatives: farthest-point subsets raised numerical spread yet left oracle success unchanged, so diversity is not itself the objective.
> - Most decisions are all-success or all-failure, so reranking value concentrates in rare mixed states, and aggregate success rates hide where a selector actually helps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

World action models (WAMs) generate executable actions alongside visual predictions of what those actions may do. At a decision point, a robot could compare several of these imagined futures and choose the action with the most promising consequence, without retraining the model. The bottleneck is whether those alternatives lead to different task outcomes, and whether a score can recognize the useful one.

The authors execute alternatives from the same recorded state, find out which ones actually succeed, and compare that hindsight answer with what a practical score would choose. In the primary same-state study, a hindsight oracle that knows which candidates actually succeed raises mean success from 68.9% under uniform selection to 79.2% at 16 candidates. This is an offline upper bound, not a deployable selector. The comparison changes only the initial candidate choice.

Success rate versus number of sampled candidates (K = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16) for the oracle, learned value, VLM progress, and SSIM. Coincident curves have equal success. All selectors use identical same-state candidate sets under a common continuation policy.

### 2. Where selection can matter

Cosmos Policy produces paired candidate action sequences and predicted futures. The 48 states come from the first four replanning decisions on three behavior trajectories for each of four tasks. At each state, the study executes the first 16 candidates' initial 16 actions, then follows a common continuation policy until success or the task limit.

Uniform selection's success is the mean fraction of successful candidate continuations at a state. The oracle counts a state as successful if any candidate continuation succeeds. The 10.3-percentage-point difference is therefore local same-state headroom. It is neither whole-episode pass@16 nor a result attainable without knowing outcomes.

The opportunity is concentrated. Only 6 of 48 states have both successful and failed continuations. The other 42 are all-success or all-failure states, where reranking cannot change terminal success. The six mixed states average 17.7% successful candidates.

Four tasks, three trajectories per task, first four decisions per trajectory, 16 candidate continuations per decision; only 6 of 48 are mixed. This is a state-outcome summary, not a map of sensitivity over complete trajectories.

### 3. Scores and wider sampling miss the opportunity

In the practical setting, a rule ranks the paired futures, selects one candidate, and executes its paired actions, while the WAM and sampling setup are held fixed. In the matched K = 16 analysis, learned value, SSIM, and VLM progress select successful continuations at 32, 33, and 32 of 48 states. These rates are 66.7%, 68.8%, and 66.7%, versus 68.9% under uniform selection. These results do not prove below-random ranking. Only six states distinguish rankings, and the outcomes arise from three behavior trajectories. On those six mixed states, outcome AUC is 0.54 for learned value and 0.40 for SSIM and VLM progress, where random ranking scores 0.5.

The authors also test whether wider-spread candidate actions would create more chances to succeed. Greedy farthest-point sampling selects subsets from the same archived pool of 32 candidates at the same 48 states. It increases normalized pairwise action spread. However, at K = 4 oracle success stays 72.92% versus 72.92% for the ordered subset, and the mixed-state fraction stays 6.3%. Because the test reuses an archived bank, it does not evaluate a broader generator.

More numerical spread, no higher oracle coverage than ordered subsets: at K = 4, oracle success is 72.92% for both; at K = 8, it is 77.08% for both. Random rows average 1,000 subsets from the same archived candidates.

A separate phase-spanning, full-chunk evaluation also leaves opportunity unrecovered. That panel changes both decision sampling and execution horizon, so it cannot isolate either change as the cause.

### 4. From images to decisions

A candidate set can contain a successful action even when no tested score identifies it. The authors propose reporting both available outcome-informed opportunity and the gain recovered by a selector, alongside visual quality. The evidence comes primarily from Cosmos Policy in RoboCasa and a small set of recorded decision states. The authors also do not establish whether a successful alternative is hidden by its predicted image or overlooked by the scoring rule.
