# TTCS: Test-Time Curriculum Synthesis for Self-Evolving

> [!summary] Summary
> A co-evolving framework, TTCS, introduces a Synthesizer policy to dynamically generate a curriculum of capability-aligned problem variants for a Solver policy, significantly enhancing large language models' reasoning abilities. This autonomous system improves performance by over 24 points on mathematical benchmarks for a 1.5B parameter model and demonstrates strong generalization across domains.

## Problem

- Existing Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) methods struggle with unreliable pseudo-labels for difficult reasoning tasks, as majority voting often yields incorrect consensus.
- LLMs face a 'lack of learnable samples' problem in TTRL when directly presented with extremely challenging test questions that are beyond their current capabilities, hindering stable learning.
- Many advanced reasoning systems still rely on external human annotations or stronger 'teacher' models, limiting their autonomy and scalability.

## Method

- A co-evolving framework couples a ==Synthesizer policy== (π_ϕ) and a ==Solver policy== (π_θ), both initialized from the same pretrained LLM, engaging in an iterative optimization loop.
- The **TTCS** Synthesizer generates a curriculum of question variants, guided by a ==capability-aware reward== that maximizes learning signal when the Solver is uncertain (self-consistency score ~0.5), and penalized for similarity to encourage diversity.
- The Solver trains on a mixed dataset of original and synthetic questions, using ==self-consistency rewards== and filtering out samples that are either too easy or too hard to focus on the capability frontier.

## Results

- TTCS achieved a **+24.19 point** average accuracy improvement for the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model on mathematical benchmarks, reaching **41.49 points**, and **+20.39 points** over Self-Consistency for the 7B model.
- Demonstrated robust gains on highly challenging problems, e.g., **+6.56 points** over TTRL on AIME24 (1.5B model) and **+5.84 points** on AIME25 (7B model), by providing learnable curriculum.
- Showed strong out-of-domain generalization, with reasoning capabilities transferring positively to MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, and unseen mathematical datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic, capability-aware curriculum synthesis at test-time is critical for stable and efficient self-evolution in LLMs, particularly on challenging reasoning problems.
> - A 'bell-shaped' reward function (peaking at a self-consistency score of 0.5) effectively guides the Synthesizer to generate problems aligned with the Solver's current learning frontier, maximizing the informative signal.
> - The co-evolutionary approach, where both generator and solver adapt iteratively, outperforms static powerful 'teacher' models, highlighting the importance of adaptive curriculum over absolute teacher strength.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has marked a significant shift in artificial intelligence, moving them from mere text generators to increasingly autonomous agents capable of complex planning, acting, and reasoning. This progress has largely been fueled by advancements in **reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)**, particularly effective in challenging domains like mathematics and science. However, RLVR inherently faces a scalability bottleneck: its reliance on extensive, high-quality ground-truth labels for reward computation.

This paper positions itself firmly within the emerging **Self-Evolving** paradigm, which aims to empower models to improve autonomously through self-generated supervision and environmental interactions, thereby reducing the dependency on external human annotations. A prominent instantiation of this paradigm is **Test-Time Training (TTT)**, specifically its reinforcement learning variant, **Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)**. TTRL allows for dynamic parameter adaptation on unlabeled test instances by optimizing a self-supervised objective, typically constructed using majority voting from multiple sampled responses.

TTCS directly addresses two critical limitations identified in existing TTRL approaches when applied to difficult reasoning problems:
1. **Unreliable Pseudo-labels:** For complex tasks (e.g., AIME24 mathematical problems), a majority of sampled responses might be incorrect, leading to a wrong consensus through majority voting. This produces systematically noisy reward signals that can misguide policy updates, reinforcing incorrect reasoning paths.
2. **Lack of Learnable Samples:** TTRL operates directly on a small set of extremely challenging test questions that often lie far beyond the model's current capabilities. Without intermediate, more tractable variants to bridge this capability gap, the learning process becomes prohibitively steep and unstable.

To overcome these challenges, TTCS draws inspiration from **Curriculum Learning**, a well-established principle that posits solving related, simpler problems can facilitate the mastery of more complex ones. The paper explicitly contrasts its approach with prior works like AlphaProof, which, while demonstrating the potential of TTT for Olympiad-level math, relies on stronger LLMs for data synthesis, thereby limiting its autonomy. TTCS, in contrast, aims for a **fully autonomous self-evolving framework** that eliminates the need for external supervision or stronger "teacher" models. Furthermore, TTCS implicitly addresses the known risk of **model collapse** when models are recursively trained on self-generated data, by focusing on generating high-quality, capability-aligned samples.

By introducing a mechanism for dynamic curriculum synthesis at test-time, TTCS represents a significant step forward in making LLMs more robust, adaptable, and genuinely capable of continuous self-improvement, especially in challenging, label-scarce environments. It integrates ideas from self-play, reinforcement learning, and curriculum design to push the boundaries of LLM reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the TTCS framework is to significantly enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) during test-time training, particularly when confronted with highly challenging problems that typically hinder conventional adaptation methods. The core motivation stems from identifying and directly addressing the critical limitations of existing Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) approaches.

The primary motivations for proposing TTCS are:

1. **Mitigating Unreliable Pseudo-labels on Difficult Problems:** Traditional TTRL relies on self-generated supervision, often via majority voting from multiple sampled responses to create pseudo-labels. For exceptionally difficult reasoning tasks, the model's initial performance might be low, leading to a scenario where the majority of sampled responses are incorrect. Consequently, the "consensus" derived from majority voting becomes a systematically noisy and misleading signal. This actively misguides the LLM during training, reinforcing erroneous reasoning paths instead of correcting them. TTCS aims to provide a more reliable source of supervision.
2. **Bridging the Capability Gap with Learnable Samples:** When LLMs are faced with a small set of extremely challenging test questions, their learning process becomes akin to trying to climb a wall that is too steep. Without intermediate steps or problems that are "just right" in terms of difficulty, the model struggles to make meaningful progress. Existing TTRL methods operate directly on these intractable test questions, leading to an "optimization lacks learnable samples" problem. TTCS is motivated by the fundamental insight from curriculum learning: by solving progressively related, more tractable variants, a model can gradually master complex problems.
3. **Enabling Fully Autonomous Self-Evolution:** Many advanced reasoning systems, even those employing self-play or test-time adaptation, often rely on external components or stronger "teacher" models for generating high-quality training data or guiding the learning process. A key objective of TTCS is to achieve fully autonomous self-evolving capabilities, where the model can improve itself without any external supervision, ground-truth labels, or even a pre-existing stronger model for data synthesis. This enables greater scalability and independence.

In summary, TTCS aims to transform the unstable and often misdirected learning of TTRL on hard problems into a reliable pathway for self-evolution. This is achieved by actively constructing a dynamic, problem-centered curriculum of diverse and solvable question variants that are carefully tailored to the solver's current capabilities, thereby ensuring that the model always has access to informative and actionable learning signals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TTCS (Test-Time Curriculum Synthesis for Self-Evolving) is a co-evolving test-time training framework that couples two distinct, yet intimately linked, agents: a **Synthesizer policy (π_ϕ)** and a **Solver policy (π_θ)**. Both agents are initialized from the *same pretrained LLM*, emphasizing the framework's commitment to fully autonomous self-evolution without relying on external stronger models. The core of TTCS lies in an iterative optimization loop, driven by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), where the two policies continuously refine each other.

#### 3.1. Co-Evolutionary Loop Overview

The framework operates in an iterative fashion, with each agent playing a crucial role in the other's development:
- The **Synthesizer** generates curriculum question variants conditioned on the original test questions. It is trained to produce questions that preserve the underlying reasoning structure while varying surface-level details, and, critically, to align these questions with the Solver's current capability frontier.
- The **Solver** performs online self-evolving by training on a mixed dataset comprising both original test questions and the synthetically generated questions. Its performance and uncertainty on these questions provide implicit feedback that guides the Synthesizer's generation process.

This closed-loop interaction ensures that the Solver always receives relevant, challenging-but-solvable training data, while the Synthesizer continuously adapts its generation strategy to maximize the Solver's learning efficiency. Both policies are updated using GRPO, a robust reinforcement learning algorithm suitable for stable online adaptation in label-free settings.

#### 3.2. Capability-Aware Synthesizer Training

The Synthesizer's role is to create a structured and localized curriculum of question variants around each original test question. This is achieved through two main components:

1. **Test Questions Guided Synthesis:**
 - For each test question (x_test), the Synthesizer (π_ϕ) generates M auxiliary question variants ({x'_i}).
 - A carefully designed prompt template (detailed in Appendix D) guides this generation, ensuring that synthetic questions preserve the *underlying reasoning structure* of x_test while introducing *surface-level variations* (e.g., changes in problem objects, settings, or constraint types). This approach aims to create focused training signals that are distributionally aligned with the reference questions.

2. **Question Quality Assessment Reward:**
 - The Synthesizer is trained using a composite reward signal (R(x'_i)) that leverages the Solver (π_θ) as an online assessor of question quality. This reward reflects two complementary objectives:
 - **Capability-Adaptive Reward (R_cap):** This component is crucial for aligning synthetic questions with the Solver's current capabilities. For each synthetic question x'_i, the Solver samples K reasoning responses, and its self-consistency score s(x'_i) (the proportion of responses matching the majority vote) is computed. The reward R_cap(x'_i) is formulated as `(4 * s(x'_i) * (1 - s(x'_i)))^γ`.
 - **Theoretical Basis (Appendix C.1):** This specific "bell-shaped" reward function maximizes its value when s(x'_i) is approximately 0.5. A self-consistency score of 0.5 indicates that the Solver is uncertain about the answer (i.e., it can neither consistently solve nor consistently fail the problem). This regime corresponds to the Solver's "capability frontier," where the learning signal (variance of advantages in GRPO) is maximized, thus driving the most informative policy updates. The exponent γ controls the curvature, sharpening the focus on this frontier.
 - **Similarity Penalty Reward (R_sim):** To encourage novelty and prevent mode collapse (e.g., the Synthesizer repeatedly generating identical or trivial variations), this reward penalizes:
 - `R_ref(x'_i, x_test)`: Similarity to the *original test question* (based on rule-based metrics like edit distance, Jaccard similarity, and skeleton similarity, as detailed in Appendix C.2).
 - `R_group(x'_i, {x'_j'})`: Redundancy *among other synthetic questions* within the same rollout group (measured using BLEU-based distance and agglomerative clustering, as in Appendix C.3).
 - **Final Reward Formulation:** The overall reward for the Synthesizer is `R(x'_i) = I_valid(x'_i) * max(0, R_cap(x'_i) - R_sim(x'_i))`. `I_valid` ensures basic format compliance. This reward guides the Synthesizer via GRPO to generate diverse, capability-aligned, and informative question variants.

#### 3.3. Online Self-Evolving for Solver

The Solver's training is designed to continuously adapt its policy using the curriculum provided by the Synthesizer:

1. **Training Data Construction at Test Time:**
 - At each iteration, the Solver's training batch `B_train` is constructed as a mixture: `B_test` (sampled original test questions, with replacement to manage ratio) and `B_syn` (synthetic questions generated by the *previous* Synthesizer's policy, π^(t-1)_ϕ).
 - This mixed data ensures the Solver remains grounded in the target domain while exploring the learned curriculum. Resampling test questions is crucial to prevent the self-generated data from dominating and potentially leading to model collapse.

2. **Self-Consistency Reward for Solver:**
 - For each question `x` in `B_train`, the Solver (π^(t)_θ) generates G responses.
 - A **pseudo-label (ŷ*)** is derived via majority voting from these G responses.
 - Each generated response `y_i` receives a binary reward `r(y_i, ŷ*)` (1 if it matches the pseudo-label, 0 otherwise).
 - The **sample consistency score s(x)** (average of these binary rewards) quantifies the model's confidence in its own consensus.

3. **Online Data Filtering:**
 - Crucially, only samples `x` where the consistency score satisfies `|s(x) - 0.5| ≤ δ` are retained for actual policy updates. This filtering mechanism, similar to DAPO, ensures that the Solver primarily trains on questions that are neither too easy (s(x) close to 1) nor too hard (s(x) close to 0), but rather those at its capability frontier where the learning signal is most potent.

By iteratively applying these steps, TTCS creates a dynamic and self-improving system where the Synthesizer adapts its curriculum to the Solver's evolving capabilities, and the Solver, in turn, masters these challenges, leading to enhanced reasoning abilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TTCS consistently demonstrates its superiority over various baselines across a range of benchmarks, underscoring its effectiveness in improving LLM reasoning through test-time curriculum synthesis.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance on Mathematical Benchmarks

- TTCS consistently outperforms all compared baselines (Pretrained model, Self-Consistency, R-Zero, and TTRL) in terms of average accuracy across diverse mathematical benchmarks, including competition-level (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23) and fundamental (MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) datasets.
- For the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model, TTCS achieved a massive average improvement of **+24.19 points** (from 17.30 to 41.49).
- On the Qwen2.5-Math-7B model, TTCS reached an average of **52.54 points**, surpassing Self-Consistency by **+20.39 points** and TTRL by **+4.12 points** (48.42 to 52.54).
- These results validate that TTCS's active test-time training is significantly more effective than passive inference-time scaling or direct TTRL, especially for unlocking reasoning potential in smaller LLMs.

#### 4.2. Strong Gains on Challenging Problems

- The performance difference is particularly stark on highly challenging benchmarks like AIME24 and AIME25, where existing methods often struggle due to unreliable pseudo-labels.
- On AIME24 (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), TTCS achieved **19.79 points**, a substantial **+6.56 point** increase over TTRL's 13.23 points.
- Similarly, on AIME25 (Qwen2.5-Math-7B), TTCS scored **19.90 points**, outperforming TTRL's 14.06 points by **+5.84 points**.
- This confirms TTCS's ability to extract high-quality supervision from synthesized curriculum problems, enabling the model to learn even when target questions are initially beyond its direct reach.

#### 4.3. Generalization to General Domains (Q1)

- Evaluations on general-domain reasoning benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, BBEH) while the model was trained on AIME25 or AIME24 demonstrated strong cross-domain generalization.
- TTCS consistently surpassed the R-Zero baseline and TTRL on MMLU-Pro and SuperGPQA, indicating that the reasoning capabilities acquired during mathematical self-evolution positively transfer to broader reasoning tasks.

#### 4.4. Out-of-Domain Mathematical Generalization (Q2)

- Experiments where the solver was trained on a single mathematical dataset and then evaluated on other *unseen* mathematical benchmarks showed consistent performance gains.
- For example, training solely on MATH-500 (moderate difficulty) still led to substantial improvements on more challenging benchmarks, with AIME24 accuracy increasing from 7.1 points to 12.9 points.
- This suggests that TTCS fosters the acquisition of universal mathematical reasoning logic rather than mere memorization, enabling the model to handle diverse and unseen problems.

#### 4.5. Importance of Co-evolution vs. Static Synthesizer (Q3)

- An ablation study replacing the co-evolving 1.5B Synthesizer with a frozen, significantly stronger Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct model revealed that the *adaptivity of the curriculum is more critical than the absolute strength of the teacher model*.
- The stronger static synthesizer yielded only a limited improvement of +2.66 points, whereas the full TTCS framework achieved a **+5.34 point** gain, effectively doubling the performance boost. This underscores the value of dynamic, capability-aware curriculum generation.

#### 4.6. Efficacy Under Limited Data (Q4)

- TTCS proved highly effective in data-scarce scenarios. With only 10% of the AIME24 test data (3 questions), TTCS boosted accuracy to **13.33 points**, significantly outperforming TTRL's 9.48 points.
- This demonstrates TTCS's capability to amplify limited supervision through curriculum synthesis, ensuring stable self-evolution even with minimal initial test data.

#### 4.7. Ablation Studies

- **Without Synthesizer Training:** Freezing the Synthesizer (using a static pretrained model for question generation) led to consistent performance drops (e.g., -7.50 on AMC23), confirming the necessity of an evolving, adaptive question synthesizer.
- **Without Online Data Filtering:** Removing the consistency-based online data filter resulted in performance declines (e.g., -2.37 on Olympiad), highlighting the importance of training on high-value, capability-aligned samples.
- **Without Diversity Penalties:** Disabling similarity penalties caused performance degradation (e.g., -7.50 on AMC23), indicating that diversity in synthesized questions is crucial to prevent overfitting and ensure broader learning.

#### 4.8. Qualitative Case Studies

- Qualitative analysis on AIME24 cases demonstrated that the Synthesizer progressively improves its question generation quality across iterations. Early iterations showed simpler variations, while later iterations generated questions exhibiting domain transfer, increased structural complexity, and novel problem formulations, validating the curriculum effect.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TTCS framework represents a significant advance in the field of large language model self-improvement, offering a scalable and robust pathway towards enhanced reasoning capabilities, particularly in challenging, data-scarce environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Truly Autonomous Self-Evolution:** By initializing both the Synthesizer and Solver from the *same pretrained model* and establishing a reciprocal co-evolutionary loop, TTCS eliminates the reliance on extensive human-annotated ground-truth labels or external, stronger "teacher" models. This marks a crucial step towards truly autonomous AI systems that can continuously refine their abilities without constant external intervention.

2. **Addressing Core Limitations of Test-Time Training:** TTCS directly tackles the fundamental challenges faced by existing Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL) methods: unreliable pseudo-labels and the lack of learnable samples on difficult problems. By dynamically generating a curriculum of "just right" questions, it converts noisy feedback into reliable supervision, making learning feasible where it previously was not.

3. **Dynamic and Capability-Aware Curriculum Synthesis:** The novel concept of a capability-aware synthesizer that actively constructs a problem-centered curriculum is a powerful contribution. This dynamic adaptation ensures that the learning process is always efficient, focusing the model's effort on its current capability frontier, where the learning signal is most potent. This principle could be extended to other domains where progressive learning is beneficial.

4. **Robustness and Generalizability:** The framework's consistent superior performance across various LLM backbones (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen3-4B-Base), diverse mathematical benchmarks, and its demonstrated generalization to entirely different general-domain reasoning tasks highlight its robustness. This suggests that the refined reasoning logic acquired through TTCS is transferable and not merely a result of overfitting to specific problem types.

5. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The efficacy of TTCS under limited test data is a critical practical advantage. In many real-world scenarios, obtaining large, labeled datasets for fine-tuning or even a substantial number of unlabeled test instances can be prohibitive. TTCS's ability to amplify minimal supervision through intelligent curriculum synthesis makes it highly valuable for practical deployment in resource-constrained settings.

6. **Paving the Way for Advanced Agentic Systems:** The authors explicitly state that they view this work as a "foundational step towards autonomous self-improvement" and plan to extend it to "more useful and practical agentic applications." The ability of LLMs to self-evolve their reasoning capabilities on-the-fly, adapt to new challenges, and generate their own learning materials is a cornerstone for building more sophisticated, adaptable, and intelligent AI agents capable of operating in dynamic and uncertain environments.

In conclusion, TTCS offers an innovative and effective solution to long-standing challenges in LLM training and adaptation. Its impact will likely resonate in the development of more intelligent, autonomous, and efficient AI systems, especially those requiring advanced reasoning in complex, evolving, and data-limited domains.
