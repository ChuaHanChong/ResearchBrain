# ZimaBlue: Evolving Generalizable World Action Models through Scalable Video Pre-training

> [!summary] Summary
> ZimaBlue is a World Action Model framework that turns action-free egocentric video into robot control through a three-stage curriculum: causal embodied video pre-training, cross-embodiment video-action mid-training over a unified 100-dimensional state-action interface, then target-robot post-training. An asynchronous Slow-Fast dual system (5B video DiT, 0.5B action DiT) delivers 30 Hz control, and zero-shot real-robot success rises from 36.1% to 77.8%.

## Problem

- Vision-language-action models acquire perceptual and physical knowledge only from expensive action-labeled trajectories, so they parse instructions yet lack the motor knowledge for unseen manipulation skills.
- Existing WAMs retrofit generic video diffusion backbones tuned for plausible synthesis under descriptive prompts, attending over whole clips instead of causal, instruction-conditioned, intervention-grounded prediction.
- Generative world models put iterative denoising on the closed-loop path, lowering control frequency and opening a reactivity gap on contact-rich tasks needing fast correction.

## Method

- **ZimaBlue**'s three-stage data pyramid: action-free ==causal video pre-training== on 120,000 hours of egocentric and robot video, cross-embodiment video-action mid-training, then target-embodiment post-training, all under one ==flow-matching== objective.
- Unified 100-dimensional ==semantic state-action interface== with chunk-relative end-effector geometry, 6D rotations, validity masks, a three-view canvas, and ==block-causal teacher-forced attention== coupling video and action noise levels.
- Asynchronous ==Slow-Fast dual system== where a frozen 5B Slow ==DiT== exports layer-wise video K/V caches injected into a 0.5B Fast DiT, trained with ==Real-Time Chunking== prefixes and ==DMD step distillation==.

## Results

- Zero-shot real-robot success scales monotonically **36.1%** to **46.1%** to **66.9%** to **77.8%** across 12 held-out tasks, beating DROID-post-trained baselines at **54.4%** and **53.6%**.
- Benchmarks: LIBERO-Plus **86.7%** zero-shot and **92.0%** after fine-tuning, RoboTwin 2.0 **94.5%** average, RoboCasa365 **49.5%** overall with Composite-Unseen doubling the best prior WAM from **7.9%** to **16.5%**.
- Ablations: the dual system lifts overall success **63.9%** to **77.8%** (Perturbed **30.0%** to **57.5%**), while latency drops **449.6 ms** to **145.6 ms** to **33.0 ms**, retaining **75.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action-free video is an economical scaling axis whose payoff concentrates under distribution shift, showing up on perturbed and unseen composite tasks rather than in-distribution imitation.
> - Decoupling helps: a slow world model exported as cached key-value features gives a fast action head world-aware context without generating full future video at control rate.
> - Caution: viewpoint generalization stays bounded by pre-training diversity (58.1% zero-shot versus 95.4% fine-tuned), and residual failures demand progress-aware replanning, not broader visual priors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of general-purpose robotics is currently addressing the challenge of enabling robots to perform a wide variety of tasks in diverse environments. A central obstacle is the acquisition of sufficiently broad physical experience for robust generalization. Traditional methods, relying on action-labeled robot trajectories, are constrained by high collection costs and limited diversity.

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, GR00T, and Gemini Robotics, have shown progress in language-conditioned robot control by leveraging pre-trained vision-language models. These models inherit strong semantic priors and can ground instructions in diverse objects and scenes. However, their generalization capabilities remain bounded by the scope of action-labeled robot data used for policy learning. This limitation suggests that while VLAs may understand semantic instructions, they may lack the explicit spatial, dynamic, and motor knowledge necessary for novel manipulation skills. The underlying bottleneck is that reactive policies often depend on action-supervised robot data not only for control but also for acquiring perceptual and physical knowledge, which could potentially be learned from larger, more accessible video corpora.

World Action Models (WAMs) represent an alternative paradigm that explicitly models how the world will evolve and how a robot should act within that evolving world. By jointly predicting video and actions, WAMs connect visual understanding to the physical consequences of actions, thereby reducing reliance on memorizing labeled trajectories and increasing emphasis on understanding achievable visual changes. This approach is particularly appealing because it can leverage abundant, action-free video data—especially egocentric human videos—which are far more scalable than robot trajectories. These videos contain rich information about object affordances, tool use, contact events, and long-horizon task structures, but they largely lack action labels, making them difficult to integrate into standard VLA training.

Existing WAMs, such as DreamZero and LingBot-VA, often adapt pre-trained video diffusion models, which were originally designed for generic video synthesis. This adaptation introduces several mismatches: generic video models prioritize visual plausibility over action-relevant, instruction-conditioned prediction for robots; they often process entire clips rather than sequential observations for real-time control; and web videos may contain non-physical content that attenuates priors for contact-rich manipulation.

ZimaBlue addresses these limitations by proposing a scalable framework that specializes WAMs through embodied video pre-training, rather than merely retrofitting generic video generators. It focuses on leveraging large-scale, causal embodied video pre-training to improve downstream robotic generalization, while also introducing architectural innovations for practical, real-time control, thereby bridging the gap between large-scale data and deployable robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind ZimaBlue is to overcome the fundamental scaling challenge in robotic manipulation, where robust generalization requires extensive physical experience, but the collection of diverse, action-labeled robot trajectories is prohibitively expensive and inherently limited. The paper identifies egocentric videos as a significantly more scalable source of embodied experience, rich in information about object interactions, contact dynamics, tool use, and long-horizon behaviors across varied environments. The central objective is to effectively convert this abundant yet action-free video experience into practical and generalizable robot control capabilities.

Specific objectives and motivations include:

- **Leveraging Scalable Data**: To tap into the vast and growing reservoir of egocentric human and robot videos, which offer a substantially larger and more diverse source of embodied data compared to action-labeled robot demonstrations. This addresses the "data bottleneck" that constrains the generalization of current VLA models.
- **Improving Generalization through Causal Dynamics**: To enable robots to move beyond memorized trajectories and acquire a deeper understanding of the physical world. By training World Action Models (WAMs) to predict future visual states and corresponding actions, the system learns the causal structure of embodied interactions, enhancing its ability to handle novel tasks, scene layouts, viewpoints, and dynamics.
- **Bridging the Modality Gap**: To establish a structured curriculum that bridges the gap between action-free video data and action-labeled robot control data. This involves initially learning broad visual dynamics from unlabeled videos, then grounding these dynamics with a smaller amount of action-labeled data from heterogeneous robot platforms, and finally specializing the model for a specific target robot.
- **Ensuring Real-Time Control**: To overcome the computational intensity and latency typically associated with generative WAMs. Existing WAMs, often relying on iterative denoising and future visual state prediction, can be too slow for the high-frequency control required in contact-rich tasks. A key objective is to design an architecture that maintains the generalization benefits of a large world model while achieving real-time (e.g., 30 Hz) closed-loop control.
- **Unified Representation for Multi-Embodiment Learning**: To create a standardized interface that allows the model to learn from diverse robot platforms with incompatible control interfaces. This promotes transferable action grounding and prevents overfitting to a single robot's specific action space.
- **Empirical Validation of Scaling Hypothesis**: To provide empirical evidence that scaling causal embodied video pre-training directly translates to improved zero-shot generalization and enhanced performance on challenging manipulation benchmarks, especially on unseen tasks and under environmental shifts.

In essence, ZimaBlue aims to establish a practical paradigm for generalist robotics by demonstrating that a strategic, multi-stage training approach leveraging large-scale, action-free video can endow robots with robust generalization, while an optimized dual-system architecture ensures efficient real-time execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ZimaBlue introduces a scalable framework for learning generalizable World Action Models (WAMs) through a three-stage training curriculum, a unified action representation, and an asynchronous Slow-Fast dual-system architecture.

#### 3.1. Unified Representation

To enable learning from heterogeneous robot platforms, ZimaBlue employs a 100-dimensional semantic state-action space. This unified representation maps diverse native robot interfaces (e.g., Cartesian end-effector commands, bi-manual systems with torso, base, and dexterous-hand controls) into a standardized coordinate system where each dimension retains consistent physical meaning across embodiments.
- **Semantic Slot Layout**: The 100 dimensions cover end-effector position and rotation (6D for rotation), gripper states, arm joints, torso, mobile base, and dexterous hand joints. Each embodiment activates only the slots defined by its physical interface; inactive coordinates are zero-filled and accompanied by a validity mask to exclude them from supervision.
- **Chunk-Relative Action Geometry**: To mitigate issues with absolute coordinate misalignment across robots and scenes, end-effector actions are represented relative to the proprioceptive state at the first frame of each action chunk. Joint targets are similarly defined relative to the chunk anchor.
- **Channel Normalization**: Applied using dataset-wide statistics to normalize relative translation to a `[-1, 1]` range, while 6D rotation representation remains unscaled. Inverse transformation restores physical units at deployment.

#### 3.2. Slow-Fast Dual-System Architecture

ZimaBlue adopts an asynchronous Slow-Fast architecture to reconcile the computational demands of a high-capacity world model with the need for low-latency, real-time control.
- **Slow DiT (5 Billion parameters)**: This branch functions as a high-capacity video-centric world model. It processes RGB observations, proprioceptive state, language instructions, and noisy future video and action tokens. Its primary objective is to model causal visual dynamics by predicting future video latents. Concurrently, it extracts multi-layer Transformer key-value (K/V) features that summarize visual transitions. Auxiliary action supervision is applied during training to enforce semantic alignment between predicted video dynamics and action sequences, though not used for runtime deployment. The Slow branch operates at a lower frequency, providing long-horizon guidance.
- **Fast DiT (0.5 Billion parameters)**: This lightweight branch is dedicated to real-time action generation. It reuses the layer-wise video K/V cache from the Slow branch as conditioning guidance. The Fast DiT also uses updated observation latents, current state tokens, language conditions, and noisy action tokens to predict the final action sequence. This design allows the robot to react immediately to the latest sensory feedback, ahead of the Slow branch's full rollout, while benefiting from its rich spatio-temporal representations.
- **Coupling Mechanism**: The Slow DiT computes self-attention over its unified tokens and caches the K/V features from its video stream at each layer. The Fast DiT's action queries attend to its own representations and cross-attend to these cached Slow video tokens, effectively transferring visual dynamics without requiring explicit generation of full future video sequences.

#### 3.3. Three-Stage Training Pipeline

ZimaBlue employs a three-level data pyramid, progressively adapting a pre-trained text-to-video diffusion transformer to embodied intelligence. This curriculum separates video and action supervision, which are typically entangled in robot learning.
1. **Stage I: Video Pre-training**:
 - **Objective**: Learn general visual dynamics from large-scale egocentric videos without action annotations, adapting generic video generation priors to causal embodied interaction.
 - **Data**: A two-phase curriculum starting with a broad mixture of egocentric human videos, simulated manipulation trajectories, and real robot demonstrations (e.g., EPIC-KITCHENS, Egocentric-100K, DreamDojo, DROID), totaling up to 120,000 hours. This is followed by continued pre-training on a curated, manipulation-centric embodied subset to align with high-quality robotic viewpoints.
 - **Training**: Optimizes only the Slow branch using a unified flow-matching objective applied to visual latents. Action and state streams are null placeholders. A block-causal teacher-forcing attention scheme enables efficient parallel denoising while preserving causal information flow.
2. **Stage II: Video-Action Mid-training**:
 - **Objective**: Ground the pre-trained visual dynamics with cross-embodiment action semantics, making representations rich in motor-relevant information.
 - **Data**: Incorporates real-robot trajectories (e.g., DROID, AgiBot, Galaxea, RoboMIND2-Franka) with synchronized multi-view video, proprioceptive states, future action chunks, and language instructions. Data is projected into the unified 100D state-action space.
 - **Training**: Optimizes the Slow branch with a unified video-action flow-matching objective, jointly predicting future video latents and action chunks. The action loss serves as an auxiliary alignment signal. The Fast branch remains inactive.
3. **Stage III: Video-Action Post-training**:
 - **Objective**: Specialize the model to a target embodiment and introduce the lightweight Fast branch for responsive closed-loop control.
 - **Target-Domain Slow Branch Specialization**: The Slow branch parameters are initialized from the mid-trained checkpoint and fine-tuned on target embodiment data (e.g., DROID for real robots, or specific simulation benchmarks). Embodiment-specific encoders/decoders are attached for unseen environments.
 - **Fast Branch Training**: The Slow branch is frozen. The Fast branch is initialized from the first 12 layers of the Slow DiT (with interpolated weights) and trained using an action-only flow-matching objective. This stage uses Real-Time Chunking (RTC) inference, where Fast receives updated observations and states to predict shifted action chunks, simulating asynchronous replanning for fast control.

#### 3.4. Acceleration Schemes

ZimaBlue integrates three mechanisms for real-time control:
- **Asynchronous Slow-Fast Inference**: Slow and Fast branches operate concurrently at different frequencies. Slow provides lower-frequency, long-horizon world-model guidance (video K/V caches). Fast performs high-frequency action generation using the latest observations and available Slow guidance, without waiting for full Slow rollouts.
- **Diffusion Step Distillation (DMD)**: After post-training, DMD is applied sequentially to the Slow and then the Fast branch, reducing the number of DiT evaluations per rollout/request from eight to two. This compresses the model's inference steps while preserving performance.
- **Torch Compile**: Further optimizes the execution speed of the model during deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ZimaBlue's performance was evaluated through real-robot zero-shot tasks and a suite of simulation benchmarks, demonstrating progressive gains with increased data scaling and the efficacy of its architectural and acceleration designs.

#### 4.1. Zero-Shot Real-Robot Evaluation

Experiments were conducted on a 7-DoF Franka arm platform across 12 zero-shot tasks (8 Standard, 4 Perturbed).
- **Performance Scaling**:
 - A baseline ZimaBlue configuration, initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B and post-trained only on DROID, achieved an average success rate of 36.1%.
 - Adding 6,000 hours of multi-embodiment video-action mid-training improved the average success to 46.1%, showing transferable action grounding, particularly on contact-rich articulated-object manipulation.
 - Further incorporating 60,000 hours of egocentric video pre-training raised the average success to 66.9%. These gains were pronounced on multi-stage tasks requiring visual progress tracking.
 - Scaling video pre-training to 120,000 hours resulted in a total average success rate of 77.8%. This highest configuration achieved 87.9% on the Standard suite and a notable 57.5% on the Perturbed suite, which features dynamic lighting, distractors, and novel appearances. The gains on Perturbed tasks were substantial (22.5 percentage points jump from 60K video to 120K video), indicating improved visual generalization and robustness to observation shifts.
- **Comparison to Baselines**: The full ZimaBlue model (77.8% average success) substantially outperformed $\pi_{0.5}$ (54.4%) and DreamZero (53.6%), which were also post-trained on DROID.
- **Qualitative Analysis**: Stronger ZimaBlue configurations exhibited improved approach geometry, multi-stage task completion, sustained contact, and robustness in cluttered, perturbed scenes. Remaining failures were attributed to difficulties in advancing from correct intermediate states or localized interaction errors.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Task Improvement by Dual-System and Acceleration

An ablation study compared the Slow-only policy with the Slow-Fast Dual-System.
- **Dual-System Performance**: The Slow-Fast Dual-System achieved 77.8% overall success, an improvement of 13.9 percentage points over the Slow-only policy (63.9%). This gain was particularly evident in tasks requiring frequent visual feedback and recovery from changing scene states, and most pronounced in the challenging Perturbed suite (30.0% to 57.5% success).
- **Acceleration**:
 - The Slow policy alone had an end-to-end inference latency of 449.6 ms.
 - The asynchronous Dual-System reduced this to 145.6 ms (a 3.1x speedup).
 - Applying Diffusion Step Distillation (DMD) and Torch compile further reduced latency to 33.0 ms, achieving a 13.6x overall speedup. This accelerated model maintained a 75.0% overall task-macro success rate, a modest decrease of 2.8 percentage points from the undistilled Dual-System.

#### 4.3. Simulation Benchmark Results

ZimaBlue (Slow System only) was evaluated on three simulation benchmarks.
- **LIBERO-Plus**:
 - **Zero-shot Transfer**: Achieved an average success rate of 86.7%, outperforming InternVLA-A1.5. It demonstrated high robustness to initial robot state and lighting variations.
 - **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)**: With task-specific fine-tuning, ZimaBlue improved to 92.0% average success, surpassing CAC-VLA. It achieved top performance across five perturbation types, with camera viewpoint robustness dramatically increasing from 58.1% (zero-shot) to 95.4% (SFT).
- **RoboTwin 2.0**: Achieved 94.7% success in the Clean setting and 94.3% in the Randomized setting, yielding an average of 94.5%. This performance surpassed all compared VLA and WAM methods, demonstrating effective manipulation capability under substantial visual domain randomization with a minimal performance gap (0.4 pp) between Clean and Randomized conditions.
- **RoboCasa365**: Achieved an overall average success rate of 49.5%. It recorded the highest average, Composite-Seen (50.4%), and Composite-Unseen (16.5%) success rates among all WAM methods. Notably, ZimaBlue ranked second overall, only behind Xiaomi-Robotics-1, which leveraged 100,000 hours of *real-robot* data compared to ZimaBlue's 6,000 hours of action-labeled data plus 120,000 hours of video-only data. The model more than doubled the Composite-Unseen success rate of ABot-M0.6 (16.5% vs 7.9%), highlighting the generalization benefits of large-scale video pre-training on novel long-horizon tasks. Qualitative comparisons demonstrated improved referential grounding and subtask execution verification on complex, unseen tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ZimaBlue presents a significant contribution to the field of generalist robotics, particularly by demonstrating a practical and scalable approach to learning generalizable robot policies. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Validating Video as a Scaling Axis**: The work provides empirical evidence that large-scale, action-free egocentric video pre-training is an effective and economical route toward generalizable World Action Models. This suggests that the vast quantities of publicly available video data can be systematically leveraged to address the data scarcity bottleneck in robot learning, moving beyond the limitations of expensive action-labeled robot trajectories.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities**: By integrating extensive video pre-training with action grounding, ZimaBlue demonstrates substantially improved zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks and enhanced robustness to environmental perturbations (e.g., dynamic illumination, novel backgrounds). This indicates that the model acquires richer physical and causal priors, enabling it to better understand and adapt to novel manipulation scenarios. The pronounced gains on "unseen" and "perturbed" tasks across various benchmarks underscore its capacity for broader real-world applicability.
- **Practical Real-Time Robot Control**: The introduction of the asynchronous Slow-Fast dual-system architecture, combined with diffusion step distillation and Torch compilation, addresses a critical limitation of previous generative WAMs: their computational latency. Achieving 33 ms closed-loop control on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 makes ZimaBlue's high-capacity world modeling approach practical for real-time interaction, including contact-rich tasks that demand rapid reactive corrections.
- **Structured and Transferable Learning Framework**: The three-stage training curriculum (video pre-training, multi-embodiment video-action mid-training, and target-robot post-training) provides a systematic methodology for combining diverse data sources. The unified 100-dimensional state-action representation further facilitates learning from heterogeneous robot platforms, promoting knowledge transfer across different robot embodiments.
- **Foundation for Future Generalist Robots**: ZimaBlue's methodology lays groundwork for developing more generalist, physics-aware foundation models for robotics. By demonstrating the efficacy of scaling video data and architecturally supporting its deployment, this work points towards future directions involving even larger data and model scales, integration with higher-level reasoning modules for hierarchical planning, and the development of in-context learning capabilities for rapid skill acquisition.

In conclusion, ZimaBlue offers a viable paradigm for scaling embodied intelligence by effectively learning broadly from videos and acting efficiently through decoupled reasoning and control, thereby advancing the state of the art in generalizable robotic manipulation.
