# Agentic World Modeling: Foundations, Capabilities, Laws, and Beyond

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a "levels × laws" taxonomy for agentic world modeling, categorizing capabilities into Predictor, Simulator, and Evolver levels and world types into physical, digital, social, and scientific regimes. The framework unifies disparate research efforts, providing criteria for decision-usable simulation and autonomous model revision, and highlights the shift from passive prediction to active, adaptable environmental understanding for AI agents.

## Problem

- Conceptual fragmentation of "world model" across diverse AI fields, leading to inconsistent definitions, evaluation practices, and challenges in comparing progress.
- Existing surveys on world models typically lack a coherent progression of capabilities across modalities and domains or a precise framework to analyze large generative models as simulators.
- The role of world modeling in emerging agentic AI applications (e.g., web agents, multi-agent systems) is often underrepresented within unified frameworks.

## Method

- Introduces a comprehensive ==levels × laws taxonomy== organizing world modeling into three levels (==L1 Predictor==, ==L2 Simulator==, ==L3 Evolver==) and four governing-law regimes (physical, digital, social, scientific).
- Defines clear, testable boundary conditions for elevating capabilities, such as ==long-horizon coherence== and ==intervention sensitivity== for L2, and ==evidence-grounded diagnosis== for L3.
- Proposes a shift towards ==decision-centric evaluation metrics== (Action Success Rate, Counterfactual Outcome Deviation) and standardized evaluation protocols.

## Results

- The proposed taxonomy unifies world modeling literature, synthesizing methodologies and challenges across **>100 representative systems**.
- Analysis revealed that **many current systems operate as L1 Predictors**, effective for local dynamics but lacking long-horizon coherence or intervention sensitivity for robust L2 simulation.
- The **L3 Evolver** concept, exemplified by autonomous laboratories in the scientific domain, formalizes the critical role of continuous, evidence-driven model adaptation for self-improving AI agents.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World modeling capabilities can be categorized into a progressive hierarchy: L1 for local prediction, L2 for decision-usable multi-step simulation, and L3 for evidence-driven model revision.
> - The nature of "governing laws" in physical, digital, social, and scientific environments dictates distinct constraints and failure modes that world models must accommodate.
> - Advanced agentic AI requires world models capable not just of prediction, but also reliable counterfactual reasoning, adherence to environmental constraints, and autonomous self-revision based on new evidence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly shifting from text generation to enabling agents to achieve goals through sustained interaction within environments. A central bottleneck for such agentic AI systems is the ability to accurately model environment dynamics, often referred to as "world models." However, the term "world model" carries diverse meanings across different research communities, leading to conceptual fragmentation and difficulties in comparing progress across various domains such as model-based reinforcement learning, video generation, web agents, multi-agent social simulation, and AI-driven scientific discovery.

Existing surveys have attempted to organize this rapidly expanding literature, often by modality (e.g., video generation) or application domain (e.g., autonomous driving, robotics). For instance, some surveys focus on generative capabilities inspired by models like Sora, while others provide roadmaps for visual world modeling or domain-specific frameworks for embodied AI, autonomous driving, or scientific discovery. Complementary surveys address agent planning and reasoning, categorizing mechanisms like decomposition, selection, and reflection. While these contributions are valuable, they tend to partition the field, creating gaps in understanding the progression of capabilities that cut across modalities and domains. They also underrepresent the role of world modeling in agentic AI applications where learned environment dynamics are crucial for planning and action selection.

This work addresses this fragmentation by proposing a unifying conceptual framework. It aims to provide a common language and a systematic taxonomy that aligns diverse communities by organizing the field through a capability-based approach that transcends specific modalities or application domains. This approach seeks to bridge previously isolated research efforts and establish a foundation for understanding and evaluating world models based on their core functional abilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a comprehensive, capability-based taxonomy for agentic world modeling, designed to resolve conceptual fragmentation across disparate research communities. This framework is intended to provide a common language and a structured approach for understanding, comparing, and advancing world models in agentic AI.

The motivation for this work stems from several factors:
- **Conceptual Fragmentation:** The term "world model" is widely used but lacks a precise, universally agreed-upon technical meaning. Different communities evaluate world models based on distinct criteria (e.g., visual fidelity for computer vision, task performance for reinforcement learning), leading to incomparable results and hindering cumulative progress.
- **Need for a Unified Language:** As AI systems move towards more complex, interactive agentic behaviors, the ability to model environment dynamics becomes critical. A unified framework is necessary to connect research efforts in diverse areas such as robotics, web agents, social simulation, and scientific discovery, which all rely on environment modeling but currently operate with different assumptions and metrics.
- **Gaps in Existing Taxonomies:** Current surveys typically organize world models by modality (e.g., visual, language) or application domain (e.g., autonomous driving, gaming). This approach fails to capture the underlying progression of world-modeling capabilities that apply across these distinctions.
- **Highlighting Capability Progression:** The authors aim to formalize a hierarchy of capabilities that reflects a progression from basic pattern matching to decision-usable simulation and, ultimately, to autonomous model revision. This progression is deemed crucial for the development of truly intelligent agents.
- **Clarifying "World Simulator" Debate:** The framework intends to bring precision to the debate on whether large-scale generative models are merely plausible generators or genuine world simulators by defining specific capabilities (e.g., rollout, intervention sensitivity, constraint consistency) beyond generic prediction.
- **Underrepresented Agentic Applications:** The work aims to emphasize the role of world modeling in agentic AI applications beyond traditional domains, including web agents, tool-use agents, and multi-agent systems, where learned environment dynamics are essential for planning and action selection.
- **Roadmap for Future Research:** By establishing clear boundary conditions and identifying open problems, the paper seeks to provide a roadmap for developing world models that can not only simulate but also adaptively reshape the environments in which agents operate.

In essence, the core motivation is to move beyond siloed research by providing a diagnostic framework that clarifies what world models can do, what constraints they must satisfy, and how their capabilities can be rigorously evaluated and progressively advanced.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this work is centered on proposing and applying a novel conceptual taxonomy for agentic world modeling, organized along two orthogonal axes: capability level and governing-law regime. This framework is used to synthesize and analyze over 400 existing research works, summarizing more than 100 representative systems. The approach is primarily a literature review and conceptual synthesis, rather than presenting new empirical results.

#### 3.1. The "Levels × Laws" Taxonomy

- **Capability Levels (L1/L2/L3):** This axis defines a hierarchy of world-modeling capabilities that an agent can invoke.
 - **L1 Predictor:** This is the most basic level, focused on local, one-step (or short fixed-horizon) prediction. An L1 model provides operators for state inference, forward dynamics (predicting the next latent state given the current state and action), observation decoding, and inverse dynamics. Its core function is to learn patterns and predict immediate future states under the training distribution, without guarantees of multi-step coherence. This stage is analogous to Hume's constant conjunction, where observed regularities are extracted without causal necessity.
 - **L2 Simulator:** This level extends L1 by enabling decision-usable multi-step simulation. An L2 model must support trajectory-level queries (predicting a sequence of future states given an initial state, action sequence, and domain constraints). The elevation from L1 to L2 is marked by three boundary conditions:
 1. **Long-horizon coherence:** Rollouts must remain usable over multiple steps without immediate degradation due to compounding error.
 2. **Intervention sensitivity:** Counterfactual changes (e.g., in actions or initial premises) must induce stable and directionally meaningful trajectory changes.
 3. **Constraint consistency:** Generated futures must respect the governing laws of the target environment regime (e.g., physics, digital logic, social norms). This stage is conceptually linked to Lewis's "closest possible worlds" for counterfactual reasoning.
 - **L3 Evolver:** This is the highest level, where the world model itself becomes an object of revision, moving from fixed simulation to evidence-driven model growth. An L3 system implements a full "design–execute–observe–reflect" loop, actively collecting new evidence to revise its dynamics model when predictions systematically fail. Three boundary conditions mark the elevation from L2 to L3:
 1. **Evidence-grounded diagnosis:** Failures are attributed to actionable causes using replayable evidence.
 2. **Persistent asset update:** Fixes are promoted as reusable assets (e.g., skills, rules, tests), not ephemeral patches.
 3. **Governed validation:** Updates pass regression and robustness tests (e.g., rollback, canary policies) before deployment. This level is inspired by epistemological concepts like Lakatos's "protective belt" and "hard core," and Popper's falsifiability.

- **Governing-Law Regimes:** This axis identifies four categories of environmental constraints that dictate what a world model must respect.
 - **Physical World:** Governed by physics (e.g., contact mechanics, gravity, kinematics). Violations are physically impossible events (e.g., interpenetration).
 - **Digital World:** Governed by deterministic program semantics (e.g., API contracts, UI state machines). Violations are logical errors (e.g., invalid API calls, ignoring error codes).
 - **Social World:** Governed by beliefs, goals, norms, and institutions. Violations undermine social coherence (e.g., breaking a promise without consequence).
 - **Scientific World:** Governed by latent causal mechanisms that must be discovered empirically. Violations contradict experimental measurements or known scientific principles.

#### 3.2. Survey and Synthesis Process

The authors synthesized over 400 works and analyzed more than 100 representative systems by:
1. **Categorization:** Assigning each system or approach to a specific capability level (L1, L2, L3) and a governing-law regime (Physical, Digital, Social, Scientific). This involved interpreting how each system's core functionality aligns with the defined capabilities and the types of constraints it addresses.
2. **Analysis of Approaches and Failure Modes:** For each level-regime pair, methods, characteristic failure modes, and current evaluation practices were examined. This included identifying the strengths and weaknesses of different architectural choices (representation, dynamics, control interface) within specific contexts.
3. **Identification of Gaps and Open Problems:** The framework was used to pinpoint areas where current capabilities are lacking (e.g., L1's inability to ensure long-horizon coherence, L2's limitations in model revision, the immaturity of L3 in certain domains).
4. **Proposal of Evaluation Principles:** The analysis informed the proposal of "decision-centric evaluation principles" (e.g., Action Success Rate, Counterfactual Outcome Deviation) and a "Minimal Reproducible Evaluation Package (MREP)" to standardize rigorous assessment.
5. **Architectural Guidance:** The survey then translates the conceptual framework into practical architectural guidance for designing world-model systems, considering tradeoffs based on latency budgets and domain-specific bottlenecks.

This systematic decomposition and re-synthesis of the literature forms the methodological core, enabling a cross-cutting analysis that aims to provide a unified understanding of agentic world modeling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings and results derived from its comprehensive analysis and synthesis of over 400 works using the proposed "levels × laws" taxonomy:

#### 4.1. Clarification of World Model Capabilities

- **L1 Predictor (Local Prediction):** The survey consolidates that L1 systems primarily excel at one-step or short-horizon prediction, with components like state inference (e.g., contrastive learning, VAEs, RSSM), forward dynamics (e.g., MuZero, Dreamer, diffusion models), observation decoding, and inverse dynamics. While crucial, L1 alone does not guarantee long-horizon coherence or decision-usability due to compounding errors.
- **L2 Simulator (Decision-Usable Multi-Step Simulation):** The analysis reveals that elevating from L1 to L2 requires satisfying three critical boundary conditions:
 1. **Long-horizon coherence:** Illustrated by systems like DreamerV3 and large-scale video generators (Sora, Genie) that can produce extended, visually plausible rollouts, though perceptual quality does not always equate to physical consistency.
 2. **Intervention sensitivity:** Exemplified by action-conditioned models (GAIA-1, Genie) that respond to explicit control signals, but this remains a fragile capability for many high-fidelity generative models.
 3. **Constraint consistency:** This is the most crucial, varying by regime. Physics simulators (MuJoCo, Isaac Gym) enforce hard physical laws. Digital world models (WebDreamer, CodeWM) must adhere to program semantics and API contracts. Social world models (Generative Agents, CICERO) attempt to maintain consistency of beliefs, goals, and norms. Scientific world models (GraphCast, ChemBO) encode causal mechanisms and conservation laws.
- **L3 Evolver (Evidence-Driven Model Revision):** The survey identifies L3 as a qualitatively distinct capability that involves actively modifying the model's structure or parameters based on deployment evidence. This "design–execute–observe–reflect" loop, akin to scientific practice, moves beyond passive data fitting to active experimentation and revision.

#### 4.2. Cross-Regime Analysis and Insights

- **Regime-Specific Constraints and Bottlenecks:** The "laws" axis highlights that each domain imposes distinct constraints, failure modes, and evaluation priorities. For example, physical worlds face contact instability and sim-to-real gaps, digital worlds struggle with grounding breaks and race conditions, social worlds contend with role drift and goal forgetting, and scientific worlds battle hallucinated mechanisms and evidence-chain validity.
- **The "Constraints-First" Principle:** A key finding is that effective simulators prioritize adherence to governing constraints over purely visual realism. Explicitly capturing and enforcing constraints (e.g., geometry, state machines, norms) often yields more robust and decision-usable world models than merely improving perceptual fidelity.
- **Interacting Regimes:** Many real-world systems are cross-regime (e.g., autonomous driving combines physical and social laws), necessitating joint constraint satisfaction.

#### 4.3. Identified Failure Modes for L2 Systems

The analysis consolidates five recurring failure modes across L2 systems that hinder their decision-usability:
1. **Compounding error:** Small errors amplify over long horizons.
2. **State aliasing and drift:** Distinct real states collapse into similar representations, leading to incorrect actions.
3. **Controllability failure:** Models are insensitive to action changes, making counterfactual planning ineffective.
4. **Exploitability and simulator escape:** Search algorithms exploit loopholes in simulators or evaluation harnesses.
5. **Calibration failure under distribution shift:** Overconfident wrong predictions occur when the environment changes.

#### 4.4. Maturity of L3 Across Domains

- **Scientific (Established):** Autonomous science platforms (Robot Scientist, CAMEO, A-Lab) demonstrate the most mature L3 capabilities, leveraging fast, structured feedback and unambiguous falsification signals to revise models.
- **Digital (Partial):** Evaluator-driven discovery systems (FunSearch, AlphaEvolve, CodeIt) show promise by generating and validating code, but often lack active information expansion.
- **Physical (Emerging):** Robotic systems (AdaptSim, Self-Modeling) are beginning to demonstrate adaptive probing and self-model revision, but failure attribution remains challenging.
- **Social (Aspirational):** This is the least mature L3 regime, due to ethical constraints on experimentation, attribution ambiguity, and noisy ground truth in human behavior.

#### 4.5. Proposed Evaluation Framework

- The paper advocates a shift from prediction-centric to **decision-centric evaluation**, introducing metrics like **Action Success Rate (ASR)** and **Counterfactual Outcome Deviation (COD)**.
- It highlights that existing benchmarks often evaluate only L1-style prediction, with significant gaps in L2- and L3-style assessment. The same benchmark can evaluate different capabilities depending on the protocol.
- A **Minimal Reproducible Evaluation Package (MREP)** is proposed to standardize evaluation through version locking, trace logs, failure taxonomy, tail statistics, and explicit boundary condition mapping.

#### 4.6. Architectural and Computational Insights

- The analysis connects different architectural choices (representation, dynamics, control interface) to their suitability for specific capability levels and governing-law regimes, outlining strengths and dominant failure modes.
- It discusses the "O(1) argument" for learned dynamics and the importance of efficiency techniques (few-step distillation, quantization, pruning, KV cache compression) for real-time deployment of L2 and L3 systems.

These findings collectively synthesize the current landscape of agentic world modeling, diagnosing its present state, outlining its trajectory, and identifying the challenges that must be addressed for future advancements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work has several significant implications and potential impacts across the field of AI and beyond:

#### 5.1. Conceptual Clarity and Unification

- **Bridging Research Communities:** By providing a common, capability-based taxonomy and language ("levels × laws"), the paper directly addresses the conceptual fragmentation in world model research. This can facilitate interdisciplinary collaboration, reduce duplicated effort, and enable more meaningful comparisons across diverse domains (robotics, vision, NLP, science).
- **Standardizing Terminology:** The precise definitions of L1 Predictor, L2 Simulator, and L3 Evolver, along with their boundary conditions, offer a robust framework for researchers to categorize their work and articulate their contributions more clearly, moving beyond ambiguous uses of "world model."

#### 5.2. Guiding Research and Development

- **A Clear Roadmap:** The proposed L1→L2→L3 progression provides a structured roadmap for advancing world models. It highlights the next steps for systems currently operating at lower levels and points towards the necessary capabilities for achieving higher intelligence.
- **Identifying Critical Bottlenecks:** By explicitly outlining the challenges for each level and regime (e.g., physical faithfulness for L1, intervention sensitivity for L2, evidence-driven revision for L3), the paper directs research efforts towards fundamental open problems rather than incremental gains on saturated metrics.
- **Architectural Guidance:** The detailed discussion of architectural building blocks (representation, dynamics, control interface) and their tradeoffs across different regimes offers practical guidance for system design, helping practitioners choose appropriate components for specific applications and capability targets.

#### 5.3. Advancing Evaluation Practices

- **Shift to Decision-Centric Metrics:** The advocacy for decision-centric evaluation principles (ASR, COD) over perceptual-quality metrics is a significant contribution. This shift encourages evaluation that directly assesses the utility of world models for downstream agentic tasks, fostering the development of models that are genuinely "decision-usable."
- **Standardized Evaluation Framework:** The proposal of the Minimal Reproducible Evaluation Package (MREP) has the potential to standardize benchmark reporting, enhance reproducibility, and enable more rigorous and fair comparisons across different world model implementations. This is particularly crucial for L3 systems, where robust validation is paramount.

#### 5.4. Addressing Safety and Governance

- **Threat Modeling:** By framing security failures as violations of governing laws, the paper offers a unified lens for understanding world model safety. This can lead to more targeted threat models and defensive strategies for physical, digital, social, and scientific AI systems.
- **Governance of Self-Evolving Systems:** The L3 framework explicitly raises governance challenges associated with continually adapting models (e.g., benchmark overfitting, knowledge contamination, misattribution cascades). Addressing these is vital for deploying reliable and trustworthy self-evolving AI agents.

#### 5.5. Long-Term Vision for AI

- **Meta-World Modeling:** The concept of "beyond L3" — meta-world modeling, where systems reason about and evolve the governing laws themselves — sets an ambitious long-term vision for AI. This pushes the frontier towards systems that can not only understand and simulate a given world but also explore and construct alternative realities, potentially accelerating scientific discovery and creative problem-solving.
- **Emphasis on Symbolic Foundations:** The argument for symbolic representations as crucial for L3 (law revision) and meta-world modeling suggests a direction for neuro-symbolic AI, aiming to combine the scalability of neural networks with the interpretability and manipulability of symbolic knowledge.

In summary, this work provides a foundational conceptual framework that can serve as a guide for future research, development, and evaluation in agentic world modeling. By offering clarity, a structured roadmap, and a focus on decision-centric capabilities, it aims to accelerate the transition from passive prediction to intelligent agents that can effectively interact with, understand, and adaptively reshape complex environments.
