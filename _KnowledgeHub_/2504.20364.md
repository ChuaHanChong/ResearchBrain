# Exploring internal representation of self-supervised networks: few-shot learning abilities and comparison with human semantics and recognition of objects

> [!summary] Summary
> Deep neural networks trained with self-supervised learning develop internal representations that closely align with human semantic organization and recognition patterns. These human-like representational structures emerge in earlier layers of self-supervised networks compared to those trained with supervised learning.

## Problem

- Understanding how deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) internally represent information, especially when trained with self-supervised learning (SSL).
- Investigating how the categorical organization within SSL DCNNs relates to human perception and semantic cognition.
- Exploring the emergence of meaningful visual representations in neural networks without explicit category labels.

## Method

- Pre-trained DCNNs (modified ResNet-18) using ==self-supervised contrastive learning (SimCLR, SimSiam)== and supervised object classification on CIFAR-100.
- Evaluated the pre-trained networks on their ==few-shot learning capabilities== for novel categories by freezing network weights and classifying based on prototype similarity.
- Compared the networks' internal representations, including ==category cluster structures== and error patterns, to human semantic organization and recognition data from CIFAR-10H.

## Results

- Self-supervised DCNNs accurately perform few-shot classification of novel object categories.
- Internal representations of self-supervised networks show high alignment with human semantic organization, with this alignment appearing in **earlier network layers** than in supervised networks.
- Self-supervised networks' recognition error patterns strongly correlate with human recognition errors, achieving high similarity in earlier layers compared to supervised networks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised networks achieve optimal few-shot learning performance and human-like semantic organization in earlier convolutional layers (conv2-conv3).
> - Supervised networks require deeper layers (fc1-fc3) to attain comparable levels of few-shot performance and alignment with human semantics and recognition patterns.
> - Self-supervised learning may lead to more efficient and distributed representations that align with human-like perceptual and cognitive structures earlier in the processing hierarchy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in visual processing tasks, but how they represent information internally remains an ongoing area of research. This is particularly interesting in the case of self-supervised learning (SSL), which enables neural networks to learn meaningful representations without explicit labeling.

Recent studies have shown that self-supervised DCNNs exhibit representational similarities to biological visual systems, raising important questions about how these networks organize categorical information and how this organization relates to human perception and cognition.

This study, conducted by researchers from the University of Tokyo and Kyoto University, explores the internal representations of self-supervised networks by examining their few-shot learning capabilities and comparing these representations to human semantic organization and recognition patterns. The research provides valuable insights into how neural networks trained without explicit supervision can develop representations that align with human cognition.

### 2. Self-Supervised Learning and Neural Networks

Self-supervised learning represents a significant departure from traditional supervised learning approaches. Instead of relying on labeled data, SSL methods generate supervisory signals from the data itself, typically by creating pretext tasks that the network must solve.

In this study, the researchers employed contrastive learning (specifically, SimCLR) as the primary self-supervised method. Contrastive learning trains the network to bring the representations of similar inputs closer together while pushing apart the representations of dissimilar inputs:

This approach is particularly interesting from a biological perspective because it offers a plausible mechanism for how the brain might learn without explicit supervision. Infants, for example, develop visual recognition capabilities before they acquire language, suggesting they learn to organize visual information without explicit category labels.

The researchers also explored another SSL method, SimSiam, to validate their findings across different self-supervised techniques. For comparison, they trained a separate network using traditional supervised learning with object classification.

### 3. Methodology

The study employed a two-step procedure to investigate the internal representations of self-supervised networks:

1. **Pre-training**: DCNNs were pre-trained using either self-supervised contrastive learning (SimCLR), another self-supervised method (SimSiam), or supervised object classification.

2. **Few-Shot Learning Evaluation**: The pre-trained networks were evaluated on their ability to recognize novel object categories using a limited number of examples.

The network architecture used was a modified ResNet-18:

The researchers used the CIFAR-100 dataset for pre-training and few-shot learning evaluation. The CIFAR-10H dataset, which contains human classification data, was used to compare the error patterns of the networks with those of human participants.

For the few-shot learning evaluation, the pre-trained networks were kept frozen, and novel categories were classified based on their similarity to prototype representations computed from a few exemplar images. The researchers then analyzed:

1. The accuracy of few-shot classification across different layers of the network
2. The structure of category clusters in the network's representations
3. The similarity between the network's error patterns and human recognition errors

### 7. Differences Between Self-Supervised and Supervised Learning

The study revealed several key differences between self-supervised and supervised learning approaches:

1. **Layer-specific performance**: Self-supervised networks achieved optimal few-shot learning performance and human-like representations in earlier layers, while supervised networks required deeper layers.

3. **Representation structure**: The self-supervised networks developed more distributed representations, with meaningful category distinctions emerging earlier in the processing hierarchy.

These differences suggest that self-supervised and supervised learning lead to fundamentally different internal organizations, despite achieving similar end-task performance. The self-supervised approach may be more biologically plausible, aligning better with how human visual and semantic systems develop.

### 8. Implications and Significance

The findings from this study have several important implications:

1. **Biological plausibility of SSL**: The alignment between self-supervised networks and human cognition supports the idea that SSL offers a biologically plausible mechanism for learning representations in the brain, particularly when explicit supervision is unavailable.

2. **Insights into human cognition**: The study provides evidence that the structure of human semantic knowledge may be shaped by similar unsupervised learning mechanisms, offering a computational framework for understanding cognitive development.

3. **More efficient representations**: The fact that self-supervised networks develop human-like representations in earlier layers suggests they may learn more efficient encodings of visual information.

4. **Language acquisition**: The findings offer insights into language acquisition, suggesting that the categorical structure of language may emerge from the separability of object representations shaped through self-supervised learning.

5. **AI development**: Understanding how SSL generates human-like representations could inform the development of more human-like artificial intelligence systems that can learn from limited data and generalize to new situations.

### 9. Conclusion

This research demonstrates that deep neural networks trained with self-supervised learning can develop internal representations that closely align with human semantic organization and recognition patterns. The study provides compelling evidence that self-supervised learning offers a plausible computational framework for understanding how semantic knowledge and perceptual recognition capabilities may develop in biological systems, particularly in the absence of explicit supervision.

The finding that self-supervised networks achieve these human-like representations in earlier layers than supervised networks suggests fundamental differences in how these learning approaches organize information. This insight may help bridge the gap between artificial and biological intelligence by identifying computational principles that underlie human cognitive abilities.

Future research directions might include exploring how different self-supervised learning methods affect representational structures, investigating the development of these representations over the course of training, and examining how self-supervised pre-training interacts with subsequent supervised fine-tuning to shape neural representations.
