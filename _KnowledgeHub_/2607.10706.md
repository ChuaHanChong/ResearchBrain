# Action Map Policy: Learning 3D Closed-loop Manipulation via Pixel Classification

> [!summary] Summary
> Action Map Policy (AMP) re-envisions 3D closed-loop manipulation policy learning as a pixel classification task, representing future actions as 2D heatmaps across multiple camera views. This approach achieves high precision and significantly faster inference, while effectively modeling multi-modal action distributions, outperforming baselines by an average of 20.7% in simulation and demonstrating robust real-world performance.

## Problem

- Robot manipulation faces challenges with high-dimensional, continuous action spaces that often have multi-modal optimal solutions.
- Existing regression-based methods struggle with 'mode collapse', predicting average actions that may be suboptimal or invalid.
- Iterative generative policies offer multi-modality but incur slow inference times due to multiple sampling steps, and naive discrete action tokenization leads to a combinatorial explosion of vocabulary.

## Method

- The **Action Map Policy (AMP)** framework reformulates 3D closed-loop manipulation as a ==pixel classification== problem over ==2D heatmaps==, representing an ==action chunk== as projected ==3D keypoints== from multi-view cameras.
- An **X-Net** neural network with a ==Multi-View Transformer (MVT)== processes multi-view images to predict dense heatmaps (logits), trained with a ==cross-entropy loss==.
- During inference, predicted heatmaps are converted into 3D end-effector actions by identifying peak pixel locations, ==triangulating== 3D keypoints, and geometrically recovering pose and gripper width.

## Results

- AMP achieved an average success rate gain of **20.7%** over the second-best baseline in simulated MimicGen tasks and maintained a theoretical precision of approximately **1 mm** translation and **1.3°** rotation at **224x224** resolution.
- In real-world long-horizon tasks, AMP demonstrated significantly higher completion rates (**80-90%**) compared to Diffusion Policy (**25-40%**) and ACT (**15-35%**), with substantially faster inference (**13.80 ms** per chunk vs. **93.53 ms** for DiffPo).
- The policy exhibited robust spatial reasoning, achieving **100%** success on real-world tasks requiring differentiation between visually identical objects based on subtle cues like a laser pointer, outperforming baselines that struggled with such distinctions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representing continuous 3D actions as dense 2D heatmaps in image space allows for high precision (e.g., 1 mm translation) without the combinatorial explosion seen in discrete tokenization.
> - Utilizing a cross-entropy loss on heatmaps effectively models multi-modal action distributions, overcoming mode collapse prevalent in regression-based policies.
> - The X-Net's multi-view attention efficiently fuses spatial information across cameras, enabling superior spatial reasoning and single-pass inference for an entire action chunk, leading to faster real-time control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning for manipulation faces challenges related to action space representation. These challenges include the high dimensionality of continuous actions (e.g., 6-DoF end-effector poses), the need to plan over long time horizons, and the presence of multi-modal optimal solutions where several distinct actions or trajectories can achieve the same goal.

Existing approaches to robot policy learning generally fall into a few categories:
- **Regression-based methods:** These directly predict continuous actions (e.g., end-effector poses) and often use an Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. While simple and efficient, they are noted to struggle with multi-modal action distributions, as minimizing MSE can lead to "mode collapse," where the policy predicts an average of multiple valid actions, which might itself be an invalid or suboptimal action.
- **Iterative generative policies:** This category includes diffusion models and flow-matching methods. These approaches model action distributions implicitly through iterative sampling and refinement steps. They can, in principle, capture multi-modal distributions. However, their implicit nature can make direct inspection or constraint of action likelihoods difficult, and they typically require multiple forward passes for inference, leading to slower execution.
- **Classification-based methods (LLM-style):** Inspired by the success of large language models (LLMs) in sequence prediction via classification, some methods discretize continuous actions into tokens and predict these tokens autoregressively. This formulation benefits from stable cross-entropy objectives and can model multi-modal distributions. However, a naive discretization of high-dimensional continuous actions leads to a combinatorial explosion of the action vocabulary. For instance, a 6-DoF action with 10 bins per dimension would require 10^6 classes, making it difficult to represent fine-grained manipulation actions accurately without an unfeasibly large vocabulary.
- **Dense action representations:** Some works, particularly for open-loop pick-and-place, represent actions as dense feature maps or goal configurations (e.g., target point clouds). This approach is less common for closed-loop, continuous 3D manipulation over a temporal horizon.
- **Keypoint-based methods:** Keypoints have been used in computer vision for pose estimation and tracking. In robotics, they can compactly represent end-effector poses. Some recent methods leverage keypoints as intermediate action representations, often diffusing their coordinates.

This paper introduces Action Map Policy (AMP) as an alternative that addresses the limitations of these existing methods. It extends the classification paradigm to 3D closed-loop robot manipulation by representing actions as dense heatmaps in image space, effectively combining aspects of dense action representation and keypoint learning within a classification framework. This aims to avoid the combinatorial explosion of discrete tokenization while naturally handling multi-modality and enabling faster, single-pass inference compared to iterative generative models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a novel framework for 3D closed-loop manipulation policy learning that can effectively address the challenges associated with high-dimensional, multi-modal action spaces and long time horizons, while maintaining precision and fast inference.

The motivation for AMP stems from the following observed limitations of current approaches:

1. **Combinatorial Explosion in Discrete Action Spaces:** Traditional discretization of continuous 3D actions (e.g., 6-DoF poses) leads to an unmanageably large number of discrete tokens or classes, making it difficult for models to learn and represent fine-grained actions accurately. This limits the application of effective classification-based learning paradigms (like those in LLMs) to robotics.
2. **Mode Collapse in Regression-based Policies:** Policies trained with regression objectives (e.g., MSE loss) tend to average multiple valid optimal solutions into a single prediction. This can result in policies that output suboptimal or invalid actions, particularly in tasks requiring multi-modal solutions.
3. **Slow Inference in Iterative Generative Policies:** Diffusion models and other iterative generative policies, while capable of modeling multi-modal distributions, typically require many iterative sampling steps for action generation. This leads to high inference latency, which can be problematic for real-time closed-loop control.
4. **Lack of Spatial Grounding and Fine-Grained Reasoning:** Some policy learning methods may collapse visual information into a single global embedding, potentially losing fine-grained pixel-level cues that are critical for precise manipulation tasks, or failing to directly leverage the geometric structure of the observation space.
5. **Difficulty in Explicitly Representing Action Distributions:** Iterative generative policies represent action distributions implicitly, making it challenging to directly inspect, constrain, or supervise the likelihood of specific action candidates. A more explicit representation could facilitate downstream tasks like reinforcement learning or action refinement.

To overcome these motivations, the authors propose AMP, which aims to:
- Cast 3D manipulation policy learning as a pixel classification problem in 2D image space, addressing the combinatorial explosion by re-framing actions as spatial distributions rather than discrete tokens.
- Naturally model multi-modal action distributions using a cross-entropy loss, preventing mode collapse.
- Achieve fast, single-pass inference for an entire action chunk, surpassing the speed of iterative generative models.
- Preserve fine-grained pixel-wise visual signals and ground actions in the spatial structure of observations, leading to better spatial reasoning.
- Provide an explicit action distribution in the form of heatmaps, potentially enabling future refinements or integration with other learning paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Action Map Policy (AMP) reformulates 3D closed-loop manipulation policy learning as a pixel classification task. It involves three main components: action chunk representation, a multi-view neural network architecture (X-Net) for heatmap prediction, and a heatmap-to-action conversion mechanism.

#### 3.1. Action Chunk Representation as 2D Heatmaps

Instead of directly regressing 3D continuous actions or discretizing them into tokens, AMP represents a sequence of future actions (an "action chunk") as 2D heatmaps across multiple camera views.

- **3D Keypoint Definition:** Each end-effector pose (translation T, rotation R, gripper aperture w) is first converted into a set of `m` (typically 5) 3D keypoints (`p1, ..., pm`) that encode the gripper's geometry. These keypoints are fixed relative to the end-effector.
- **Projection to 2D Image Space:** These 3D keypoints are then projected onto the 2D image planes of `n` cameras using known camera intrinsics and extrinsics. This yields 2D pixel coordinates `(u, v)` for each keypoint in each camera view.
- **Soft Heatmap Labels:** The projected 2D coordinates are discretized into pixel locations. For training, soft heatmap labels (`h_ijk`) are created for each keypoint `j` at each future timestep `i` in each camera view `k`. These labels are normalized 2D Gaussian distributions centered at the projected pixel coordinates, with a specific width `σ` (e.g., `σ=2`). This provides a smooth, localized target for classification.
- **Spatiotemporal Heatmap Volumes:** Stacking these heatmaps across all `l` future timesteps for a single keypoint and camera view creates a spatiotemporal volume (`H_jk`). Collecting these volumes for all `m` keypoints and `n` cameras forms the complete action representation (`H = (H_jk)_m,n`). This converts the policy learning problem into a dense classification task over image pixels.

#### 3.2. Predicting Actions as Distributions over Pixels (X-Net Architecture)

AMP employs a neural network, termed X-Net, designed to process multi-view image inputs and output dense heatmaps.

- **Multi-View Input:** The X-Net typically takes `n` camera images as input (e.g., one in-hand view and two side views).
- **Encoder-Decoder Structure:** The architecture uses a U-Net-like encoder-decoder structure.
 - **Encoder:** Each input image is processed by a U-Net encoder, which generates dense feature maps.
 - **Multi-View Transformer (MVT):** The dense feature maps from each view are flattened into positionally-encoded tokens and fed into an MVT module. This transformer incorporates "in-image attention layers" (processing tokens within a single view) and "cross-image attention layers" (enabling communication and feature fusion across different camera views). This preserves spatial structure while integrating multi-view information.
 - **Decoder:** The output tokens from the MVT are reshaped back into spatial dimensions and passed through a U-Net decoder. Skip connections link corresponding layers of the encoder and decoder.
- **Heatmap Output:** The decoder generates `l × m` channels of heatmaps for each target camera view (e.g., only side views), where each channel corresponds to a specific keypoint at a specific future timestep. These outputs are raw logits (`ˆh_ijk`).
- **Loss Function:** The network is trained using a cross-entropy loss between the predicted logits (`ˆh_ijk`) and the ground-truth soft heatmap labels (`h_ijk`). The loss is averaged across all timesteps, keypoints, and cameras. This objective naturally models the multi-modal distribution of actions.

#### 3.3. Heatmap-to-Action Conversion (Inference)

During inference, the policy converts the predicted heatmaps into executable 3D end-effector actions. The network is not trained with action extraction in the loop; supervision occurs entirely in heatmap space.

- **Argmax Pixel Selection:** For each keypoint `j` at each timestep `i` in each camera view `k`, the pixel with the highest probability is identified using `arg max` on the `softmax` output of the predicted heatmap (`(u, v)_ijk`).
- **3D Keypoint Reconstruction via Triangulation:** The `n` 2D pixel coordinates corresponding to the same 3D keypoint from different camera views are then triangulated using camera matrices to reconstruct the 3D coordinates of each keypoint (`p_j`).
- **Pose Recovery:** From the trajectory of `m` 3D keypoints, the per-step end-effector pose (`(T, R, w)`) is recovered geometrically:
 - **Translation (T):** Calculated as the centroid of four grasp points (`p1` to `p4`).
 - **Rotation (R):** Constructed from two geometric axes (antipodal and approach axes) derived from the keypoints, then orthonormalized.
 - **Gripper Width (w):** Determined by the normalized distances of a fifth keypoint (`p5`) to the two fingertip midpoints.
- **Closed-Loop Control:** The policy generates a sequence of `l` actions. The robot executes a subset of these (`l_e` steps), and then the policy re-plans by consuming the latest observation, enabling closed-loop visuomotor control.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Image Resolution:** Typically 224x224 pixels.
- **Camera Setup:** Two side-view cameras and one in-hand camera, with heatmaps predicted for side views only.
- **Network Depth:** Encoder has four ResBlocks (16x downsampling), MVT has six layers (two intra-image, four cross-image attention), decoder has four upsampling ResBlocks.
- **Output Channels:** 60 channels per side view (5 keypoints x 12 timesteps).
- **Data Augmentation:** Extensive equivariant data augmentation is applied, jointly transforming input images and their corresponding heatmap labels (random rotations and translations). This leverages the spatial alignment of observations and actions to improve robustness.
- **Training:** 200 epochs on a single GPU, learning rate 1e-4 with cosine annealing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents findings from both precision analysis and experimental evaluations in simulated and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. Precision and Robustness Analysis

- **Theoretical Precision:** The heatmap action representation was analyzed for its theoretical precision as a function of image resolution. At 224x224 resolution, it achieved approximately 1 mm translation precision and 1.3° rotation precision. Increasing resolution to 1024x1024 improved this to 0.22 mm and 0.28°, respectively.
- **Comparison to Conventional Discretization:** To achieve comparable precision, conventional discretization (binning each action dimension independently) would require an exponentially larger number of codebook tokens. For instance, 1 mm translation and 1.3° rotation precision would require around 2.1 x 10^10 tokens, a prohibitively large vocabulary. This highlights AMP's ability to achieve high precision without combinatorial explosion.

#### 4.2. Simulated Experiments

Experiments were conducted on six tasks from the MimicGen benchmark, covering rigid and articulated object manipulation with varying precision requirements (e.g., stack-three-d1, coffee-d2, threading-d2).
- **Baselines:** AMP was compared against Diffusion Policy (DiffPo), ACT (regression-based), OAT (token-based), and Motion Track (keypoint-based diffusion).
- **Performance:** AMP outperformed baselines on five of the six tasks, with an average success rate gain of 20.7% over the second-best baseline (DiffPo). Notable improvements included a 52% gain on `stack-three-d1`. AMP demonstrated better performance across all tasks compared to regression-based (ACT) and token-based (OAT) methods, without requiring action pretraining. It also largely outperformed Motion Track, attributed to AMP's cross-view design maintaining consistent predictions, unlike Motion Track's independent inference.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **In-hand camera:** Removing the in-hand camera led to performance drops (e.g., 14% on `hammer-cleanup-d1` and `coffee-d2`), indicating its value for tasks with occlusion.
 - **Soft vs. One-Hot Labels:** Using soft Gaussian labels (σ=2) improved performance by up to 10% compared to one-hot labels (σ=0), suggesting that a smooth gradient around the target pixel is beneficial for training stability and precision.
 - **Equivariant Data Augmentation:** Disabling this augmentation resulted in significant performance drops (e.g., 32% on `stack-three-d1`), confirming its role in encouraging symmetry-consistent mappings and robustness.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

Five real-world experiments were conducted using a UR5 arm with multi-camera setups.

- **Visuomotor Policy Learning (Long-Horizon Tasks):** Three challenging tasks were designed: `making-coffee` (opening lid, inserting pod, placing mug, pressing button), `toast-bread` (inserting slices, pressing lever), and `steam-egg` (iterative egg placement, lid placement).
 - **Performance and Speed:** AMP achieved substantially higher completion rates (80-90%) compared to DiffPo (25-40%) and ACT (15-35%). AMP also demonstrated significantly faster inference (13.80 ms per action chunk) compared to DiffPo (93.53 ms for 16 DDIM steps), while ACT was faster (7.16 ms) but had lower success rates. AMP maintained strong spatial generalization under object variations, where baselines degraded.
- **Spatial Reasoning:** Two tasks (`grasp-target-cup`, `stack-target-block`) were designed to test spatial reasoning in the presence of visually identical objects, with the target indicated by a laser pointer.
 - **Performance:** AMP achieved a 100% success rate on both tasks. DiffPo achieved an average of 20%, often ignoring the laser cue or failing to manipulate the correct object. ACT performed better than DiffPo (65-75%) but sometimes collapsed towards a middle location between identical objects, attributed to its regression-based formulation.
 - **Reasoning:** The authors hypothesize that AMP's ability to capture fine-grained observation signals and express complex action distributions via classification allowed it to correctly respond to the subtle laser-point cues and differentiate between identical objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Action Map Policy (AMP) framework represents a contribution to robot manipulation learning by presenting a classification-based approach that addresses several limitations of existing methods.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Novel Framework:** The paper introduces a new paradigm that reformulates 3D closed-loop manipulation policy learning as a pixel classification problem using dense action keypoint heatmaps. This effectively bridges the gap between the expressive power of classification (for multi-modal distributions) and the high-dimensional continuous nature of robotic actions.
- **Efficient and Precise Action Representation:** AMP demonstrates that millimeter-level accuracy can be achieved with image resolutions common in robot vision, without incurring the combinatorial explosion of discrete action tokenization. This allows for fine-grained manipulation while maintaining a manageable action space.
- **Multi-Modal Action Modeling:** By utilizing a cross-entropy objective on heatmaps, AMP naturally models multi-modal action distributions, avoiding the "mode collapse" observed in regression-based methods. This is crucial for tasks where multiple valid ways to interact with objects exist.
- **Fast Inference:** AMP generates an entire action chunk in a single forward pass, resulting in substantially faster inference speeds compared to iterative generative methods like diffusion policies. This speed is beneficial for real-time closed-loop robotic control.
- **Enhanced Spatial Reasoning:** The proposed X-Net architecture, which fuses multi-view images while preserving dense spatial structure, allows the policy to reason more effectively in image space. This enables responsiveness to small, task-critical visual cues (e.g., a laser pointer), contributing to more precise and responsive real-world manipulation.
- **Strong Experimental Performance:** The method significantly outperforms strong baselines across a range of simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, including challenging long-horizon and high-precision scenarios.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Foundation for Future Robot Learning:** The classification-based approach offers a robust and interpretable framework for learning complex manipulation skills. Its alignment with vision-language models (VLMs) and classification objectives could enable a unified interface for vision, language, and action, potentially leveraging pretrained models for faster learning and improved generalization.
- **Improved Real-World Robotics:** The combination of high precision, fast inference, and strong spatial reasoning makes AMP suitable for deployment in various real-world applications requiring dexterous manipulation, such as industrial automation, logistics, and assistive robotics.
- **Exploration of Reinforcement Learning:** The explicit action distribution provided by the heatmaps could serve as a natural interface for learning Q-functions in reinforcement learning, potentially leading to more efficient and stable policy optimization.

**Limitations Acknowledged by Authors:**
- **Minor Jittering:** While generally smooth, the generated actions exhibit minor jittering, which could be addressed by adding geometric consistency losses.
- **Workspace Coverage:** The workspace is limited by the calibrated camera coverage, suggesting a need for larger field-of-view cameras or dual in-hand cameras for broader action scope.
- **Gripper Specificity:** The current keypoint design is tailored for a parallel-jaw gripper, though it is stated to be adaptable to dexterous or bimanual setups.
