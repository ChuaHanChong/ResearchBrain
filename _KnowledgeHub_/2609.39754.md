# ChunkTrust: Adapting Execution Horizons for Robot Policies with Action-Expert Evidence

> [!summary] Summary
> ChunkTrust treats how many actions of a predicted chunk a robot executes before replanning as a latent variable, inferred from action-expert evidence rather than fixed. A training-free selector fuses spectral stability of the flow-generation trace with continuity against executed motion, tracked by a forgetting Beta posterior; an optional learned adapter adds a context prior. It lifts frozen policies, e.g. +6.80 points for pi0.5 on 50 RoboTwin2.0 tasks.

## Problem

- Fixed execution horizons are brittle: the best open-loop prefix length shifts with task phase, task length and setting, yet deployments typically hard-code a single value.
- Adaptive chunking methods based on attention, entropy, cross-horizon agreement or denoising variance judge a chunk in isolation, so an internally consistent prediction can still clash with already-executed motion.
- Per-replan horizon evidence is noisy, and existing selectors discard it rather than accumulating reusable preferences across execution contexts that recur within and across episodes.

## Method

- ==Intra-chunk spectral stability==: ==Fourier-transforms== each candidate prefix of the flow velocity trace along the horizon and scores RMS drift of high-frequency energy fraction across denoising steps.
- ==Inter-chunk continuity==: stitches each candidate prefix onto recently executed actions and penalizes the speed coefficient of variation, catching boundary jumps and stop-and-go motion; the two scores are mixed equally.
- **Action-aware Horizon Selector (AHS)** keeps per-horizon Beta states with ==Thompson sampling==, Gaussian-kernel sharing and exponential forgetting; the optional **Query-based Horizon Adapter (QHA)** distills a dense context-conditioned prior via cross-attention and ==KL loss==.

## Results

- AHS improves every evaluated base policy: pi0.5 rises from **56.70% to 63.50%** on 50 RoboTwin2.0 tasks, Qwen3GR00T from **47.83% to 57.50%** on RoboCasa GR1, Fast-WAM from **86.81% to 88.13%**.
- AHS+QHA lifts pi0.5 eight-task success to **39.06%** versus **36.25%** for AHS alone, and reaches **42.75%** versus **40.00%** on two tasks held out from QHA training.
- On a real bimanual robot, mean process score rises from **50.4% to 57.5%** over four household tasks; five candidates capture most gain (**22.0% to 33.0%**) at **0.67 ms** per-replan overhead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Execution horizon is phase dependent and worth inferring online: free-space approach tolerates long open-loop prefixes, while contact and delicate transport call for earlier re-observation.
> - A generative policy's own denoising trace carries usable reliability signal; failure rose from 50.2% to 97.4% when both spectral-instability and continuity risks were high.
> - Stacking a learned prior on online evidence can hurt specific tasks such as bimanual handover, so aggregate gains should always be checked against per-task breakdowns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot policy predicts a sequence of future actions, but the robot must decide how many of them to execute before it observes the scene again. A long open-loop prefix can keep a movement coherent, but it delays correction. A short prefix brings feedback sooner, but it can interrupt motion. In bread manipulation, that choice may change between approach and contact. ChunkTrust replaces one fixed execution length with a prefix selected from evidence already available inside the policy and its action history. With this change, the authors report higher aggregate success in simulation and higher partial-progress scores on a real robot.

Author hero demo of robot bread manipulation (“Autonomous 1x”).

### 2. Two signals, one execution decision

The selector, called AHS, checks each candidate prefix in two ways. It asks how stable the prefix appears while the action is being generated. It also asks how smoothly the prefix would continue the motion already executed.

The first check follows how the high-frequency part of a candidate action-prefix velocity spectrum changes over the generation trace. Greater deviation from its early reference counts against that candidate. The second check joins the candidate prefix to a suffix of already executed actions. It then measures variation in the stitched motion’s first-difference speeds, so irregular boundary motion or stop-and-go behavior counts against the candidate.

AHS combines the two qualities and keeps an episode-local Beta reliability state. That state shares evidence across nearby horizons and forgets older evidence as the task phase changes. Its feedback comes from action evidence, not an observed task-success label. The evidence is a proxy for horizon preference, not a calibrated probability of task success.

AHS is training-free. Neither AHS nor the optional QHA head changes the base action generator’s weights or the generated action values. QHA learns a context-conditioned dense horizon prior offline and fuses it with current evidence and episode memory. It still needs online evidence at deployment.

Method overview. In the upper flow, a frozen VLM/flow-based policy produces action chunks and a velocity trace. One branch depicts intra-chunk spectral stability. Another stitches executed history to candidate actions for inter-chunk continuity. The flow then combines evidence, Beta reliability states, a kernel-forgetting update, and a selection distribution. The lower, offline QHA flow produces a context-conditioned prior.

### 3. What the results show

The principal simulation test uses the full 50-task RoboTwin2.0 evaluation. There, multitask-post-trained π0.5 improves from **56.70% to 63.50% success, a +6.80 percentage-point gain** with AHS. Each Base–AHS comparison uses the same policy checkpoint. The evaluation covers Easy (clean) and Hard (randomized) settings, with 20 rollouts per task and setting. Success rate is the percentage of successful rollouts. Easy and Hard are averaged within each task, and tasks are weighted equally. The gain is an aggregate: task-level results include regressions.

A real-robot test provides corroboration on a different metric. The authors ran π0.5 on four bimanual household tasks: fold towel, move bread to plate, move drink to basket, and put duck toy in drawer. On these tasks, the equal-task mean normalized process score rose from **50.4% with fixed K=25 to 57.5% with AHS**. This score measures partial progress, not binary task success. Each substep scores 0, 0.5, or 1, and each task and method received 15 rollouts.

### 4. What it does and does not establish

ChunkTrust does not treat long or short execution as always best. Instead, it uses evidence about the current generated prefix and that prefix’s join to executed motion to decide when the robot gets feedback.

The method has clear boundaries. AHS requires access to action chunks and generation-time velocity traces. It decides at replanning time, so it cannot react directly to a disturbance that occurs during the selected prefix. Low-risk evidence does not guarantee success. The real-world evaluation covers four bimanual tasks, not all embodiments, safety-critical tasks, or long-horizon mobile manipulation.
