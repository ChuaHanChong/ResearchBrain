# CycleManip: Enabling Cyclic Task Manipulation via Effective Historical Perception and Understanding

> [!summary] Summary
> CycleManip is an end-to-end imitation framework for cyclic manipulation (shaking, hammering, cutting) that adds a cost-aware history sampling strategy plus a multi-task cycle-progress prediction head to a diffusion policy, letting it track repetitions and stop at the right count. It reaches 53-97% success across 8 cyclic tasks, far above prior imitation baselines.

## Problem

- Imitation and VLA policies use short observation windows, so near-identical frames across repeated cycles make it impossible to tell whether to continue or stop.
- Naively widening the observation horizon to fix this is computationally expensive since encoding and fusing high-dimensional visual inputs at every timestep is costly.
- No benchmark exists with sufficient cyclic-task data, configurable repetition counts, and automatic evaluation tools to assess cyclic manipulation performance.

## Method

- ==Cost-aware history sampling==: dense sampling of cheap low-overhead proprioceptive (end-effector pose-difference) signals plus sparse heuristic frame sampling of expensive visual observations to expand horizon cheaply.
- ==Multi-task historical understanding==: an auxiliary 10-way classification head predicts discretized task-progress bins alongside the ==diffusion action loss==, forcing progression-discriminative features.
- **CycleManip** Benchmark: built on RoboTwin 2.0 with configurable cyclic-action data collection and automatic evaluation via ==contact state-machines== or ==pose peak-detection== to verify correct cycle counts.

## Results

- Averaged across 8 cyclic benchmark tasks CycleManip reaches **53-97%** success versus DP3's best of **48%**, RDT's **42%**, and Pi-0's **19%**, with far lower cycle-count deviation.
- Real-world tests across gripper, dexterous-hand, and humanoid platforms show large gains over DP3, e.g. knife cutting **75%** vs **0%** and drum beating **90%** vs **0%**.
- Ablations show each component adds value (block hammering **37.5%** to **62.5%** with sampling alone, to **93.75%** with progress prediction), and plugging into Pi-0 lifts bottle shaking from **19%** to **72%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Non-Markovian cyclic tasks need explicit progress-tracking signals, not just longer context, since raw historical frames alone can look ambiguous.
> - Splitting sampling budget by observation cost (dense cheap proprioception, sparse expensive vision) is a general way to extend temporal horizon without inflating compute.
> - Auxiliary progress-prediction objectives can be a lightweight plug-and-play add-on that improves other pretrained imitation and VLA policies without architectural surgery.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing towards enabling robots to autonomously perform a wide array of daily tasks. Current dominant methodologies, such as Imitation Learning (IL) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, have demonstrated capabilities in modeling complex data distributions and predicting actions based on current observations for sequential tasks. These approaches typically rely on short observation windows for action prediction.

However, a specific subset of tasks, termed "cycle-based manipulation" or "cyclic tasks," involving repetitive actions with an expected terminal time, presents a challenge for these traditional methods. Examples include shaking a bottle a specified number of times or tapping an object repeatedly. The core issue is the non-Markovian nature of these tasks, where the appropriate action at any given moment depends not only on the current observation but also on the accumulated progress within a cycle. Policies relying on short observation windows struggle to infer cycle progression because observations across different cycles can appear visually similar, leading to issues such as premature termination or infinite looping.

Previous work has addressed historical modeling in other contexts, such as large language models, vision-language navigation, and video generation, often through retrieval, large kernels, or memory caching. In robot manipulation, some methods integrate historical visual information for visual memory, primarily for long-horizon tasks. For cyclic tasks, some traditional control strategies exist, but they can be limited in adaptability. Deep learning methods have been attempted for simpler cyclic tasks, but these often involve fixed cycle times, are limited to specific scenarios, or rely on external auxiliary models.

This paper positions itself by identifying a gap in existing robotic manipulation research concerning the effective and end-to-end handling of diverse cyclic tasks with variable cycle times. It seeks to overcome the limitations of short-term observation windows and the lack of historical understanding in current imitation learning policies without introducing significant computational overhead or requiring additional auxiliary models. Furthermore, the paper addresses the absence of a standardized benchmark with sufficient data and automatic evaluation tools for cycle-based manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to perform cycle-based manipulation tasks reliably and accurately, where repetitive actions must be executed for a specified number of cycles and terminated at the correct moment. This capability is considered crucial for robots operating in daily life scenarios, as many household and industrial tasks involve repetitive actions, such as dispensing syrup multiple times or mixing contents by shaking.

The motivation for this work stems from two main challenges identified in the current state of robotic manipulation:

1. **Ineffective Utilization of History in Imitation Learning:** Existing imitation learning methods and VLA models primarily focus on predicting actions based on short observation windows. For cyclic tasks, this approach is insufficient because the visual observations after each repetition within a cycle often appear nearly identical. Consequently, the model struggles to discern the current stage of the cycle or the number of repetitions completed, leading to failures such as continuing actions indefinitely or stopping prematurely. An intuitive solution of expanding the observation horizon is problematic due to the substantial computational cost and latency associated with encoding and fusing high-dimensional visual inputs at every timestep. This necessitates a method that can efficiently perceive and understand historical information without excessive computational burden.

2. **Absence of a Standardized Benchmark and Evaluation Tools:** The lack of a dedicated benchmark with diverse cyclic task data and automatic evaluation mechanisms has hindered the development and rigorous assessment of solutions for this specific problem domain. Without such resources, comparing different methodologies and tracking progress in cyclic manipulation becomes difficult.

To address these challenges, the paper proposes the CycleManip framework with the following detailed objectives:

- To develop an end-to-end imitation learning framework that can perform cycle-based manipulation without requiring extra models, hierarchical structures, or significant computational overhead.
- To enhance the policy's ability to effectively perceive historical information by proposing a cost-aware sampling strategy that selectively samples high- and low-overhead observations.
- To improve the policy's understanding of historical progression within cycles by incorporating a multi-task learning objective that encourages the model to predict the current phase of the task.
- To introduce a comprehensive cycle-based task manipulation benchmark that provides diverse simulated tasks, automated data generation, and an accurate, automatic evaluation system to facilitate future research in this area.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to make robots more capable of handling common, repetitive tasks in dynamic, real-world environments, thereby advancing the state-of-the-art in autonomous robotic behavior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CycleManip framework is designed to address cyclic manipulation tasks using an end-to-end imitation learning paradigm. The core of the approach involves enhancing the robot's ability to perceive and understand historical information without incurring substantial computational overhead.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework Architecture

The policy $\pi$ learns to predict the next action $a_t$ based on a language command $lan$ and the historical observations $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^t$. The framework processes user instructions and robot observations to execute operational tasks containing cyclic actions.

#### 3.2. Effective Historical Perception

To address the challenge of limited observation horizons and high computational costs, the framework introduces a **cost-aware history sampling strategy**. This strategy differentiates between observation types based on their computational overhead:

- **Low-overhead observations ($o_l$):** These include robot proprioception data, such as the pose difference of the end effector. The end effector pose difference is selected because its cyclic nature is more apparent and less prone to bias from absolute positions. For these observations, a **dense and broad sampling strategy** is employed, incorporating all past low-overhead observations. This is feasible due to their inexpensive encoding cost.
- **High-overhead observations ($o_h$):** These include high-dimensional visual inputs like RGB images or point clouds. For these, a **heuristic frame sampling strategy** is used to select past frames that offer greater temporal diversity while maintaining a consistent and manageable number of samples ($K_{high}$). Specifically, it collects $0.5 \cdot K_{high}$ frames using right-sided binary sampling from the first frame to the current frame $t$, and another $0.5 \cdot K_{high}$ frames using exponential sampling (e.g., $t-2^k$) from the latest frame $t$.

The sampled high- and low-overhead observations, along with the language command, are then used as input to the policy.

#### 3.3. Effective Historical Understanding

To enable the policy to truly understand the progression of cyclic tasks, the framework integrates a **multi-task learning strategy**. This component aims to encourage the learning of progression-discriminative features:

- **Auxiliary Task:** An auxiliary task is introduced where the model predicts the current phase or progress of the overall task. This progress ($b_t$) is defined as the current frame number divided by the total number of frames for that task, normalized to the interval.
- **Discretization:** The continuous progress value $b_t$ is uniformly partitioned into ten bins, and its corresponding class label $y_t$ is used for a 10-way classification objective. This classification encourages the model to develop distinct feature representations for different stages of the cycle, as the supervisory signals change with task progression.
- **Feature Fusion:** A multi-layer MLP fuses the low- and high-overhead observation features before they are used for both the action prediction and the single-layer MLP for progress prediction, mitigating the risk of overfitting the multi-task head.

#### 3.4. Framework Implementation

- **Input Encoding:**
 - Language command ($lan$) is encoded by a CLIP encoder.
 - High-overhead observation features ($f_h$) are encoded by a point encoder (e.g., for point clouds).
 - Low-overhead observation features ($f_l$) are processed by a Transformer encoder, extracting global features from a CLS token and local features from recent frames using an MLP.
- **Feature Concatenation and Fusion:** The encoded language feature $f_{lan}$ and the fused observation features ($f_{lh}$ from $f_l$ and $f_h$ via MLPs) are concatenated to form a condition feature.
- **Action Prediction:** A diffusion model (DDIM) is employed for action prediction, using FiLM conditioning with the concatenated condition feature.
- **Loss Function:** The overall loss function combines a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for action prediction ($a_t$ vs. ground truth $a_t^*$) and a Cross-Entropy (CE) loss for the auxiliary progress prediction ($b_t$ vs. ground truth $b_t^*$), weighted by coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ respectively.

#### 3.5. CycleManip Benchmark

To support the framework and facilitate research, a new benchmark for cyclic manipulation tasks is introduced, built upon the RoboTwin 2.0 platform:

- **Task Environments:** The benchmark includes 8 diverse simulated cyclic manipulation tasks (e.g., Block Hammering, Bottle Shaking, Carrot Cutting).
- **Automated Data Generation:** Loop control functionality is integrated into the data collection pipeline, allowing for generation of expert demonstrations with varying numbers of repetitions (1 to 8 cycles). Each demonstration is annotated with the target cycle time, completed cycles per timestep, and object 6D poses. 200 trajectories are collected per task.
- **Automatic Evaluation System:** An automated system is designed to determine successful task completion and accurate cycle counts. For tasks involving physical contact (e.g., hammering, cutting), a state-machine-based collision detection system counts cycles. For non-contact tasks (e.g., shaking, stirring), a peak detection algorithm on object pose trajectories estimates cycle times. This system generates a detailed report, and its accuracy was manually validated.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted in both simulation (CycleManip and RoboTwin 2.0 benchmarks) and real-world settings. Real-world evaluations used diverse robotic platforms, including single-arm and dual-arm grippers (AgileX Piper), dexterous hands (BrainCO Revo2), and a humanoid robot (Unitree G1). Visual observations were captured by an Intel RealSense L515 depth camera. 6 real-world cyclic tasks were used, with 50-150 teleoperated demonstrations collected for each using Gello, TypeTele, and OpenWBC platforms.

Evaluation metrics included **Success Rate (Suc.)** for both task completion and correct cycle count, and **Cycle Count Deviation (Cyc.)** for cyclic tasks. For general tasks, the Success Rate from the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark was used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive experimental results in both simulation and real-world environments to validate the CycleManip framework.

#### 4.1. Performance on Cyclic Manipulation Tasks

- **Simulation (Table 1):** CycleManip significantly outperformed state-of-the-art baselines (DP, DP3, RDT, Pi-0) across all 8 simulated cyclic manipulation tasks in terms of both Success Rate (Suc. %) and Cycle Count Deviation (Cyc.). For example, in 'block hammering', CycleManip achieved 86% success with 0.25 cycle deviation, compared to DP (8% Suc., 8.33 Cyc.) and DP3 (23% Suc., 5.55 Cyc.). Pi-0, relying on 1-frame observations, showed the lowest success rates. This indicates that CycleManip's enhanced historical perception and understanding addressed the core challenges that hindered previous methods.
- **Real-world (Table 2):** In real-world experiments, CycleManip demonstrated superior performance compared to the DP3 baseline across 6 diverse cyclic tasks on various robotic platforms. For instance, in 'block hammering' with a single gripper, CycleManip achieved a 93.75% success rate and 0.125 cycle deviation, significantly outperforming DP3's 37.5% success rate and 1.12 cycle deviation. This consistency between simulation and real-world results validates the framework's robustness and practical applicability.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Historical Perception and Understanding (Ablation Studies)

Ablation studies were conducted, particularly shown in Table 2 for real-world tasks, to assess the contribution of the core components:

- **Historical Perception:** Augmenting the baseline (DP3) with the cost-aware sampling strategy (labeled "w/o Task," meaning CycleManip without the multi-task learning for historical understanding) led to a notable performance increase across all tasks. For example, in 'block hammering', success increased from 37.5% (DP3) to 62.5% ('w/o Task'). This demonstrates that the cost-aware sampling strategy effectively provides the policy with necessary historical observations.
- **Historical Understanding:** Further integration of the Historical Understanding component (multi-task learning for progress prediction) into the framework resulted in additional performance improvements (e.g., 'block hammering' success rose from 62.5% to 93.75%). This suggests that the multi-task objective enables the model to not only perceive history but also to actively understand its structure and current progression stage, leading to more reliable and precise cyclic task execution.

#### 4.3. Performance in General Manipulation Tasks

- **Generalization (Table 3):** The CycleManip method was evaluated on general manipulation tasks from the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark. It achieved the highest success rates across all evaluated tasks (e.g., 'place cans', 'handover block', 'open microwave'), outperforming RDT, Pi-0, DP, and DP3. This indicates that the historical perception and understanding mechanisms developed for cyclic tasks also provide benefits for general manipulation scenarios, demonstrating the method's robustness and generalization capability.

#### 4.4. Adaptability to Heterogeneous Embodiments

- **Diverse Platforms (Table 2 & Figure 1):** The real-world experiments showed that CycleManip adapted effectively to diverse robotic platforms, including single-arm grippers, bi-arm grippers, dexterous hands, and humanoid robots. The model consistently delivered robust performance by extending proprioceptive sensing and action dimensions to match each robot's specific configurations, showcasing its versatility.

#### 4.5. Plug-and-Play Experiment

- **VLA Model Integration (Table 4):** CycleManip was demonstrated to be compatible as a plug-and-play component with existing imitation policies, specifically Vision-Language-Action models like Pi-0. When integrated into Pi-0, the framework significantly improved performance on cyclic tasks (e.g., 'bottle shaking' success increased from 19% to 72%). This highlights its modularity and potential to enhance other advanced robotic control systems.

#### 4.6. Efficiency Analysis

- **Computational Overhead (Table 5):** An efficiency analysis comparing CycleManip with DP3 on the 'carrot-cutting' task showed that CycleManip achieved a higher success rate (86% vs. 38%) with only marginal increases in per-step training time (0.102s vs. 0.073s), inference time (0.0953s vs. 0.0893s), and GPU memory usage (17342MB vs. 16796MB training; 6003MB vs. 5801MB testing). This indicates that the enhancements in historical awareness were achieved without a substantial increase in computational overhead.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CycleManip framework addresses a significant and previously underexplored challenge in robotic manipulation: the reliable execution of cyclic or repetitive tasks with specified termination conditions. This work contributes to the field in several ways:

1. **Enabling Cyclic Manipulation:** By explicitly tackling the non-Markovian nature of cyclic tasks through enhanced historical perception and understanding, CycleManip enables robots to perform a crucial category of daily tasks that were previously difficult for conventional imitation learning methods. This expands the repertoire of tasks that robots can autonomously manage in real-world environments.

2. **Novel End-to-End Framework:** The proposed framework provides an end-to-end imitation learning solution that integrates historical information efficiently without relying on additional models, complex hierarchical structures, or substantial computational overhead. This approach promotes practicality and scalability, making it more feasible for real-world deployment.

3. **Cost-Aware Historical Perception:** The introduction of a cost-aware sampling strategy is a significant methodological contribution. By selectively and efficiently sampling low- and high-overhead observations, the framework effectively expands the observation horizon without incurring prohibitive computational costs, which is critical for real-time robotic control.

4. **Effective Historical Understanding:** The multi-task learning objective for predicting task progression offers a mechanism for policies to develop a deeper understanding of the temporal structure of cyclic tasks. This fosters discriminative feature learning, leading to more accurate decision-making regarding action continuation or termination.

5. **Introduction of a Standardized Benchmark:** The CycleManip benchmark, complete with diverse tasks, automated data generation, and evaluation tools, provides a valuable resource for the robotics community. It establishes a platform for consistent evaluation and comparison of future methods in cyclic manipulation, which is essential for accelerating research progress in this area.

6. **Robustness, Generalization, and Adaptability:** The experimental results demonstrate the framework's robustness across various cyclic tasks, its generalization capabilities to general manipulation tasks, and its adaptability to diverse robotic embodiments (grippers, dexterous hands, humanoid robots). This versatility suggests broad applicability and potential for integration into different robotic systems.

7. **Plug-and-Play Compatibility:** The demonstration of CycleManip as a plug-and-play component for existing VLA models indicates its potential to enhance the capabilities of other advanced robot control systems, making them more adept at handling repetitive actions.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. By enabling robots to reliably perform cyclic tasks, CycleManip contributes to the development of more autonomous and adaptable robotic agents. This could lead to advancements in various domains, including household robotics, industrial automation, laboratory automation, and human-robot collaboration, where repetitive actions are common. The framework represents a step towards robots that can interact with their environment in a more sophisticated and human-like manner, executing tasks not just sequentially but also with a learned understanding of cyclical progression.
