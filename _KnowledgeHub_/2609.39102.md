# False Frontiers: Diagnosing and Mitigating Co-Cheating in Self-Evolving Search Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> CrossFit is a feedback rule for self-evolving proposer-solver search agents. It splits source documents into two fixed folds, and each fold's new questions are scored by an auxiliary solver trained only on the other fold. This breaks the loop in which shared pseudo-label errors come back as proposer reward. On Qwen3.5-4B and 9B, it roughly halves false agreement and raises the seven-benchmark QA average by 8.8 and 8.4 points over Dr. Zero.

## Problem

- Self-evolving proposer-solver loops such as Dr. Zero reward agreement between the solver and pseudo-labels, so agreement becomes an in-loop stand-in for correctness that can drift away from truth.
- The solver learns a source's wrong pseudo-labels and then repeats them on later questions from that source, so false agreement rises over rounds while lost credit falls.
- Checking proposals before training with majority voting from the same model cannot remove shared errors, costs six extra generations per candidate, and leaves the feedback-ancestry path open.

## Method

- ==Co-cheating audit==: an external judge builds evidence-backed references from source documents and measures false-agreement mass, meaning pairs that match on the same wrong answer, without affecting training.
- ==Multi-sample verification (MSV)==: three samples with the source and three without it, all from the same model. A task is admitted only if both majorities exist and agree, and their answer replaces the draft label.
- **CrossFit**: each source document is fixed to one of two folds. Auxiliary solvers trained on one fold score the other fold's questions under Dr. Zero's ==frontier reward==, and the main solver still trains on all admitted tasks.

## Results

- After round 3, CrossFit cuts false-agreement mass from **6.1% to 3.0%** at 4B and from **8.8% to 3.7%** at 9B. MSV reaches only **5.7% and 7.2%**.
- The seven-benchmark Cover-EM average reaches **0.488 and 0.512**, beating Dr. Zero by **8.8 and 8.4 points** and Search-R1 by **8.7 and 7.8**. Multi-hop gains average **10.0 and 10.9 points**. MSV adds only **0.3** on top.
- On a fixed replay bank of **3,000** questions, excluding by source ID cuts false agreement to **0.4% and 0.1%**. Same-source, full-data and random-row auxiliary solvers stay between **5.0% and 8.7%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - In closed self-training loops, rising internal agreement cannot be trusted without an external audit. Shared errors can look like progress, so track false agreement as well as reward.
> - Where the feedback comes from matters more than label quality. Keeping an evaluator off the data behind the question beats extra verification, which shares the same model's blind spots.
> - Splits for held-out evaluators must follow provenance, such as the source document, not individual examples. Row-level splits leak correlated errors and give only modest gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In self-evolving search training, a **proposer** turns source documents into questions and draft answers. A **solver** trains on the admitted question–answer pairs, and the solver's agreement with later drafts rewards the proposer. The practical appeal is less reliance on human-written QA training data. The risk is that agreement is a useful learning signal only while it tracks correctness. A wrong draft can train the solver to repeat the error on questions from the same source, and that agreement can then earn proposer reward. The authors' remedy is to have a solver trained on different source documents provide the proposer's feedback.

The standard coupled loop over 129 scheduled training steps for Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B. Panels (a,b) plot adopted-label truth, solver truth, and agreement against training step. Panels (c,d) plot lost-credit mass L against false-agreement mass F, with arrows marking round order. Agreement rises as false agreement grows and lost credit falls.

### 2. When agreement stops meaning truth

The authors call this failure **co-cheating**. The term names a self-reinforcing optimization outcome, not intentional collusion. They measure it with **false-agreement mass**, the fraction of evaluated label–response pairs that match the same answer even though the audit judges that answer wrong. **Lost credit** is the opposite case: a correct solver response is rejected by a wrong label.

The audit is post-hoc. It saves the source, the adopted label, and the five solver responses used for proposer reward. An external LLM judge then checks them against an evidence-backed reference. Unresolved cases remain unresolved, and coverage is about 86%. In the standard coupled loop, false agreement rises across rounds while lost credit falls. It goes from 0.4% to 6.1% for Qwen3.5-4B and from 0.3% to 8.8% for Qwen3.5-9B. These are audited rates, not evidence of intentional coordination.

### 3. Exclude the source, not just the label

**CrossFit** assigns each source document permanently to one of two fixed folds. Auxiliary feedback solvers train only on their own fold. A question is then scored by the solver trained on the other fold, so a same-source pseudo-label cannot be directly replayed by the solver that rewards the proposer. The split is made by source rather than by question. The feedback rule itself is unchanged; only the solver supplying the signal changes. The main solver is not split and still trains on all admitted tasks.

Sources produce question–label tasks, and documents are divided into fixed Fold 0 and Fold 1 groups. In the next round, frozen auxiliary solvers trained on one fold score questions from the other. Five rollouts per question produce a match count and reward for the proposer. The main solver trains on all tasks before downstream evaluation.

The strongest test replays the same 3,000 proposals while varying only how the feedback solver was trained. On this fixed bank, source-ID exclusion reduces false agreement from 5.8% to 0.4% at 4B and from 7.3% to 0.1% at 9B. Same-source and full-data auxiliary feedback stay near the coupled result. Random-row splitting helps only modestly. A half-budget source-ID control performs nearly as well as the full source-ID result. In the adaptive runs, CrossFit reduces round-three false agreement to 3.0% and 3.7%.

This supports feedback ancestry as an important mechanism in the tested setup. It does not show that ancestry is the only source of error, because solvers may still share pretraining errors or overlapping evidence.

### 4. Downstream result and cost

On a seven-benchmark evaluation, Qwen3.5-4B CrossFit reaches **0.488 average Cover-EM**, compared with **0.400** for standard Dr. Zero. Cover-EM counts a prediction correct when a nonempty normalized reference answer appears in the normalized prediction. The average weights the seven benchmark scores equally. Evaluation uses one greedy search trajectory per question, with identical tool budgets and answer extraction.

The result shows better measured benchmark performance, not better efficiency. CrossFit adds 50 auxiliary solver updates per round. At 4B, its reserved H200-hour budget is 650, compared with Dr. Zero's 379. These reserved hours include waiting time.

### 5. Track the feedback's history

Solver agreement can reward errors when the evaluator has learned pseudo-labels from the same source it scores. CrossFit's source-level split reduces that false agreement and improves measured benchmark scores, at added training cost. The authors conclude that feedback should be tracked by the training history of its evaluator. Shared pretraining errors remain a possible source of correlated mistakes.
