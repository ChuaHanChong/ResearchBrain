# Reverse to Advance: Teleoperation-Cost Effective Hard Policy Learning from Reversed Easy Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Hong Kong developed Auto-E2H, a framework that reduces the teleoperation cost for learning complex robotic manipulation by leveraging demonstrations of easier, temporally reversed tasks. This system autonomously collects and refines data, achieving an average hard-task success rate of 96.3% in Isaac Lab simulations and 70.0% in real-world experiments, while reducing active recording time by up to 69.7% per demonstration compared to direct methods.

## Problem

- Collecting high-quality expert demonstrations for complex robotic manipulation tasks through teleoperation is labor-intensive, costly, and prone to human inconsistencies.
- Existing imitation learning and reinforcement learning methods struggle with data efficiency, especially for tasks requiring high precision and intricate contact dynamics.
- Temporal reversal techniques in robotic learning often focus on enforcing physical reversibility or shaping rewards, but do not fully exploit the asymmetry between a hard forward task and an easier reverse task for data acquisition.

## Method

- The **Auto-E2H** framework integrates ==closed-loop data collection==, ==hierarchical data refinement==, and ==iterative policy learning== to leverage easily collected reverse-task demonstrations.
- It employs an autonomous closed-loop collection strategy, alternating between hard and easy task policies to generate diverse trajectory data and reset environments without constant human intervention.
- A hierarchical data refinement pipeline filters reversed easy-task trajectories, using ==kinematic prior filtering== to remove unrealistic motion and ==critic-guided advantage filtering== to retain segments that actively contribute to hard-task progress.

## Results

- Auto-E2H consistently improved hard-task success rates over iterations, reaching **96.3%** average in Isaac Lab simulations and **70.0%** in real-world robot experiments, surpassing baselines like TR-DRL-DP by **45.0 percentage points** in real-world tasks.
- The framework reduced active human teleoperation recording time by **50.0%** to **69.7%** per demonstration compared to directly collecting hard-task demonstrations for the same policy budget.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of both kinematic (improving success from **28.1%** to **96.3%**) and critic-guided filters (from **44.4%** to **96.3%**) for effectively utilizing reversed data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Many complex manipulation tasks exhibit a directional asymmetry where the 'forward hard task' is much more difficult to teleoperate than its 'reverse easy task'.
> - Temporally inverting demonstrations from mastered easy reverse tasks can provide a rich, scalable source of supervision for the corresponding hard task, despite initial noise.
> - Effective noise filtering, combining kinematic priors with a progress-aware critic, is crucial to transform noisy reversed trajectories into reliable training data for precision-demanding tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically addressing challenges in imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) for complex tasks. Imitation Learning has demonstrated capabilities in mapping observations to actions, notably with Diffusion Policies. However, its effectiveness is often constrained by the availability and quality of expert demonstrations. Collecting these demonstrations is labor-intensive, costly, and prone to human inconsistencies, especially for tasks requiring high precision or involving intricate contact dynamics.

Existing research has focused on improving data efficiency and utility in teleoperation. This includes approaches such as portable teleoperation systems, data augmentation, human-in-the-loop refinement, and leveraging pre-trained multimodal models. Some methods, like GR-RL, refine data by identifying low-quality transitions based on task progress, while TR-DRL exploits dynamics-consistent filters to identify reversible transitions for data augmentation and reward shaping.

A distinct line of research explores temporal or time-reversal symmetries, primarily within reinforcement learning contexts, to improve sample efficiency or exploit structural properties. While these methods acknowledge the concept of reversibility, they often focus on enforcing physical reversibility or shaping rewards based on object states. The current work differentiates itself by integrating automated data collection, explicit noise filtering, and policy learning from temporally reversed easy-task trajectories, specifically to address the high teleoperation cost of acquiring expert demonstrations for complex, "hard" tasks. The paper positions its contribution as a method for learning complex manipulation policies under teleoperation cost constraints, by leveraging an observed forward-reverse task asymmetry that is often under-exploited in current approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the significant cost associated with collecting high-quality teleoperation datasets, particularly for complex and challenging robotic manipulation tasks. The authors observe a directional asymmetry in many manipulation scenarios: the "forward hard task" (e.g., inserting a peg into a hole, stacking blocks) is considerably more difficult to execute precisely than its "reverse easy task" (e.g., extracting a peg, de-stacking blocks). The forward task typically involves navigating narrow state-space funnels with high-precision contact, whereas the reverse task often expands into free space, making it more amenable to human teleoperation or automated execution.

This observation leads to the central objective: to determine if easily collected demonstrations from these reverse tasks can be effectively leveraged to bootstrap the learning of the original hard task, thereby drastically reducing human labor. The core hypothesis is that trajectories from mastered easy reverse tasks, when temporally inverted, could provide a rich source of supervision for the corresponding hard task.

However, the authors identify several challenges that need to be addressed to realize this objective:
1. **Automatic Data Collection:** The need to construct a largely automated pipeline for acquiring reversed-task demonstrations to minimize human teleoperation effort. This involves developing a system that can generate continuous and diverse trajectories without constant manual intervention for environment resets.
2. **Data Filtering:** The recognition that simply reversing easy-task trajectories is often insufficient, as they can be noisy, contain suboptimal behaviors, and lack the corrective actions necessary for the precision requirements of the hard task. Therefore, a method is needed to identify and extract high-quality, valuable segments from this noisy reversed data to serve as reliable supervision.
3. **Progressive Learning:** The necessity of integrating the automated data collection and filtering processes into an online learning loop. This loop should allow the policy to continuously improve by leveraging the filtered data, progressively approaching optimal performance on the hard task while adapting to covariate shift.

The overarching goal is to propose a teleoperation-cost effective framework, named Auto-E2H (Automated Easy-to-Hard Data Acquisition and Refinement), that converts easy reverse executions into useful supervision for hard manipulation, reducing reliance on costly forward demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Auto-E2H, is designed for time-reversal-based self-improving hard-task policy learning. It integrates three main components to address the identified challenges: closed-loop data collection, hierarchical data refinement, and iterative policy learning. The underlying policy backbone utilized across the framework and for baselines is an image-conditioned Diffusion Policy.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work considers a finite-horizon manipulation task, T_h, characterized by high difficulty due to complex dynamics. A corresponding reverse-direction task, T_e, is defined, assumed to be easier to solve. Both tasks share common observation (S), action (A), and horizon (H) spaces. A successful easy-task trajectory τ_e is assumed to yield meaningful supervision for T_h when temporally reversed (˜τ_h = Reverse(τ_e)). The objective is to learn a policy π_h for T_h using a limited set of hard-task demonstrations (D_h) combined with a larger, easily collected set of easy-task demonstrations (D_e).

#### 3.2. Overview of Auto-E2H

Auto-E2H operates in an iterative loop:
1. **Closed-Loop Data Collection:** Alternates between executing an easy-task policy and the evolving hard-task policy to autonomously reset the environment and generate diverse trajectory data buffers (R_h, R_e).
2. **Hierarchical Time-Reversal Refinement:** Easy-task trajectories from R_e are reversed, then filtered in two stages: kinematic prior filtering (removes static/low-speed segments) and critic-guided advantage filtering (prunes low-progress segments). This yields a refined dataset (D_filt).
3. **Iterative Policy Learning:** The refined dataset is combined with the initial hard-task demonstrations (D_h) to update the hard-task policy (π_h), which then informs the next data collection cycle.

#### 3.3. Closed-Loop Data Collection

To enable efficient, scalable, and minimally human-reliant data acquisition, Auto-E2H employs an autonomous closed-loop collection strategy. This strategy alternates between executing the current hard-task policy (π_h) and a fixed initial easy-task policy (π_e) to reset the environment for each other.
- In each collection cycle, the current π_h is executed on T_h, yielding a trajectory τ_h.
- Subsequently, starting from the terminal state of τ_h, π_e is executed on T_e, producing τ_e.
- The terminal state of τ_e then serves as the initial state for the subsequent hard-task rollout, forming a self-sustaining interaction loop.
This process, after an initial external reset, can be repeated over multiple cycles without manual intervention, supporting scalable data generation. All hard-task trajectories (including failures) are recorded in R_h for critic training, while only successful easy-task trajectories are retained in R_e, as they provide meaningful supervision upon temporal inversion. This mechanism expands the initial-state distribution for the hard-task policy but necessitates subsequent data refinement due to introduced noise.

#### 3.4. Hierarchical Data Refinement

To address the noisiness of reversed trajectories, a three-step hierarchical refinement process is applied to easy-task rollouts:

##### 3.4.1. Data Preprocessing

For each successful easy-task rollout τ_e, a candidate hard-task trajectory ˜τ_h is formed by reversing its state sequence. The action sequence (a'\_t) for ˜τ_h is not directly reused from τ_e but reconstructed from adjacent reversed end-effector poses and gripper states using a conversion function h(·). This process generates the initial candidate dataset D_cand.

##### 3.4.2. Kinematic Prior Filtering (F_kin)

This filter aims to remove physically unrealistic or inefficient motion segments. It comprises two sub-filters:
- **Static Filter (F_static):** Identifies and eliminates contiguous intervals where the end-effector's instantaneous displacement (v_t) is below a threshold (ϵ_s), and the total spatial drift over these steps does not exceed another threshold (ϵ_d) for a minimum number of steps (L_s). This targets segments of unnaturally slow or frozen motion, potentially caused by latency or oscillations.
- **Speed Filter (F_speed):** For segments where velocity is above ϵ_s but still below a low-speed threshold (ϵ_l), the motion is resampled to accelerate it towards a target step size (∆H). This regularizes the action distribution towards more dynamic and consistent trajectories. Segments with velocity exceeding ϵ_l are considered natural and left unaltered.
The output of this stage is the kinematically cleaned dataset D_kin.

##### 3.4.3. Critic-Guided Advantage Filtering (F_critic)

This stage ensures that the remaining kinematically clean segments actively contribute to hard-task progress.
- A value function V_ϕ(s) (critic) is trained from scratch at each iteration using all hard-task rollouts in R_h. The critic uses a sparse terminal reward (1 for success, 0 otherwise) and a small per-step penalty, calibrated to reflect state proximity to hard-task success. Retraining at each iteration allows the critic to track the current policy's state distribution and failure modes.
- For each reversed trajectory, an optional truncation removes irrelevant post-contact retreat phases.
- A per-step progress score is computed using temporal-difference advantages. Frames with a smoothed advantage score below a threshold (α_drop, set to the 30th percentile) are marked for removal. Minimum run lengths are enforced for both retained and dropped segments to prevent excessive fragmentation.
The final output of the hierarchical refinement is the dataset D_filt, consisting of high-quality, progress-aligned reversed trajectories.

#### 3.5. Iterative Policy Learning

The hard-task policy π_h is progressively improved over N iterations. In each iteration:
1. The current hard-task policy π_h and the fixed initial easy-task policy π_e are used to collect closed-loop rollouts (R_h, R_e).
2. Successful easy-task rollouts are inverted and refined through the hierarchical filtering process to obtain D_filt.
3. The hard-task policy π_h is updated by training on a combined dataset of the initial limited hard-task demonstrations (D_h) and the newly refined reversed trajectories (D_filt). This continuous loop leverages the expanded hard-task coverage while the iteration-specific critic suppresses time-reversal noise.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Auto-E2H was conducted across various simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against several baselines: TR-DRL, RECAP, TR-DRL-DP (a Diffusion Policy adaptation of TR-DRL), and a direct hard-demo baseline (Direct-H).

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

Auto-E2H consistently achieved monotonic performance gains over multiple self-improvement iterations.
- In Isaac Lab simulations (4 tasks), the average hard-task success rate increased from 33.1% to 96.3%.
- In Robosuite simulations (2 tasks), the average success rate rose from 0.0% to 85.0%.
- In real-world experiments (4 tasks), the average success rate improved from 40.0% to 70.0%.
In all suites, the 95% Wilson binomial confidence intervals for the initial and final policies did not overlap, indicating statistically significant improvements. The easy-task policy, utilized for environment resets, demonstrated an average success rate of 84.4% in real-world rollouts, reducing manual intervention.

#### 4.2. Simulation Benchmarks (Isaac Lab and Robosuite)

- **Isaac Lab (Figure 4, tasks 1-4):** Auto-E2H achieved an average success rate of 96.3% at the final iteration, with individual tasks ranging from 92.5% to 100.0%. This performance exceeded TR-DRL-DP by 83.8 percentage points (pp) and RECAP by 46.9 pp, with non-overlapping confidence intervals.
- **Robosuite (Figure 4, tasks 5-6):** Starting from 0% success, Auto-E2H reached an 85.0% two-task average (88.0% for nut assembly, 82.0% for peg insertion). This surpassed TR-DRL by 17.0 pp, indicating that Auto-E2H's supervised policy-update signal produced stronger hard-task policies and was compatible with stable real-world rollouts.

#### 4.3. Real-world Robot Experiments

- **Real-world (Figure 5, 4 tasks):** Over four iterations, Auto-E2H's average success rate increased by 30.0 pp (from 40.0% to 70.0%). At the final iteration, Auto-E2H outperformed TR-DRL-DP by 45.0 pp and RECAP by 52.5 pp. Both baselines experienced performance collapse on three of the four real-world tasks.

#### 4.4. Filtering Behavior Comparison

A visualization of filtering decisions (Figure 6) demonstrated Auto-E2H's hierarchical approach effectively removing static segments, resampling low-speed segments, and pruning low-progress segments identified by the critic. For instance, a decline in critic value was observed to correspond to a misalignment, indicating potential failure. In contrast, TR-DRL's dynamic filter primarily rejected segments with object contact or fast gripper motion, retaining many static or hesitant frames that provided little environmental interaction, thus yielding a weaker improvement signal.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies on Isaac Lab tasks (Figure 7) showed the contribution of each component:
- The full Auto-E2H pipeline achieved 96.3% average success.
- Removing the kinematic filter (w/o kin.) reduced success to 28.1%.
- Removing the critic-guided filter (w/o critic) resulted in 44.4% success.
- Training with only successful hard-task rollouts (Direct-H) yielded 16.3% success.
These results indicate the complementary nature of the components: kinematic filtering addresses artifacts, the critic selects progress-making segments, and reversed data mitigates the sparsity of hard-task successes.

#### 4.6. Data Collection Strategies

A comparison of data collection efforts (Table 1) revealed that collecting 50 easy-task demonstrations for Auto-E2H reduced active recording time by 50.0–69.7% per demonstration compared to directly collecting 50 hard-task demonstrations. Furthermore, Auto-E2H's resulting policies achieved a 10–20 pp higher final task success rate across all four real-world tasks with the same budget. This suggests that Auto-E2H significantly reduces operator workload due to shorter recording times for simpler tasks and a high automatic-reset rate.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Auto-E2H as a framework that addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: the high cost and difficulty of collecting expert demonstrations for complex tasks. By systematically exploiting the observed directional asymmetry between "hard" forward tasks and "easy" reverse tasks, the framework offers a method for scalable supervision.

The significance of this work lies in several aspects:
- **Cost Reduction in Data Collection:** Auto-E2H substantially reduces the human teleoperation effort required for acquiring high-quality datasets. This is achieved through its closed-loop data collection mechanism, which automates environment resets, and by leveraging easier-to-demonstrate reverse tasks rather than directly recording challenging forward tasks. This can broaden the accessibility of advanced robotic learning to scenarios with limited expert resources.
- **Improved Policy Performance and Stability:** The framework consistently demonstrates higher success rates and more stable training compared to existing reversal-based and reinforcement learning baselines in both simulated and real-world environments. This suggests an effective utilization of reversed data as a supervisory signal.
- **Robust Data Refinement:** The hierarchical data refinement pipeline, combining kinematic and critic-guided filtering, is critical for transforming noisy reversed trajectories into reliable supervisory signals. This ensures that the policy learns from high-quality, task-relevant motion, even when direct reversal introduces artifacts or suboptimal behaviors.
- **Online Learning Capabilities:** The iterative policy learning scheme allows for continuous self-improvement, progressively refining the hard-task policy without needing additional human demonstrations after initial training. This moves towards more autonomous and adaptive robotic systems.

The potential impact of Auto-E2H is in enabling more efficient and reliable learning of complex, high-precision manipulation tasks with minimal human intervention. This could accelerate the deployment of robots in various domains requiring dexterous manipulation, such as manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics.

While the framework is most suitable for tasks exhibiting directional asymmetry where temporal reversal maintains useful geometric supervision, the authors acknowledge limitations in scenarios with substantial changes in contact dynamics, strong frictional effects, unstable grasps, or non-reversible grasp/release events. Future research directions include developing stronger dynamics-aware reversal techniques, enhancing task-progress critics, and incorporating diversity objectives to further improve reversed supervision, thereby potentially extending the applicability of this approach to a wider range of manipulation problems.
