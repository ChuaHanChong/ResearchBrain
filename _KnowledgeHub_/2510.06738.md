# AWM: Accurate Weight-Matrix Fingerprint for Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed AWM, a training-free fingerprinting method for large language models that accurately verifies model lineage by analyzing weight matrices. AWM exhibits strong robustness against extensive post-training modifications and malicious weight manipulations, achieving perfect classification metrics and a near-zero false-positive rate across a diverse dataset of 150 LLM pairs.

## Problem

- Protecting the intellectual property of Large Language Models (LLMs) is crucial due to their high development costs, but verifying their true lineage amidst claims of independent training versus derivation is challenging.
- Existing white-box fingerprinting methods for LLMs often lack robustness against extensive post-training modifications (e.g., SFT, continued pretraining, pruning, upcycling) and deliberate weight manipulations (e.g., scaling, permutation, rotation).
- Current fingerprinting techniques frequently suffer from a high false-positive rate, leading to unreliable accusations of model theft and hindering fair evaluation.

## Method

- **AWM** (Accurate ==Weight-Matrix Fingerprint==) is a training-free method that leverages a theoretical analysis of how LLM architectural components (RMSNorm, RoPE, residual connections) constrain admissible weight manipulations that preserve model behavior.
- The method employs a two-stage similarity detection procedure: first, it extracts permutation and signature matrices from shared word embeddings using the Hungarian algorithm; second, it utilizes Unbiased Centered Kernel Alignment (UCKA) to robustly measure similarity between transformed Query and Key matrices across all layers.
- UCKA is specifically chosen for its invariance to orthogonal transformations and constant rescaling, which addresses the theoretically derived manipulations that maintain attention scores, making the comparison robust against various modifications.

## Results

- AWM achieved perfect classification metrics (AUC=1.0, pAUC=1.0, TPR@**1%**FPR=1.0) on a comprehensive testbed of 150 LLM pairs, flawlessly distinguishing between related and unrelated models.
- The method demonstrated a near-zero false-positive rate, with mean similarity scores for independent model pairs being **0.49%** (**7B** LLMs) and **0.26%** (**13B** LLMs), significantly outperforming baselines which exhibited high false positives.
- AWM maintained consistently high robustness across 60 offspring models subjected to diverse post-training processes (SFT, continued pretraining, RL, multi-modal extension, pruning, upcycling) and malicious weight manipulations, achieving an average absolute Z-score of 302.936, indicating superior separability compared to state-of-the-art methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Specific architectural elements within LLMs, such as RMSNorm and Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE), impose stringent mathematical constraints on how weight matrices (especially word embeddings and Q/K matrices) can be manipulated without altering model functionality.
> - By first identifying and neutralizing basic column-wise transformations (permutation and signature flips) on word embeddings and then applying a metric invariant to orthogonal transformations (UCKA) to Q/K matrices, robust and accurate lineage verification is achievable.
> - A truly effective LLM fingerprinting method must possess both extreme robustness to aggressive post-training and malicious manipulations, alongside a near-zero false-positive rate, to be reliable in real-world scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to significant advancements in artificial intelligence applications. However, the substantial computational resources and vast datasets required for training LLMs from scratch have resulted in many open-source models being released under specific licenses or requiring approval processes to protect intellectual property. This landscape has concurrently seen instances of developers circumventing these protections by modifying existing base LLMs and subsequently claiming them as independently trained models. Such practices necessitate robust methods for verifying the lineage of LLMs.

The broader research landscape for protecting intellectual property in machine learning models is generally categorized into two main approaches: watermarking and fingerprinting.

**Watermarking** involves embedding identifiable signals into models, typically during an additional training phase, to later prove ownership. Previous watermarking methods for smaller deep neural networks (DNNs) focused on encoding signals into weights or injecting triggers for specific outputs. For LLMs, watermarking techniques have been developed to inject identifiable patterns into generated text or model weights. However, a limitation of these methods is their potential to compromise LLM performance and their lack of robustness against extensive post-training modifications, as they must be applied prior to model release.

**Fingerprinting** methods, in contrast, aim to extract intrinsic model features as signatures without requiring additional training, thereby preserving model performance. These methods are further divided by the level of access available to the auditor:
- **White-box fingerprinting** assumes full access to model parameters. Prior work on small DNNs explored analyzing model behaviors on preset test cases. For LLMs, existing white-box methods include:
 - **Static methods:** HuRef derives invariant terms from weight matrices. However, it reportedly lacks robustness to extensive training. Other approaches use statistical measures like standard deviation distributions of attention matrices, but these may carry a risk of false positives.
 - **Dynamic methods:** These utilize intermediate activation values (e.g., DeepJudge, EasyDetector) or measure geometric similarity of representation spaces (e.g., REEF). REEF, in particular, has been noted for its high false-positive rate. More recent methods have investigated statistical features of gradients (e.g., TensorGuard).
- **Black-box fingerprinting** relies solely on API input-output interactions, common in Model-as-a-Service (MaaS) scenarios. These approaches either analyze output distributions for unique stylistic idiosyncrasies (e.g., LLMmap) or construct specific trigger queries designed to elicit predefined responses. A significant challenge for black-box methods is their robustness, as relying on surface-level outputs can lead to information loss regarding internal mechanisms, making them susceptible to disruption by post-training modifications like instruction tuning or system prompt changes.

The current work positions itself to address the identified limitations in existing fingerprinting methods, particularly the lack of robustness against aggressive and varied post-training processes and the persistent risk of false positives, especially in white-box scenarios. By proposing a training-free, weight-matrix-based fingerprinting method, this research aims to provide a reliable solution for LLM lineage verification that is resilient to extensive modifications while maintaining a low false-positive rate.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the substantial investments required to develop Large Language Models (LLMs) and the subsequent need to safeguard their intellectual property. The authors highlight several critical aspects driving the urgency for reliable model lineage verification:

- **High Development Costs:** Training LLMs from scratch demands considerable computational resources and vast amounts of data. This economic investment necessitates mechanisms to protect the intellectual property of model owners.
- **Lineage Verification:** There is an explicit need for both model owners to assert their rights and for third parties to verify whether a suspect LLM has been genuinely developed from scratch or is derived from an existing base model.
- **Challenges from Post-Training Processes:** LLMs frequently undergo intensive post-training modifications, which can drastically alter their parameters, outputs, and even their architectural structure. These processes include:
 - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
 - Extensive Continued Pretraining
 - Reinforcement Learning (RL)
 - Multi-modal Extension
 - Architectural Pruning
 - Upcycling (e.g., converting models into Mixture-of-Experts architectures)
 These modifications complicate the task of reliably identifying the original base model.
- **Malicious Manipulations:** Beyond standard post-training, malicious actors may intentionally manipulate model weight matrices through operations such as scaling, permutation, pruning, or rotation to deliberately obscure a model's origin and evade detection.
- **Real-world Incidents:** Recent controversies and public disputes regarding alleged model theft underscore the immediate practical need for accurate and robust lineage verification tools.

To address these motivations, the authors established a set of critical conditions that a practical LLM fingerprinting method must satisfy, which served as the key objectives for their proposed work:

- **Robustness against Extensive Post-Training Processes:** The method must be able to withstand significant modifications resulting from common and advanced post-training techniques without losing its discriminative power.
- **Resilience to Malicious Weight Manipulations:** The fingerprinting technique should not be circumvented by intentional alterations of weight matrices designed to conceal the model's origin.
- **Performance Preservation:** The act of fingerprinting itself should not degrade the LLM's capabilities, as model performance is a high priority for both developers and end-users. This implies a preference for training-free methods.
- **High Fidelity:** The method must possess sufficient discriminative power to accurately identify related models and, crucially, exhibit an extremely low false-positive rate to prevent erroneous accusations of intellectual property infringement.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Given the immense parameter counts of modern LLMs, the comparison process must be lightweight and computationally feasible, allowing for rapid lineage verification.

The authors explicitly aimed to develop an approach that concurrently meets all these five conditions, which they argued existing methods largely fail to achieve.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed "Accurate Weight-Matrix Fingerprint (AWM)," is a training-free fingerprinting technique designed to verify the lineage of LLMs by analyzing their weight matrices. The methodology builds upon a theoretical analysis of how LLM weight manipulations are constrained by architectural components, followed by a two-stage similarity detection procedure.

**Theoretical Analysis of Weight Manipulations:**
The authors began by formalizing plausible LLM weight manipulations (Definition 4.1), which involve row-wise (`L`) and column-wise (`R`) transformation matrices, and an error term (`E`). The analysis primarily focuses on word embeddings (`W_emb`) and the Query (`W_Q`) and Key (`W_K`) matrices within Transformer blocks, as these are critical components that can constrain manipulations while preserving model behavior.

1. **Residual Connections (Proposition 4.2):** The paper first considered how residual connections in Transformer blocks propagate manipulations. If a manipulation `T` is applied to an input `X`, and `T` commutes with all constituent functions `f_k` within a component (i.e., `T ◦ f_k = f_k ◦ T`), then the manipulation `T` will propagate through the entire component (`f ◦ T = T ◦ f`). This suggests that successful manipulations must either commute with or be absorbed by the subsequent functions to preserve model outputs.
2. **RMSNorm (Theorem 4.3):** The Root Mean Square Normalization (RMSNorm) layer, which processes inputs to the self-attention mechanism, was analyzed for its interaction with manipulations. The authors demonstrated that RMSNorm is primarily susceptible to embedding manipulations composed of constant multiplications, permutations, and sign flips (represented as `cPD`, where `c` is a scalar, `P` is a permutation matrix, and `D` is a signature matrix). Other types of manipulations, such as general orthogonal or non-orthogonal transformations, were found not to commute with RMSNorm or be invariant to it, thus imposing constraints.
3. **RoPE and Attention Scores (Theorem 4.4):** Further constraints arise from Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) and the attention score computation. To preserve attention scores, `W_Q` and `W_K` matrices must undergo specific transformations. These include input-related manipulations (to counteract `cPD` effects from RMSNorm, resulting in `c^-1 PD` transformations on `W_Q` and `W_K`) and RoPE-related manipulations (involving special orthogonal matrices `U`). These `U` matrices can significantly change `W_Q` and `W_K` while maintaining attention scores.

**Derived Potential Attacks (Section 4.5):**
Based on this analysis, the authors concluded that admissible manipulations on word embeddings and Q/K matrices are restricted to specific forms (Equations 4 and 5 in the paper). Crucially, the non-linear usage of `W_emb`, `W_Q`, and `W_K` substantially limits the complexity of these manipulations. The paper demonstrates that by appropriately adjusting `W_V`, `W_O`, and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) weights, these derived transformations can effectively recover the base model's behavior at the output of Transformer blocks and the final language model head.

**Proposed Two-Stage Similarity Detection (Algorithm 1):**
The AWM method implements a two-stage procedure based on the theoretical findings:

1. **Stage 1: Extracting Permutation and Signature from Word Embeddings:**
 - **Shared Vocabulary:** The process begins by identifying the common vocabulary tokens between the base model and the suspect model.
 - **Cosine Similarity Matrix:** A matrix `C` is constructed where each entry `C_k,l` represents the cosine similarity between the `k`-th column of the base model's shared word embeddings and the `l`-th column of the suspect model's shared word embeddings.
 - **Linear Assignment Problem (LAP):** The Hungarian algorithm is applied to `C` to solve the LAP, which finds an optimal permutation `P` that maximizes the sum of absolute cosine similarities between matched columns. This step is invariant to scalar factors.
 - **Signature Matrix (D) Reconstruction:** The signs of the cosine similarities at the optimally matched pairs are then used to reconstruct the diagonal signature matrix `D`. This stage effectively recovers the `cPD` transformations identified in the theoretical analysis.

2. **Stage 2: Robust Recovery of Weight Similarities using UCKA:**
 - **Unbiased Centered Kernel Alignment (UCKA):** To measure similarity between the Q and K matrices, the authors employed UCKA. UCKA is a parameter-free metric that is invariant to orthogonal transformations and constant rescaling (Theorem 3.1). This invariance is crucial for handling the `U` orthogonal transformations derived from the RoPE analysis.
 - **Transformation Application:** Before computing UCKA, the Q and K matrices of the base model are transformed using the inverse of the permutation and signature matrices (`D^T P^T`) derived from the word embeddings. This neutralizes the effects of column-wise manipulations.
 - **Layer-wise Comparison:** UCKA scores are computed for the transformed Q and K matrices across all layers.
 - **Overall Similarity Score:** The final similarity score is calculated as the average of the absolute UCKA scores for Q and K matrices across all layers.
 - **Handling Differing Layer Counts:** For models with different layer counts (e.g., due to pruning), an additional LAP is solved to find the optimal layer pairing based on pairwise UCKA scores before calculating the overall similarity.

**Computational Efficiency:** The entire similarity computation is reported to complete within 30 seconds on a single NVIDIA 3090 GPU, indicating practical efficiency for large models. The method is also explicitly stated as training-free, ensuring no degradation of LLM performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AWM involved a comprehensive testbed comprising 60 positive (base-offspring) and 90 negative (independent) model pairs, totaling 150 LLM pairs. The study investigated robustness against six major post-training categories: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), extensive Continued Pretraining, Reinforcement Learning (RL), Multi-modal Extension, Pruning, and Upcycling. Performance was compared against state-of-the-art baselines: HuRef (a weight-based method) and REEF (a representation-based method), with supplementary baselines like Intrinsic Fingerprint and PCS for overall evaluation.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness Verification

- **Discriminative Power:** AWM demonstrated a clear distinction between related and unrelated models (Table 1). Offspring models derived from LLaMA2-7B and LLaMA2-13B consistently exhibited high similarity scores (e.g., 99.99% for WizardMath-7B, 99.98% for Selfrag\_llama2\_13b). In contrast, independently trained models showed negligible similarity (e.g., 0.58% for Baichuan-7B, 0.17% for Baichuan-13b).

#### 4.2. False Positive Risk Evaluation

- **Low False Positive Rate:** On a set of 90 independent model pairs (45 for 7B and 45 for 13B LLMs), AWM yielded near-zero mean similarity scores (0.49% for 7B, 0.26% for 13B) (Figure 2). The maximum similarity scores observed for AWM were 1.5% (7B) and 0.8% (13B), indicating a low risk of false positives.
- **Baseline Comparison:** HuRef showed higher mean similarities (3.56% for 7B, 2.17% for 13B). REEF exhibited significantly higher mean similarities (42.47% for 7B, 47.44% for 13B), with many pairs exceeding 80% and some over 95%, suggesting a substantial risk of false accusations.

#### 4.3. Robustness Verification on 60 Offspring Models

- **Resilience to Post-Training:** To quantify robustness against diverse post-training modifications, the absolute Z-score (|Z|) was used, measuring how many standard deviations a positive pair's similarity deviates from the mean of negative pairs' similarities (Table 2).
 - **AWM's Robustness:** AWM consistently maintained high |Z| scores across all 60 positive pairs and all six post-training categories. For instance, in SFT, scores ranged from 343.14 to 355.23. Even under extensive continued pretraining (e.g., Qwen2.5-Coder with 5.5T tokens), AWM maintained a high |Z| of 135.17, and for pruning (Llama3-1B), it was 12.14.
 - **Baselines' Limitations:** HuRef's effectiveness decreased under heavy modifications, with |Z| dropping to 0.57 for certain continued pretraining scenarios. REEF consistently produced low |Z| scores, often below 2.0, indicating poor separability for positive pairs from the distribution of negative pairs.

#### 4.4. Overall Evaluation

- **Perfect Classification Metrics:** Across the full 150-pair dataset, AWM achieved perfect scores (1.0) on all classification metrics: Area Under the Curve (AUC), partial AUC (pAUC for False Positive Rate < 0.05), and True Positive Rate at 1% False Positive Rate (TPR@1%FPR) (Figure 1, Table 3). This indicates flawless discrimination between related and unrelated LLMs.
- **Superior Baseline Performance:**
 - HuRef achieved an AUC of 0.957 but showed vulnerabilities with TPR@1%FPR dropping to 0.500 under Continual Pre-Training.
 - REEF's overall AUC was 0.778, with pAUC and TPR@1%FPR collapsing to 0.0 in several scenarios (e.g., Upcycling and Multi-modal post-training).
- **Overall Separability:** AWM's average absolute Z-score of 302.936 across all scenarios was substantially larger than HuRef (28.142) and REEF (1.285), demonstrating superior robustness and reliability.

#### 4.5. Empirical Validation of Robustness against Weight Manipulations (Appendix F)

- **Theoretical Alignment:** AWM achieved a 100% detection rate when subjected to deliberate weight manipulations, including permutation, signature matrix multiplication, constant scaling, and orthogonal transformations, across five representative LLMs (Llama-2-7B, Qwen2-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama-3-8B, and Gemma-7B). These empirical results support the theoretical analysis presented in Section 4.

#### 4.6. Ablation Studies (Appendix C)

- **Vocabulary Overlap:** AWM's performance (measured by average absolute Z-score) remained effective even when the number of overlapping vocabulary tokens was as low as 100, indicating that a large shared vocabulary is not strictly necessary.
- **CKA Kernel and Biasedness:** The use of an unbiased CKA variant was found to be crucial for the robustness of AWM. While RBF kernels showed slightly stronger performance, the linear kernel provided a balance of strong performance and computational efficiency, justifying its selection in the final method.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AWM has several significant implications and potential impacts for the LLM ecosystem and intellectual property protection:

- **Reliable Lineage Verification:** AWM addresses a critical gap in the ability to reliably determine whether a suspect LLM has been trained from scratch or derived from an existing base model. This capability is essential for upholding intellectual property rights in a rapidly evolving field.
- **Protection for Model Owners:** By demonstrating high robustness against a wide array of post-training processes (SFT, continued pretraining, RL, multi-modal extension, pruning, upcycling) and resilience to malicious weight manipulations (scaling, permutation, rotation), AWM provides a robust tool for model owners to protect their substantial investments in LLM development. It offers a mechanism to detect unauthorized appropriation even after aggressive modifications.
- **Fairness for Third Parties:** The near-zero false-positive rate of AWM is crucial. It minimizes the risk of false accusations of model theft, fostering a more equitable and transparent environment for LLM development and deployment. This prevents unwarranted legal or reputational damage.
- **Support for Licensing and Governance:** The ability to verify LLM lineage robustly can underpin more effective licensing agreements and governance frameworks for open-source and proprietary models. It provides a technical basis for enforcing compliance with usage terms and intellectual property laws.
- **Practical Applicability:** AWM's training-free nature ensures that it does not compromise the performance of the LLM being investigated. Its computational efficiency, completing comparisons within seconds on standard GPU hardware, makes it practical for real-world application, even with large-scale models.
- **Advancement in Forensic AI:** This work contributes to the field of forensic AI by providing a robust methodology for analyzing model internals to establish provenance. It moves beyond superficial outputs to examine fundamental architectural elements and their transformations.
- **Ethical AI Development:** By enabling transparent and verifiable model lineage, AWM contributes to more ethical practices in AI development, discouraging undeclared derivation and promoting originality.

Overall, AWM establishes a strong technical foundation for trustworthy LLM lineage verification, with the potential to influence how LLMs are licensed, shared, and governed across academic and industrial landscapes.
