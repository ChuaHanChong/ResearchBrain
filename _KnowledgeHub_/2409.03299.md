# Bringing the RT-1-X Foundation Model to a SCARA robot

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Universiteit van Amsterdam's Intelligent Robotics Lab investigated the RT-1-X robotic foundation model's capacity for generalization to a 40-year-old SCARA robot, an embodiment with distinct kinematics. While zero-shot transfer failed, fine-tuning with 100 demonstrations enabled the robot to achieve a 23% success rate on a pick-up task, though object-specific knowledge transfer proved limited.

## Problem

- Current robotic foundation models struggle to generalize to entirely new robotic embodiments, particularly those with fundamentally different kinematic structures not represented in their training datasets.
- Traditional robotic systems typically require extensive, specific training data for each unique task, environment, and robot form, hindering widespread adaptability.
- Existing large-scale datasets and foundation models primarily focus on robots with spherical workspaces, leaving a gap in understanding generalization to distinct kinematics like SCARA robots.

## Method

- The ==UMI-RTX==, a 40-year-old ==SCARA robot== with a unique kidney-shaped workspace not present in the [[2310.08864|RT-1-X]] training data, was used as the novel robotic embodiment.
- The ==RT-1-X transformer-based model== was fine-tuned using 100 manual ==teleoperated demonstrations== for a specific task: "picking up a banana from the workspace."
- Performance was evaluated for in-distribution task execution and the transfer of object-specific knowledge, using both the fine-tuned object (banana) and a novel object (coke can) from RT-1-X's pre-training data.

## Results

- Initial experiments confirmed that RT-1-X failed to generalize in a zero-shot manner to the UMI SCARA robot.
- After fine-tuning, the model achieved a **23%** success rate for the "picking up a banana" task, with **80%** of attempts resulting in a success or a "near miss" (within 5cm of the target).
- When instructed to pick up an unseen object (coke can) that was part of the original pre-training data, the robot achieved a **10%** success rate, but its ability to differentiate and pick up specific objects deteriorated significantly to **0%** success when both the banana and coke can were present.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art robotic foundation models like RT-1-X do not generalize zero-shot to robots possessing fundamentally different kinematic structures.
> - Fine-tuning with a relatively small dataset of demonstrations (100 episodes) can effectively adapt powerful pre-trained models to new, kinematically distinct robotic embodiments.
> - Skill transfer (e.g., general manipulation actions) can occur, but object-specific knowledge transfer is limited and fragile when confronted with new kinematic contexts or multiple objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a crucial, yet underexplored, challenge in the rapidly evolving field of robotic learning: the generalization of robotic foundation models to entirely new robotic embodiments, particularly those with fundamentally different kinematics.

**The Rise of Robotic Foundation Models:** The broader research landscape is currently dominated by the exciting promise of robotic foundation models. Inspired by the success of large language models and vision transformers, these models (like Google’s RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA) aim to learn generalizable policies by training on massive, diverse datasets of real-world robotic experiences. The goal is to develop models that can adapt to new tasks, environments, and objects with minimal or zero-shot retraining, thus overcoming the traditional limitations of task- and robot-specific data collection. The Open X-Embodiment dataset, which combines over 1 million robot trajectories from 22 different robot types and 60 datasets across 34 institutions, represents a monumental effort in this direction, and RT-1-X (RT-1 trained on Open X-Embodiment) is a prime example of a model leveraging this scale.

**The Underexplored Gap: Kinematic Generalization:** While these foundation models have demonstrated impressive generalization capabilities across various tasks and environments, the authors correctly identify a critical blind spot: their ability to generalize across *entirely new robotic embodiments*, especially those with distinct kinematic structures. The existing foundation models, including RT-1-X, have primarily been trained on robots that move their end-effectors within a "spherical working-space," such such as the Franka, Kuka iiwa, UR5, and EveryDay robot. These robots typically feature serial manipulators with revolute joints allowing for movement in all three dimensions.

**The SCARA Robot as a Litmus Test:** The contribution of Salzer and Visser's work is precisely in testing the limits of this generalization by introducing a SCARA (Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm) robot. SCARA robots, like the UMI-RTX, have a distinct kinematic design where the z-axis movement is decoupled from the x-y plane movement, resulting in a unique "kidney-shaped" working-space. This fundamental difference makes the SCARA robot an ideal, and challenging, testbed for evaluating the true cross-embodiment capabilities of models like RT-1-X. By selecting a robot type explicitly *not* present in the Open X-Embodiment training dataset, the researchers directly probe whether the learned representations in RT-1-X are truly abstract and embodiment-agnostic, or if they are implicitly tied to the kinematic configurations observed during pre-training.

The paper’s discussion section further emphasizes this gap by noting that even newer foundation models like RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA continue to focus on robots with spherical workspaces. This reinforces the unique and pertinent nature of Salzer and Visser’s investigation, positioning it at the forefront of understanding the boundaries of current robotic generalization capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this study stems from the observed limitations of traditional robotic systems, which necessitate extensive, specific training data for each unique task, environment, and robot form. While recent advancements in machine learning, particularly robotic foundation models, have made significant strides in generalizing across tasks and environments, the challenge of adapting these models to entirely *new robotic embodiments* remains largely unexplored.

The key objectives of this research are therefore structured around probing this specific frontier of generalization:

1. **Evaluate Zero-Shot Generalization to an Unseen Robot Type:** The primary objective is to investigate whether the RT-1-X robotic foundation model, pre-trained on a diverse dataset of robots, can generalize in a zero-shot manner (i.e., without any further training) to a SCARA robot, a type of robot completely absent from its training data. This aims to determine the inherent transferability of learned policies across fundamentally different kinematic structures.
2. **Assess Fine-Tuning for Task Learning on a Novel Embodiment:** If zero-shot generalization proves unsuccessful (as preliminary experiments already suggested), the next objective is to determine if the RT-1-X model can be effectively fine-tuned through demonstration to enable the SCARA robot to learn a specific manipulation task. This explores a practical pathway for adapting powerful pre-trained models to new hardware. The chosen task, "picking up a banana," is a common element within the original foundation model's dataset, allowing for a direct comparison of skill transfer.
3. **Investigate Transfer of Object-Specific Knowledge:** Beyond mere skill transfer, a crucial objective is to examine whether object-specific knowledge learned during the RT-1-X's extensive pre-training can transfer to the fine-tuned SCARA robot. Specifically, the study aims to determine if the robot can recognize and manipulate objects (like a coke can) that were part of the foundation model's pre-training data but were *not* included in the SCARA robot's fine-tuning demonstrations. This helps to dissect whether the model learns abstract object representations or if its object recognition is implicitly tied to the embodiments seen during pre-training.

In essence, the researchers are driven by the ambition to push the boundaries of robotic generalization, critically examining how "universal" current robotic foundation models truly are when confronted with significant architectural divergence in robot bodies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study is a practical, experimental approach focused on fine-tuning a state-of-the-art robotic foundation model on a novel embodiment and rigorously evaluating its performance and generalization capabilities.

**Initial Assessment (Zero-Shot):** The study begins by implicitly confirming that RT-1-X does not generalize zero-shot to the UMI SCARA robot, a finding from prior internal experiments that is also stated in the paper's abstract. This sets the stage for the core experimental approach: fine-tuning.

**Robotic Embodiment:**
The chosen robot is the UMI-RTX, a SCARA-type robot from Universal Machine Intelligence Ltd., housed at the Intelligent Robotics Lab. This robot has 7 degrees of freedom (DoF), controlling elbow/shoulder rotation, wrist yaw/pitch/roll, z-axis height, and gripper opening. Its distinctive kidney-shaped workspace is a key differentiator from the spherical workspaces of robots typically found in the RT-1-X training data.

**RT-1-X Model Architecture Overview:**
The RT-1-X model, based on the original RT-1, is a transformer-based architecture designed for real-world robotic control. Its key components include:
- **Input:** Natural language instruction (e.g., "Pick up the banana") and a history of six RGB images.
- **Language Processing:** A Universal Sentence Encoder transforms the language instruction into a 512-dimensional embedding.
- **Image Processing:** An EfficientNet-B3 (pre-trained on ImageNet) processes the images.
- **Conditioning:** Feature-wise Linear Manipulation (FiLM) layers integrate the language embedding to condition the image encoder, extracting task-relevant image features.
- **Tokenization:** Images are converted into 81 visual-language tokens per image. TokenLearner further compresses these into 8 final tokens, reducing computational load.
- **Transformer:** These tokens are fed into a Transformer network.
- **Output:** An 11-dimensional action description (7 for arm movement (x,y,z,roll,pitch,yaw,grip), 3 for base movement, and 1 for episode termination). For this stationary robot, only the 7-dimensional arm movement vector is relevant.

**UMI Dataset for Imitation Learning:**
- **Task and Object:** To fine-tune RT-1-X, a single scenario was chosen: "picking up a banana from the workspace." This task and object were deliberately selected because they are already well-represented within the Open X-Embodiment dataset, facilitating the investigation of skill transfer.
- **Data Collection:** 100 manual demonstrations were collected by teleoperating the UMI robot using a PlayStation controller. A "Side" camera position was chosen for consistency, as it had shown the largest effect on performance in prior zero-shot evaluations.
- **Data Format:** Demonstrations were recorded as episodes of approximately 30 steps, with actions logged every five seconds (0.2Hz) due to the UMI robot's slow movement. This data was then converted into the RLDS (Reinforcement Learning Datasets) format, aligning with the Open X-Embodiment dataset structure.
- **Data Verification:** A crucial step involved replaying each recorded episode in a simulated environment to ensure the accuracy and integrity of the stored movements, preventing issues from false or inaccurate action recording.

**Fine-Tuning RT-1-X:**
- **Layer Selection:** Initial experiments with freezing the first Transformer layers and fine-tuning only the last 3 out of 8 blocks did not yield better results. Consequently, the entire RT-1-X model was fine-tuned for subsequent experiments.
- **Hyperparameter Optimization:**
 - **Batch Size:** Due to hardware limitations (24GB vRAM), a batch size of 5 episodes was chosen, significantly smaller than the 1024 used during pre-training. This was found to be the largest feasible size and yielded less noisy training loss curves compared to smaller batch sizes.
 - **Learning Rate:** Using the Adam optimizer, various learning rates were experimented with. A learning rate of 5e-6 was ultimately selected, which is lower than the initial training rate of 1e-4, a common practice for fine-tuning foundation models.
 - **Training Steps:** The model was trained for approximately 50,000 steps. Checkpoints were saved every 1000 steps, and the best-performing checkpoint (found at 55,000 steps after evaluation) was used for experiments to mitigate noise in the training process.

**Validation:**
The fine-tuning process was validated by running inference with images and language instructions from the UMI training dataset. The predicted actions of the fine-tuned model were compared against the ground truth actions recorded during demonstrations to confirm effective learning.

**Evaluation Experiments:**
1. **In-Distribution Performance:** The fine-tuned model was evaluated on its ability to perform the `banana pickup` task, the exact task it was fine-tuned on, within the UMI environment.
2. **Transfer of Object Knowledge:**
 - Experiment 1: The model was instructed to pick up a "coke can," an object present in the Open X-Embodiment pre-training data but *not* in the UMI fine-tuning dataset, with only the can in the workspace.
 - Experiment 2: Both a "coke can" and a "banana" were placed in the workspace, and the robot was instructed to pick up one, to assess its ability to differentiate and select objects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several critical findings regarding the generalization capabilities of the RT-1-X model to a novel robotic embodiment:

1. **Zero-Shot Generalization Failure:**
 - Initial experiments (referenced from Salzer's Master's thesis and confirmed in the abstract) unequivocally showed that the RT-1-X model *does not generalize zero-shot* to the UMI SCARA robot. This confirms that despite the diversity of the Open X-Embodiment dataset, fundamental differences in robot kinematics prevent direct transfer of learned policies without adaptation.

2. **Successful Fine-Tuning for Skill Transfer (In-Distribution Performance):**
 - After fine-tuning with 100 manual demonstrations, the model achieved a **23% success rate** in picking up the banana from the workspace.
 - A significant **80% of all evaluation runs resulted in either success or a "near miss"**, where the robot executed the correct sequence of actions but was off from the target object by less than five centimeters.
 - This indicates that fine-tuning was effective in transferring the general `pickup` skill and behavior patterns to the UMI robot. The model learned *how* to approach and manipulate an object, even if its spatial accuracy was sometimes lacking.
 - The model also exhibited a bias towards certain positions, successfully picking up objects when they were moved to these preferred locations.

3. **Limited Transfer of Object-Specific Knowledge:**
 - **Picking up an unseen object (coke can only):** When instructed to pick up a "coke can" (an object seen during RT-1-X pre-training but *not* in the UMI fine-tuning dataset), the robot achieved a **10% success rate** and a **60% success or near-miss rate**. This suggests some level of skill transfer to a different object, but with reduced accuracy compared to the fine-tuned object.
 - **Object Recognition with multiple objects:** When both the "coke can" and the "banana" were present, the model's performance significantly deteriorated, with **no successful pick-ups**. In half of the attempts, the robot made no significant pick-up attempt at all. In the other half, it attempted to pick up one of the objects, but without reliable identification.
 - **Conclusion on Object Knowledge:** These results demonstrate that while the fine-tuned model learned the *skill* of picking up an object from the workspace, it did *not* reliably transfer concrete, object-specific knowledge from its pre-training. The model struggled to accurately identify and differentiate between objects, even the one it was specifically fine-tuned on, as soon as another object was introduced. This implies that the learned object features or associations are heavily coupled with the original embodiments and do not robustly transfer to the new kinematic context.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study makes several significant contributions and has substantial implications for the development and deployment of robotic foundation models:

1. **Defining the Boundaries of Generalization:** The most critical finding is the clear demonstration that state-of-the-art robotic foundation models like RT-1-X *do not* generalize zero-shot to robots with fundamentally different kinematic structures (e.g., SCARA vs. spherical-workspace robots). This is a crucial insight that refines our understanding of "X-embodiment" generalization, revealing that current models' cross-robot capabilities are constrained by the types of embodiments present in their massive training datasets. It highlights that "embodiment" is not a monolithic concept, and kinematic divergence presents a formidable barrier.

2. **Validating Fine-Tuning as an Adaptation Strategy:** Despite the failure of zero-shot generalization, the research successfully demonstrates that fine-tuning with a relatively small dataset of demonstrations (100 episodes) can enable a powerful pre-trained model to learn a task on a completely novel robotic embodiment. This provides a practical and viable pathway for deploying foundation models on diverse, previously unseen hardware, offering a balance between generalizability and specific adaptation without requiring training from scratch.

3. **Disentangling Skill Transfer from Object Knowledge Transfer:** The study effectively distinguishes between the transfer of a general manipulation skill (e.g., "pickup") and the transfer of object-specific knowledge. While the general skill transferred, the inability of the fine-tuned model to reliably recognize or differentiate objects when multiple were present suggests that object-level understanding might be more deeply intertwined with the visual and kinematic context of the pre-training robots. This finding is vital for designing future models that aim for truly robust and abstract object recognition across diverse embodiments.

4. **Implications for Dataset Diversity:** The results implicitly call for even greater diversity in future robotic learning datasets. If foundation models are to achieve genuine embodiment-agnostic generalization, these datasets will need to include a broader spectrum of robot kinematics, beyond the predominantly spherical-workspace manipulators that currently dominate. This includes SCARA robots, delta robots, mobile manipulators, and other unique configurations.

5. **Guiding Future Research:** This work opens several avenues for future research:
 - **Kinematic-Agnostic Representations:** Developing new model architectures or representation learning techniques that can intrinsically account for and adapt to broad kinematic variations in a zero-shot manner.
 - **Modular Adaptation Strategies:** Exploring more sophisticated fine-tuning or adaptation methods that can selectively transfer skills while robustly adapting object recognition and other high-level cognitive abilities to new embodiments.
 - **The Role of Language in Kinematic Adaptation:** Investigating if and how natural language conditioning could be used to facilitate adaptation to different kinematic structures, perhaps by providing explicit kinematic descriptions.
 - **Leveraging Older Hardware:** The successful "revival" and use of the UMI-RTX robot highlights the potential for older, specialized hardware to serve as crucial testbeds for fundamental research questions, especially those probing the limits of generalization.

In conclusion, Salzer and Visser's work serves as an important reality check and a guiding light for the field of robotic learning. It underscores that while foundation models are immensely powerful, the journey towards truly universal robotic intelligence capable of operating on *any* robot is still long, necessitating continued focused research on the challenging problem of cross-embodiment generalization, particularly concerning fundamental kinematic differences.
