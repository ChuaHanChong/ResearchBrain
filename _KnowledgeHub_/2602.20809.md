# Regret-Guided Search Control for Efficient Learning in AlphaZero

> [!summary] Summary
> Regret-Guided Search Control (RGSC) enhances AlphaZero's learning efficiency by identifying and prioritizing "high-regret states" for self-play restarts. This approach led to an average 77-89 Elo point improvement over baselines and demonstrated continued performance gains on already highly proficient models in games like Go, Othello, and Hex.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents, such as AlphaZero, require extensive self-play (millions of games) to achieve mastery, exhibiting lower learning efficiency compared to human players.
- Standard AlphaZero training paradigms inefficiently extract learning signals by consistently restarting self-play from initial states and uniformly updating all positions based on final outcomes.
- Existing search control methods, like Go-Exploit, treat all potential restart states uniformly, failing to prioritize states with higher learning potential and becoming less effective as the agent improves.

## Method

- The Regret-Guided Search Control (**RGSC**) framework extends ==AlphaZero== by integrating a specialized regret network and a Prioritized Regret Buffer (PRB).
- A regret network, comprising both ranking and value heads, is used to identify "high-regret states" where the agent's Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) value significantly deviates from the actual game outcome.
- The PRB stores these high-regret states, and new self-play games are preferentially started from sampled states within this buffer, with higher-regret states being prioritized via a softmax distribution.

## Results

- RGSC consistently outperformed AlphaZero and Go-Exploit baselines, achieving an average 77 Elo points improvement over AlphaZero and 89 Elo points over Go-Exploit across 9x9 Go, 10x10 Othello, and 11x11 Hex.
- When applied to an already well-trained 9x9 Go model, RGSC further increased its win rate against KataGo from **69.3%** to **78.2%**, demonstrating continued performance gains where baselines showed minimal progress.
- Ablation studies confirmed the regret ranking network's superiority in selecting states with substantially higher true regret compared to the regret value network, and analysis showed regret values for states in the PRB consistently decreased upon repeated revisits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly identifying and prioritizing states where the agent demonstrates significant MCTS mis-evaluations (high-regret states) for self-play restarts significantly accelerates learning efficiency and robustness.
> - A regret ranking network, which focuses on differentiating relative regret scores, is more effective for identifying critical learning opportunities than a direct regret value prediction network due to the dynamic and imbalanced nature of regret targets.
> - Using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) to update regret values for states in the PRB ensures a gradual decrease in regret, allowing for sustained practice on challenging positions until true mastery is achieved.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) agents, while achieving high performance in complex domains, often exhibit significantly lower learning efficiency compared to humans. A prominent example is AlphaZero, which requires millions of self-play games to master games like Go. In contrast, human players can rapidly improve by reviewing critical positions and correcting mistakes. This discrepancy highlights a fundamental challenge in current RL paradigms: the inefficient extraction of useful learning signals from extensive self-play.

This work addresses the challenge of RL learning efficiency within the framework of "search control," a concept that advocates for selecting critical starting states for simulated experiences rather than consistently beginning from a fixed initial state. The idea of search control originated from the Dyna framework and has been explored in various RL contexts. Examples include Go-Explore for addressing hard-exploration problems by maintaining a database of promising states, reverse curriculum generation for sparse reward environments, and Jump-Start RL utilizing expert demonstrations. These methods aim to modify the restart distribution over states to accelerate learning. Prioritized restart strategies, which rank states based on their temporal-difference (TD) error, have also been investigated. Furthermore, search control principles have been applied to curriculum learning for task selection, where agents focus on levels with higher learning potential based on estimated regret.

Within the AlphaZero framework specifically, several efforts have integrated search control to alleviate the computational burden of starting every self-play game from an empty board. KataGo, for instance, generates starting states by playing random moves or sampling from past trajectories. Another approach involves starting self-play from later game stages and gradually moving towards the initial position. Go-Exploit is a notable prior work that systematically applies search control to AlphaZero by maintaining a buffer of states collected from self-play trajectories or Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) nodes. This method uniformly samples states from the buffer as new starting positions.

The current work identifies a key limitation in existing AlphaZero search control methods, particularly Go-Exploit: the uniform treatment of all states. This uniform sampling fails to account for the varying learning potential of different states. Many states may already be mastered by the agent, especially in later training stages, making a uniform sampling strategy inefficient for identifying and addressing critical weaknesses. This research aims to fill this gap by proposing a method that prioritizes states with higher learning potential, specifically by identifying "high-regret states," thereby refining the search control mechanism in AlphaZero.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the learning efficiency and robustness of AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning agents by introducing a regret-guided search control mechanism.

The motivation stems from the observed disparity in learning efficiency between modern RL agents and human learners. AlphaZero, despite its superhuman performance in complex games, demands an extensive amount of self-play (millions of games) to extract meaningful learning signals. This contrasts sharply with human learning, where players can rapidly improve by focusing on and correcting mistakes made in critical game positions. AlphaZero's standard training paradigm, which invariably restarts self-play from the initial state and updates all positions uniformly based on the final game outcome, is identified as a significant contributor to this inefficiency. This approach requires an enormous number of episodes to achieve mastery.

To mitigate this inefficiency, the concept of search control has emerged, advocating for the strategic selection of intermediate starting states for simulated experiences. While prior works have explored various restart strategies in general RL and within AlphaZero, a critical limitation persists: many existing methods, such as Go-Exploit, treat all potential restart states uniformly. The authors argue that this uniform sampling is suboptimal because not all states offer equal learning opportunities. As an RL agent improves, the number of unmastered or "mistake" states decreases, making it increasingly difficult for uniform sampling to effectively identify and revisit the most informative positions for further learning. This problem becomes more pronounced in the later stages of training, where subtle weaknesses are rare but crucial for reaching higher performance ceilings.

Against this background, the key objectives of the proposed Regret-Guided Search Control (RGSC) are:
1. **Develop a robust mechanism to identify high-regret states:** These are defined as states where the agent’s current evaluation significantly diverges from the actual game outcome, indicating a misunderstanding or a mistake that needs correction.
2. **Integrate a prioritization system:** Move beyond uniform sampling by prioritizing these high-regret states for restarting self-play, thereby mimicking the human learning process of repeatedly reviewing and correcting critical errors.
3. **Improve AlphaZero's training efficiency:** By focusing computational resources on challenging, unmastered states, RGSC aims to accelerate the learning curve and reduce the total number of self-play games required to achieve a given performance level.
4. **Enhance AlphaZero's robustness and continued improvement:** Demonstrate that RGSC can facilitate continued performance gains even when applied to already highly proficient, near-converged models, where identifying further learning opportunities is particularly challenging for conventional methods.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to advance the field of reinforcement learning towards more human-like, efficient, and robust learning paradigms in complex strategic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Regret-Guided Search Control (RGSC) framework extends AlphaZero by integrating a regret network and a prioritized regret buffer (PRB). The core idea is to identify and prioritize states where the agent's evaluation significantly deviates from the game outcome, referring to these as "high-regret states," and then use them as starting points for subsequent self-play games.

**Regret Definition:**
The regret for a state $s_t$ within a completed self-play trajectory ($s_0, s_1..., s_T$) with a final game outcome $z$ is defined as:
$R(s_t) = \frac{1}{T-t} \sum_{i=t}^T (V_{\text{selected}}(s_i) - z)^2$
Here, $V_{\text{selected}}(s_i)$ represents the Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) value of the action chosen at state $s_i$. This definition measures the average discrepancy between the agent's MCTS-guided value estimation and the actual game outcome, accumulated from state $s_t$ to the terminal state $s_T$. A higher regret indicates a greater mis-evaluation by the agent, potentially impacting the long-term game outcome. This value is computed only after a game is finished, and a single state can have varying regret values across different trajectories depending on subsequent moves and outcomes.

**Regret Network:**
RGSC incorporates a specialized regret network into the AlphaZero architecture, comprising two heads:
1. **Regret Ranking Network:** This network outputs an unnormalized score, $\gamma_s$, for a given state $s$. A higher $\gamma_s$ indicates a state with higher regret relative to other states.
2. **Regret Value Network:** This network estimates the actual regret value, $g_s$, for a given state $s$.

The motivation for using a ranking network, rather than solely a value network, is to address challenges associated with directly predicting precise regret values. These challenges include:
- **Imbalanced distribution:** Most states typically have near-zero regret, while high-regret states are rare.
- **Non-stationary target:** The target regret values are dynamic, as repeatedly revisiting and correcting a mistake causes its regret to decrease.

To overcome these, the regret ranking network is trained with a ranking-based objective. Instead of predicting exact regret values, the network is guided to differentiate states by their relative regret. The objective encourages the model to assign higher probabilities to states with higher true regrets:
$J_{\text{rank}} = \sum_{s \in S} \rho(s|S)R(s)$, where $\rho(s|S) = \frac{\exp(\gamma_s)}{\sum_{s' \in S} \exp(\gamma_{s'})}$
A surrogate loss function, $L_{\text{rank}} = -\log \sum_{s \in S} \exp(\log(\text{softmax}(\gamma_s)) + R(s))$, is derived. This loss can be interpreted as adding regret as an additive bias to the log-softmax scores, providing a smooth approximation to selecting the highest-regret states. While the ranking network identifies high-regret states, the regret value network provides a quantitative estimate of their regret.

**Prioritized Regret Buffer (PRB) for Search Control:**
The PRB is a fixed-capacity buffer (capacity $K$) designed to store high-regret states identified by the regret network. The procedure for managing and utilizing the PRB is as follows:
1. **State Evaluation and Selection:** After each self-play game, the regret ranking network evaluates all states encountered, both along the self-play trajectory and within the MCTS search tree. The state with the highest ranking score is selected as a candidate for the PRB.
2. **Regret Assignment:** If the selected state originates from the self-play trajectory, its true regret $R(s)$ is computed using the defined formula. If it comes from the MCTS search tree, its regret is estimated by the regret value network.
3. **Buffer Insertion:** If the PRB is not full, the selected state (with its associated regret) is added directly. If the buffer is full, the state is added only if its regret is higher than that of the lowest-regret state currently in the PRB, ensuring that the buffer always contains the most informative states.
4. **Self-Play Initialization:** For each new self-play game, the agent starts from the empty board with a probability of $1-\lambda$. With probability $\lambda$, it samples a state from the PRB as the starting position.
5. **Prioritized Sampling from PRB:** When sampling from the PRB, a softmax distribution is applied to prioritize higher-regret states: $P(s_i) = R(s_i)^{1/\tau} / \sum_j R(s_j)^{1/\tau}$, where $\tau$ is a sampling temperature.
6. **Exponential Moving Average (EMA) Update:** When a state $s_i$ is sampled from the PRB and used as a restart point, its stored regret value in the buffer is updated after the game concludes using an EMA: $R_{\text{new}}(s_i) \leftarrow (1-\alpha) \times R_{\text{old}}(s_i) + \alpha \times R(s_i)$. This mechanism ensures that regret values decrease gradually as the agent masters a state, preventing abrupt drops and enabling the state to remain in the buffer for sustained practice until fully understood.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology was validated across three board games: 9x9 Go, 10x10 Othello, and 11x11 Hex. A toy example on an n-level sparse-reward binary tree was also used for initial concept validation.
- **Baselines:** AlphaZero (no search control) and Go-Exploit (GEVC variant, uniform sampling).
- **Network Architecture:** A 3-block residual network with 256 filters for training from scratch, and a 15-block network for continued training on well-trained models.
- **MCTS Simulations:** 200 simulations per move (400 for well-trained models).
- **Training Schedule:** 300 iterations for scratch training (160,000 states/iter for 9x9 Go, 120,000 states/iter for others); 40 iterations for continued training.
- **Hyperparameters:** Key RGSC hyperparameters were $\lambda=0.5$ (sampling probability from PRB), $\tau=0.1$ (buffer sampling temperature), $K=100$ (buffer size, $K=500$ for well-trained models), and $\alpha=0.5$ (EMA coefficient).
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Elo ratings (against a reference AlphaZero model) and win rates against established open-source programs (KataGo for Go, Ludii Alpha-Beta for Othello, MoHex for Hex).
- **Computational Overhead:** Analysis showed minimal additional computational cost for RGSC, especially with larger networks.
- **Ablation Studies:** Comparisons between the regret ranking network and the regret value network, as well as analyses of regret evolution and opening-length distributions in the PRB, were performed.
- **Generalization:** A preliminary experiment demonstrated RGSC's applicability to MuZero on the Atari game Ms. Pac-Man.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that support the effectiveness and robustness of Regret-Guided Search Control (RGSC) across various settings.

**Toy Example Performance:**
In a preliminary evaluation on an n-level sparse-reward binary tree, the regret-guided approach demonstrated superior performance compared to both random state sampling and no search control. The regret-based method achieved higher average rewards and a lower mean squared error (MSE) of Q-values, indicating its ability to effectively prioritize states with higher learning potential in a simplified environment.

**Performance in Board Games (Training from Scratch):**
RGSC was compared against AlphaZero (no search control) and Go-Exploit (uniform sampling) across 9x9 Go, 10x10 Othello, and 11x11 Hex.
- **Elo Ratings (Figure 4):** RGSC consistently outperformed both baselines in all three games. It achieved an average improvement of 77 Elo points over AlphaZero and 89 Elo points over Go-Exploit. Specifically, in 9x9 Go, RGSC surpassed AlphaZero by 76 Elo and Go-Exploit by 96 Elo. In 10x10 Othello, the improvements were 70 Elo over AlphaZero and 50 Elo over Go-Exploit. For 11x11 Hex, RGSC was 84 Elo better than AlphaZero and 122 Elo better than Go-Exploit.
- **Go-Exploit Behavior:** Go-Exploit initially showed an advantage over AlphaZero in the early stages of training, but its performance advantage diminished as training progressed and converged. This observation supports the hypothesis that uniform sampling becomes less effective when the agent has already mastered many states and critical learning opportunities become rare.
- **Win Rates Against Open-Source Programs (Table 1):** The final RGSC checkpoints consistently achieved higher win rates against established open-source programs. For 9x9 Go, RGSC had a 53.6% win rate compared to AlphaZero's 45.5% and Go-Exploit's 49.5%. In 10x10 Othello, RGSC achieved 57.8% (vs. AlphaZero 51.7%, Go-Exploit 52.9%). For 11x11 Hex, RGSC secured 91.1% (vs. AlphaZero 83.6%, Go-Exploit 89.2%).

**Performance on Well-Trained Models:**
A critical experiment involved continuing training from an already well-trained 15-block AlphaZero model in 9x9 Go, which had an initial win rate of 69.3% against KataGo.
- **Elo Ratings (Figure 5):** RGSC demonstrated continued performance improvement, whereas AlphaZero and Go-Exploit showed minimal or no progress. RGSC achieved an Elo rating increase of 42 points over AlphaZero and 87 points over Go-Exploit during this phase.
- **Win Rates Against KataGo:** After continued training, AlphaZero reached 70.2% and Go-Exploit 69.2%, indicating no meaningful improvement. In contrast, RGSC significantly boosted the win rate to 78.2%, demonstrating its capacity to identify and correct remaining subtle weaknesses even in highly proficient models.

**Comparison Between Regret Ranking and Regret Value Networks:**
An ablation study compared RGSC using a regret ranking network versus a variant relying solely on a regret value network.
- **Elo Ratings (Figure 6):** The regret ranking network consistently led to stronger playing performance across all three board games.
- **Average Regret of Selected States (Figure 7):** The regret ranking network was shown to select states with substantially higher true regret values than the regret value network. Both outperformed Go-Exploit's uniform sampling, which consistently yielded the lowest average regret among selected states, further supporting the claim that uniform sampling becomes less effective as the agent improves.

**Regret Dynamics in the Prioritized Regret Buffer (PRB):**
Analysis of states within the PRB revealed a consistent pattern (Figure 8, Figure 19).
- The regret distributions for states, from their first entry into the PRB to their eventual removal, consistently shifted towards lower regret values.
- Average regret values decreased significantly across all games (e.g., in 9x9 Go, from 0.655 at entry to 0.296 before removal), indicating that the agent successfully learns from and corrects its mistakes through repeated revisits.

**Analysis of High-Regret States (Appendix E):**
Specific examples of high-regret states in Go, Othello, and Hex illustrated that RGSC agents learned optimal strategies for these positions, leading to a rapid increase in their win rates (e.g., 9x9 Go example's win rate increased from 47% to over 90%). The regret associated with correct plays decreased, while regret for incorrect plays increased, confirming the agent's learning progression.

**Opening-Length Distributions (Appendix F):**
RGSC exhibited a phase-aware curriculum, initially favoring mid-game positions (e.g., 20-39 moves in 9x9 Go), which are often the most complex. As the agent's strength improved, the distribution shifted towards shorter opening lengths, indicating an adaptive focus on progressively earlier game stages where new learning opportunities arose. This dynamic was not observed in the RGSC variant without the regret ranking network.

**Generalization to Atari Games (Appendix G):**
Preliminary experiments integrating RGSC into MuZero on Ms. Pac-Man showed that RGSC-MuZero achieved an average score of 5166 points, outperforming standard MuZero's 3704 points under the same training budget, suggesting the generality of the approach beyond board games.

**Hyperparameter Robustness (Appendix D):**
Ablation studies on RGSC's hyperparameters (sampling probability, buffer sampling temperature, buffer size, EMA coefficient) indicated that the method is not highly sensitive to these choices, demonstrating overall robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Regret-Guided Search Control (RGSC) as an effective mechanism for improving the learning efficiency and robustness of AlphaZero-style reinforcement learning agents. The findings carry several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of RL.

**Enhanced Learning Efficiency and Robustness:** The consistent outperformance of RGSC over both standard AlphaZero and the uniform-sampling Go-Exploit baseline, across multiple complex board games and a toy environment, demonstrates a tangible improvement in learning efficiency. The average Elo gain of 77 and 89 points over the baselines, respectively, indicates that RGSC enables agents to reach higher skill levels more efficiently. Furthermore, RGSC's ability to drive significant performance improvements (e.g., from 69.3% to 78.2% win rate against KataGo) even when starting from well-trained, near-converged models is particularly significant. This addresses a common challenge in advanced RL: finding and exploiting the remaining marginal learning opportunities when an agent is already highly proficient, a task where uniform sampling methods typically falter. This capability suggests a more robust and scalable learning framework for achieving truly expert-level performance.

**Mimicking Human-Like Learning:** By explicitly identifying and revisiting "high-regret states"—positions where the agent's evaluations diverge from actual outcomes—RGSC introduces a computational analogue to human learning strategies. Human players frequently review their mistakes and focus practice on challenging positions to refine their understanding. RGSC's mechanism of using a regret network and a prioritized buffer to cycle through and gradually resolve these challenging states directly mirrors this cognitive process, bridging a conceptual gap between artificial and natural intelligence in complex strategic tasks.

**Increased Interpretability:** The regret network, by identifying specific states where the agent's understanding is weak, offers a degree of interpretability into the agent's learning process. Analyzing these high-regret states, as demonstrated in the paper with examples from Go, Othello, and Hex, can reveal specific tactical or strategic weaknesses. This interpretability is valuable for researchers to understand why an agent fails in certain situations and to potentially guide further algorithmic improvements or design targeted training curricula. The tracking of regret reduction for specific states also provides a clear signal of learning progress.

**Generalizability and Scalability:** While primarily demonstrated on board games, the underlying principles of AlphaZero and MuZero are general frameworks for sequential decision-making. The preliminary success of RGSC-MuZero on an Atari game (Ms. Pac-Man) suggests its potential applicability beyond board games to other large-scale and complex RL domains, including stochastic environments and continuous control tasks. The minimal computational overhead, especially in larger neural network architectures, further supports its scalability to more challenging problems like 19x19 Go, where efficient self-play is crucial.

**Future Directions:** The success of RGSC opens several avenues for future research. This includes:
- Exploring the application of RGSC to a wider variety of RL environments, particularly those with highly sparse rewards or complex action spaces.
- Further developing the regret network to potentially provide richer diagnostic information about agent weaknesses.
- Investigating how regret-guided search control could be combined with other efficiency-enhancing techniques in RL.
- Contributing to the broader goal of developing more data-efficient and robust RL agents that can learn and adapt continuously, even in advanced stages of training.

In conclusion, RGSC represents a significant step towards more efficient and robust reinforcement learning by intelligently guiding self-play towards high-value learning opportunities, thereby contributing to the development of more capable and human-like AI agents.
