# Optimal Dynamic Regret by Transformers for Non-Stationary Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Tokyo and RIKEN AIP show that transformers can achieve minimax optimal dynamic regret in non-stationary reinforcement learning, by learning to internally approximate advanced adaptive strategies like window schedulers and restart mechanisms through supervised pretraining. Empirical results in linear bandit environments demonstrate performance comparable to or exceeding specialized non-stationary algorithms.

## Problem

- Existing theoretical analyses of transformers in reinforcement learning primarily focus on static environments, leaving their performance in non-stationary settings largely unexplored.
- Real-world environments often involve dynamically changing reward distributions or task dynamics, posing a challenge for traditional RL algorithms that typically assume stationarity or require explicit adaptation mechanisms.
- The understanding of how transformers, with their in-context learning capabilities, can implicitly adapt to evolving environments without gradient updates was lacking.

## Method

- The study proposes ==supervised pretraining== a transformer architecture on trajectories generated by a 'base algorithm' (expert policy) that incorporates strategies for non-stationarity.
- A "non-continuous transformer" model is introduced, which is structurally equivalent to a standard transformer, to approximate an expert non-stationary algorithm (MASTER) that uses window scheduling and restart mechanisms.
- Specific transformer architectural components, like multi-head attention layers and MLPs, are designed to implement the probabilistic scheduling, masking, selection, and restart functionalities of the expert algorithm.

## Results

- Theoretically, a pre-trained transformer achieves a dynamic regret bound of O(min⁡{JT,Δ1/3T2/3}+T)O(\min\{JT}, Δ^{1/3}T^{2/3}\} + √(T))O(min{JT,Δ1/3T2/3}+T), which is minimax optimal for non-stationary environments, under conditions of sufficient training data and model capacity.
- Empirical evaluations in linear bandit settings, with both low and high degrees of non-stationarity, showed that the transformer's performance matched or in some cases surpassed specialized non-stationary expert algorithms like MASTER+LinUCB/TS.
- The research provides detailed architectural requirements, showing that a transformer with specific depth, attention heads, and activation functions can approximate the complex operations of an expert non-stationary algorithm within arbitrary error bounds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transformers can implicitly learn and execute sophisticated adaptive strategies for non-stationary environments, such as window-based data processing and restart policies, solely through in-context learning and pretraining.
> - The transformer's hidden states can be conceptualized as maintaining multiple hypothetical policies, leveraging learned randomness in self-attention to sample among them for dynamic adaptation.
> - Achieving optimal dynamic regret in non-stationary settings requires transformers of sufficient expressivity (small approximation error) and training on a sufficiently large and diverse dataset.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Transformers have emerged as a prominent class of sequence models, demonstrating considerable capabilities across various domains, including natural language processing, computer vision, and reinforcement learning. A key feature of these models is their ability to perform "in-context learning" (ICL), where they adapt to new tasks by processing example input-output pairs within their input sequence, without requiring gradient-based parameter updates. Theoretical and empirical studies have established that transformers can approximate universal functions and even simulate iterative algorithms, including certain reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms like LinUCB and Thompson Sampling in multi-armed bandit settings, and UCB value iteration in tabular Markov Decision Processes (MDPs).

Despite these advancements, a significant gap in the understanding of transformers' capabilities lies in their adaptation to non-stationary environments. Most existing theoretical analyses of transformers assume a static task or reward distribution. However, real-world scenarios frequently involve environments where the reward-generating process or underlying dynamics change over time, rendering traditional static-environment assumptions invalid. Classical algorithms developed for non-stationary multi-armed bandits employ strategies such as sliding windows, periodic resets, or change-point detection to achieve sublinear dynamic regret, effectively tracking a moving optimal policy. This paper addresses the unexplored area of how transformers perform in such dynamic, non-stationary settings, aiming to bridge the theoretical and empirical understanding of their adaptive potential.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized gap in understanding how transformers, despite their established in-context learning capabilities in stationary settings, perform in environments where the underlying reward distributions or task dynamics evolve over time. Non-stationary environments are prevalent in real-world applications, posing a significant challenge for existing RL algorithms and, by extension, for transformer-based agents that operate without explicit parameter updates.

The key objectives of this study are outlined as follows:

1. **Establish Regret Optimality in Non-Stationary Bandits**: To theoretically demonstrate that a transformer, when trained in a supervised in-context learning setting, can achieve cumulative dynamic regret bounds that match the minimax optimal rates established for non-stationary environments. Specifically, the objective is to show that transformers can achieve a rate such as $T^{2/3}$ for environments with bounded changes, which is the optimal rate derived in previous literature.
2. **Generalization Analysis under Distribution Shifts**: To extend existing offline-to-online generalization theories for transformers to the non-stationary context. This involves deriving the conditions related to pretraining data diversity and model size necessary to guarantee low dynamic regret when the transformer encounters new, non-stationary environments. This objective seeks to understand how well a transformer, trained on a diverse set of tasks, can generalize its learned "algorithm" to unseen environmental shifts.
3. **Identify Architectural Requirements for Non-Stationarity Handling**: To determine the specific architectural features of transformers—such as depth, number of attention heads, and activation functions—that enable them to internally implement strategies for adapting to non-stationary conditions. This includes mechanisms for "forgetting" outdated information and executing restart-style policies, similar to those employed by specialized non-stationary algorithms.
4. **Develop a Novel Proof Technique**: To introduce a new proof approach that conceptualizes the transformer's internal hidden state as maintaining multiple hypothetical policies. This approach leverages the transformer's learned randomness in self-attention mechanisms to effectively sample among these policies, thereby contributing to tighter regret bounds in dynamic environments.

In essence, the research aims to provide both theoretical guarantees and empirical evidence that transformers are not only capable of operating in non-stationary reinforcement learning scenarios but can also do so with near-optimal performance, by internally approximating and executing sophisticated adaptive strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study investigates the ability of transformers to achieve optimal dynamic regret in non-stationary reinforcement learning environments through a combination of theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup and Definitions

The work defines an RL environment with a sequence of state, action, and reward spaces ($S_t, A_t, R_t$), a transition model $T_t$, and a reward function $R_t$. The action space $A$ is assumed to be finite. A trajectory $D_T$ consists of observed state/action/reward tuples. An algorithm, $\text{alg}$, maps historical data and current state to a distribution over actions.
Non-stationarity is quantified by a measure $\Delta(t)$, representing the maximum change in the reward function between consecutive rounds, $|R_t(s,a) - R_{t+1}(s,a)|$. $\Delta_I$ denotes the accumulated change over an interval $I$, and $J_I$ is the number of changes. The goal is to minimize dynamic regret, defined as the difference between the accumulated maximal achievable reward and the algorithm's expected reward ($R_{\text{alg}}(T) := R^*(T) - R_{\text{alg}}(T)$).

#### 3.2. Transformer Architecture and Algorithm Induction

The study employs a standard transformer architecture comprising masked attention layers with multiple heads and feedforward MLP layers. For an input sequence $H \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, the transformer produces an output $H' \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$. In the context of RL, observed state/action/reward tuples ($s_t, a_t, r_t$) are embedded into a sequence $H$. A linear mapping $A \in \mathbb{R}^{A \times d}$ is applied to the transformer's output to generate a probability distribution over actions, defining the transformer-induced algorithm $\text{alg}_\theta( \cdot | D_{t-1}, s_t) = \text{softmax}(A \cdot \text{TF}_\theta(H))$.

#### 3.3. Supervised Pretraining

The transformer's parameters ($\theta$) are learned through supervised pretraining. This involves maximizing the log-likelihood of actions taken by a "base algorithm" ($\text{alg}_B$) that provides high-quality actions on offline trajectories. The base algorithm generates actions by observing the full trajectory and environment.

#### 3.4. Approximation of Non-Stationary Strategies

The core of the methodology is to demonstrate that a transformer can approximate established strategies for handling non-stationary environments, specifically the MASTER algorithm (MALG with Stationarity Tests and Restarts) by Wei and Luo (2021). MASTER combines a window scheduler and a restart mechanism.

- **Window Scheduler (WS)**: The WS conceptually creates multiple copies of trajectories and applies a masking operator ($\sigma_1$) to probabilistically schedule instances with varying window sizes ($W^{(i)}$) and probabilities ($p^{(i)}$). A selection operator ($\sigma_2$) then chooses a single active trajectory instance, typically the one with the lowest order among all scheduled instances.
- **Stationary Test and Restart Mechanism (RM)**: After interacting with the environment, the base algorithm performs stationary tests that output a change-detection signal ($C_t$). If $C_t=1$ (a significant change is detected), the algorithm is effectively reset, and historical auxiliary memory is cleared.

#### 3.5. Transformer Implementation of WS and RM

The paper proposes a "non-continuous transformer" architecture for conceptual simplicity, which is described as structurally equivalent to a standard transformer with augmented inputs.
- **Random Number Generation**: A two-head attention layer is constructed to approximate a cumulative distribution function, which can generate a random number sampled from a uniform distribution. This is essential for the probabilistic scheduling in WS.
- **Approximating $\sigma_1$ (Masking)**: Bernoulli masks, which probabilistically select time points based on window sizes and probabilities, are approximated using a two-layer MLP combined with the random number generation module. Block masks, which ensure contiguous time windows, are then approximated using a multi-head masked attention layer.
- **Approximating $\sigma_2$ (Selection)**: The selection of the lowest-order active instance is approximated using another multi-head masked attention layer, configured to identify and propagate information from the first non-zero (active) entry.
- **Approximating RM (Restart)**: The restart mechanism, involving zeroing out certain values and applying indicator functions for change detection, is stated to be implementable by a two-layer MLP.

These approximations collectively enable the transformer to mimic an expert algorithm ($\text{alg}_E$) that incorporates both the window scheduler and restart mechanism (Theorem 5).

#### 3.6. Theoretical Analysis Framework

The analysis leverages standard learning theory concepts:
- **Covering Number** ($\mathcal{N}_\Theta(\rho)$): Quantifies the complexity of the transformer's algorithm class, measuring how many distinct policies are needed to cover the space of possible transformer behaviors.
- **Distribution Ratio** ($\mathcal{R}_{\text{alg}_1, \text{alg}_2}$): Measures the discrepancy between the output distributions of two algorithms.
- **Assumptions**: The analysis relies on:
 - **Assumption 1 (Non-stationarity)**: The base algorithm outputs an auxiliary quantity that tracks the optimal reward and bounds cumulative errors under certain non-stationarity conditions.
 - **Assumption 2 (Realizability)**: A sufficiently large transformer can approximate the log-likelihood of the base algorithm's actions within an arbitrary error $\epsilon$.

#### 3.7. Empirical Evaluation

Experiments are conducted in a linear bandit setting with varying degrees of non-stationarity. The transformer model (a GPT-2 variant with 16 layers and 16 heads) is compared against Linear UCB, Thompson Sampling (TS), and their non-stationary variants (MASTER+LinUCB/TS). Two environments are used: "Low Non-Stationarity" with discrete reward shifts and "High Non-Stationarity" with sinusoidal reward drift.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical guarantees and empirical evidence regarding the performance of transformers in non-stationary reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Regret Optimality (Theorem 1 and Corollary 2)**: The central theoretical finding is that a transformer-induced policy ($\text{alg}_{\hat{\theta}}$), trained via supervised pretraining, can achieve a dynamic regret bound that matches the minimax optimal rate for non-stationary environments.
 - **Theorem 1** provides a detailed dynamic regret bound composed of three terms:
 - **$\mathcal{T}_{\text{comp}}$**: Represents the error from pretraining the transformer on $N$ supervised samples. This term is inversely related to $N$.
 - **$\mathcal{T}_{\text{aprx}}$**: Quantifies the approximation error incurred when the transformer attempts to imitate the base policy in the non-stationary setting. This term is directly related to the approximation error $\epsilon$.
 - **$\mathcal{T}_{\text{algo}}$**: Corresponds to the intrinsic regret of the optimal base algorithm in a non-stationary environment. This term takes the form $\mathcal{O}(\min\{\sqrt{JT}, \Delta^{1/3}T^{2/3}\} + \sqrt{T})$, where $J$ is the number of changes and $\Delta$ is the amount of changes. This rate is known to be minimax optimal.
 - **Corollary 2** refines this, stating that under conditions of a sufficiently large training dataset ($N \ge C T^3 \log T$) and a sufficiently expressive transformer (small approximation error $\epsilon \le T^{-3}$), the dynamic regret of the transformer-based algorithm reduces to $\mathcal{O}(\min\{\sqrt{JT}, \Delta^{1/3}T^{2/3}\} + \sqrt{T})$. This directly indicates that the transformer can achieve the optimal dynamic regret, matching bounds from prior works on non-stationary RL.
- **Architectural Requirements for Approximation (Theorem 5)**: The study details the architectural specifications necessary for a transformer to approximate the complex operations of an expert non-stationary algorithm like MASTER. A transformer with specific dimensions (D, M, L, D1) and parameter norms (~$\hat{\theta}$~) is shown to approximate the expert algorithm's actions within an arbitrary error $\epsilon$. This includes:
 - **Proposition 3**: Demonstrates the existence of a transformer block capable of approximating the window scheduler (WS), including generating random numbers for stochastic scheduling. This block requires specific scaling of attention heads ($M$) and hidden dimension ($D_1$) relative to $T$ and $\epsilon$.
 - **Proposition 4**: Shows that the restart mechanism (RM) can be implemented by a two-layer MLP.
 - The combined proof (Theorem 5) outlines how a "non-continuous transformer" can replicate the full MASTER algorithm by integrating these approximation blocks and handling environmental interactions and stationary tests. The "non-continuous transformer" is motivated for proof simplicity but is described as structurally equivalent to a standard transformer.

#### 4.2. Empirical Results

- **Linear Bandit Experiments**: The transformer's performance was evaluated in linear bandit settings against standard algorithms (LinUCB, Thompson Sampling) and their non-stationary "expert" variants (MASTER+LinUCB/TS).
- **Performance in Low Non-Stationarity**: In environments with discrete reward shifts, transformers exhibited cumulative regret comparable to, and in some cases, outperforming, the expert algorithms. They maintained a consistently low suboptimality rate across rounds.
- **Performance in High Non-Stationarity**: In environments with sinusoidal reward drift (e.g., $b=0.018$), transformers again demonstrated performance comparable to, or better than, the expert algorithms. However, when the level of non-stationarity became very high (e.g., $b=0.025$), the transformer's performance did not consistently outperform other models, suggesting potential limitations in generalizing to extreme unseen non-stationary scenarios, possibly due to training data coverage.
- **Generalization**: The experiments assessed generalization to unseen non-stationary environments (different drift parameters $b$ than in training data), indicating that transformers can adapt to novel dynamic conditions within a certain range.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate a theoretical foundation for transformers' near-optimal performance in non-stationary RL, supported by empirical results showing their ability to match or surpass specialized algorithms in various dynamic settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a significant theoretical contribution to the understanding of transformer capabilities in dynamic environments and has potential implications for the development of more robust AI agents.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

The most notable theoretical contribution is the proof that transformers can achieve minimax optimal dynamic regret bounds in non-stationary reinforcement learning. This result extends previous work on transformers' in-context learning abilities, moving beyond static environments to dynamic settings. By demonstrating that transformers can internally approximate and execute sophisticated adaptive strategies like window schedulers and restart mechanisms, the paper deepens the understanding of their algorithmic expressivity. The identification of specific architectural requirements (e.g., depth, attention heads, activation functions) for these approximations offers insights into the internal mechanisms transformers might employ to handle temporal shifts and forget outdated information. The novel proof technique, viewing the transformer's hidden state as maintaining multiple hypothesis policies, offers a new lens for analyzing complex transformer behaviors.

#### 5.2. Practical Implications and Potential Impact

- **Robustness in Real-World Applications**: Many real-world applications of RL, such as recommendation systems, online advertising, and robotic control, operate in environments that are inherently non-stationary due to evolving user preferences, market conditions, or physical wear-and-tear. The finding that transformers can achieve near-optimal regret in such settings suggests their potential for developing more robust and adaptable AI systems that can learn and adjust without explicit reprogramming or fine-tuning for every environmental change.
- **Foundation Models for Decision Making**: This work reinforces the vision of "foundation models" for decision-making. If a single pre-trained transformer model can learn to implement a broad class of optimal RL algorithms, including those for non-stationary environments, it could generalize to a wide array of online learning tasks, reducing the need for task-specific algorithm design.
- **In-Context Learning Advancement**: The study advances the understanding of in-context learning, showing that it can encompass not just task execution but also meta-learning of adaptive strategies for changing dynamics. This implies that pre-training transformers on diverse non-stationary scenarios could lead to agents capable of implicitly inferring and applying appropriate adaptation mechanisms.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work

The paper acknowledges several limitations and areas for future research:
- **Regret Bound Efficiency**: While optimal, the theoretical regret bounds require "sufficiently large" model sizes (small $\epsilon$) and training datasets ($N$). The paper notes that modern LLMs often meet these scales, but efficiency relative to traditional RL algorithms remains an open question.
- **Proof Method**: The use of a "non-continuous transformer" for proof simplicity, while described as structurally equivalent to a standard transformer, suggests that developing more elegant and direct proofs for standard transformer architectures is a valuable future direction.
- **Training Data Non-Stationarity**: The current theoretical guarantees do not assume specific properties of non-stationarity in the training data. Future work could explore how different types and degrees of non-stationarity in pretraining data influence the transformer's generalization and performance in online, non-stationary settings.
- **Empirical Scope**: The empirical evaluation was limited to two RL algorithms (LinUCB and Thompson Sampling) and their MASTER variants in linear bandit settings. Future work should expand the experimental setups to include broader algorithm comparisons and more complex RL problems (e.g., full MDPs) to further validate the findings and assess scalability. The observed struggle with very high non-stationarity in experiments also warrants further investigation into the generalization limits.

Overall, this research is a foundational step in establishing the theoretical and empirical capabilities of transformers for non-stationary reinforcement learning, opening avenues for more capable and adaptable AI systems in dynamic real-world scenarios.
