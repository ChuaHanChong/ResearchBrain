# Reduced-Order Model-Guided Reinforcement Learning for Demonstration-Free Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Reduced-Order Model-Guided Reinforcement Learning (ROM-GRL) is a framework that generates naturalistic humanoid locomotion without requiring motion capture data or extensive reward engineering. It distills gait guidance from a simplified, dynamically consistent reduced-order model into a full-body controller, yielding walking gaits with up to 44.4% lower average mean squared error compared to pure-reward baselines.

## Problem

- Objective-driven Reinforcement Learning (RL) for humanoid locomotion struggles with designing complex reward functions that reliably produce fluid, human-like motion, often leading to unnatural artifacts.
- Imitation-driven RL methods achieve high realism but depend heavily on extensive, curated motion capture datasets, limiting learned skills to the data's scope and availability.
- There is a need for a method that combines the natural fluidity of imitation learning with the data-independence of objective-driven RL, reducing manual design effort for humanoid locomotion control.

## Method

- A two-stage framework is developed where a teacher policy for a planar 4-DOF reduced-order model (ROM) is trained with ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== using simple forward-velocity rewards to generate biomechanically plausible gait templates.
- These ROM-generated gait features (pelvis vertical position, foot horizontal/vertical positions) serve as reference data for a full-body humanoid student policy in a MuJoCo environment.
- An adversarial discriminator is introduced to distinguish between ROM-generated and student-generated gait feature snippets, providing a 'soft imitation bonus' that guides the student policy (trained with Soft Actor-Critic, SAC) to statistically match the ROM's gait feature distribution.

## Results

- The ROM-GRL policy achieved a **44.4%** average reduction in Mean Squared Error (MSE) across key gait features compared to a pure-reward RL baseline when tracking ROM teacher references.
- Qualitative analysis demonstrated that ROM-GRL policies produced more accurate leg trajectories, improved foot clearance, and enhanced gait symmetry compared to baseline methods.
- The framework showed generality by successfully guiding a 1 m/s full-body policy to produce ROM-like gait patterns using a ROM teacher trained at 4 m/s, indicating transferable gait characteristics across speeds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simplified, dynamically consistent reduced-order models can effectively serve as 'teachers' to distill high-level gait guidance, capturing essential dynamics like center-of-mass oscillation and periodic footfall timing.
> - Embedding ROM behavior into the RL objective via distribution constraints, rather than strict trajectory tracking, ensures statistical similarity in gait dynamics, leading to more robust and naturalistic full-body locomotion.
> - An adversarial discriminator can bridge the gap between low-dimensional ROM outputs and high-dimensional full-body policies, enabling the synthesis of naturalistic gaits without direct motion capture data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid locomotion aims to develop bipedal robots and virtual characters capable of exhibiting natural, stable, and versatile movement. Historically, this has involved traditional model-based control methods, which are often labor-intensive in design and may not capture the nuanced realism of human motion. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, data-driven approach, allowing agents to learn complex behaviors through trial-and-error.

Current RL methods for locomotion generally fall into two categories:
1. **Purely Objective-Driven RL:** These methods train locomotion policies from scratch by optimizing carefully designed reward functions, without relying on motion capture (mocap) data. They encourage physically plausible and human-like traits such as forward speed, energy efficiency, stability, upright posture, and symmetric gait. While capable of producing stable gaits and demonstrating that symmetric walking can emerge from reward design alone, this approach is challenged by the difficulty of reward engineering, which often requires extensive hand-tuning and expertise. Policies may also exhibit subtle artifacts or unnatural behaviors due to the absence of a direct human motion template.
2. **Imitation-Driven RL:** This paradigm utilizes human motion capture data to guide the RL agent, providing rewards for matching reference poses, velocities, or using adversarial critics to judge realism against a motion dataset. Approaches like DeepMimic, ASE, and CALM have demonstrated high fidelity to mocap examples, enabling characters to reproduce dynamic skills and exhibit human-like movement quality. More recently, Behavioral Foundation Models pre-trained on large motion datasets have shown potential for zero-shot task performance while retaining human-like gait. The primary limitation of this approach is its heavy reliance on extensive, curated motion datasets, meaning learned skills are bounded by the diversity and availability of these datasets.

The presented work, Reduced-Order Model-Guided Reinforcement Learning (ROM-GRL), aims to bridge the gap between these two paradigms. It seeks to achieve natural, human-like locomotion without requiring human motion capture data (a limitation of imitation learning) and without relying on elaborate reward shaping (a challenge for objective-driven RL). ROM-GRL proposes using a simplified, dynamically consistent reduced-order model (ROM) as a "stand-in" for motion capture data. This ROM generates high-level gait guidance, which is then distilled into a full-body controller. This approach integrates insights from model-based gait generation with the flexibility of RL, maintaining a physics-driven training regime while inducing realistic movement patterns. It distinguishes itself from prior ROM-guided RL by embedding the ROM's behavior into the RL objective via distribution constraints, rather than strict trajectory tracking, to ensure statistical similarity in gait dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reinforcement learning framework that can generate natural, stable, and energy-efficient humanoid locomotion without requiring external motion capture data or extensive, hand-tuned reward functions.

This objective is motivated by several challenges inherent in existing approaches to humanoid locomotion:
- **Difficulty of Reward Engineering in Objective-Driven RL:** While purely objective-driven RL can discover stable gaits, designing comprehensive reward functions that reliably produce fluid, human-like motion is complex and often leads to unnatural artifacts or inconsistencies. Policies are highly sensitive to reward term choices and weightings, necessitating significant expert intervention.
- **Dependency on Motion Capture Data in Imitation Learning:** Imitation-based RL methods achieve high realism by tracking human demonstrations. However, this necessitates access to large, diverse datasets of human motion, which can be expensive to acquire, limited in scope, and often not applicable to novel tasks or different robot morphologies. The learned behaviors are inherently constrained by the distribution of the training data.
- **Bridging the Gap:** There is a need for a method that combines the advantages of both paradigms—achieving the natural fluidity and subtle timing typically associated with imitation learning, while retaining the data-independence and exploratory potential of objective-driven RL.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the ROM-GRL framework are:
- To leverage a simplified, dynamically consistent reduced-order model (ROM) to generate energy-efficient and biomechanically plausible gait templates that capture the essence of natural walking.
- To develop a two-stage reinforcement learning framework where these ROM-generated gait templates provide high-level guidance to a full-body humanoid controller.
- To distill the dynamically consistent gait features from the ROM into a high-dimensional full-body policy, ensuring the student policy's gait feature distribution statistically matches that of the ROM teacher.
- To demonstrate that this approach can produce robust, stable, and naturally fluid walking gaits across different speeds, requiring only simple forward-velocity rewards and no additional reward shaping or human motion data.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a novel solution for synthesizing life-like humanoid behaviors, minimizing the reliance on external data or intricate manual design, and thus making humanoid locomotion learning more accessible and scalable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Reduced-Order Model–Guided Reinforcement Learning (ROM-GRL) framework operates in two distinct stages:

#### 3.1. Stage 1: ROM Trajectory Generation and Teacher Policy Training

The first stage focuses on creating dynamically consistent gait representations using a simplified model.
- **Reduced-Order Model (ROM) Implementation:** A planar, 4-Degree-of-Freedom (DOF) ROM is implemented. This model consists of a central square of mass (representing the center of mass), two massless linear springs connecting it to point feet, and revolute hip joints with continuous torque actuation. This structure is designed to capture essential gait dynamics such as center-of-mass oscillation, pendulum-like leg swing, and periodic footfall timing, while remaining computationally lightweight.
- **Environment and Observation/Action Space:** The ROM is exposed as a Gymnasium environment, built using C++ with the Box2D physics engine for efficiency. The observation vector is 20-dimensional, encompassing the body's planar position and velocity, each spring's length and velocity, hip joint angles and angular velocities, and the target forward speed. The action space is 2-dimensional, applying continuous torques to the left and right hip joints within the range [−1, 1].
- **Teacher Policy Training:** A teacher policy for this ROM is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The reward function is defined as `r_t = exp(−α |v_COM − v_target|)`, where `v_COM` is the horizontal velocity of the central mass and `v_target` is the commanded speed, yielding a bounded reward between (0, 1]. The policy and value networks are two-layer MLPs with 512 hidden units and ReLU activations. Training utilizes Adam optimizer (learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁴), a discount factor γ = 0.99, and a clipping ratio of 0.2, collecting experience from eight parallel actors over one million environment steps.
- **Reference Trajectory Generation:** After the teacher policy converges (approximately 5 × 10⁵ steps), it is rolled out for `T` time steps (2,000 steps) to record 5-dimensional trajectory snippets. These snippets include the vertical position of the body's center of mass (`y_COM`) and the horizontal and vertical positions of the left and right feet relative to the center of mass (`x_L`, `y_L`, `x_R`, `y_R`). These signals are selected for their ability to minimally yet expressively represent global balance dynamics, foot placement, stride length, and periodic timing. These ROM-produced signals form the reference dataset (`τ_ROM`) for the next stage.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Student Policy Training with Distribution Constraint

The second stage focuses on distilling the ROM-guided behaviors into a full-body humanoid controller.
- **Full-Body Humanoid Environment:** A modified HumEnv benchmark, based on MuJoCo, is used. To simplify the problem and improve training efficiency, lateral translation and yaw rotation at the root are fixed, restricting locomotion to the sagittal plane. The observation space is 358-dimensional, including root height, local position, rotation (6D), linear velocity, and angular velocity for each of the 24 rigid body segments. The action space is a 21-dimensional vector of normalized hinge-joint torques, one for each actuated revolute joint.
- **Adversarial Discriminator:** To enforce smooth, human-like locomotion similar to the ROM, an adversarial discriminator (D) is introduced. This discriminator is trained to distinguish between "real" 5-D gait feature snippets sampled from the ROM-generated reference data (`τ_ROM`) and "fake" snippets generated by the full-body student policy (`τ_student`). The discriminator's architecture is a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers (64 and 32 units) and LeakyReLU activations, with dropout (p=0.2). Gaussian noise (σ=0.05) is injected into features, and label smoothing (0.9 for expert, 0.1 for agent) is applied during training. The discriminator is optimized with Adam (learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁵) and includes a gradient penalty (coefficient λ = 10) on random interpolations to enforce a 1-Lipschitz constraint.
- **Imitation Bonus and Combined Objective:** The discriminator's output is used to compute a soft imitation bonus (`r_im(τ) = -log(1 - D(τ))`). This bonus is large when the discriminator perceives the student's trajectory snippet `τ` as highly likely under the ROM distribution.
- **Student Policy Training with Soft Actor–Critic (SAC):** The student policy (π) is trained using Soft Actor–Critic (SAC), an off-policy actor-critic method that includes an entropy term for exploration. SAC is chosen for its sample efficiency and robustness. The student policy maximizes a combined objective:
 `J(π) = E_(s,a)∼π [η * r_env(s, a)] + E_τ∼π [(1 - η) * r_im(τ)]`
 Here, `r_env` is the standard environment reward (forward-velocity matching), and `η = 0.5` balances task performance with imitation fidelity. The discriminator is updated once every five SAC actor-critic updates, starting at global step 5,000. Early termination is implemented if validation loss on a 10% holdout subset of ROM trajectories fails to improve for ten consecutive updates.

This two-stage approach allows the framework to leverage the simplicity and dynamic consistency of the ROM to guide the learning of a complex, high-dimensional full-body policy, achieving natural gaits without motion capture or elaborate reward engineering.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The ROM-GRL method was evaluated against a pure-reward RL baseline (a full-body MuJoCo policy trained with SAC using only the forward-velocity environment reward) at two target speeds: 1 m/s and 4 m/s. The ROM teacher was trained for a target speed of 4 m/s.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Trajectory Comparison

- **Pelvis and Foot Trajectories (Figure 3):** Visualizations of pelvis and foot trajectories demonstrated that the ROM-GRL policy (blue) tracked the ROM teacher reference (green) more closely than the pure-reward baseline (orange) at both 1 m/s and 4 m/s. The ROM-GRL policy exhibited improved foot clearance and gait symmetry. In contrast, the baseline showed substantial deviations during the swing phase, resulting in lower foot height and asymmetric strides.
- **Generality at 1 m/s:** Although the ROM teacher was trained at 4 m/s, the discriminator successfully guided the 1 m/s policy to produce ROM-like gait patterns. This indicated that the low-dimensional ROM outputs captured transferable gait characteristics across different speeds, suggesting the method's generality without needing to retrain the ROM for each speed.

#### 4.2. Temporal Tracking Performance at 4 m/s

- **Time-Series Analysis (Figure 4):** At a target speed of 4 m/s, time series plots for pelvis vertical position, and left/right foot horizontal positions showed close overlap between the ROM teacher (solid lines) and the ROM-GRL policy (dashed lines). This demonstrated minimal phase lag and amplitude error, indicating that the student policy accurately reproduced both the timing and magnitude of key gait features.

#### 4.3. Quantitative Error Analysis at 4 m/s

- **Mean Squared Error (MSE) Comparison (Figure 5, Table 1):** Quantitative analysis at 4 m/s focused on the mean squared error (MSE) between each policy and the ROM teacher across five channels: pelvis Y, left foot X, left foot Z, right foot X, and right foot Z.
 - ROM-GRL consistently achieved substantially lower MSE across all channels compared to the pure-reward baseline.
 - Specific MSE reductions were:
 - Pelvis Y: 71.0% reduction (from 0.01407 to 0.00408)
 - Left Foot X: 24.1% reduction (from 0.25035 to 0.18992)
 - Left Foot Z: 2.4% reduction (from 0.01750 to 0.01708)
 - Right Foot X: 62.1% reduction (from 0.18821 to 0.07136)
 - Right Foot Z: 62.2% reduction (from 0.02543 to 0.00962)
 - The overall average MSE reduction achieved by ROM-GRL was 44.4%.

#### 4.4. Discussion of Results

The experimental findings confirm that incorporating ROM guidance via a simple five-dimensional discriminator substantially improves humanoid gait tracking. Qualitatively, ROM-GRL policies replicate ROM teacher leg trajectories more accurately, exhibiting clearer swing-phase motion and enhanced left-right symmetry. Temporally, both phase lag and amplitude mismatches in pelvis vertical motion and foot horizontal excursions are reduced. Quantitatively, ROM-GRL demonstrates a significant average MSE reduction compared to a pure-reward baseline. The ability of the discriminator to guide a policy at 1 m/s using a ROM trained at 4 m/s indicates the transferability of the learned gait characteristics. These results suggest that even a compact, dynamically consistent ROM can effectively teach robust and naturalistic walking gaits to a full-body policy without requiring motion capture data or extensive reward engineering.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ROM-GRL framework presents a significant advancement in humanoid locomotion by offering a method that overcomes key limitations of existing reinforcement learning paradigms.

- **Bridging the Gap Between Objective-Driven and Imitation Learning:** The primary significance lies in its ability to generate naturalistic, stable, and energy-efficient humanoid gaits without the need for human motion capture data (as required by imitation learning) or intricate, hand-tuned reward functions (a challenge in objective-driven RL). This innovation provides a pathway to achieve high-quality locomotion where mocap data is unavailable or difficult to acquire, and where complex reward shaping is impractical.
- **Reduced Development Effort:** By distilling guidance from a simple, lightweight ROM, the framework substantially reduces the manual effort typically involved in designing reward functions or collecting extensive motion datasets. The use of a simple forward-velocity reward for both ROM and full-body training simplifies the overall control design process.
- **Enhanced Gait Quality:** The experimental results demonstrate that ROM-GRL produces gaits with improved symmetry, foot clearance, and temporal fidelity compared to pure-reward baselines. This leads to more physically plausible and visually natural movements for simulated humanoids.
- **Generality and Transferability:** The ability of the framework to guide a 1 m/s policy using a ROM trained for 4 m/s suggests that the low-dimensional ROM can capture fundamental gait characteristics that are transferable across different speeds. This indicates potential for developing more generalizable controllers.
- **Foundation for Further Research:** ROM-GRL establishes a novel teacher-student learning paradigm where the teacher is a computationally inexpensive dynamic model. This approach can inform future research in hierarchical control and knowledge distillation for complex robotic systems.

**Potential Impact:**
The development of demonstration-free, naturalistic locomotion methods like ROM-GRL could have several impacts:
- **Robotics:** It could enable more autonomous and versatile bipedal robots that can learn robust and agile movements for various tasks without requiring human-specific demonstrations. This is particularly relevant for scenarios where human demonstrations are difficult or dangerous to obtain.
- **Computer Animation and Gaming:** The framework could facilitate the creation of more realistic and diverse virtual characters, capable of generating fluid and natural movements procedurally, without relying on extensive mocap libraries. This could reduce animation production costs and enhance character realism.
- **Accessibility of Humanoid Control:** By lowering the barriers to entry (no mocap, simpler reward design), ROM-GRL could make advanced humanoid locomotion control more accessible to researchers and developers with limited resources.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
Despite its successes, the authors acknowledge several limitations that indicate areas for future impact:
- **ROM Simplification:** The current ROM assumes fixed-length legs and strictly planar dynamics, limiting its applicability to a narrow walking regime. Future work aims to develop variable leg ROMs (e.g., SLIP-style springs) with actuated leg extension to generate templates for running, hopping, and high-impact maneuvers, thus enabling ROM-GRL to teach a wider range of gaits.
- **Discriminator Feature Set:** The discriminator currently operates on only five key gait features. While effective, this may omit subtler aspects of high-dimensional human motion. Expanding or refining the feature set could lead to even more nuanced and realistic behaviors.
- **Validation Scope:** The full-body policy has only been validated in simulation on flat terrain. Future work will involve deployment on hardware and testing in more challenging environments, such as slopes, variable friction surfaces, or obstacle-rich settings, to assess robustness and real-world applicability.
- **Latent Control:** Introducing latent control in the student policy, by conditioning it on a low-dimensional latent variable, could parameterize target speed and gait style. This would enable continuous interpolation between different locomotion modes (walking, jogging, sprinting) without retraining, providing richer and style-controllable guidance to the agent, further enhancing versatility.

By addressing these limitations, the ROM-GRL framework has the potential to evolve into a more comprehensive and robust solution for generating highly dynamic, speed-adaptive, and versatile humanoid locomotion policies, with potential for sim-to-real transfer.
