# RAPID: Robust and Agile Planner Using Inverse Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Based Drone Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> RAPID, an inverse reinforcement learning framework from Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology, enables autonomous drones to navigate complex, cluttered environments at high speeds using vision-based perception. The system demonstrated real-world agile flight up to 8.8 m/s without real-world training, achieving superior collision avoidance compared to baselines in diverse simulated and physical settings.

## Problem

- Classical drone navigation methods suffer from high computational costs and latency, making them unsuitable for agile, high-speed flight in cluttered environments.
- Existing learning-based approaches (Behavior Cloning, Reinforcement Learning, and Inverse Reinforcement Learning) face challenges such as compounding errors, complex reward function design, sample inefficiency, and difficulties in vision-based continuous action spaces.
- A significant sim-to-real gap prevents the direct deployment of simulation-trained policies to real-world drones due to discrepancies in sensor noise and dynamics.

## Method

- The **RAPID** framework utilizes inverse soft Q-learning to infer an implicit reward function directly from expert demonstrations, mitigating the need for manual reward design and improving sample efficiency.
- An auxiliary autoencoder loss function is integrated to learn compact representations from high-dimensional depth images, enhancing learning robustness and efficiently handling visual input complexity.
- The framework accounts for controller tracking error during training and uses realistic simulated depth images to bridge the sim-to-real gap, enabling successful real-world deployment without real-world fine-tuning.

## Results

- RAPID achieved the highest collision avoidance performance in simulations, with a **94.44%** Mission Progress and 9/10 Success Rate in lower density environments, consistently outperforming classical and other learning-based methods.
- A simulation-trained RAPID policy successfully enabled autonomous high-speed flight in real-world natural and urban cluttered environments, reaching maximum speeds of **8.8** m/s without any additional real-world training or tuning.
- The system demonstrated efficient real-time operation with an inference time of **10.24** ms on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX and exhibited adaptive speed control, decelerating near obstacles and accelerating in open areas.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inverse Reinforcement Learning, specifically inverse soft Q-learning, can be successfully applied to high-speed, vision-based continuous control tasks for drones, effectively learning robust policies from expert behavior.
> - Explicitly modeling controller tracking errors and generating realistic simulated sensor data during training are critical for achieving robust sim-to-real transfer in agile drone navigation.
> - Combining an auxiliary autoencoder loss with the policy learning process enhances the ability to extract meaningful features from raw visual inputs, improving learning efficiency and overall performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous drone navigation, particularly agile flight in complex and cluttered environments, presents significant challenges in perception, control, and real-time motion planning. Traditional approaches, often termed "classical methods," rely on modular pipelines that sequentially process perception (e.g., depth images to 3D point clouds), mapping (e.g., occupancy grid maps or ESDFs), and planning (e.g., trajectory optimization). While interpretable, these methods suffer from high computational costs, latency, discretization artifacts, and increased pose-estimation errors at high speeds, limiting their applicability for agile flight.

In response, learning-based methods have emerged as alternatives. These methods aim to integrate perception and planning into a single neural network, reducing latency and enabling faster real-time decision-making. Within learning-based approaches, several paradigms exist:

- **Imitation Learning (IL):** Behavior Cloning (BC) is a supervised approach that directly learns policies from expert demonstrations. While simple, BC is prone to compounding errors from limited expert imitation and distribution shift, struggling to generalize to unseen states. Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) attempts to mitigate these by iteratively collecting expert labels in new scenarios, but it relies on a fast, readily available expert.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** RL methods train agents to interact with an environment and optimize policies through reward maximization. They offer robustness but face challenges in reward function design, sample inefficiency, and extensive exploration, especially in vision-based high-dimensional spaces. Parallel simulators and techniques like privileged learning or curriculum learning are often required for convergence.
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** IRL aims to infer the underlying reward function directly from expert demonstrations and then derive an optimal policy from that reward. It shares similarities with BC in using expert data but can achieve more robust policies by mitigating compounding errors and generating new states. Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) is a prominent IRL method, but it can suffer from mode collapse and biased reward problems. Recent non-adversarial IRL approaches, such as Inverse Soft Q-learning (IQ-learning) and Least-Squares Inverse Q-learning (LS-IQ), have improved stability and bias handling, but still struggle with the curse of dimensionality and precise flight attitude control in vision-based continuous action spaces.

A significant challenge across all learning-based methods is the **sim-to-real gap**, where policies trained in simulation perform poorly in real-world environments due to discrepancies in sensor noise characteristics, environmental dynamics, and drone dynamics.

This paper positions its work, RAPID, as an advancement in IRL for high-speed visual drone navigation. It specifically targets the limitations of existing IRL methods when applied to complex, vision-based continuous action spaces and high-speed scenarios, while also addressing the sim-to-real gap. The authors state that this is the first work to successfully apply an IRL framework for high-speed visual navigation of drones.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generalizable and sample-efficient visual navigation algorithm for drones using high-dimensional visual information, capable of performing reliably in real-world scenarios without requiring real-world training data. The authors aim to achieve agile, collision-free drone flight in cluttered environments by generating waypoints in milliseconds.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several identified limitations in current drone navigation paradigms:

- **Limitations of Classical Methods:** These methods, with their modular architectures (separate perception, mapping, planning), incur high computational costs and latency, rendering them unsuitable for agile, real-time drone flight in dynamic, complex environments like forests or factories.
- **Inherent Limitations of Behavior Cloning (BC):** BC, while straightforward, suffers from compounding errors due to limited expert imitation and distribution shift, meaning it struggles to generalize to states not precisely represented in the training data. This makes it unreliable for robust navigation in diverse, unknown environments.
- **Challenges in Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Pure RL struggles with the intricate design of effective reward functions, which are critical for guiding learning, and exhibits low sample efficiency, requiring vast amounts of interaction with the environment. These issues are exacerbated in high-speed, vision-based scenarios with high-dimensional state spaces.
- **Difficulties in Existing Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** While IRL promises to learn reward functions from expert behavior, its application to vision-based tasks, especially for drones, has been largely unexplored. Drone navigation involves complex 3D spatial awareness and precise attitude control, making policy learning more demanding than in other domains like autonomous driving. Current IRL methods, even non-adversarial ones like LS-IQ, reportedly struggle with the curse of dimensionality when processing vision-based continuous action spaces and effectively managing absorbing states (e.g., goal or collision states) in high-speed contexts.
- **Vision-Based Feature Learning:** Extracting meaningful, task-relevant features directly from raw, unstructured, and noisy visual data is a significant hurdle. This demands substantial data and careful network design, adding to the complexity of vision-based IRL.
- **The Sim-to-Real Gap:** A fundamental challenge in all learning-based approaches is the discrepancy between simulated and real-world environments, particularly concerning visual noise characteristics and drone dynamics. This gap hinders the direct deployment of simulation-trained policies to real hardware.

To address these motivations, the specific contributions proposed in this paper are:

- **Development of an inverse soft Q-learning-based framework:** This framework is tailored for high-speed visual navigation, aiming for robust and sample-efficient learning without requiring manual reward function design. It integrates a specialized treatment of absorbing states for high-speed scenarios.
- **Introduction of an auxiliary autoencoder loss function:** This component is designed to mitigate the complexity arising from high-dimensional visual inputs, thereby improving learning efficiency by forcing the network to learn compact, meaningful feature representations.
- **Reduction of the sim-to-real gap:** The framework accounts for controller tracking error during training. This aims to ensure that the learned policies generate feasible trajectories that can be accurately executed by real-world drone hardware, enabling successful high-speed flight experiments in diverse real environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the Robust and Agile Planner using Inverse Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Based Drone Navigation (RAPID), a learning-based visual planner designed to generate collision-free waypoints from depth images and drone state inputs. These waypoints are subsequently converted into continuous trajectories for execution by a tracking controller. The overall framework involves expert data generation, policy learning with an implicit reward, inference, and trajectory generation and tracking.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The vision-based navigation problem is modeled as an infinite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP), characterized by states ($s$), actions ($a$), initial state distribution ($p(s_0)$), next states ($s'$), transition probabilities ($p(s', s|a)$), a reward function ($r(s, a)$), and a discount factor ($\gamma$). The policy $\pi$ is stochastic, mapping states to actions. Expert data is denoted $D_{\pi_E}$ and learner data $D_\pi$.

#### 3.2. States and Actions

The policy network $\pi(a_t|s_t)$ takes the state $s_t = [I_t, v_t, q_t, g_t]$ as input.
- $I_t$: A $64 \times 64$ depth image. To bridge the sim-to-real gap, simulated stereo depth images are generated using the semi-global matching (SGM) method, mimicking real depth sensor noise characteristics. A lower resolution ($64 \times 64$) is used to reduce overfitting and improve robustness.
- $v_t$: Drone's 3D velocity.
- $q_t$: Drone's attitude quaternion.
- $g_t$: Relative 3D goal vector.

The action $a_t$ consists of $N=10$ waypoints, each separated by a fixed time interval $T=0.1s$. Each waypoint is defined in cylindrical coordinates by a relative distance $\Delta r_i$ and a relative angle $\Delta \psi_i$. This representation, $a_{raw_t} = \{(\Delta r_1, \Delta \psi_1), \dots, (\Delta r_N, \Delta \psi_N)\}$, is chosen to constrain the action range and stabilize training, aligning it more closely with expert actions compared to position-based or Cartesian velocity-based actions. These raw actions are then transformed into absolute Cartesian positions $a_t = \{p_1, \dots, p_N\}$ recursively using cumulative heading angles.

#### 3.3. Sample Efficient Training with Image Reconstruction

To address challenges with high-dimensional visual inputs and improve learning efficiency:
- **Auxiliary Loss Function for Autoencoder:** A $\beta$-variational autoencoder ($\beta$-VAE) is employed to learn compact state representations from depth images ($I_t$). It consists of a convolutional encoder $g_\phi$ mapping $I_t$ to a latent vector $z_t$, and a deconvolutional decoder $f_\theta$ reconstructing $I_t$ from $z_t$. An $\ell_2$ penalty on $z_t$ and weight decay on $\theta$ are applied as auxiliary objectives. The actor and critic share the same convolutional encoder parameters, but to prevent negative impact, the actor's gradients are blocked from updating the encoder. The encoder is updated solely by the critic's gradients, and a faster Polyak averaging rate is applied to the target Q-function's encoder parameters.
- **Skipping Connection Networks:** The D2RL (Deep Dense RL) architecture, incorporating skip connections, is utilized. This design aims to preserve important input information, enable faster convergence in deeper networks, and improve mutual information between input and output, which can be degraded by non-linear transformations in deep RL architectures. Network weights are initialized using orthogonal initialization for fully-connected layers and delta-orthogonal initialization for convolutional/deconvolutional layers to improve training stability.

#### 3.4. Policy Learning with Implicit Reward

RAPID employs the Least Squares Inverse Q-learning (LS-IQ) algorithm to learn an implicit reward function.
- **Learning Implicit Reward:** LS-IQ directly learns a Q-function by leveraging the inverse Bellman operator $T_\pi Q$, which allows the reward function $r(s,a)$ to be expressed entirely in terms of Q-functions. This eliminates the need for explicit reward network training and transforms the optimization problem into learning only the Q-function. To enhance stability, a regularizer $\psi(r)$ is applied using a mixture of distributions from both expert and learner data, mitigating instability in continuous action spaces.
- **Managing Absorbing States in High-Speed Visual Navigation:** A specific treatment for absorbing states (goal or collision) is proposed to address instability caused by recursive bootstrapping. RAPID combines bootstrapping for non-terminal states with an analytical computation for absorbing states. Furthermore, the maximum ($r_{max}$) and minimum ($r_{min}$) rewards for absorbing states are asymmetrically set to $r_{max}=0$ and $r_{min}=-2$. This prevents the agent from receiving excessively high rewards for reaching terminal states when dangerously close to obstacles, thereby enhancing obstacle avoidance.
- **Soft Actor-Critic Update:** The policy $\pi$ is updated using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), an off-policy maximum entropy deep reinforcement learning algorithm suitable for continuous action spaces. SAC optimizes a trade-off between maximizing expected return and maximizing policy entropy, promoting exploration.

#### 3.5. Trajectory Generation and Control

Discrete waypoints generated by the network are converted into a continuous, differentiable trajectory. A 4th-degree piecewise polynomial function is used for trajectory representation, minimizing the integral of squared acceleration. This optimization problem is solved using a closed-form solution from prior work, ensuring velocity continuity at intermediate waypoints and zero terminal velocity/acceleration at the final waypoint. A geometric controller is chosen for trajectory tracking due to its low latency and ease of implementation, which is beneficial for the learning process compared to computationally intensive Model Predictive Control (MPC).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of RAPID was evaluated through extensive simulations and real-world flight experiments, comparing its performance against classical and other learning-based methods.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Comparison Methods:** RAPID was compared with EGO-planner (a classical map-based planner, in low-speed EGO-LOW at 4 m/s and high-speed EGO-HIGH at 7 m/s), Behavior Cloning (BC), LS-IQ, and AGILE (a DAgger-based planner). All learning-based methods used a geometric controller for fair comparison of waypoint generation.
- **Validation on Test Environments:** Experiments were conducted in environments with varying tree densities (1/80, 1/50, 1/30, 1/25 trees/m$^2$), obstacle sizes, and shapes. Metrics included Mission Progress (MP), average speed, and Success Rate (SR).
- **Performance Overview:**
 - **EGO-planner:** Performed well at low speeds (EGO-LOW: 90.62% MP, 8/10 SR at 1/80 density) but significantly degraded at high speeds (EGO-HIGH: 75.40% MP, 5/10 SR at 1/80 density), attributed to planning latency and accumulated pose errors.
 - **BC:** Showed the lowest performance (48.36% MP, 4/10 SR at 1/80 density), suffering from overfitting and compounding errors due to strict reliance on expert actions and limited generalization.
 - **LS-IQ:** Outperformed BC but exhibited limitations, prioritizing high speed over robust collision avoidance in denser environments, leading to diminishing robustness as tree density increased (58.26% MP, 5/10 SR at 1/80 density).
 - **AGILE:** Demonstrated strong performance in lower-density environments (82.12% MP, 6/10 SR at 1/80 density) but showed performance drops with increasing complexity. Its tendency for large directional adjustments could increase collision likelihood in dense maps, and it struggled to account for controller estimation errors.
 - **RAPID (Ours):** Achieved the highest collision avoidance performance across all tested conditions (94.44% MP, 9/10 SR at 1/80 density; 88.69% MP, 6/10 SR at 1/25 density), consistently surpassing other methods. Its advantage derived from incorporating additional samples through online interactions, which mitigates distribution shift, and directly integrating controller tracking errors into the learning process. RAPID also demonstrated adaptive speed control, flying faster in open areas and slowing down in dense regions (average speed 7.90 m/s at 1/80 density). The authors highlighted that RAPID, an IRL-based method, effectively generalized across various environments and handled constrained data conditions better than the DAgger-based AGILE, which requires frequent real-time labeled data from an expert.

#### 4.2. Hardware Setup and System Overview

- **Drone Hardware:** A lightweight drone, similar to racing drones, was custom-built with Velox 2550 kV motors, Gemfan Hurricane 51466 propellers, and Cyclone 45A BLHeli S ESCs. The drone weighed 1.1 kg and achieved a thrust-to-weight ratio of 3.57.
- **Onboard Computation:** An NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX was used. RAPID demonstrated an inference time of 10.24 ms, which is approximately six times faster than AGILE (63.89 ms), despite having more parameters, due to its threefold lower FLOPS (26M vs. 97M).
- **Sensors:** An Oak-D Pro depth camera provided stereo images (for VIO) and stereo depth images (for neural network input) at 20 Hz.
- **System Architecture:**
 - **VIO (Visual Inertial Odometry):** OpenVINS was used for robust state estimation, taking image and IMU (200 Hz) measurements, publishing local odometry at 20 Hz.
 - **Local Planner:** The RAPID module generates collision-free waypoints at 10 Hz. These are converted to continuous trajectories using minimum acceleration-based trajectory generation.
 - **Controller:** A geometric controller receives target position and velocity commands (sampled at 50 Hz from the continuous trajectory) and computes body rates and thrust commands, which are then sent to the PX4 controller at 250 Hz.

#### 4.3. Experiment Results (Real-World)

The trained model was deployed in real-world natural and urban environments without additional training or tuning, demonstrating successful sim-to-real transfer.
- **Natural Environments:**
 - **Long Forest:** In an environment with trees spaced 5 meters apart and a 60-meter goal, the drone successfully performed obstacle avoidance, reaching a maximum speed of 7.5 m/s.
 - **Short Forest:** A denser environment with curved trees spaced within 2 meters and a 30-meter goal. The drone navigated successfully at higher speeds, with waypoints generated for a 0.9-second duration, achieving a maximum speed of 8.8 m/s.
 - **Behavioral Observation:** The IRL training allowed the policy to exhibit adaptive acceleration and deceleration behaviors, not explicitly present in the constant-velocity expert dataset. The drone reduced speed near obstacles before avoidance maneuvers, suggesting the learned policy effectively captured the intention of collision avoidance.
- **Urban Environments:**
 - **Large Block:** Geometrically simple but large obstacles. The drone generated avoidance paths early and reached its destination, achieving a maximum speed of 6.2 m/s, with an average speed of 6 m/s (speed was intentionally reduced due to higher safety risks in hard environments).
 - **Columns:** An environment with six large column-shaped obstacles. The drone decelerated while avoiding obstacles and accelerated afterward, reaching a maximum speed of 6.5 m/s.
 - **Generalization:** Experiments in urban environments demonstrated the model’s ability to generalize to novel obstacle shapes beyond those encountered in the simulation training (tree-like structures). Minimal performance degradation was observed compared to simulation, indicating partial mitigation of the sim-to-real gap.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents RAPID as a novel and effective learning-based planner for agile, high-speed drone navigation in complex, cluttered environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Pioneering IRL Application for High-Speed Visual Drone Navigation:** The paper claims to be the first to successfully apply an Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) framework for high-speed visual navigation of drones. This demonstrates the viability of IRL in a highly complex and dynamic domain, extending its utility beyond previously explored applications.
- **Real-time and Agile Flight Capabilities:** RAPID enables drones to generate collision-free waypoints in milliseconds and perform agile maneuvers at average speeds of 7 m/s (up to 8.8 m/s maximum in real-world experiments). This capability is crucial for applications requiring rapid response and navigation in constrained spaces, surpassing the limitations of slower, modular classical approaches.
- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency and Robustness without Manual Reward Design:** By leveraging inverse soft Q-learning and tailored absorbing state treatment, RAPID addresses key challenges of traditional RL, such as difficult reward function design and sample inefficiency. The implicit reward learning from expert demonstrations leads to a robust policy without explicit manual tuning of rewards, which is a major hurdle in many RL applications.
- **Effective Handling of High-Dimensional Visual Inputs:** The introduction of an auxiliary autoencoder loss function effectively mitigates the complexity of high-dimensional visual inputs, enhancing learning efficiency and robustness to visual noise. This contributes to more stable and efficient feature extraction from raw depth images.
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The methodology specifically addresses the sim-to-real gap by accounting for controller tracking error during training and generating simulated depth images that closely match real sensor characteristics. The successful deployment of a simulation-trained policy to real-world drones in diverse natural and urban environments, without further real-world training, demonstrates significant progress in bridging this gap.
- **Broadening Autonomous Drone Applications:** The ability of drones to navigate rapidly and robustly in complex, unknown environments expands their potential for critical applications. These include search and rescue operations in disaster zones, exploration of hazardous or inaccessible urban and indoor environments, and autonomous target tracking, where high-speed and agile capabilities are essential.
- **Advancements in Learning-Based Robotics:** This work contributes to the broader field of learning-based robotics by providing a practical framework that overcomes several limitations of existing imitation learning and reinforcement learning paradigms, particularly in the context of vision-based continuous control tasks.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as the lack of temporal awareness, potential for infeasible trajectories during exploration, issues with episodic incompleteness in expert datasets, and imperfect mitigation of the sim-to-real gap, the demonstrated performance in real-world high-speed flight positions RAPID as a notable advancement, offering a robust foundation for future research in autonomous drone navigation.
