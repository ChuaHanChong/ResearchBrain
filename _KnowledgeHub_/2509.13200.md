# StageACT: Stage-Conditioned Imitation for Robust Humanoid Door Opening

> [!summary] Summary
> FieldAI researchers developed StageACT, a stage-conditioned imitation learning framework, enabling a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to autonomously open doors in real-world office environments, achieving a 55% success rate on an unseen door, which is more than double baseline performance. This framework demonstrated robust failure recovery and the ability to guide robot behaviors through explicit stage prompting.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face difficulties performing complex, long-horizon tasks requiring tightly coupled locomotion and manipulation, such as autonomous door opening.
- Standard imitation learning struggles with partial observability and 'mode collapse' when applied to multi-stage tasks, leading to poor robustness in dynamic, real-world settings.
- Achieving autonomous door opening by humanoids in unstructured environments often relies on external sensors or privileged information, limiting generalizability.

## Method

- The **StageACT** framework extends the ==Action Chunking Transformer (ACT)== by incorporating explicit task-stage inputs.
- The door-opening task is decomposed into five distinct stages (Search, Approach, Rotate, Push, Stop), which are annotated and fed as one-hot vectors to the policy's transformer decoder.
- A dataset of 135 whole-body teleoperated demonstrations was collected from a Unitree G1 humanoid in real office environments, using an Apple Vision Pro for upper-body control and an Xbox controller for locomotion.

## Results

- StageACT achieved a **55%** success rate for autonomous door opening on a previously unseen door, a substantial improvement over baseline ACT (**20%**) and ACT with history (**10%**).
- The framework enabled robust recovery from task failures and adaptation to new scenarios through explicit stage prompting, a behavior not observed in baseline policies.
- StageACT demonstrated lower upper-body and root tracking errors and a shorter average task completion time of **20.7** seconds, indicating more precise and efficient task execution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit stage-conditioning successfully resolves observational ambiguities in multi-stage tasks, preventing mode collapse by providing temporal context to the policy.
> - Decomposing long-horizon tasks into discrete stages enables the policy to intelligently recover from failures by reverting to earlier stages and retrying actions.
> - Stage prompting offers a powerful form of controllability, allowing the robot to adapt its behavior for novel task variations or to intentionally skip certain stages.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research makes a significant contribution to several interconnected areas within robotics: humanoid loco-manipulation, imitation learning for long-horizon tasks, and robust control under partial observability.

**Humanoid Robotics:** Humanoid robots are envisioned to operate alongside humans in everyday environments. A key challenge is their ability to perform tasks requiring both locomotion and dexterous manipulation—dubbed "loco-manipulation." Historically, these capabilities have often been treated separately. While impressive strides have been made in humanoid agility (e.g., jumping) and general manipulation, tightly coupled, contact-rich loco-manipulation remains a formidable challenge. The paper explicitly states that "humanoid systems typically decouple locomotion from manipulation, whereas successful door interaction requires tight coupling of the two." StageACT directly addresses this by demonstrating autonomous door opening, a task that inherently demands coordinated whole-body control, balancing, and manipulation. Previous humanoid demonstrations of door opening were primarily teleoperated, making StageACT's fully autonomous achievement a notable advancement.

**Door Opening as a Canonical Benchmark:** Opening a door has been a benchmark task in robotics since the DARPA Robotics Challenge in 2015. It is complex because it involves articulated objects, contact-rich interactions, and dynamic changes (e.g., handle tension, door hinges). While quadrupeds and wheeled manipulators have shown progress, these often rely on explicit state estimation (e.g., AR markers) or prior knowledge of door parameters. Humanoids face additional difficulties due to their complex dynamics and balance requirements while exerting forces. This paper tackles the "holy grail" of door opening for humanoids by removing external dependencies and achieving robust performance in varied real-world settings.

**Imitation Learning (IL) for Long-Horizon Tasks:** Imitation learning has shown great promise in acquiring complex behaviors from human demonstrations. However, scaling IL to long-horizon, multi-stage tasks with partial observability is difficult. Challenges include "mode collapse" (where the policy blends actions from different stages) and difficulty reasoning about unobservable states (e.g., whether a door latch is disengaged). Traditional IL policies, like standard Behavior Cloning or even advanced models like Action Chunking Transformer (ACT), often struggle because they operate on a limited context window. This work positions itself within the trend of augmenting IL policies with higher-level conditioning signals—such as language, skill sets, or sub-goals—to provide temporal context and guidance. StageACT's core idea of "stage-conditioning" offers a lightweight yet highly effective solution for this problem, contrasting with more complex hierarchical or modular approaches. It builds upon ACT, a strong baseline for fine-grained manipulation, and extends its capabilities to the more challenging domain of contact-rich loco-manipulation.

**Robustness to Partial Observability:** The paper specifically highlights that door opening is "inherently non-Markovian and partially observable." The unobservable latch state or articulation direction means that visually similar observations can correspond to different underlying task states. StageACT directly addresses this by providing an explicit "task-stage" input, which serves as a form of temporal context, enabling the policy to disambiguate observations that would otherwise lead to errors or mode collapse in unconditioned policies. This is a critical step towards making robots robust in real-world, dynamic environments where full state information is rarely available.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable **autonomous and robust door opening by humanoid robots in diverse, real-world human environments**. This ambitious goal is driven by several key motivations stemming from the inherent challenges of the task:

1. **Bridging the Locomotion-Manipulation Gap:** A primary motivation is to demonstrate tight coupling between locomotion and manipulation in humanoids. While humanoids are morphologically well-suited for tasks like door opening, past efforts often treat these two capabilities in isolation. The authors seek to develop a policy that can seamlessly integrate walking, balancing, and dexterous manipulation to complete a complex, contact-rich task.

2. **Addressing Partial Observability in Long-Horizon Tasks:** Door opening is characterized by partial observability, particularly regarding internal states like the door latch, and its long-horizon nature. Standard imitation learning struggles with such tasks, leading to "mode collapse" where the policy averages actions or executes them out of sequence. The paper aims to overcome this limitation by introducing a mechanism that provides crucial temporal context to the low-level policy.

3. **Achieving Autonomy Without External Aids:** Many existing robotic door-opening solutions rely on external sensing (e.g., AR tags, explicit door models) or are limited to teleoperated settings. A core motivation for StageACT is to achieve fully autonomous operation without such privileged information, making the solution more generalizable and applicable to unstructured environments. This pushes the frontier of truly capable and adaptable humanoid robots.

4. **Enabling Robustness and Recovery:** Real-world deployment demands robustness to unexpected variations and the ability to recover from failures. The authors are motivated to develop a system that not only successfully executes the task but can also adapt to deviations and return to a viable state, a capability often missing in direct imitation learning from successful demonstrations.

To achieve these motivations, the paper sets out two explicit contributions:
- To demonstrate, for the first time (to the best of their knowledge), an autonomous loco-manipulation policy for humanoid door opening, trained solely from human demonstrations without external sensing or privileged door information.
- To introduce StageACT, a stage-conditioned imitation learning framework for long-horizon tasks, proving its significant performance gains over standard behavior cloning, especially in resolving observational ambiguity and facilitating failure recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The StageACT framework combines whole-body teleoperation for data collection with a stage-conditioned imitation learning policy built upon the Action Chunking Transformer (ACT).

**A. Robot Platform and Teleoperation Setup:**
The robot platform used is a **Unitree G1 humanoid equipped with Dex-3 hands**. The door-opening task involves rotating a spring-loaded handle and pushing the door open, requiring simultaneous contact force management, door articulation, and locomotion.
A sophisticated **whole-body teleoperation setup** was employed for data collection, extending previous work to include locomotion.
- **Upper-body control:** An Apple Vision Pro (AVP) headset captured operator poses, which were mapped to the robot's arms via inverse kinematics (IK) using Pinocchio and to the Dex-3 hands via an optimization tool. The AVP's IMU also mapped yaw to the robot's waist joint.
- **Lower-body control:** An Xbox controller provided velocity commands to Unitree's locomotion API.
- **Decoupled Control:** This separation of control (AVP for manipulation, Xbox for locomotion) significantly reduced operator burden while maintaining effective whole-body coordination.
- **Manipulation Strategy:** Due to the bulkiness of Dex-3 fingers, the robot was teleoperated to press down on the handle with the bottom of its left fist, a more stable and effective strategy than grasping.
- **Sensing:** An egocentric RealSense camera provided RGB image observations.

**B. Dataset Collection:**
A dataset of **135 successful door-opening demonstrations** was collected over two days in two different office environments. These environments varied in lighting, background, and door dynamics (handle stiffness, hinge damping). Each trial lasted 20-30 seconds, and only successful trajectories were retained to ensure data quality.
- **Observations:** Consisted of an RGB image ($I_t \in \mathbb{R}^{480 \times 640 \times 3}$) from the head-mounted camera and the upper-body joint state ($q^{upper}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{29}$) (arms, hands, waist).
- **Actions:** Defined as target joint positions combined with locomotion velocity commands ($V^{xyz}_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$), yielding $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$.
- **Data Structure:** The dataset was structured for supervised learning, following ACT, by dividing trajectories into fixed-length action chunks of horizon H (100 steps or ~3 seconds). This process expanded the 135 demonstrations into thousands of training examples.

**C. StageACT Framework (Imitation Learning with Stage-Conditioning):**
StageACT builds directly upon the **Action Chunking Transformer (ACT)**, which frames imitation learning as a generative modeling problem using a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE).
- **ACT Core:** The CVAE encoder maps the current robot joint state and the demonstrated action sequence into a latent variable `z`. The decoder, a transformer, receives the current camera image, robot joint state, and the latent variable `z` to output future action sequences (joint positions and locomotion velocities). Training involves a reconstruction loss and a KL-divergence regularizer. ACT's chunking and temporal ensembling strategy are used for stability.
- **Stage Conditioning (Novelty):** The key innovation is augmenting the low-level ACT policy with task-stage inputs. The door-opening task is naturally decomposed into **five distinct stages**:
 1. **S1: Search:** Searching for the handle.
 2. **S2: Approach:** Approaching the handle with the left arm.
 3. **S3: Rotate:** Rotating the handle.
 4. **S4: Push:** Pushing against the door with the right arm.
 5. **S5: Stop:** Stopping locomotion and lowering arms to rest.
- **Stage Annotation:** These stages were defined and annotated post-processing by human annotators. Crucially, annotations relied on both visual inspection and proprioceptive cues (joint positions and torques). Proprioceptive cues were vital for disambiguating visually similar observations (e.g., S2 and S4) and detecting contact transitions.
- **Policy Input:** The stage labels are encoded as one-hot vectors and concatenated with the robot's observations (image, joint state, latent `z`) as input to the transformer decoder. This provides explicit temporal context to the policy.
- **Training and Inference:** The model was trained on an RTX5090 GPU for about 4 hours, achieving an inference rate of 30 Hz.

This methodology combines a robust data collection pipeline with a state-of-the-art imitation learning architecture, strategically enhanced with a simple yet powerful conditioning mechanism to address the inherent complexities of long-horizon, partially observable loco-manipulation tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of StageACT focused on two core questions: (Q1) how much does stage-conditioning improve performance over baselines, and (Q2) can it enable novel behaviors through stage prompting? The results demonstrate clear advantages for StageACT.

**A. Door Opening Performance:**
The policies were tested on a **previously unseen door**, ensuring robustness beyond memorization. The test environment differed in appearance and door dynamics from the training data, and initial robot distance and orientation were randomized. Three policies were compared:
- **ACT:** The standard Action Chunking Transformer baseline.
- **ACT with history:** ACT augmented with the last 5 observations to mitigate partial observability.
- **StageACT (ours):** The proposed stage-conditioned policy.

The results, summarized in Table I, were compelling:
- **Success Rate:** StageACT achieved a **55% success rate**, more than doubling the best baseline (ACT: 20%, ACT with history: 10%). This clearly demonstrates the significant impact of stage-conditioning on task completion.
- **Task Completion Time:** StageACT also resulted in the **shortest average completion time of 20.7 seconds**, compared to 27.5s for ACT and 22.2s for ACT with history. This indicates more efficient and directed task execution, reducing robot "wandering."
- **Tracking Error:** StageACT showed the **lowest upper-body and root tracking errors** (0.34 and 0.04 respectively), suggesting it more faithfully replicated expert demonstrations.

A per-stage breakdown (Table II) provided critical insights into StageACT's superiority:
- The **"Approach" stage (S2)** was the most challenging for baselines, where visual and proprioceptive cues were ambiguous (resembling both S2 and S4). This often led to mode collapse, with baselines blending trajectories or skipping stages entirely (e.g., jumping from search to push). ACT only succeeded in 7 out of 20 attempts for S2, and ACT with history in 5 out of 8.
- StageACT, however, achieved **17 out of 19 successes** in the "Approach" stage, demonstrating its ability to resolve visual ambiguities using temporal context.
- The main failure mode for StageACT was the **"Rotate" stage (S3)**, which is inherently complex due to the precise interaction required to disengage the spring-loaded latch. Despite this, StageACT performed significantly better than baselines in this critical stage (12/17 for StageACT vs. 6/7 for ACT and 1/5 for ACT with history).

**B. Behavior Guidance and Recovery:**
A crucial finding was StageACT's ability to enable **recovery behaviors**, which were absent in baseline policies because failed demonstrations were explicitly removed from the training data.
- When a failure occurred (e.g., incomplete handle rotation), StageACT, given an explicit stage prompt to return to S1 (Search) or S2 (Approach), could **revert to an earlier stage and retry the problematic action sequence**. This demonstrates a significant increase in policy robustness and adaptability in the face of unexpected events. An example in Fig. 7 illustrates how StageACT suppresses premature right-arm motion, allowing the policy to retry and complete the handle rotation after an initial failure.
- The research also explored **intentional non-sequential stage prompting** (e.g., guiding the policy through S1→S4→S5, skipping S2 and S3). Despite the visual ambiguity between S2 and S4, the policy executed actions consistent with the *prompted stage* rather than immediate observations. This highlights a powerful form of controllability, allowing the robot to adapt to scenarios not present in training (e.g., a door with a disabled latch that only needs pushing).
- An **ablation study on the stage input** (Table III) confirmed its importance: using constant-zero or random stage labels resulted in 0% success, validating that the policy actively leverages the stage-conditioning as a meaningful control signal.

These findings collectively underscore StageACT's effectiveness in enhancing the robustness, success rate, efficiency, and controllability of humanoid robots for complex loco-manipulation tasks under real-world conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The StageACT framework represents a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, with substantial potential impact across several domains:

1. **Enabling Autonomous Humanoid Operation in Human Environments:** The most immediate impact is the successful demonstration of autonomous door opening by a humanoid robot in an unstructured, real-world office environment, without reliance on external sensors or prior door information. This moves beyond teleoperation, simulation, or highly constrained settings, bringing humanoids closer to truly operating in everyday human spaces and performing essential tasks. Doors are ubiquitous, and mastering their interaction unlocks vast new possibilities for robot deployment in homes, offices, and industrial facilities.

2. **Addressing Core Challenges in Imitation Learning:** Stage-conditioning provides a lightweight yet powerful solution to critical problems in imitation learning, namely partial observability and mode collapse in long-horizon, multi-stage tasks. By providing explicit temporal context, StageACT allows policies to disambiguate visually similar states and execute actions in the correct sequence. This approach offers a generalizable strategy that can be applied to many other complex robotic tasks that involve sequential contact interactions and dynamic transitions.

3. **Enhancing Robot Robustness and Practical Utility:** The ability for StageACT to enable **recovery behaviors** is crucial for real-world deployment. Robots inevitably encounter unforeseen circumstances or execute imperfect actions. A policy that can intelligently retry or adjust its approach without requiring human intervention or full re-training dramatically increases its practical utility and reliability. Furthermore, the demonstrated **controllability through stage prompting** allows for greater flexibility and adaptation, enabling operators to guide the robot's behavior for novel scenarios (e.g., a door that only needs pushing) not explicitly demonstrated during training. This signifies a step towards more adaptable and less brittle robotic systems.

4. **Foundation for Future Loco-Manipulation Capabilities:** The success with door opening lays a strong foundation for tackling a broader range of contact-rich loco-manipulation tasks for humanoids. The authors themselves highlight natural extensions, such as adapting to door pulling, generalizing across diverse door types (different hinges, articulation styles), and other articulated objects. The principles of stage-conditioning, especially combined with advanced perception, can be extended to tasks like operating appliances, opening drawers, or interacting with tools.

5. **Implications for Robot Foundation Models and Scalable Learning:** The paper explicitly connects stage-conditioning to the broader trend of robot foundation models. By using stages to decompose and label trajectories, it proposes a method for organizing and sharing "sub-trajectories and task segments across task categories." This could be instrumental in building large-scale, general-purpose loco-manipulation policies, particularly when coupled with future work on automated stage annotation using Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Such automated labeling would unlock the ability to scale up data collection and learning for an even wider array of complex behaviors.

In conclusion, StageACT offers a compelling demonstration of how a simple, biologically inspired concept—task decomposition into stages—can profoundly enhance the capabilities of modern imitation learning for humanoid robots. Its success in autonomous door opening marks a significant step towards humanoids that are not only agile but also intelligently capable of navigating and interacting with the complex and dynamic human world.
