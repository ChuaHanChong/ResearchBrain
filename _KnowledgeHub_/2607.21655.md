# Progress Reward Modeling for Robotic Learning: A Comprehensive Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey provides a comprehensive review of progress reward modeling for robotic learning, introducing a unified framework to categorize diverse methods based on their interfaces, construction mechanisms, and data/evaluation protocols. It synthesizes the fragmented literature, identifies key challenges, and outlines future research directions.

## Problem

- Robotic learning for complex, long-horizon tasks often suffers from sparse, terminal success signals, leading to inefficient credit assignment and slow policy learning.
- Accurately estimating intermediate task progress is challenging because it is often latent, history-dependent, and requires inferring subtasks and distance to goal.
- The existing research on progress reward modeling is fragmented, with varied approaches, observations, and evaluation protocols hindering direct comparison and unified understanding.

## Method

- The survey introduces a ==three-step analytical framework== that organizes progress models by their input/output interfaces, construction mechanisms, and data/benchmarking protocols.
- It categorizes input types (task-state representation, task-goal specification) and output forms (scalar scores, progress deltas, rankings, reward functions) used by progress models.
- The work systematizes methods for constructing progress rewards, including leveraging ==frozen foundation models==, learning from ==temporal/relative supervision==, ==instruction tuning==, and ==programmatic generation==.

## Results

- The survey successfully developed and applied a unified three-part framework for analyzing a diverse and rapidly growing body of literature on progress reward modeling.
- It systematically categorized various progress representation types, goal specifications, and output forms, providing a clearer map of the field's complexity.
- The research identified and organized different construction mechanisms for progress rewards, highlighting their underlying assumptions and methodologies.
- The work distilled current practical limitations of progress models, including coarse-grained sensitivity, lack of adaptive temporal modeling, high inference latency, and insufficient long-horizon memory.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The field employs a wide spectrum of approaches for representing task states, specifying goals, and outputting progress signals, reflecting the multifaceted nature of 'progress' researchers aim to capture.
> - Different construction mechanisms for progress rewards rely on distinct underlying assumptions about the source and nature of the progress signal.
> - Significant heterogeneity exists in data generation strategies (human-driven to automated) and evaluation benchmarks, implying that reported 'success' often pertains to specific properties rather than a holistic measure.
> - Several fundamental trade-offs are identified, such as balancing input context richness with online usability and human semantic accuracy with automated scalability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This survey paper is situated within the rapidly expanding field of robotic learning, specifically addressing the challenge of providing effective feedback for robots operating in complex, dynamic, and high-dimensional environments. Traditional robotic learning systems often rely on sparse, terminal success signals, which indicate only whether a task has been completed. This approach is increasingly insufficient for long-horizon tasks, where successful outcomes are rare, and numerous intermediate decisions are made before any feedback is received.

The research landscape has seen a significant increase in studies exploring "progress rewards," which aim to provide denser, intermediate feedback during task execution. These rewards can inform the robot whether its current behavior is advancing the task, causing stagnation, or undoing prior progress. The paper highlights that despite this growth, the literature on progress reward modeling has remained fragmented. Various methods employ different observations, goal specifications, output signals, supervision sources, and evaluation protocols, impeding direct comparison and a unified understanding of their contributions.

This survey positions itself as a critical synthesis effort within this fragmented landscape. By providing a unified framework, it seeks to bring structure and clarity to the diverse approaches in progress reward modeling. This systematization is intended to facilitate comparison among methods, identify underlying assumptions, and clarify the properties validated by different evaluation methodologies, thereby advancing the field towards more coherent development and rigorous assessment. The paper also documents the rapid evolution of this subfield, noting a sharp increase in relevant publications from 2017 to 2026 (as depicted in Figure 1 of the original text), demonstrating its growing importance in robotic learning research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey stems from the inherent difficulties of training robots for complex, sequential tasks in dynamic physical environments. Robotic tasks often involve large behavior spaces where the impact of actions can vary significantly based on the current state and goal. Relying solely on a terminal success signal presents several limitations:
- **Sparse Feedback:** A binary success/failure signal at the end of a long task provides very little information for credit assignment, making it challenging for a robot to learn which intermediate actions contribute to progress.
- **Inefficient Learning:** Without intermediate feedback, policies struggle to explore the vast action space effectively, particularly when successful outcomes are rare.
- **Lack of Granularity:** A terminal signal cannot inform the robot if an action is advancing the task, causing stagnation, or actively undoing previous work. This is crucial for identifying useful behaviors and diagnosing failures early.

Progress rewards are proposed as a solution to these limitations by offering richer, dense feedback throughout task execution. Beyond merely accelerating policy learning, reliable progress signals can support various functionalities, including:
- Online monitoring of robot behavior.
- Reranking of trajectories.
- Filtering of demonstrative data.
- Informing planning and action selection.
- Facilitating failure recovery mechanisms.

However, the authors identify that estimating progress is significantly more complex than detecting final success. Progress estimation requires the model to infer completed subtasks, understand current activities, and gauge the remaining distance to the goal, often conditioned on the specific task. This process is inherently latent and history-dependent, meaning progress is not always directly observable from a single visual frame or state. The lack of a unified framework within the current literature exacerbates these challenges, making it difficult to understand and compare the diverse methods.

Given this context, the key objectives of the survey are explicitly stated:
1. **Introduce an interface-based view:** Organize progress models by their input (current task-state representation, task-goal specification) and output (form of progress-related signal). This aims to provide a common structure for comparison across different architectures.
2. **Provide a taxonomy of construction mechanisms:** Categorize how progress rewards are built, clarifying the underlying assumptions and methodologies (e.g., using foundation models, temporal supervision, instruction tuning, or programmatic approaches).
3. **Connect data-construction pipelines with evaluation protocols:** Explain how progress supervision is obtained (e.g., human annotation, automated methods) and what different benchmarks actually measure (e.g., fidelity, robustness, downstream utility), emphasizing the distinction between them.
4. **Summarize main limitations and discuss future research directions:** Identify current shortcomings in the field to guide future innovation.

By achieving these objectives, the survey aims to offer a "unified view" that connects the conceptual definition of a progress model with its construction and validation, thereby clarifying the field and fostering more effective future research in robotic learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a comprehensive survey, the methodology employed in this paper involves a structured review, categorization, and synthesis of the existing literature on progress reward modeling for robotic learning. The authors adopt a three-step analytical framework to organize and analyze the diverse methods.

### 4. Step 1: Interface of Progress Models (Section 2)

This step examines progress models from an external perspective, treating them as task-conditioned black boxes. The focus is on their input and output interfaces, categorizing how models receive information and how they produce progress signals.
- **Input Interface: Current Task State Representation:**
 - **Single Observation:** Models estimate progress from a single current state/frame, assuming sufficient information (e.g., perceptual reward models, CLIP/VLM-based similarity). This offers low latency but can suffer from ambiguity.
 - **Temporal Context:** Models use a sequence of observations (recent window or full trajectory) to infer historical context. This provides stronger temporal evidence but can increase latency for online use.
 - **Relational Comparison:** Progress is judged by comparing two or more states (e.g., before-after an action, initial-current, current-goal, two rollouts). This provides comparative insight but may accumulate errors over long horizons.
 - **State-access Interface:** Utilizes explicit information like environment code, simulator APIs, or proprioceptive features. Highly effective in structured environments but limited in real-world settings without clean APIs.
- **Input Interface: Task Goal Specification:**
 - **Language-conditioned Goals:** Goals are specified through natural language descriptions. This is common due to ease of encoding and compatibility with large language models, but language can underspecify physical details.
 - **Vision-conditioned Goals:** Goals are provided via images, states, or demonstration trajectories. This offers direct physical grounding but requires curated data and can be sensitive to visual variations.
 - **Structured/Programmatic Goals:** Goals are defined through predicates, object states, constraints, or generated objective functions, primarily in simulated or API-rich environments. This allows for precise and compositional rewards but relies on explicit state variable access.
- **Output Interface: Model Prediction Form:**
 - **State-wise Scalar Score:** A single numerical value assigned to a state/clip, interpreted as progress, success likelihood, value, or goal similarity. Direct and reusable but meaning depends on calibration.
 - **Progress Delta:** Predicts the change in progress between two observations, indicating local improvement or regression. Aligned with RL but requires accumulation for total progress.
 - **Ranking:** Orders states, clips, or trajectories by advancement or quality. Easier to supervise than absolute scores but defines progress relatively.
 - **Reward Function:** Outputs an executable program, code, or structured logic that computes rewards. Flexible and interpretable but relies on task decomposition and reliable state access.

### 5. Step 2: Methods of Progress Reward Construction (Section 3)

This step "opens the black box" to categorize the mechanisms by which progress signals are generated and converted into usable rewards.
- **Frozen Foundation Models as Semantic Reward Scorers:** Directly extracts reward-like signals from pretrained models (e.g., CLIP for image-text similarity, VLMs for token probabilities as hidden rewards, VLM for temporal ordering). Offers zero-shot usability but provides semantic priors rather than calibrated progress.
- **Learning Rewards from Temporal and Relative Supervision:** Trains reward models from various forms of supervision:
 - **Temporal Order:** Assumes later states in successful demonstrations are closer to completion (e.g., goal-proximity, visual representation learning).
 - **Preference/Ranking:** Compares states or trajectories to determine which is better, often easier to collect (e.g., training scalar reward from VLM-generated preferences, learning from passive video rankings).
- **Instruction-Tuned Progress Prediction:** Formulates progress estimation as an explicit instruction-following task for foundation models, fine-tuning them with task-specific prompts and target responses (e.g., predicting absolute progress, success, relative improvement with structured rubrics, or explicit progress reasoning).
- **Programmatic Reward Construction:** Generates executable reward programs (code, predicates, feature functions) from task descriptions. Effective for decomposable tasks with accessible state variables but shifts challenge to task decomposition and feature design.

### 6. Step 3: Data and Benchmarks (Section 4)

This step analyzes how progress supervision is produced and how models are evaluated, connecting data sources to the properties measured by benchmarks.
- **Data Construction (by degree of human involvement):**
 - **Human-driven Construction:** Direct human input (teleoperation, video recording, scalar scoring, keyframe annotation, trajectory-level reward sketching, preference comparison). Provides strong semantic grounding but is costly and subjective.
 - **Human-in-the-loop Construction:** Sparse human input guides or verifies automated processing (e.g., interpolating dense labels from sparse anchors, human correction of model-proposed labels, active learning for uncertain states, LLM/script generation with human design of prompts/APIs). Balances semantic control with scalability.
 - **Fully Automated Construction:** Derives supervision without instance-level human annotation: temporal structure (later frames are closer to completion), structured environments (simulator states, ground-truth rewards), foundation models as annotators (LLM/VLM for subgoals, scores, preferences), and augmentation (truncating, reversing, perturbing trajectories). Highly scalable but assumes correlation with time/available states and can inherit model biases.
- **Benchmarking Progress Fidelity:** Evaluates whether model outputs accurately represent progress:
 - **Scalar Calibration:** Agreement with explicit progress references (e.g., completion percentages, rubric scores).
 - **Temporal Consistency:** Recovers the progression of a trajectory (e.g., ordering accuracy, monotonicity).
 - **Relative Progress:** Correctly orders states/trajectories by advancement.
 - **Task Grounding:** Progress estimates are conditioned on the specified goal, not generic changes.
 - **Answerability and Uncertainty:** Recognizes when progress cannot be reliably inferred due to ambiguity.
- **Benchmarking Robustness and Generalization:** Assesses transferability under changes:
 - **Task Generalization:** Performance on unseen tasks, objects, or families.
 - **Viewpoint and Scene Robustness:** Stability under camera changes, occlusion, distractors.
 - **Cross-embodiment Generalization:** Transferability across different robot platforms or to human videos.
 - **Non-monotonic and Imperfect Execution:** Evaluates performance on failures, regressions, retries.
- **Benchmarking Downstream Utility:** Measures practical impact on robot learning and decision-making:
 - **Online Policy Learning:** Improved success rate, sample efficiency, learning stability in RL.
 - **Offline Learning and Reward Relabeling:** Assigning useful signals to fixed datasets for offline RL.
 - **Retrieval, Filtering, and Reranking:** Identifying useful data, detecting failures, selecting best rollouts.
 - **Planning and Action Selection:** Providing smooth, informative, and efficient reward landscapes for planning.

By meticulously breaking down the field into these interconnected perspectives, the authors aim to provide a structured overview that clarifies the "what, how, and why" of progress reward modeling, along with its current status and future potential.

### 7. Main Findings and Results

The primary "findings" of this survey are the comprehensive categorization and structured analysis of the progress reward modeling field itself, rather than novel experimental results. The paper effectively demonstrates the landscape's complexity and fragmentation while identifying commonalities and critical distinctions. Key findings include:

- **Unified Framework for Analysis:** The successful development and application of a three-part framework (Interface, Methods, Data/Benchmarks) that systematically organizes a diverse and rapidly growing body of literature. This framework provides a common language and structure for understanding different progress models.
- **Diverse Approaches to Progress Representation:** The survey reveals a wide spectrum of approaches for representing current task states (from single observations to full trajectories and relational comparisons), specifying goals (language, vision, programmatic), and outputting progress signals (scalar scores, deltas, rankings, executable functions). This diversity highlights the multiple facets of "progress" that researchers attempt to capture.
- **Varied Construction Mechanisms:** The analysis of construction methods demonstrates the field's evolution from relying on frozen foundation models for semantic priors to learning from various forms of temporal or relative supervision, employing instruction-tuned prediction, and generating programmatic rewards. Each approach makes distinct assumptions about the source and nature of the progress signal.
- **Heterogeneity in Data and Evaluation:** A significant finding is the wide range of data generation strategies, from heavily human-driven to fully automated methods, each with trade-offs between semantic grounding and scalability. Correspondingly, evaluation benchmarks measure different aspects (fidelity, robustness, utility), indicating that reported "success" often pertains to specific properties rather than a holistic measure of progress. This highlights the difficulty in comparing methods across different evaluation protocols.
- **Critical Trade-offs Identified:** The survey implicitly and explicitly identifies several fundamental trade-offs:
 - Between the richness of input context (e.g., single frame vs. full trajectory) and online usability/latency.
 - Between the abstractness/flexibility of goal specification (e.g., language) and the precision/physical grounding (e.g., vision, programmatic).
 - Between the semantic accuracy and nuance provided by human supervision versus the scalability and consistency of automated data generation.
 - Between the directness of a progress fidelity metric and the ultimate utility for downstream robotic tasks.
- **Systematic Identification of Limitations:** The survey distills the current practical limitations of existing progress models into four key areas:
 - **Coarse-grained sensitivity:** Models often miss subtle but important physical changes.
 - **Lack of adaptive temporal modeling:** Progress rates are uneven in real tasks, challenging models that assume smooth, time-correlated progress.
 - **High inference latency:** Large models are often too slow for real-time online deployment.
 - **Insufficient long-horizon memory:** Models struggle with history-dependent progress and distinguishing visually similar states at different task stages.

### 8. Significance and Potential Impact

This comprehensive survey holds significant implications for the field of robotic learning, particularly in the domain of reward modeling and task execution.

- **Clarifying a Fragmented Field:** By providing a unified view and structured framework, the survey significantly reduces the fragmentation in progress reward modeling. This clarity enables researchers to better understand, categorize, and compare existing methods, preventing redundant efforts and fostering more informed design choices. It offers a much-needed intellectual map for navigating a rapidly evolving subfield.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The detailed analysis of limitations and the explicit discussion of future directions are crucial. By highlighting specific technical challenges—such as the need for fine-grained sensitivity, adaptive temporal modeling, efficient online inference, and robust long-horizon memory—the survey directs future research efforts toward critical areas that currently impede the widespread adoption of progress rewards in real-world robotics.
- **Promoting Standardization:** The proposed categorization of interfaces, construction methods, and evaluation protocols can serve as a foundation for developing more standardized terminology, problem definitions, and benchmarking practices. This could lead to more rigorous and directly comparable research outcomes, ultimately accelerating progress by allowing researchers to build upon each other's work more effectively.
- **Enhancing Robot Learning Performance:** Reliable progress rewards have the potential to significantly improve the sample efficiency, robustness, and generalizability of robotic learning algorithms. By providing dense, informative feedback, these models can facilitate better credit assignment, enable learning in long-horizon tasks, and allow robots to diagnose and recover from failures proactively.
- **Broadening Applications of Progress Signals:** Beyond policy learning, the survey underscores the wider utility of progress signals in areas like online monitoring, data curation, trajectory reranking, and proactive planning. This expanded view positions progress models not just as reward functions but as general evaluators of ongoing robot behavior, suggesting a broader impact on various aspects of autonomous systems.
- **Informing Educational and Review Processes:** The survey can serve as a valuable resource for newcomers to the field, providing a structured entry point to a complex topic. For reviewers and researchers, it offers a robust framework for critically assessing new contributions against the backdrop of existing work and identified challenges.

In summary, this survey paper significantly advances the understanding of progress reward modeling by synthesizing a disparate body of work into a coherent framework. Its detailed analysis of methods, data, and evaluations, coupled with a clear outline of limitations and future directions, positions it as an influential guide for the continued development and practical implementation of progress-aware robotic learning systems.
