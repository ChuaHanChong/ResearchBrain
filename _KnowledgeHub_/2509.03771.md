# Co-Evolving Complexity: An Adversarial Framework for Automatic MARL Curricula

> [!summary] Summary
> An adversarial framework automatically generates training curricula for multi-agent reinforcement learning by co-evolving a cooperative agent team with a generative adversary. This approach enabled the emergence of complex, adaptive strategies, leading to a four-fold increase in agent survival time and consistent use of advanced tactics by both sides in simulated environments.

## Problem

- Intelligent agents trained with reinforcement learning are limited by the finite, hand-crafted, and potentially biased nature of current training environments.
- Manually designing diverse and progressively challenging curricula for multi-agent systems is resource-intensive and does not scale effectively.
- Traditional procedural content generation methods often lack the ability to create novel, adaptive, and increasingly complex training scenarios organically.

## Method

- A two-team, ==nearly zero-sum==, ==partially observable Markov game== framework is introduced, pitting a cooperative ==Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)== team against a generative adversarial agent.
- The generative Attacker agent procedurally creates diverse hostile units with customizable parameters, effectively designing a ==dynamic curriculum== for the Defender team.
- Both the Defender team and the Attacker agent are simultaneously trained using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)==, with competitive reward signals driving a ==co-evolutionary arms race==.

## Results

- Trained agents demonstrated a **four-fold** increase in average episode survival length, lasting **83 steps** compared to **19 steps** for random baseline agents.
- The generative Attacker developed and consistently applied sophisticated strategies, such as 'Tandem' attacks (over **98%** of episodes) and 'Flanking' maneuvers (**94%** of episodes).
- The cooperative Defender team implicitly coordinated to execute adaptive strategies like 'Cooperative Spreading' (**92.6%** of episodes) and 'Cooperative Focusing' (**81.4%** of episodes) in response to dynamic threats.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The co-evolutionary adversarial dynamic is essential for driving the emergence of complex and intelligent strategies, as evidenced by ablation studies showing limited strategic development without a learning adversary.
> - The generative Attacker acts as an adaptive curriculum designer, consistently creating challenges at the edge of the Defenders' capabilities by being rewarded for exploiting their weaknesses.
> - A sustained 'arms race' dynamic, characterized by oscillating performance, leads to robust and adaptive policies by preventing agents from overfitting to static challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), Procedural Content Generation (PCG), and adversarial learning for automatic curriculum generation.

The advancement of general-purpose intelligent agents has demonstrated a reliance on the environments in which they are trained. While scaling model and dataset sizes has led to increased capabilities, the complexity, diversity, and interactivity of training environments often remain a limiting factor. Existing hand-crafted environments are finite, can introduce implicit biases, and limit the development of truly generalizable and robust agent skills. This research proposes an approach to address this bottleneck by automating the generation of complex and diverse training scenarios.

In the field of MARL, a central challenge is the non-stationarity encountered by individual agents as other agents adapt their policies. This work leverages this non-stationarity as a primary driver for learning, establishing a competitive dynamic between a generative adversary and a cooperative agent team. The approach utilizes the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which have been demonstrated as effective in cooperative MARL settings.

Regarding Procedural Content Generation (PCG), traditional methods are often constructive or search-based, while more recent PCG via Machine Learning (PCGML) relies on imitation from existing content, thereby limiting creative potential. PCG via Reinforcement Learning (PCGRL) offers an inventive approach where an agent learns to generate content by maximizing a reward function. This paper builds upon PCGRL by replacing a static, hand-crafted reward with a dynamic reward signal derived from the performance of another learning agent.

The core mechanism of the presented system draws from adversarial learning, a concept deeply rooted in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and exemplified by self-play algorithms like AlphaGo and AlphaZero. These systems demonstrate that adversarial interactions can generate complexity and emergent behaviors without human data. While previous work, such as Generator-Solver frameworks, explored adversarial PCG for single-solver agents, this research extends the paradigm to a multi-agent cooperative team, shifting the focus from generating static solvable puzzles to orchestrating a dynamic, self-scaling curriculum. It aligns with the principle of co-evolution, similar to the POET algorithm, where environments and agent policies develop in tandem to generate increasingly novel and complex challenges.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for automatically generating a continuous and adaptive curriculum of challenges for multi-agent systems, thereby overcoming the limitations of manually designed training environments.

The motivation stems from several key observations:
- **Environmental Bottleneck:** The development of sophisticated intelligent agents, particularly those trained with reinforcement learning, is intrinsically linked to the richness and diversity of their training environments. Current methods for environment design, primarily manual crafting, are resource-intensive, prone to human biases, and inherently finite, which can lead to agents overfitting to specific scenarios rather than developing generalizable and robust skills.
- **Scaling Environmental Complexity:** While progress in machine learning has often followed scaling laws (increasing model size, dataset volume, computational power), a similar scaling in environmental structure, fidelity, and diversity is identified as critical for advancing agent intelligence. The goal is to move beyond static datasets and foster environments that are perpetually novel and interactively rich.
- **Automated Curriculum Generation:** The manual creation of increasingly difficult and diverse challenges is an intractable task. This work seeks to automate this process by framing environment generation as an adversarial game. The intention is to create a "self-scaling" system where complexity arises organically from the interaction between learning agents.
- **Emergence of Complex Behaviors:** A specific objective is to demonstrate that this adversarial co-evolutionary dynamic can lead to the rapid emergence of complex, intelligent behaviors and coordinated strategies in both the environment generator and the agents being trained. This includes tactics that are not explicitly programmed but arise from the competitive pressure.
- **Foundation for Generalization and Robustness:** By continually challenging agents with tailored, novel problems, the framework aims to promote the development of more generalizable and robust agent policies that can adapt to unseen situations.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for an efficient, scalable, and unbiased method for generating diverse and appropriately challenging training data to drive the development of more advanced, adaptive, and generally intelligent agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes an adversarial framework for automatic MARL curricula, formalized as a two-team, nearly zero-sum, partially observable Markov game (POMG).

#### 3.1. System Design

- **Game Structure:** The game involves two teams: Team D, consisting of four cooperative Defender agents, and Team A, a single adversarial Attacker agent.
- **Environment:** The game unfolds on a discrete 2D grid measuring 10 lanes (x-axis) by 30 tiles of depth (y-axis). Defenders are restricted to the initial four rows (y ∈), while the Attacker operates from the opposite end (y = 29). Time progresses in discrete timesteps.
- **Win Conditions:** The Defenders aim to survive for as long as possible. The Attacker wins if any generated unit reaches the Defenders’ baseline (y < 0) or if any Defender's health drops to zero, and its objective is to achieve this as quickly as possible.

#### 3.2. Agents and Actions

- **The Defender Team:**
 - **Roles:** Comprises four agents, each assigned a unique role (Mage, Healer, Tank, Sharpshooter) with distinct statistics, damage types, and a powerful, role-specific special ability requiring energy expenditure (e.g., area-of-effect damage, party heal, defense debuff, lane clear).
 - **State:** Each Defender possesses a persistent state including its position (x, z), current health (max 100), and current energy (max 1000), which regenerates at 1 unit/tick.
 - **Action Space:** At each timestep, each Defender selects one action from a discrete set: Move Left/Right (5 energy), Shoot (10 energy), Heal (50 energy), use Special Ability (200 energy), or Do Nothing (0 energy).
 - **Observation:** Defenders receive partial observations, including their own status, the status of other defenders, and the attributes of up to 16 nearby enemy units. They do not observe the Attacker's internal state or energy reserves.
 - **Architecture:** A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers of 128 neurons each (ReLU activation), taking a 126-dimensional input observation vector and outputting a 6-node layer for discrete actions via a softmax function.
- **The Attacker (Generative) Agent:**
 - **Role:** The Attacker's primary function is to procedurally generate hostile units, thereby creating challenges for the Defenders.
 - **State:** The Attacker's state is defined by its current and maximum energy, which slowly increases over time.
 - **Action Space:** At each timestep, the Attacker chooses to either generate a unit with a specific set of parameters or to do nothing. The unit generation involves selecting parameters for:
 - **Placement:** Lane (x ∈).
 - **Core Stats:** Health (1-15), Damage (1-5), Speed (1-5), Range (1-25).
 - **Special Attributes:** Regeneration (0-3 health/tick), Leech (0-5 health on attack).
 - **Defenses:** Physical Defense (0-5), Magic Defense (0-5).
 - **Offenses:** Physical and Magic Penetration (0-5), Type (Physical/Magic).
 This parameter space creates a combinatorially vast range of possible enemies.
 - **Energy Cost:** The cost of generating a unit is a superlinear, multiplicative function of its selected parameters, incentivizing strategic trade-offs between unit quantity and quality.
 - **Observation:** The Attacker observes the full game state, including its own energy, the complete status of all defenders, and up to 16 active enemy units.
 - **Architecture:** An MLP with two hidden layers of 128 neurons (ReLU activation), taking a 254-dimensional input. Its action output is complex, modeled with 13 separate heads, one for each unit parameter, with a softmax applied to each head independently to learn a joint distribution over unit attributes.
- **Generated Unit Behavior:** Units spawned by the Attacker are not dynamically controlled. They follow a simple hard-coded behavior: moving forward (decreasing y-coordinate) each tick. If a Defender is in the same lane and within attack range, the unit ceases movement and attacks. This design ensures that challenge complexity arises from the Attacker's generative choices rather than from complex unit AI.

#### 3.3. Game Dynamics and Objectives

- **Reward Structure:** Reward functions are designed to create a competitive dynamic:
 - Defenders receive a large negative reward upon losing (-1.0), a small positive reward for each tick survived (+0.001), and a shaping reward for destroying an enemy unit (+0.05).
 - The Attacker's reward structure is symmetrical: a large positive reward for winning (+1.0), a small penalty per tick (-0.001), and a penalty for attempting to spawn a unit with insufficient energy (-0.03). This structure reinforces a nearly zero-sum game dynamic.

#### 3.4. Training Procedure

- **Algorithm:** Both the Defender team and the Attacker are trained simultaneously using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an on-policy, actor-critic algorithm known for its stability in complex multi-agent settings.
- **Shared Policy:** The four Defender agents are trained using a shared policy to encourage the emergence of cooperative strategies.
- **Implementation:** The system was implemented using the Unity game engine and the ML-Agents Toolkit.
- **Experimentation:** 100 independent training runs were conducted, each lasting 500 episodes (where an episode concludes with a Defender loss). Training was performed on consumer-grade hardware (Intel Core i5-1035G7 CPU).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated that the adversarial co-evolutionary process led to the emergence of complex, intelligent, and adaptive strategies in both the generative Attacker and the cooperative Defender team. This was assessed qualitatively through observed behaviors and quantitatively via specific metrics.

#### 4.1. Baseline Comparison

Against a random baseline, where both Attacker and Defender agents selected actions uniformly at random, episodes were short, and Defenders were quickly overwhelmed. No discernible intelligent behavior was observed from either side.

#### 4.2. Emergent Behaviors

After 500 training episodes, distinct intelligent strategies emerged:
- **Emergent Attacker Strategies:** The Attacker developed temporal and spatial reasoning in its unit generation:
 - **Rusher:** Generating units with high speed to bypass Defender lines quickly.
 - **Tandem:** A notable tactic involving the generation of a high-health unit immediately followed by a high-damage unit in the same lane, where the high-health unit acts as a shield.
 - **Flanking:** Exploiting Defender mobility limitations by generating simultaneous threats on opposing sides of the board.
- **Emergent Defender Strategies:** Despite having a shared policy and no explicit communication, the Defender team exhibited implicitly coordinated behaviors:
 - **Cooperative Spreading:** When faced with multiple, spatially distributed threats (e.g., from Flanking), Defenders learned to spread out to cover relevant lanes effectively.
 - **Cooperative Focusing:** Conversely, against high-priority, single-lane threats (e.g., from a Tandem attack), multiple Defenders would converge on the same lane to concentrate firepower.

#### 4.3. Quantitative Analysis

Quantitative metrics were collected from 100 independent runs, focusing on specific "signature behaviors" defined for each strategy (Table 7 in the appendix).
- **Average Episode Length:** Trained agents survived an average of 83 steps per episode, a four-fold increase compared to the random baseline's 19 steps. This indicates a significantly improved ability to sustain against the adversary.
- **Attacker Strategies:** The trained Attacker consistently employed its learned strategies. The Tandem strategy was utilized in over 98% of episodes with an average of 8.01 uses per episode, while Flanking was seen in 94% of episodes with 4.85 uses per episode. In contrast, random agents exhibited these behaviors in less than 11% of episodes, with minimal uses per episode, suggesting chance occurrences.
- **Defender Strategies:** The Defender team also demonstrated consistent strategic execution. Cooperative Spreading was observed in 92.6% of episodes (3.61 uses/episode), and Cooperative Focusing in 81.4% of episodes (2.97 uses/episode). Baseline random agents rarely triggered these patterns, with usage rates below 1%.
These statistics provide quantitative validation that the adversarial co-evolution not only improved performance but also led to the adoption of specific, recognizable, and effective multi-agent tactics.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study: The Necessity of Co-Evolution

To determine the critical role of the adversarial dynamic, two ablation experiments were conducted where one agent was trained against a non-learning, random opponent.
- **Trained Defender vs. Random Attacker:** Defenders trained against a random Attacker survived significantly longer (average 216 steps) due to the simplicity of the challenge. However, they rarely employed cooperative strategies (Spreading at 13.2% usage, Focusing at 9.30%), showing minimal improvement over the random baseline in strategic depth. The learning plateaued rapidly.
- **Trained Attacker vs. Random Defender:** When the Attacker trained against random Defenders, episode length plummeted to 14 steps. The Attacker developed little strategic depth; Flanking and Tandem strategies appeared at rates of only 13.7% and 21.2%, respectively. Without a competent opponent, the incentive for developing complex strategies diminished.
These ablation results underscore that the mutual, reciprocal adaptation inherent in co-evolution is the primary driver for the emergence of rich, intelligent behaviors.

#### 4.5. Training Dynamics

The training dynamics, depicted by the average Defender survival time over episodes, showed a general upward trend interspersed with significant oscillations. These dips in survival time are interpreted as moments when the Attacker discovered a new, effective strategy that temporarily overcame the Defenders, prompting the Defenders to adapt and leading to a subsequent rise in survival time. This oscillating pattern is characteristic of a co-evolutionary arms race, indicating an ongoing adaptive process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates that adversarial co-evolution is a viable and powerful mechanism for automatically generating rich, adaptive curricula in multi-agent learning environments.

#### 5.1. Self-Scaling Curriculum Generation

The framework effectively transforms environment generation into a continuous process where the Attacker agent intrinsically learns to produce challenges at the dynamic frontier of the Defenders' current capabilities. The Attacker is rewarded for defeating the Defenders, naturally guiding it to probe and exploit weaknesses in the Defenders' collective policy. This dynamic makes the environment generator an adaptive "loss function" for the solver agents. It efficiently searches the vast combinatorial space of possible environmental configurations to identify maximally informative challenges. This leads to an "infinite stream of novel, never-ending worlds" for training, a quantitative outcome observed in the high frequency of complex strategy utilization by both agents in the main experiment, which stands in contrast to the limited strategic depth seen in ablation studies.

#### 5.2. Nature of the Co-evolutionary Arms Race

The observed oscillating performance curves reflect a sustained co-evolutionary arms race, akin to the Red Queen hypothesis. This perpetual non-stationarity is a desirable feature for open-ended learning, preventing agents from converging to brittle, fixed policies. Instead, the system continually navigates a cycle of strategy and counter-strategy development (e.g., Attacker employs Flanking, Defenders learn Spreading; Attacker pivots to Tandem, Defenders learn Focusing). This dynamic compels agents to maintain and evolve a broad and robust repertoire of behaviors, as any abandoned strategy could be re-exploited by the adversary.

#### 5.3. Implications for Generalization and Robustness

By design, the adversarial framework directly promotes generalization. Agents are compelled to learn adaptive policies rather than memorizing solutions to a static set of problems because the environment is actively hostile and non-stationary. The Attacker effectively functions as an automated "red-teaming" agent, systematically discovering edge cases and blind spots in the Defenders' policies. This provides a more efficient and exhaustive method for enhancing agent robustness against a wide range of potential exploits compared to manual testing or curated test cases. Consequently, such adversarial generation frameworks could become a standard component in development pipelines for safe, reliable, and generally intelligent autonomous agents.

#### 5.4. Limitations and Future Work

While the findings are promising, the work has limitations. The training duration of 500 episodes on consumer-grade hardware is relatively short; longer training periods could potentially lead to the emergence of even more sophisticated, multi-layered strategies.

Future research directions include:
- **Integration with Large Language Models (LLMs):** Exploring LLMs as the generative Attacker to formulate high-level strategic goals that are then translated into specific unit generation actions, or for the Defender team for high-level planning and explicit communication.
- **Scaling Generator Complexity:** Empowering the Attacker to modify environment topology, place obstacles, or design new unit types with unique behaviors, expanding beyond compositional unit attributes to a richer set of environmental "tools."
- **Deeper Policy Analysis:** Utilizing explainable AI techniques to dissect agent decision-making processes, offering clearer insights into the mechanics of co-evolutionary learning.
- **Population-Based Training:** Investigating the co-evolution of multiple species of Attackers and Defender teams to foster a richer and more diverse ecosystem of emergent strategies.

In conclusion, this work contributes to the evidence supporting adversarial self-play as a powerful paradigm for generating complexity without extensive human data. It presents a practical method for scaling environments to meet the demands of advanced autonomous agents, advocating for a shift from manual content design to the architectural design of self-scaling, complexity-generating systems.
