# Massively Parallel Methods for Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google DeepMind introduced Gorila, a massively distributed architecture for deep reinforcement learning that significantly scales up the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm. This approach led to superior agent performance on 41 out of 49 Atari games and reduced training time by an order of magnitude compared to single-GPU DQN.

## Problem

- Deep Q-Network (DQN) implementations were constrained to single-machine architectures, resulting in prolonged training times (e.g., 12-14 days per Atari game).
- The lengthy training periods limited the scalability and practical application of deep reinforcement learning agents.
- Existing distributed reinforcement learning methods primarily focused on simpler function approximators or specific batch-oriented solutions, lacking a comprehensive distributed architecture for deep RL.

## Method

- Developed **Gorila** (General Reinforcement Learning Architecture), a massively distributed framework for deep reinforcement learning.
- The architecture separates core RL components into parallel ==Actors==, ==Learners==, a distributed ==Experience Replay==, and a ==Parameter Server==.
- It employs ==asynchronous stochastic gradient descent (ASGD)== to enable concurrent experience generation, learning, and parameter updates.

## Results

- Gorila DQN achieved higher scores than the single-GPU DQN on **41 out of 49** Atari games when evaluated with "human starts."
- Training time was reduced by approximately an **order of magnitude**, with Gorila surpassing single-GPU DQN performance on **30 games** within **24 hours**.
- The distributed approach demonstrated improved generalization, performing at or above human professional level in **25 games** from human-sampled starting points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Massively parallel processing can effectively scale deep reinforcement learning, significantly reducing training time and enhancing agent performance.
> - Distributing agent-environment interactions across multiple actors increases the diversity and quantity of generated experience, leading to improved generalization capabilities.
> - Stable deep reinforcement learning is achievable in an asynchronous, distributed environment through careful architectural design and safeguards like gradient filtering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep learning methods have demonstrated capabilities in various domains, including vision and speech processing, by learning high-level features from supervised data. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has also seen advancements through the integration of deep learning, notably with the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm. DQN employs a deep convolutional neural network to approximate the action-value (Q) function, enabling agents to learn control policies from high-dimensional visual inputs, such as those in Atari 2600 games. The original DQN implementation achieved human-level performance on many Atari games using a single network architecture and hyperparameters.

However, prior DQN implementations operated on single-machine architectures, which resulted in prolonged training times, specifically 12-14 days for a single Atari game on a GPU. This constrained the scalability and practical application of the method. The broader field of deep learning has increasingly focused on massively distributed architectures to leverage extensive computational resources and process larger datasets more efficiently. Frameworks like DistBelief have successfully distributed neural network parameters and parallelized training using asynchronous stochastic gradient descent (ASGD), demonstrating improved speed over single-GPU training in supervised and unsupervised learning contexts.

This paper addresses the gap in distributed deep reinforcement learning. While other forms of parallel or distributed RL exist—such as distributed multi-agent systems (cooperative agents in a shared environment) or concurrent reinforcement learning (agents interacting with a distributed environment)—this work focuses on improving the efficiency of solving a *single-agent* problem through parallel computation. Previous efforts like MapReduce for MDP solution methods or parallel Sarsa with linear function approximation also exist, but they are limited to batch methods, linear function approximation, or simpler function approximators and communication schemes. This research distinguishes itself by developing a comprehensive distributed architecture specifically for deep reinforcement learning, aiming to extend the successes of DQN to a massively parallel setting and thereby enable more efficient training and potentially enhanced performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop a massively distributed architecture for deep reinforcement learning algorithms. This architecture was designed to leverage extensive computational resources to scale up the training of deep RL agents, specifically focusing on the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm.

The motivation behind this objective stemmed from several factors:
- **Addressing Training Time Limitations:** Existing single-machine implementations of DQN, despite their performance capabilities, required substantial training durations. For instance, training the DQN algorithm on a single Atari game typically took 12-14 days on a GPU. This lengthy training period restricted the practical deployment and experimental iteration speed of deep RL systems.
- **Exploiting Computational Resources:** Deep learning methods are inherently amenable to parallelization. The researchers aimed to exploit the availability of massive computational resources by designing an architecture that could distribute the various components of a reinforcement learning system. This approach sought to capitalize on the performance benefits observed in distributed supervised and unsupervised deep learning, where parallel processing led to faster training and the ability to handle larger datasets.
- **Enhancing Data Generation and Exploration:** In reinforcement learning, the agent's actions directly influence the distribution of its training data. By deploying multiple agents (actors) to interact with multiple instances of an environment in parallel, the architecture could generate a significantly larger quantity of diverse experience data. This increased data generation was hypothesized to lead to more effective exploration of the state space, potentially improving the agent's learning capabilities and generalization.
- **Improving Performance and Generalization:** The central hypothesis was that by scaling up the training process—through increased data generation, parallel learning, and the use of large distributed neural networks—the performance of deep RL algorithms like DQN could be further enhanced. This included not only achieving higher scores but also improving the agent's ability to generalize to novel, unseen states, which is a significant challenge in deterministic environments like the Arcade Learning Environment.

In summary, the motivation was to overcome the computational bottlenecks of single-machine deep RL, unlock the potential of massive parallelism, and thereby achieve faster training, superior performance, and enhanced generalization in complex reinforcement learning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Gorila (General Reinforcement Learning Architecture), a framework for massively distributed reinforcement learning, which was used to implement a distributed version of the DQN algorithm. Gorila's architecture comprises four main components:

#### 3.1. Gorila Architecture Components

- **Actors (N_act):** This component consists of multiple parallel processes, each interacting with an independent instance of the environment. Each actor generates its own trajectories of experience (state, action, reward, next state). This parallel acting increases the total quantity of experience generated and facilitates broader exploration of the state space, as each actor may follow a slightly different policy due to asynchronous updates. Each actor maintains a replica of the Q-network, which it uses to determine its behavior (e.g., via an $\epsilon$-greedy policy). These Q-network parameters are periodically synchronized from a central parameter server.

- **Experience Replay Memory:** The experience tuples generated by the actors are stored in a replay memory. Two forms were considered:
 - **Local Replay Memory:** Each actor stores its experience locally. This increases the overall memory capacity to M * N_act if a single machine can store M tuples.
 - **Global Replay Memory:** Experience from all actors is aggregated into a distributed database. This allows for scalable memory capacity independent of the number of actors but incurs additional communication overhead. For the experiments, the local replay memory approach was implicitly used within a "bundled mode."

- **Learners (N_learn):** Multiple parallel learner processes exist. Each learner possesses a replica of the Q-network and is responsible for computing updates to its parameters. A learner samples minibatches of experience tuples from either a local or global experience replay memory. It then applies an off-policy RL algorithm, specifically the DQN algorithm, to this minibatch to calculate a gradient vector. These gradients are subsequently communicated to the parameter server. The Q-network parameters within the learners are also periodically updated from the parameter server.

- **Parameter Server (N_param):** This central component maintains a distributed representation of the global Q-network parameters ($\theta^{+}$). The parameter vector is sharded across multiple machines, with each machine handling updates to a subset of the parameters. The parameter server receives gradients from the learners and applies these updates using an asynchronous stochastic gradient descent (ASGD) algorithm. It then provides the latest version of the parameters to the actors and learners upon request.

#### 3.2. Gorila DQN Implementation Details

The DQN algorithm, which uses two Q-networks (a current Q-network with parameters $\theta$ and a target Q-network with parameters $\theta^{-}$) and experience replay, was adapted for the Gorila architecture:
- The parameter server maintains the central, current parameters $\theta^{+}$.
- Actors and learners sync their replicas of the current Q-network $Q(s, a; \theta)$ from the parameter server before each acting step.
- Each learner additionally maintains its own target Q-network $Q(s, a; \theta^{-})$. This target network's parameters are updated from the parameter server's $\theta^{+}$ after a global count of N gradient updates performed in the parameter server, ensuring a consistent update schedule across distributed learners.
- Learners calculate gradients based on the DQN loss function (Equation 2 in the paper) and send them to the parameter server, which aggregates and applies these updates asynchronously.

#### 3.3. Stability Measures

To ensure stable training in a large-scale distributed environment with potential network and machine issues, Gorila DQN incorporated several safeguards:
- **Gradient Discarding:** A maximum time delay parameter was set. Gradients older than this threshold (indicating stale parameter information) were discarded by the parameter server.
- **Loss-Based Gradient Filtering:** Each actor/learner maintained a running average and standard deviation of the absolute DQN loss. Gradients associated with an absolute loss exceeding the mean plus several standard deviations were discarded, mitigating the impact of potentially unstable or outlier updates.
- **Adaptive Learning Rate:** The AdaGrad update rule (Duchi et al., 2011) was utilized for parameter updates in the parameter server, which dynamically adjusts learning rates based on past gradients.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Environment:** 49 Atari 2600 games within the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) were used.
- **Input Preprocessing:** Raw 210x160 RGB images were downsampled to 84x84 and converted to a luminance channel. Four consecutive preprocessed frames were stacked to form the 84x84x4 input to the neural network.
- **Network Architecture:** A convolutional neural network was used, consisting of three convolutional layers followed by a 512-unit fully-connected hidden layer, and finally a linear fully-connected output layer with one unit per valid action. Rectifier nonlinearities were applied after hidden layers.
- **Action Repetition:** Actions were repeated over 4 frames (frame skipping).
- **Configuration:** Experiments used N_param = 31 (parameter servers), and N_learn = N_act = 100 (learners and actors), operating in a "bundled mode" where each actor was paired with a local replay memory and a learner.
- **Replay Memory:** A replay memory size of 1 million frames was used.
- **Exploration Policy:** An $\epsilon$-greedy policy was employed, with $\epsilon$ annealed from 1 to 0.1 over the first one million global updates.
- **Target Network Update:** Learners synchronized their target network parameters ($\theta^{-}$) from the parameter server's $\theta^{+}$ after every 60,000 parameter updates applied at the central parameter server.
- **Hyperparameter Selection:** Hyperparameters were informally searched on Breakout, Pong, and Seaquest, and then fixed for all 49 games.
- **Replication and Evaluation:** Each game was trained 5 times with different random network initializations. The best-performing network parameters during training were selected for final evaluation.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Protocols

Two evaluation methods were used, with scores normalized such that 0 represented a random agent and 100 represented a professional human player:
- **Null Op Starts:** The standard DQN evaluation. Agents were tested on 30 episodes, starting with a random number of "do nothing" actions to vary initial conditions. Each episode lasted up to 18,000 frames (5 minutes) or until game termination.
- **Human Starts:** A generalization test. Agents started from 100 distinct starting points sampled from human professional gameplay. Episodes ran until game end or 108,000 total frames (30 minutes, including frames played by the human to reach the start point). Only points accumulated by the agent were counted.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented results demonstrating the performance and efficiency of the Gorila distributed architecture when applied to the DQN algorithm across 49 Atari 2600 games.

#### 4.1. Performance vs. Single-GPU DQN

- **Overall Performance (Human Starts Evaluation):** Gorila DQN significantly outperformed the non-distributed single-GPU DQN. When evaluated using the "human starts" protocol, Gorila DQN achieved higher scores than single-GPU DQN on 41 out of 49 games. This was accomplished with approximately half the training time (6 days for Gorila DQN compared to 12-14 days for single-GPU DQN).
 - On 22 games, Gorila DQN obtained double the score of single-GPU DQN.
 - On 11 games, Gorila DQN's score was five times higher than single-GPU DQN.
 - Gorila DQN performed at a level similar to or superior to a human professional (defined as 75% or more of the human score) in 25 games, even when starting from states sampled from human play.
- **Overall Performance (Null Op Starts Evaluation):** Using the "null op starts" evaluation, Gorila DQN outperformed single-GPU DQN on 31 out of 49 games.
- **Generalization Improvements:** The comparison between "human starts" and "null op starts" results suggested that Gorila DQN exhibited improved generalization capabilities compared to single-GPU DQN. This was attributed to the significantly increased diversity and quantity of states encountered by using 100 parallel actors during training.

#### 4.2. Training Time Reduction

- **Wall-Time Efficiency:** Gorila DQN demonstrated a substantial reduction in the wall-time required to achieve strong performance.
 - It surpassed the best performance of single-GPU DQN on 19 games within 6 hours of training.
 - It exceeded single-GPU DQN's best scores on 23 games within 12 hours.
 - Within 24 hours, it surpassed single-GPU DQN on 30 games.
 - After 36 hours, it surpassed single-GPU DQN on 38 games.
- **Order of Magnitude Speedup:** This represented an approximate order of magnitude reduction in training time necessary to reach the performance levels of the single-process DQN.
- **Continued Improvement:** While Gorila DQN reached its best score on some games within two days, performance generally continued to improve with longer training durations across the majority of games.

The findings indicate that the massively parallel architecture enabled both superior learning outcomes and significantly faster training for the DQN algorithm in the Atari 2600 domain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the first massively distributed architecture for deep reinforcement learning, named Gorila, and demonstrates its application to the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm. The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

- **Scalability of Deep Reinforcement Learning:** The work establishes that deep reinforcement learning algorithms, specifically DQN, can effectively scale with increased computational resources. Prior to this research, it was not definitively known whether the performance benefits of DQN would continue to hold or improve in a massively distributed setting. The results confirm that greater computation can lead to improved learning outcomes.
- **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** Gorila DQN achieved superior performance compared to the single-GPU DQN on a majority of the 49 Atari games. Notably, it outperformed the single-GPU version on 41 games using the "human starts" evaluation, suggesting improved generalization to novel game states. This advancement indicates the potential for deep RL agents trained with massively parallel methods to develop more robust and adaptable policies.
- **Significant Reduction in Training Time:** The most direct impact is the order of magnitude reduction in wall-time required to train high-performing deep RL agents. Reducing training from 12-14 days to a matter of hours or a few days accelerates the research cycle, allowing for faster experimentation, iteration, and deployment of deep RL models.
- **Foundation for Future Distributed RL Research:** The Gorila architecture, with its distinct components of parallel actors, learners, a distributed neural network, and a distributed experience replay, provides a foundational framework for developing and testing other massively distributed deep reinforcement learning algorithms. Its modular design offers flexibility for researchers to explore various parallelization strategies for different RL paradigms.
- **Broader Applicability:** By demonstrating the efficacy of massively parallel deep RL in a complex domain like Atari games, this work suggests the potential for applying similar architectures to other challenging real-world problems that require extensive exploration, large datasets, and long training times. This could include robotics, complex control systems, and other domains where the cost of data generation or interaction is high.
- **Addressing Bottlenecks:** The architectural design specifically addresses key bottlenecks in single-machine deep RL, such as limited data generation rate and finite replay memory capacity, by distributing these functions across many machines. This approach contributes to making deep RL more practical for large-scale applications.

In summary, this research advances the state-of-the-art in deep reinforcement learning by providing a proven, scalable architecture. It paves the way for the development of more capable and efficient deep RL systems, accelerating progress in artificial intelligence research and its potential applications.
