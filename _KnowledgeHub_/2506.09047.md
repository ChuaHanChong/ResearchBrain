# Same Task, Different Circuits: Disentangling Modality-Specific Mechanisms in VLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Technion and UC Berkeley investigated the persistent performance gap in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) between visual and textual tasks, finding that visual representations align slowly with textual ones. They propose an inference-time intervention called "back-patching" that re-injects later-layer visual activations into earlier layers, closing approximately 32% of this performance gap by improving visual task accuracy by an average of 4.6%.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) consistently exhibit a performance gap, achieving higher accuracy on textual inputs than on analogous visual inputs for the same task.
- The underlying computational mechanisms and reasons for this modality-specific performance discrepancy within VLMs are not fully understood.
- Improving VLM performance on visual tasks typically requires computationally intensive retraining or fine-tuning, lacking insights for training-free interventions.

## Method

- A custom dataset of five analogous visual and textual question-answering tasks was created to precisely compare VLM performance and internal processing across modalities.
- ==Mechanistic interpretability techniques==, specifically ==Attribution Patching with Integrated Gradients==, were used to identify and compare modality-specific computational "circuits" within VLMs.
- A training-free, inference-time intervention called =="back-patching"== was developed, which records and re-injects textually-aligned visual token activations from later layers back into earlier layers.

## Results

- Back-patching achieved an average absolute accuracy gain of **4.6%** on visual prompts across various tasks and models, effectively closing approximately **32%** of the performance gap between analogous visual and textual tasks.
- Control experiments confirmed that back-patching's improvements for visual tasks stemmed from accelerating textual alignment rather than merely adding computation.
- The intervention's efficacy was validated on general VQA benchmarks (VQAv2 and RealWorldQA) with statistically significant accuracy increases, and it generally improved performance across different scales of PaliGemma2 models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLMs utilize largely disjoint sets of computational components (circuits) for processing analogous visual and textual tasks, with only 18% average overlap.
> - While data processing sub-circuits are modality-specific and functionally non-interchangeable, query processing and answer generation sub-circuits, despite structural differences, are functionally equivalent across modalities.
> - Visual token representations align gradually and slowly with their analogous textual counterparts across model layers, with robust alignment typically achieved only in later stages of processing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several critical and rapidly evolving fields: Vision-Language Models (VLMs), mechanistic interpretability, and multi-modal representation learning.

**Advances in VLMs and the Performance Gap:** Recent years have seen an explosion in the capabilities of VLMs, enabling them to understand and generate content across visual and textual modalities. Models like Qwen2-VL, Pixtral, and Gemma-3-12B-it (analyzed in this paper) demonstrate impressive multi-modal reasoning. However, a well-documented "performance gap" persists, where VLMs often perform analogous tasks significantly better on textual inputs than on visual inputs (Fu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a; Cohen et al., 2024). This paper directly addresses this fundamental challenge, seeking to understand its underlying causes.

**Mechanistic Interpretability:** The core methodology builds upon the burgeoning field of mechanistic interpretability, which aims to reverse-engineer the internal workings of neural networks by identifying "circuits"—minimal sets of interconnected components responsible for specific computations (Elhage et al., 2021). While initially prominent in Large Language Models (LLMs), this paper extends these sophisticated causal analysis techniques (e.g., Attribution Patching with Integrated Gradients) to the more complex domain of VLMs. It contributes to the broader effort of making deep learning models more transparent and explainable.

**Multi-modal Representation Alignment:** The work delves into how visual and textual representations are processed and aligned within VLMs. Previous studies have explored how representations from different modalities can be aligned (Merullo et al., 2022; Koh et al., 2023) and how visual tokens gradually align with textual counterparts (Wu et al., 2024). This paper elaborates on this by showing *when* and *where* this alignment happens within the model's layers and *how* its timing impacts performance. It directly connects the "modality gap" in performance to the representational differences and their processing dynamics.

**Bridging Theory and Practice:** Uniquely, this research doesn't just interpret; it proposes a practical, training-free intervention ("back-patching") to improve VLM performance. This demonstrates a growing trend in interpretability research where understanding internal mechanisms can directly inform methods for enhancing model capabilities, moving beyond mere diagnostics to actionable insights. By doing so, it contributes to the development of more robust and efficient multi-modal AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed and persistent performance disparity in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) when performing analogous tasks across different modalities. Despite advanced VLM architectures and extensive training, models consistently exhibit higher accuracy on tasks presented as text (e.g., counting words) compared to the same tasks presented visually (e.g., counting objects in an image). This gap is prevalent even in models where the decoder is jointly trained from scratch on both modalities. The authors argue that to effectively address this performance discrepancy, a deep understanding of its root causes is necessary.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out with three key objectives:

1. **To Explain the Performance Gap (Explanatory Objective):** The primary objective is to mechanistically explain *why* this accuracy gap exists. This involves investigating the structural and functional differences in how VLMs process visual versus textual inputs for the *same* task. The authors aim to identify and compare the specific "circuits"—the task-specific computational sub-graphs—that VLMs employ for each modality.
2. **To Localize Functional Differences (Diagnostic Objective):** Beyond identifying overall differences, the research seeks to pinpoint *where* within the VLM's processing pipeline these functional differences are most pronounced. Specifically, it aims to determine if the discrepancies lie in the processing of the data tokens (the image or descriptive text), the query tokens (the question being asked), or the answer generation tokens.
3. **To Develop Training-Free Interventions for Improvement (Interventional Objective):** Leveraging the insights gained from the mechanistic analysis, a practical objective is to devise a method to mitigate the performance gap. The goal is to improve visual task accuracy without requiring additional model training, focusing instead on inference-time interventions based on the identified internal mechanisms. This objective highlights a commitment to translating interpretability findings into tangible improvements.

In essence, the research is driven by a desire to peel back the "black box" of VLMs to understand the nuances of their multi-modal processing, diagnose the specific points of failure or inefficiency in visual tasks, and then use that understanding to make these models more capable and balanced across modalities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology combining custom dataset creation, sophisticated circuit analysis techniques, and targeted interventional experiments.

**Custom Analogous Task Dataset:**
To ensure direct comparability, the authors created a dataset of five diverse question-answering tasks: Object Counting, Two-operand Arithmetic, Spatial Ordering, Factual Recall, and Sentiment Analysis. Each task has analogous textual and visual prompt variants designed to convey the same information and elicit the same type of answer. Prompts were meticulously structured into three parts: data (the image or descriptive text), query (the question), and generation (the last token position for the answer). Constraints were applied to ensure single-word answers and positional alignment, crucial for circuit analysis.

**Model Selection:**
The study analyzed three distinct transformer-based VLMs: Qwen2-7B-VL-Instruct, Pixtral-12B, and Gemma-3-12B-it. These models utilize an "adapter" architecture, comprising an image encoder, an optional adapter layer, and a language decoder, but differ in their training schemes and data. This selection enhances the robustness and generalizability of the findings across various VLM implementations.

**Circuit Discovery and Evaluation:**
- **Definition of Circuit Components:** A circuit component is defined as either an entire attention head or an individual Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neuron at a specific output position.
- **Circuit Discovery (Causal Analysis):** Causal analysis techniques were used to score the importance of each model component. Specifically, Attribution Patching with Integrated Gradients (AP-IG) was employed. This method estimates a component's importance by measuring the change in logit difference between the correct and a counterfactual answer when the component's activation is patched from a counterfactual prompt.
- **Circuit Construction:** Circuits were constructed by selecting a specific percentage of the highest-scoring components.
- **Circuit Evaluation (Faithfulness):** The faithfulness metric was used to evaluate a discovered circuit. Faithfulness quantifies the proportion of the full model's task performance that can be attributed to the circuit by ablating non-circuit components using counterfactual activations. A circuit was considered "sufficient" if it achieved over 80% faithfulness with minimal components.

**Cross-Modality Circuit Analysis (Structural and Functional):**
- **Structural Intersection:** The degree of overlap between textual and visual circuits was quantified using a Normalized Intersection over Union (NIoU) score. Circuits were first split into three sub-circuits based on position: data (c_D), query (c_Q), and generation (c_G). A bidirectional mapping was used to align positions between modalities for comparison, with special consideration for data positions where direct one-to-one mapping is not always possible. The NIoU score was normalized against a random baseline to account for varying circuit sizes.
- **Functional Interchangeability:** To assess functional equivalence, sub-circuits were swapped between modalities. For instance, the query sub-circuit of the textual task (c_Q_L) would replace the query sub-circuit of the visual task (c_Q_V) and vice versa, and the faithfulness of the resulting mixed circuit was measured. High faithfulness after swapping indicated functional equivalence despite structural differences.

**Improving Visual Tasks Performance (Back-patching):**
- **Observation:** The analysis of representation alignment (cosine similarity between visual and analogous textual token activations) across layers showed that visual tokens only gradually align with textual counterparts, with higher similarity in later layers.
- **Intervention (Back-patching):** Based on this observation, a test-time intervention called "back-patching" was devised. This involves performing an initial forward pass, recording visual token activations from later layers (l_src) where they are more textually aligned, and then re-injecting these recorded activations back into earlier layers (l_dst) during a second forward pass. The optimal (l_src, l_dst) layers and window size were determined by systematically exploring different configurations.
- **Validation:** The efficacy of back-patching was evaluated by measuring accuracy improvements on the custom dataset and extended to general VQA benchmarks (VQAv2, RealWorldQA). A control experiment applying back-patching to textual prompts was conducted to isolate the effect of early textual alignment versus mere additional computation. Iterative back-patching and scaling effects across PaliGemma2 models (3B, 10B, 28B) were also explored.

This rigorous methodological framework allowed the authors to not only diagnose the source of the VLM performance gap but also to propose and validate a practical solution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings that shed light on the internal workings of VLMs and the origins of the multi-modal performance gap:

1. **Structural Disjointness of Modality-Specific Circuits:**
 - VLMs largely employ distinct sets of components (circuits) for processing analogous vision and language tasks. On average, only 18% of components were shared between modalities.
 - This disjointness was most pronounced in the **data processing sub-circuits**, which showed virtually no structural intersection across tasks and models, as expected given their specialized role in handling distinct input data distributions (images vs. text).
 - **Query processing sub-circuits** exhibited low overlap (12% on average), despite operating on identical textual query tokens.
 - **Answer generation sub-circuits** showed moderately higher intersection rates (38% on average), reflecting a more shared function in producing the final output regardless of input modality.

2. **Functional Equivalence vs. Modality-Specificity:**
 - Despite their structural disjointness, the **query processing and answer generation sub-circuits were found to be functionally equivalent** between modalities. Swapping these sub-circuits between textual and visual tasks maintained high faithfulness, suggesting that different components in these parts of the model implement similar underlying functionalities.
 - In stark contrast, the **data processing sub-circuits were highly modality-specific and functionally non-interchangeable**. Swapping them between modalities caused a significant drop in faithfulness, indicating that the core functional difference contributing to the accuracy gap lies in how the initial visual and textual data tokens are processed.

3. **Delayed Visual Representation Alignment:**
 - An analysis of token activations revealed that visual token representations gradually align with their analogous textual counterparts across model layers. However, this alignment process is *slow*, with higher cosine similarity between visual and textual representations typically occurring only in later layers of the model. This implies that visual information is transformed into textually-aligned (and thus more usable by the text-heavy decoder) representations relatively late in the processing pipeline.

4. **Effectiveness of Training-Free Back-patching:**
 - To leverage the observation of delayed alignment, the authors implemented "back-patching"—re-injecting these more textually-aligned visual representations from later layers back into earlier layers.
 - This simple, inference-time intervention significantly improved visual task performance, achieving an **average absolute accuracy gain of 4.6%** on visual prompts across various tasks and models.
 - Crucially, back-patching effectively **closed approximately 32% of the performance gap** between analogous visual and textual tasks.
 - Control experiments, where back-patching was applied to textual prompts, showed significantly smaller accuracy gains in 79% of cases, supporting the hypothesis that the improvements for visual tasks stem from accelerating the textual alignment of visual tokens rather than just adding computation.
 - The efficacy of back-patching was further validated on general VQA benchmarks (VQAv2 and RealWorldQA), where it also led to statistically significant accuracy increases.
 - Experiments across PaliGemma2 model scales (3B, 10B, 28B) demonstrated that back-patching generally increases accuracy, with the 10B model showing the largest gains.
 - Iterative back-patching (applying the intervention multiple times) was found to degrade performance after the first application, suggesting that repeatedly forcing representations out of distribution is detrimental.

In summary, the core findings indicate that while VLMs maintain a common functional core for query processing and answer generation, the critical bottleneck for visual task performance lies in the early, modality-specific processing of visual data tokens, which align too slowly with the more performant textual representations. Back-patching offers a direct and effective way to mitigate this by proactively making better-aligned visual representations available earlier in the model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for both the fundamental understanding and practical improvement of Vision-Language Models.

**Fundamental Understanding of VLM Mechanics:**
The paper offers crucial mechanistic insights into the inner workings of VLMs. By demonstrating that modality-specific circuits are largely disjoint but functionally aligned in later stages (query, generation) while diverging significantly in early data processing, it pinpoints the precise computational locus of the performance gap. This moves beyond mere observational statistics to provide a causal explanation, deepening our understanding of how multi-modal information flows and transforms within these complex models. It highlights that the "multi-modal" aspect of VLMs is not always a seamless integration from the outset but rather a learned alignment process that happens over layers.

**Practical, Training-Free Performance Enhancement:**
The introduction of "back-patching" is a direct and actionable consequence of the mechanistic analysis. As a training-free inference-time intervention, it offers a practical method to boost VLM accuracy on visual tasks. This is particularly impactful because it bypasses the computationally expensive and time-consuming process of retraining or fine-tuning models. This approach could be readily integrated into existing VLM deployment pipelines, providing immediate performance benefits without altering the underlying model architecture or weights. The ability to close a third of the performance gap with such a simple technique is a testament to the power of mechanistic interpretability.

**Guiding Future VLM Architecture and Training:**
The findings suggest critical directions for future VLM design. If visual representations only align effectively with textual ones in later layers, architects might consider:
- **Flexible Compute Allocation:** Designing VLMs that dynamically allocate more computational resources or specialized processing units to visual tokens in early layers to accelerate their alignment. This resonates with emerging paradigms like test-time compute scaling (Geiping et al., 2025).
- **Early Fusion Mechanisms:** Developing new architectural components or training objectives that explicitly encourage and enforce earlier and more robust alignment of visual features with semantic concepts, potentially through more sophisticated adapter layers or earlier cross-attention mechanisms.
- **Optimized Pre-training Strategies:** Tailoring pre-training data and tasks to emphasize early and efficient visual-to-textual mapping, reducing the latency of alignment observed.

**Advancement in Mechanistic Interpretability:**
The work further validates the utility of circuit analysis and causal interventions as powerful tools for understanding complex, black-box deep learning models, extending their application from LLMs to the multi-modal domain. It showcases how interpretability can serve not just as a diagnostic tool but also as a direct pathway to model improvement.

**Broader Impact on Multi-modal AI:**
Ultimately, by contributing to the development of more balanced and robust VLMs, this research has broader implications for a wide array of AI applications. From enhanced visual question answering and image captioning to more reliable multi-modal agents interacting with the world, a deeper understanding of modality-specific processing errors can lead to more capable, trustworthy, and fair multi-modal AI systems that perform consistently across different input types. The focus on reducing performance disparities is crucial for equitable AI development.
