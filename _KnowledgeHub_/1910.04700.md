# Assistive Gym: A Physics Simulation Framework for Assistive Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Assistive Gym is an open-source PyBullet simulation framework for physical human-robot interaction, providing six activities-of-daily-living environments (itch scratching, bed bathing, drinking, feeding, dressing, arm manipulation) with programmatic human models carrying impairments, pose-dependent joint limits, and a unified human-preference reward. PPO baselines for four commercial robots show co-optimizing an active human policy raises task success 30.4% on average.

## Problem

- Physical human-robot assistance research is costly and risky on real hardware, since mistakes during learning put people with disabilities in direct physical danger.
- Prior assistive simulation work optimized robots for one task at a time, giving no common interface or benchmark for comparing control algorithms across caregiving activities.
- Existing robot simulators target manipulation, navigation, or visual tasks and omit human-centric modeling of body variation, impairment, and preferences for receiving care.

## Method

- Six ==PyBullet== ADL environments in the ==OpenAI Gym== interface, with programmatically generated 40-joint male/female human models plus tremor, joint-weakness, and limited-range-of-motion impairment models.
- A unified ==human-preference reward== combining seven weighted penalty costs (end-effector velocity, off-target force, high force, spilling, ingestion velocity, garment force, pressure) with the robot's task reward.
- A learned ==pose-dependent joint-limit classifier== rejecting invalid arm poses, plus base-pose selection by sampling 100 poses and ranking on collision-free ==IK== count and joint-limit-weighted kinematic isotropy.

## Results

- **PPO** baselines for PR2, Jaco, Baxter, and Sawyer over 10 million steps show wide robot-task variation; best static-human success ranged from **88%** (feeding) down to **26%** (dressing) and **8%** (arm manipulation).
- Co-optimizing robot and active human policies under a shared reward raised best-robot task success by **30.4%** on average, with bed bathing **24%→81%** and dressing **26%→89%**.
- Reward differences expose morphology effects: the PR2's shorter arm span hurt itch scratching (**55.1** vs Jaco's **280.8**), while Sawyer led drinking at **436.0** reward.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating the assisted person as a second trained agent rather than a static obstacle turns hard reaching tasks into collaborative ones and sharply improves attainable assistance.
> - Encoding caregiving preferences as explicit weighted penalty terms lets one reward template transfer across heterogeneous tasks instead of bespoke per-task shaping.
> - Deformable and contact-heavy tasks remain the bottleneck: cloth simulation cost days of training and physically demanding lifting still failed under safety penalties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous robots capable of providing physical assistance to individuals with disabilities is a significant area of research aimed at enhancing quality of life for a substantial global population. This field, known as assistive robotics, frequently involves complex physical human-robot interaction (HRI). However, conducting research in real-world HRI scenarios presents challenges such as high costs, safety risks to human participants, and difficulties in scalable data collection.

Physics simulations have emerged as a tool to mitigate these challenges, allowing robots to learn from mistakes safely and enabling the parallelization of data collection for thousands of trials. While existing simulation environments have been used to optimize and train robots, many have historically focused on single tasks (e.g., manipulation or navigation) or lacked a strong emphasis on detailed human-robot physical interaction within the context of activities of daily living (ADLs).

This work addresses these gaps by introducing Assistive Gym, an open-source physics simulation framework specifically designed for assistive robotics. It builds upon established simulation platforms like OpenAI Gym and leverages physics engines such as PyBullet. Unlike many existing robotic simulation environments, Assistive Gym prioritizes human-centric interactions across multiple ADLs, incorporating models of human physical capabilities, limitations, and preferences.

The paper situates itself within a body of prior research on robotic assistance for specific tasks (e.g., feeding, drinking, dressing, itch scratching, bed bathing) and methods like co-optimization for collaborative HRI. By providing a unified, multi-task framework, Assistive Gym aims to standardize benchmarking, facilitate the development of new control algorithms, and enable systematic comparison of different robot platforms in assistive contexts, thereby advancing the field towards more versatile and intelligent robotic caregivers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop and release Assistive Gym, an open-source physics-based simulation framework designed for physical human-robot interaction and robotic assistance. This overarching goal was motivated by several challenges inherent in assistive robotics research and aimed to achieve specific capabilities:

**Motivation:**

1. **Addressing Real-World Research Challenges:** The development of autonomous robots for versatile physical assistance faces obstacles such as high costs associated with robotic hardware and human subject recruitment, as well as the inherent safety risks of physical interaction between robots and vulnerable people. Physics simulations offer a means to safely experiment, learn from errors, and gather data at a scale not feasible in real-world settings.
2. **Scalable Data Collection and Modeling:** Simulations enable the parallelization of data collection, performing numerous human-robot interaction trials in a short timeframe. They also allow for the programmatic generation of diverse human models, representing variations in body shapes, weights, and physical capabilities or impairments, which is crucial for developing robust assistive robots.
3. **Gap in Existing Simulation Environments:** While general robotics simulation environments exist, they often lack a dedicated focus on the intricate physical interaction between robots and humans in the context of assistance. Prior simulations frequently addressed single tasks, rather than a suite of common activities of daily living (ADLs).

**Key Objectives:**

1. **Develop a Multi-Task Simulation Framework:** To create an environment capable of modeling multiple ADLs commonly requiring assistance, moving beyond single-task simulations. This includes tasks such as itch scratching, drinking, feeding, body manipulation, dressing, and bathing.
2. **Emphasize Human-Robot Interaction Modeling:** To place a strong focus on accurately modeling the physical interaction between robots and humans, including detailed human body models, joint limits, and potential physical limitations.
3. **Incorporate Human Capabilities and Preferences:** To integrate models of a person’s physical capabilities (e.g., tremor, weakness, limited range of motion) and preferences for assistance (e.g., preferred force levels, movement speeds). These preferences are designed to inform the reward function for learning robot behaviors.
4. **Provide a Benchmarking Tool:** To offer a standardized platform for the research community to compare different robot control algorithms and evaluate the performance of various robotic platforms for assistive tasks.
5. **Facilitate Environment and Controller Development:** To provide a foundational framework that researchers can extend to develop new assistive tasks and corresponding robot controllers.
6. **Demonstrate the Value of Co-optimization:** To show that modeling human motion, particularly through a co-optimization approach where both human and robot policies are trained simultaneously, can lead to improved assistive performance compared to scenarios where the human remains static.

In essence, the research aimed to create a comprehensive, extensible, and safe virtual laboratory for assistive robotics, thereby lowering the barrier to entry for researchers and accelerating the development of intelligent, human-aware assistive technologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Assistive Gym is presented as an open-source physics simulation framework built upon the PyBullet physics engine and integrated into the OpenAI Gym framework. This design aims to provide a familiar interface for developing control algorithms and leverages PyBullet's capabilities for real-time simulation, soft body dynamics (e.g., cloth), and programmatic model generation.

**A. Human Model and Limitations:**
Assistive Gym includes default male and female human models, with body sizes, weights, and 40 controllable joints (head, torso, arms, waist, legs) based on published 50th percentile data. The human model supports self-collision. To reflect realistic challenges in assistive scenarios, the framework can randomly apply one of three types of physical limitations to the human upon creation:
- **Tremor:** Modeled as an oscillating offset to target joint configurations of the arm or head.
- **Joint Weakness:** Simulated by scaling down the maximum torque of human joints.
- **Limited Range of Motion:** Implemented by scaling the pose-independent joint limits for human joints.

A key aspect of the human model is the incorporation of **realistic, pose-dependent human arm joint limits**. This is achieved by integrating a neural network model, trained on human motion capture data, which predicts the validity of an arm configuration. If a simulated human pose is deemed invalid (potentially uncomfortable or unsafe), the arm is reverted to its previous valid configuration, enhancing safety training for robots.

**B. Robot Models:**
The framework supports four commercial collaborative robots commonly used in HRI research: PR2, Jaco, Baxter, and Sawyer. These robots are modeled within the simulation environments.

**C. Assistive Tasks (Environments):**
Assistive Gym provides six distinct simulation environments, each representing a different activity of daily living (ADL). For most tasks, the robot uses a task-specific tool. Prior to each task, the robot's base pose relative to the person is optimized, and a random perturbation is applied to the end-effector's starting position. The tasks include:
- **Itch Scratching:** Robot uses a scratching tool to reach and perform small motions near a target on the person's arm, while applying minimal force (<10 N).
- **Bed Bathing:** Robot uses a washcloth to wipe specified markers along a person’s arm while they lie on a bed.
- **Drinking Water:** Robot holds a cup with particles (water) and helps a person drink by moving the cup to the mouth, tilting it, and pouring water.
- **Feeding:** Robot holds a spoon with particles (food) and brings it to the person’s mouth without spilling.
- **Dressing:** Robot pulls a hospital gown sleeve up a person’s arm, rewarding movement towards the hand, onto the arm, and along the arm's axis.
- **Arm Manipulation:** Robot uses a scooping tool to lift a person's arm, hanging off a bed, and place it back on the bed.

Each task's reward function is designed to guide the robot towards successful task completion while adhering to safety and preference constraints.

**D. Robot Base Positioning:**
To ensure effective robot operation, the optimal 2D position and orientation of the robot's base with respect to the person are determined at the start of each simulation. This optimization is based on a method called Task-centric Optimization of Configurations (TOC), specifically using Joint-Limit-Weighted Kinematic Isotropy (JLWKI). This process involves sampling 100 base poses, finding inverse-kinematics (IK) solutions for goal end-effector poses, and selecting the pose that allows collision-free IK solutions for the most goals, prioritizing higher JLWKI in case of ties.

**E. Modeling Human Preferences:**
A unified model of human preferences is integrated across all tasks, contributing to the overall reward function. This human preference reward, `r_H(s)`, is combined with the robot's task success reward, `r_R(s)`, to form a total reward `r(s)`. The human preference model includes seven penalty terms (`C_v` for high end-effector velocities, `C_f` for applying force away from the target, `C_hf` for high forces near the target, `C_fd` for spilling food/water, `C_fdv` for high food/water entry velocities, `C_d` for fabric forces on the body, and `C_p` for high pressure with large tools), each with configurable weights. This mechanism encourages robots to learn behaviors that are not only effective but also aligned with human comfort and safety preferences.

**F. Policy Learning and Control:**
The framework supports position control, where robot actions are represented as changes in joint positions (∆P). Robot actuator strength is limited to reduce the likelihood of learning unsafe high-force policies. Observations provided to the robot are designed to mimic real-world sensor data (e.g., 3D end-effector position/orientation, robot joint positions, forces, and 3D positions/orientations of human task-relevant joints relative to the robot's base).

**Reinforcement Learning:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to train control policies. A fully-connected neural network with two hidden layers and tanh activations serves as the policy representation.

**Co-optimization:** For scenarios involving an active human, a co-optimization approach is employed. Both the human and robot are treated as active agents, with separate control policies trained concurrently using PPO and sharing the same combined reward function `r(s)`. The human's motor strength is set just above that required to lift against gravity, encouraging collaboration rather than full independence.

**G. Evaluation Protocol:**
Policies for each robot and task were trained using 36 concurrent simulation actors over 10,000,000 time steps (50,000 rollouts), with each rollout lasting 200 time steps (20 seconds simulation time). Training was repeated with three random seeds, and the best performing policy (based on average reward from the last 360 rollouts) was selected. Evaluation involved running each trained policy over 100 simulation rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Assistive Gym focused on two primary scenarios: robotic assistance to a static human and collaborative assistance involving an active human through co-optimization.

**A. Robotic Assistance (Static Human)**

For the scenario where the robot provided assistance to a person maintaining a static pose, 24 unique policies (4 robots × 6 tasks) were trained and evaluated.

- **Policy Learning Outcomes:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) generally facilitated the learning of control policies for all four robots across the six assistive tasks. However, the performance levels varied among robots and tasks.
- **Task Difficulty:** The "dressing" and "arm manipulation" tasks proved to be more challenging for all robots. For dressing, robots struggled to pull the hospital gown sleeve up the arm consistently, often succeeding only when the sleeve's starting position was favorable. In arm manipulation, robots frequently applied high pressure with the tool, leading to negative human preference rewards and indicating difficulty in learning safe lifting motions.
- **Robot-Specific Performance:** The results indicated differences in physical capabilities among the robots. For instance, the PR2 robot's shorter arm span was noted as a factor that complicated tasks requiring extensive reach, such as itch scratching.
- **Quantitative Performance (Table I Summary):**
 - **Highest Success Rates:** Feeding (Baxter: 88%), Drinking (Sawyer: 72%), Itch Scratching (Jaco: 54%).
 - **Lower Success Rates:** Bed Bathing (Sawyer: 24%), Dressing (Baxter: 26%), Arm Manipulation (PR2: 8%).
 - The results indicated that while reasonable policies could be learned, there was considerable room for improvement in task success rates across all robots and tasks in this static human scenario.

**B. Collaborative Assistance (Active Human via Co-optimization)**

The research investigated the impact of allowing the human to be an active agent, modeled through co-optimization, where both human and robot policies were trained simultaneously using a shared reward function. This scenario was applied to five of the six tasks (excluding arm manipulation, where an active human could often complete the task unaided).

- **Improved Assistance and Rewards:** In nearly all cases, robots achieved better assistance and higher rewards when learning to assist an active human who performed collaborative motions, compared to assisting a static human.
- **Increased Task Success:** The best-performing robot, on average, demonstrated a 30.4% increase in task success when assisting an active human.
- **Examples of Collaboration:** For tasks like itch scratching, an active human policy learned to rotate their arm, thereby making a target location more accessible for the Jaco robot to scratch (as illustrated in Fig. 5).
- **Quantitative Performance (Table II Summary):**
 - **Significant Improvements in Success Rates:**
 - Dressing: Increased from 26% (static, Baxter) to 89% (active, Sawyer).
 - Bed Bathing: Increased from 24% (static, Sawyer) to 81% (active, Jaco).
 - Itch Scratching: Increased from 54% (static, Jaco) to 68% (active, Jaco).
 - Feeding: Increased from 88% (static, Baxter) to 99% (active, PR2).
 - Drinking: Increased from 72% (static, Sawyer) to 79% (active, PR2).
 - These results indicated that modeling and training the human as a collaborative agent significantly improved the robot's ability to successfully complete assistive tasks.

Overall, the findings demonstrated Assistive Gym's utility for benchmarking, developing new assistive environments and controllers, and comparing different robotic platforms. The co-optimization approach highlighted the benefits of designing for human-robot collaboration in assistive contexts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Assistive Gym represents a significant contribution to the field of assistive robotics by providing a dedicated, open-source physics simulation framework. Its potential impact spans several critical areas:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Reduced Cost in Research:** By enabling extensive experimentation in simulation, Assistive Gym mitigates the inherent safety risks associated with physical human-robot interaction and reduces the high costs of real-world robotic hardware and human subject trials. This allows researchers to iterate rapidly and learn from mistakes without adverse consequences.
2. **Acceleration of Assistive Robotics Development:** The framework provides a standardized environment for developing and testing control algorithms for assistive robots. This standardization can accelerate the pace of research and development, as researchers can build upon existing tasks and easily compare new approaches against established baselines.
3. **Comprehensive Benchmarking and Robot Comparison:** Assistive Gym offers a tool for benchmarking various control algorithms and for systematically comparing the capabilities of different robot platforms across a diverse set of activities of daily living (ADLs). This capability is crucial for understanding the strengths and weaknesses of different robot designs and control strategies in assistive contexts.
4. **Modeling Complex Human-Robot Interactions:** The framework's emphasis on modeling realistic human physical capabilities, limitations (e.g., tremor, weakness, pose-dependent joint limits), and preferences for assistance (e.g., force, velocity, pressure) allows for the development of more sophisticated and human-centric robot behaviors. This can lead to robots that not only perform tasks effectively but also do so in a way that is comfortable, safe, and preferred by the person receiving assistance.
5. **Facilitating Collaborative Robotics:** The demonstration that co-optimization of both human and robot policies leads to substantially improved task success highlights the importance of designing for human-robot collaboration rather than purely independent robot action. This finding encourages future research into active human roles in assistance, where people with limited motor function can still contribute to task completion, fostering a sense of agency and efficacy.
6. **Open Source Contribution:** Releasing Assistive Gym as an open-source framework promotes community engagement, transparency, and reproducibility in assistive robotics research. It lowers the barrier for new researchers to enter the field, fostering broader participation and collective advancement.
7. **Foundation for Future Work:** Assistive Gym provides a foundation for developing new assistive tasks and control strategies, as well as for exploring advanced concepts such as curriculum learning to balance task success with human preferences, and bridging the sim-to-real gap for controllers learned in the simulation.

Ultimately, Assistive Gym contributes to the long-term vision of developing autonomous robots that can serve as versatile caregivers, with the potential to significantly improve the quality of life for millions of people worldwide who require physical support with everyday tasks.
