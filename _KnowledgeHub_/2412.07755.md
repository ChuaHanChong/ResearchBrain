# SAT: Dynamic Spatial Aptitude Training for Multimodal Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Boston University, University of Washington, Allen Institute for AI, Microsoft Research, and NYU introduced Spatial Aptitude Training (SAT), a framework utilizing 3D simulators to generate a large, perfectly annotated spatial question-answer dataset. This training improved multimodal language models' static and dynamic spatial reasoning capabilities on real-world images and videos, showing gains on benchmarks like CVBench and a custom dynamic test set.

## Problem

- Current large multimodal language models (MLMs) exhibit critical weaknesses in spatial reasoning, especially when dealing with dynamic relationships and understanding motion.
- The scarcity of high-quality, scalable spatial annotations, particularly for complex 3D movements and their causal effects, limits MLMs' ability to learn these skills.
- Manual annotation of dynamic spatial relationships in real-world data is prohibitively expensive and prone to errors.

## Method

- Developed ==Spatial Aptitude Training (SAT)==, a data generation framework that leverages ==3D simulators== to procedurally create a large-scale dataset of perfectly annotated static and dynamic spatial question-answer pairs.
- The SAT dataset includes five categories of ==dynamic spatial reasoning tasks==: egocentric movement, object movement, allocentric perspective, goal aiming, and action consequence prediction.
- Instruction-tuned existing multimodal language models (LLaVA-1.5-13B and LLaVA-Video-7B) with the SAT data, incorporating a portion of their original training data to maintain general vision-language capabilities.

## Results

- SAT training improved LLaVA-1.5-13B's static spatial reasoning on CVBench and BLINK by an average of **23.9%**, achieving **75.6%** accuracy and often matching or surpassing proprietary models.
- Models fine-tuned with SAT data demonstrated sim-to-real transfer, with LLaVA-1.5-13B improving dynamic spatial reasoning on the real-image SAT test set by **13.3%** to **54.9%**.
- SAT-trained LLaVA-Video-7B showed enhanced video spatial reasoning, including a **5.2%** gain in route planning on VSI-Bench and a **4%** average improvement on out-of-domain outdoor videos from MME-RealWorld-Lite.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perfectly annotated synthetic data from 3D simulators proves more effective for imparting spatial reasoning to MLMs than noisy pseudo-annotated real-image data.
> - Training with dynamic spatial tasks facilitates robust sim-to-real transfer, enabling models to apply learned spatial intelligence to real-world images and out-of-domain videos.
> - Procedural generation of spatial QA data offers a scalable and cost-effective approach to overcome the limitations of manual annotation for complex spatial reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of large multimodal language models (MLMs) has positioned them as general-purpose intelligent agents, capable of processing and reasoning across various data modalities. However, a consistent challenge highlighted in recent literature is their struggle with spatial reasoning, particularly when it comes to understanding the relationships between objects in images and videos. Existing research has primarily focused on "static" spatial relationships (e.g., "is the cup on the table?"), often pointing to the scarcity of spatial annotations in training data as a key limitation. Solutions proposed to address this for static scenarios include generating pseudo-annotations on real images, which, while helpful, require extensive engineering and can be noisy.

This paper, "SAT: Dynamic Spatial Aptitude Training for Multimodal Language Models," positions itself at the forefront of extending this research into the more complex and less explored domain of "dynamic" spatial reasoning. Dynamic spatial reasoning involves understanding how spatial relationships change due to ego-motion (camera movement) or object movement, and predicting the consequences of actions. This capability is paramount for real-world applications such as embodied AI (e.g., robots navigating and interacting in dynamic environments), smart glasses, and autonomous systems, where agents must continuously update their spatial understanding based on motion and interaction.

The authors draw inspiration from cognitive science, emphasizing that dynamic spatial reasoning is a fundamental human cognitive capability, crucial for aptitude in fields like geometry and physics. They argue that manually annotating complex 3D movements and their consequences in real images is prohibitively expensive and difficult to scale. Therefore, the work explores a novel approach: leveraging 3D simulators to procedurally generate vast amounts of perfectly annotated, high-quality training data for both static and dynamic spatial tasks. This contrasts with previous efforts that largely rely on pseudo-annotations or limited human annotations on real images, which often suffer from noise and lack the compositional and interactive control offered by simulations.

By introducing the Spatial Aptitude Training (SAT) dataset and methodology, the paper contributes to the broader landscape by:
- **Shifting focus to dynamic spatial reasoning:** It explicitly addresses a critical gap in current MLM capabilities and benchmarks, moving beyond static relationships.
- **Championing synthetic data for complex tasks:** It demonstrates the surprising effectiveness of simulated data for imparting spatial intelligence that transfers to real-world scenarios, challenging conventional wisdom regarding the sim-to-real gap, particularly for abstract reasoning.
- **Providing a scalable solution:** Procedural generation in 3D simulators offers an arbitrarily scalable method for creating diverse and complex spatial QA data, overcoming annotation bottlenecks.
- **Developing diagnostic tools:** The introduced real-image dynamic test set and comprehensive evaluations across various benchmarks serve as crucial tools for assessing and improving MLMs' spatial intelligence.

The paper extensively references existing 2D and 3D vision-language datasets, spatial relation datasets (e.g., GQA, VG, Omni3D, ScanQA, VSR, 2.5VRD, CVBench, BLINK), and works on synthetic training data and vision-language models. It explicitly highlights how SAT surpasses these by offering dynamic spatial QA, generation of new scenes/tasks, and object interaction, which are largely absent or limited in previous efforts (as summarized in their Table 1). This work is thus a significant step towards enabling MLMs to function more effectively in dynamic, interactive real-world environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **significantly improve the spatial reasoning capabilities of multimodal language models (MLMs)**, specifically extending their understanding beyond static object relationships to encompass dynamic awareness of motion and space. The authors aim to enable MLMs to reason about the effects of egocentric (camera) and object motions on spatial relationships, a fundamental cognitive capability essential for advanced AI applications.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several critical observations:

1. **Limitations of Current MLMs:** Despite their impressive progress as general-purpose intelligent agents, recent studies consistently show that MLMs struggle with even basic static spatial reasoning tasks, such as predicting relative object positions. This deficiency is often attributed to the scarcity of detailed spatial annotations in their vast training datasets.
2. **Need for Dynamic Reasoning in Real-World Applications:** Many emerging real-world applications, particularly in embodied AI (e.g., robotic navigation, manipulation) and augmented reality (e.g., smart glasses), demand advanced dynamic spatial reasoning. Agents in these scenarios must understand how actions (their own movements or object movements) alter spatial configurations and relationships. For example, a robot needs to know if turning left will bring it closer to a target object, or if moving an object will obstruct a path.
3. **Cost and Complexity of Annotation:** Manually annotating the complex 3D movements of objects and cameras, along with their causal effects on spatial relationships, in real-world images or videos is an incredibly expensive, time-consuming, and difficult task. Existing methods for generating pseudo-annotations for static relationships are already complex and not easily transferable to dynamic scenarios, where predicting action causality from real images is non-trivial.
4. **Inspiration from Cognitive Science:** The paper is motivated by insights from human cognitive development, which highlight that dynamic spatial reasoning capabilities (like understanding ego-motion, object tracking, and perspective-taking) are crucial for human children and correlate with aptitude in subjects like geometry and physics. This suggests that instilling such capabilities in AI could lead to more robust and human-like intelligence.
5. **Unexplored Potential of Simulation Data:** The authors hypothesize that simulation data, with its perfect 3D information and controllable environments, offers a simple yet powerful solution to impart both static and dynamic spatial reasoning to MLMs, bypassing the limitations of real-world data annotation.

To address these motivations, the research sets forth several specific objectives:
- **Develop SAT (Spatial Aptitude Training):** A framework for generating a large-scale, high-quality, and perfectly annotated spatial question-answer (QA) dataset using 3D simulators. This dataset should include both static and, crucially, dynamic spatial reasoning tasks.
- **Create a Real-World Dynamic Spatial Benchmark:** Construct a challenging, human-annotated test set using real-world images to rigorously evaluate the sim-to-real transfer capabilities of models trained with SAT.
- **Systematically Evaluate Training Data Efficacy:** Investigate how different types and sources of spatial training data (synthetic static, synthetic dynamic, pseudo-annotated real, human-annotated real) influence MLMs' performance on both static and dynamic spatial reasoning tasks across various benchmarks.
- **Demonstrate Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Prove that spatial intelligence learned from simulated environments can effectively generalize and improve performance on real-world images and videos.
- **Maintain General Capabilities:** Ensure that the specialized spatial training does not lead to catastrophic forgetting or degradation of other pre-trained vision-language commonsense reasoning abilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology revolves around the creation and utilization of the **Spatial Aptitude Training (SAT)** dataset, a procedurally generated collection of spatial question-answer (QA) pairs designed to impart advanced spatial reasoning capabilities to Multimodal Language Models (MLMs).

#### 3.1. SAT Data Generation Pipeline

The SAT dataset is generated entirely without human supervision, leveraging 3D simulators. Specifically, the authors use 22,000 procedurally generated scenes from the ProcTHOR-10K dataset, composed of 1,000 assets, to create 175,000 QA pairs. The use of a simulator provides perfect 3D information and precise control over camera and object movements, which is crucial for generating complex dynamic questions.

- **Static Spatial QAs (127,000 pairs):** These questions focus on relative relationships between objects within a single static scene, aligning with existing benchmarks.
 - **Relative Spatial Relations:** Questions like "Is object X to the left/right/above/below object Y?" or "Which object (A or B) is closer to object C?". These are determined by projecting 3D object poses into camera coordinates and analyzing their relative positions.
 - **Relative Depth:** Questions like "Is object X closer to the camera than object Y?", calculated directly from 3D distances in the simulator.
 - **Count:** Questions about the number of object instances, derived from simulator metadata.

- **Dynamic Spatial QAs (48,000 pairs):** Inspired by human cognitive spatial tests, these tasks require reasoning about changes induced by motion and perspective, involving two frames (before and after an action).
 - **Egocentric Movement (EgoM):** Simulating a "moving room test," the camera rotates or moves (left/right, forward). Questions ask, "How did the camera taking the video likely rotate/move?", with answers being the actual action sequence.
 - **Object Movement (ObjM):** An object is randomly moved within the scene. Questions ask, "Did any of the objects move from the first frame to the second frame?", with the answer describing the object's movement (e.g., "Toaster moved left and away from camera"). The model must disentangle object movement from potential camera movement.
 - **Allocentric Perspective (Pers):** The agent "teleports" to a different 3D location in the scene and adopts a new viewpoint. Questions ask, "For someone at the mark ‘X’ facing left/right by 90 degrees, would the <object> be to their left or right?", requiring perspective-taking.
 - **Goal Aiming (Aim):** Given a static scene, the agent needs to identify the direction to turn to face a specific object. Questions like "I need to go to the countertop. Which direction should I turn to face it?" are posed, with answers framed as rough angular turns (left/right).
 - **Action Consequence (EgoAct):** The agent is shown a first frame and asked to predict the spatial consequences of a hypothetical action (e.g., "If I rotate left by 90 degrees and move forward, would I move further from the sofa?").

#### 3.2. Real-Image Dynamic Spatial Test Set Construction

Recognizing the absence of real-world benchmarks for dynamic spatial reasoning, the authors meticulously construct a small (150 image-QA pairs) but challenging test set using real images. This set covers the five dynamic reasoning types (EgoM, ObjM, Pers, Aim, EgoAct) and is expertly human-annotated to ensure high quality and accuracy for evaluating sim-to-real transfer. A larger synthetic test set (over 4,000 QAs on 805 images) is also generated for detailed diagnostic analysis of individual dynamic splits.

#### 3.3. MLM Finetuning with SAT

- **Base Models:** Two popular open-source MLMs are used: LLaVA-1.5-13B (an image-based VLM) and LLaVA-Video-7B (a video-based VLM).
- **Instruction Tuning:** The models are instruction-tuned using the generated SAT data. For each QA pair, the MLM is presented with the image(s) and question, and asked to choose between a correct answer and a distractor answer (e.g., "left" vs. "right").
- **Preventing Catastrophic Forgetting:** To ensure the models retain their general vision-language capabilities, a portion (40%) of the original LLaVA Instruct-tune dataset is mixed in during training.
- **Training Details:** LLaVA-1.5-13B is LoRA-tuned (rank 256, alpha 512), while LLaVA-Video-7B is full fine-tuned. Training is performed for approximately one epoch of synthetic data, using a batch size of 8 and a learning rate of 5e-6.
- **Prompting Strategy:** A specific chat-based prompt format is used, and answer options are provided explicitly in natural language (e.g., "Choose between left or right") rather than as abstract options (e.g., A or B), which was found to improve performance.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Benchmarks

The evaluation strategy is comprehensive, covering a wide range of existing and newly introduced benchmarks:
- **Static Spatial Reasoning:** CVBench, BLINK (spatial subsets), Visual Spatial Relations (VSR), and GQA-Spatial.
- **Dynamic Spatial Reasoning:** The custom-built SAT real-image dynamic test set and the larger SAT synthetic test set.
- **Video Spatial Reasoning:** VSI-Bench (for long video spatial reasoning, including route planning) and MME-RealWorld-Lite (for out-of-domain outdoor videos with humans).
- **General VQA:** GQA, OK-VQA, and VQAv2, to assess retention of non-spatial commonsense reasoning.

This multi-faceted approach allows for a thorough investigation of SAT's effectiveness, sim-to-real transfer capabilities, and impact on various aspects of MLM performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments reveal several key findings regarding the spatial reasoning capabilities of MLMs and the effectiveness of Spatial Aptitude Training (SAT):

1. **MLMs Critically Struggle with Spatial Reasoning, Especially Dynamic Tasks:**
 - Both proprietary (GPT4-V/O, Gemini 1.5-flash/pro) and open-source (LLaVA-1.5-13B, LLaVA-Video-7B) MLMs show significant weaknesses in spatial reasoning. While proprietary models generally perform better on static tasks, all models struggle considerably with dynamic spatial questions on the custom SAT real-image test set. For instance, LLaVA-1.5-13B scores 41.6% on SAT Real, and even strong models like Gemini 1.5-pro achieve only 64.8%, barely above random chance for some sub-categories (e.g., GPT4-V at 32.7% on ObjM). This highlights a critical gap in current MLM capabilities.

2. **SAT Training Significantly Improves Static Spatial Reasoning on Real Images:**
 - Tuning with SAT data leads to substantial improvements in performance on existing static spatial benchmarks for both LLaVA models. LLaVA-1.5-13B sees an average absolute improvement of 23.9% on CVBench and BLINK, achieving 75.6% accuracy. Similarly, LLaVA-Video-7B improves by 13.2% to 78.4%. These SAT-tuned open-source models often match or even outperform larger, closed-source models and spatially-tuned baselines like Robopoint-13B on these static benchmarks, demonstrating the high quality and effectiveness of the simulated training data.

3. **SAT Training Demonstrates Strong Sim-to-Real Transfer for Dynamic Spatial Reasoning:**
 - Despite being trained on simulated data, SAT significantly boosts dynamic spatial reasoning performance on the challenging real-image SAT test set. LLaVA-1.5-13B improves by 13.3% to 54.9%, and LLaVA-Video-7B improves by 9.9% to 63.4%. This strong sim-to-real transfer is a crucial validation of the SAT approach, showing that skills learned in simulation can generalize effectively to complex real-world scenarios.

4. **SAT Training Enhances Spatial Reasoning in Videos, Including Out-of-Domain Scenarios:**
 - Even though SAT training data primarily consists of image pairs (up to 2 frames), it positively impacts spatial reasoning in longer videos. LLaVA-Video-7B, when trained with SAT, shows improvements on the VSI-Bench, notably a 5.2% increase in route planning capabilities and an 8.3% increase in action sequence ordering.
 - Surprisingly, SAT training also yields improvements on out-of-domain outdoor videos containing humans (MME-RealWorld-Lite), despite the SAT dataset being rendered using indoor simulators without humans. LLaVA-Video-7B sees an average 4% improvement on MME-RealWorld-Lite, with specific gains in Motion (+6.4%), Interaction (+7%), and Position Reasoning (+8%). This suggests that the fundamental spatial principles learned in simulation are robust and generalize across diverse visual domains.

5. **Simulated Perfect Annotations Outperform Noisy Real-Image Pseudo-Annotations:**
 - A comparative analysis reveals that high-quality, perfectly annotated simulated SAT data is more effective than pseudo-annotated real-image data or existing human-annotated spatial datasets (like VSR/2.5VRD) for improving spatial reasoning. While pseudo-annotations show some gains, their inherent noise (e.g., from bounding box inaccuracies or depth estimation errors) limits their effectiveness, especially for 3D perception. SAT's ability to provide flawless 3D information directly from the simulator proves superior.

6. **Dynamic QAs Further Improve Performance Over Static-Only Training:**
 - Adding dynamic spatial questions to the training regimen consistently leads to further improvements in both dynamic and static spatial reasoning performance, especially as the data scales. This indicates that dynamic tasks provide richer, more generalized spatial understanding than static relationships alone.
 - However, certain complex tasks, like abstract spatial relationships (e.g., "looking away," "surrounding" in BLINK SpRel) and subtle camera movements (BLINK MV), remain challenging, suggesting areas for future refinement.

7. **Preservation of Non-Spatial Reasoning Capabilities:**
 - Evaluation on standard VQA benchmarks (GQA, OK-VQA, VQAv2) shows that SAT-tuned models maintain their general vision-language commonsense reasoning abilities. This indicates that the specialized spatial training does not cause catastrophic forgetting of existing knowledge, making SAT a viable and beneficial approach for enhancing MLMs.

In summary, the results strongly support the efficacy of simulation-generated, perfectly annotated spatial training data, particularly for dynamic reasoning. SAT training equips MLMs with enhanced spatial intelligence that transfers well to real-world images and videos, often surpassing baseline and proprietary models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "SAT: Dynamic Spatial Aptitude Training for Multimodal Language Models" carries significant implications for the field of Multimodal AI, particularly in bridging the gap between passive perception and active, embodied intelligence.

1. **Challenging Conventional Wisdom on Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The most striking significance lies in the demonstration that "perfect annotations in simulation are more effective than existing approaches of pseudo-annotating real images." This finding challenges a common assumption that real-world data, even if noisy, is inherently superior for training models to operate in real environments. By proving strong sim-to-real transfer for complex cognitive tasks like dynamic spatial reasoning, this work opens new avenues for leveraging high-fidelity simulators to develop advanced AI capabilities that are otherwise intractable to acquire through manual annotation.

2. **Enabling Advanced Embodied AI and Real-World Applications:** The ability of MLMs to reason about motion, egocentric perspective, and action consequences is absolutely critical for the next generation of embodied AI systems (e.g., robots, autonomous vehicles) and pervasive computing devices (e.g., smart glasses). Robots need to plan routes, understand the impact of their movements, and manipulate objects based on dynamic spatial changes. Smart glasses could offer context-aware assistance by understanding the user's perspective and potential interactions. SAT directly addresses these needs by providing a mechanism to instill these crucial skills, promising more robust, intelligent, and deployable AI agents. The observed gains in route planning accuracy on video benchmarks directly support this potential.

3. **Scalability and Cost-Effectiveness of Data Generation:** Manual annotation of dynamic 3D spatial relationships is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. SAT's procedural generation pipeline within 3D simulators provides an arbitrarily scalable and cost-effective solution for producing vast quantities of high-quality training data. This removes a significant bottleneck in the development of spatially intelligent MLMs, allowing researchers to explore more complex reasoning tasks without being limited by data availability.

4. **Advancing Cognitive AI Research:** By grounding its dynamic spatial tasks in human cognitive development and tests (e.g., "moving room test," perspective-taking), the research offers a computational framework for exploring fundamental aspects of spatial intelligence in AI. The finding that models can learn and transfer these cognitive skills from simulation to reality provides valuable insights into how such intelligence might be acquired and represented in artificial systems. It also highlights areas where MLMs still struggle, such as abstract spatial relationships and subtle camera movements, pointing towards future research directions inspired by human cognition.

5. **Diagnostic Tool for Spatial Understanding:** The introduction of the SAT real-image dynamic test set and the comprehensive synthetic test set serve as vital diagnostic tools for the AI community. These benchmarks allow for a more granular and rigorous evaluation of MLMs' spatial aptitude, moving beyond generic VQA or static relationship questions. Researchers can now systematically probe models for specific types of spatial reasoning, fostering targeted improvements.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays a strong foundation for several exciting future research directions. These include:
 - **Embodied Navigation and Manipulation:** Direct evaluation of SAT-trained models in embodied environments for tasks like object navigation and manipulation, leveraging the improved action prediction accuracy.
 - **Dynamic and Causal Reasoning:** Further exploration of the interactive nature of simulated scenes to facilitate more advanced dynamic and chain-of-thought causal reasoning, potentially leading to more sophisticated world models.
 - **Language-Controlled World Models:** Using causal annotations in language and corresponding image frames to advance the development of language-controlled world models, where MLMs can predict and simulate the effects of actions in a virtual environment.

In conclusion, SAT represents a significant methodological contribution, offering a scalable and effective strategy to equip MLMs with essential dynamic spatial reasoning capabilities. This work is pivotal for the development of more capable and context-aware AI systems, marking a crucial step towards bridging the gap between current passive AI perception and the demands of active, intelligent agents operating in dynamic real-world environments.
