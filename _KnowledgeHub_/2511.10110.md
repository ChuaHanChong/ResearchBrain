# Learning a Thousand Tasks in a Day

> [!summary] Summary
> MT3 (Multi-Task Trajectory Transfer) is a retrieval-based imitation learning method that splits every manipulation trajectory into an alignment phase, solved by pose estimation plus motion planning, and an interaction phase, solved by replaying a demonstration retrieved via language and point-cloud geometry matching. Learning from one demonstration per task, it taught a robot 1,000 everyday tasks in under 24 hours at 78.25% success.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art behavioural cloning needs 175-250 demonstrations per task, and bimanual systems roughly 8,000, making scaling to thousands of real-world tasks prohibitively expensive.
- Prior alignment-interaction decomposition work studied single tasks with one fixed policy pairing, never systematically comparing pose estimation against learned alignment across many tasks.
- Existing retrieval methods retrieve data before policy training rather than at test time, and prior nearest-neighbour imitation matches RGB frames step-by-step instead of whole trajectories.

## Method

- ==Trajectory decomposition== into an alignment phase (end-effector positioning) and an interaction phase (precise manipulation), instantiated as four policy combinations pairing behavioural cloning or retrieval in each phase.
- ==Hierarchical retrieval==: template-based language filtering selects the micro skill, then a ==PointNet++== occupancy-trained encoder picks the demonstration whose object geometry and pose best match by cosine similarity.
- **MT3** executes the retrieved demonstration via ==Trajectory Transfer== pose mapping refined with generalised ICP for alignment, then open-loop replay of end-effector velocities in the end-effector frame.

## Results

- Across 3,450 real-world rollouts, **MT3** with three demonstrations per task beat every alternative given 50 demonstrations per task, on both seen and unseen tasks.
- Decomposition beat the monolithic behavioural cloning baseline across all data regimes, largest gap under scarce data, while the monolithic policy scaled faster from 10 to 50 demonstrations.
- In 2,200 rollouts over 1,000 single-demonstration tasks with 402 objects and 17 hours of collection, **MT3** reached **78.25%** seen and **68%** unseen success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structural priors buy data efficiency but cap capacity: analytic decomposition wins under scarce data, while monolithic policies keep improving and eventually overtake as data grows.
> - Test-time retrieval cannot interpolate between demonstrations, so it fails when the needed trajectory lies between demonstrated ones or depends on unseen dynamics like deformability.
> - Global-geometry matching and pose estimation miss small task-critical features, leaving open-loop replay vulnerable on millimetre-precision tasks with no mid-execution correction available.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper directly addresses a fundamental limitation in contemporary robot manipulation: the immense data requirements of state-of-the-art imitation learning (IL) systems. Humans and animals demonstrate remarkable learning efficiency, often acquiring new manipulation skills from just a few demonstrations. In stark contrast, modern robotic IL systems, particularly those based on Behavioural Cloning (BC) with large neural networks, typically necessitate hundreds or thousands of demonstrations per task.

The authors highlight this inefficiency by citing prominent examples:
- **BC-Z:** Required approximately 26,000 demonstrations for 100 tasks (~260 demonstrations/task).
- **Robotics Transformer 1 (RT-1):** Utilized around 130,000 demonstrations across 744 tasks (~175 demonstrations/task).
- **Multi-Task Action-Chunking Transformers (MT-ACT):** Collected 7,500 demonstrations for 38 tasks (~197 demonstrations/task).
- **ALOHA Unleashed (bimanual):** Suggested the need for ~8,000 demonstrations *per task*.

Such extensive data collection demands enormous financial and human resources, making scaling to thousands of tasks impractical. This paper positions itself as a critical intervention by investigating two fundamental priors aimed at drastically improving learning efficiency in the "few-demonstrations-per-task" regime:

1. **Trajectory Decomposition:** Unlike monolithic policies that learn entire manipulation trajectories with a single neural network, this work proposes decomposing trajectories into a sequential "alignment" phase (positioning the robot's end-effector) and an "interaction" phase (performing the precise object manipulation). While previous work has explored decomposition for single tasks, this paper systematically compares various design choices for these phases across multiple tasks.
2. **Retrieval-based Generalization:** As an alternative to BC, which embeds behaviors into network weights during training, this approach leverages stored demonstrations at test time. This differs from other recent retrieval methods (e.g., FlowRetrieval, SAILOR, Behavior Retrieval) that primarily retrieve data *before* policy training. The authors' method retrieves *complete demonstrations* based on language and geometry *prior* to task execution, providing context to guide the robot's actions. This builds upon and extends prior work on `Trajectory Transfer`.

By focusing on these priors and systematically evaluating their impact, the paper aims to bridge the gap between biological and robotic learning efficiency, offering a scalable solution for robot autonomy that minimizes data collection overhead.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the significant disparity between human and robotic learning efficiency in manipulation tasks. While humans can readily acquire new skills from minimal demonstrations, robots currently demand vast datasets, making broad task acquisition prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. The overarching goal is to enable robots to learn a large repertoire of manipulation tasks with significantly less data, mimicking human-like learning capabilities.

To achieve this, the authors set forth several key objectives:
1. **Systematic Evaluation of Priors:** To comprehensively analyze the impact of trajectory decomposition and retrieval-based generalization on learning efficiency. This involves comparing different design choices for the alignment and interaction phases (e.g., pose estimation vs. Behavioral Cloning for alignment; open-loop replay vs. Behavioral Cloning for interaction).
2. **Addressing the Few-Demonstrations-Per-Task Gap:** To specifically investigate and improve performance in scenarios where only a limited number of demonstrations (e.g., fewer than 10) are available per task, a regime largely under-explored in current literature.
3. **Development of Multi-Task Trajectory Transfer (MT3):** To synthesize the insights gained from the systematic evaluations into a novel imitation learning method that leverages retrieval for both alignment and interaction, aiming for learning from as little as a single demonstration per task.
4. **Large-Scale Validation:** To challenge the practical boundaries of their proposed method by teaching a robot 1,000 distinct everyday manipulation tasks from single demonstrations within a compressed timeframe (under 24 hours of human demonstrator time). This objective aims to demonstrate the scalability and understand the capabilities and limitations of such data-efficient approaches in diverse real-world settings.

Ultimately, the motivation is to reduce the data requirements for creating highly capable and general robotic systems, paving the way for more adaptable and autonomous robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research revolves around two fundamental priors: **trajectory decomposition** and **retrieval-based generalization**. The authors systematically evaluate various combinations of these priors against a monolithic baseline.

**Trajectory Decomposition:**
Manipulation trajectories are divided into two distinct sequential phases:
1. **Alignment Phase:** The robot's end-effector (or grasped object) is moved to a suitable pose relative to the target object. The exact path taken is less critical than achieving the correct final position.
2. **Interaction Phase:** The actual object manipulation is performed, requiring precise trajectory execution.

**Policy Designs:**
The study compares five distinct policy designs:
- **Monolithic Behavioural Cloning (MT-ACT+):** This serves as a baseline, using a single transformer-based neural network (adapted from MT-ACT) trained to learn entire manipulation trajectories without decomposition.
- **Decomposition-Based Methods (Four Combinations):** These methods deploy two specialized policies (one for alignment, one for interaction), with each phase potentially using either Behavioral Cloning (BC) or a retrieval-based approach.
 - **BC-BC:** BC for alignment, BC for interaction.
 - **BC-Ret:** BC for alignment, Retrieval for interaction.
 - **Ret-BC:** Retrieval for alignment, BC for interaction.
 - **Ret-Ret (MT3):** Retrieval for alignment, Retrieval for interaction. This method is the central focus of the paper.

**Retrieval-based Policies (for Alignment and Interaction):**
These policies utilize demonstrations at test time as context, rather than learning behaviors into network weights.
- **Hierarchical Retrieval:** Occurs once prior to task execution.
 1. **Language-based Filtering:** Extracts the "micro skill" (e.g., "open bottle") from the task description using template matching (with potential for more sophisticated LLM-based approaches, as explored in Appendix F). This filters the demonstration dataset to only those relevant to the specific skill.
 2. **Geometry-based Matching:** A PointNet++ based encoder extracts geometry embeddings from the segmented target object point cloud. The system then selects the demonstration with the highest cosine similarity to the test object's embedding, considering both object shape and pose similarity.
- **Retrieval-based Alignment:** After retrieval, `Trajectory Transfer` is used. This method employs geometric reasoning to map the demonstrated initial end-effector pose (relative to the target object) to the test scene. This mapping is refined using Generalised ICP, and the robot then reaches this inferred pose via motion planning.
- **Retrieval-based Interaction (Open-Loop Replay):** The retrieved trajectory is replicated in the test scene by executing the demonstrated end-effector velocities directly in the end-effector frame. This is an open-loop approach, meaning no online adaptation occurs during execution.

**Behavioural Cloning Policies (for Alignment, Interaction, and Monolithic Baseline):**
- **Network Architecture:** A transformer-based backbone, adapted from MT-ACT, is used. It processes segmented point clouds (via PointNet++) and language descriptions (CLIP embeddings conditioned with FiLM). Variational inference is incorporated to model the multi-modal nature of actions. The architecture also includes action history as input and a terminal action output.
- **Training Data:**
 - **Interaction BC:** Trained on recorded interaction phases of demonstrations.
 - **Alignment BC:** Trained on *synthetically generated* linear trajectories. For each demonstration, 1,000 alignment trajectories are simulated, starting from diverse poses and ending at the demonstrated initial interaction pose. These synthetic trajectories use the same target object point cloud geometry but vary end-effector poses and corresponding actions.
 - **Monolithic BC (MT-ACT+):** Combines simulated alignment trajectories with real interaction trajectories to form complete manipulation sequences.
- **Loss Function and Augmentations:** All BC policies use a VAE objective (reconstruction loss + Gaussian prior regularization) with learned weighting. Data augmentation includes random masking of point clouds and Gaussian noise. Interaction BC also perturbs end-effector poses to improve robustness to covariate shift.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Hardware:** Sawyer robot with a 2F-85 Robotiq gripper. Perception is provided by a single RealSense D415 RGB-D camera mounted on the robot's head.
- **Inputs:** Segmented point cloud of the target object and a natural language description of the task.
- **Task Definition:**
 - **Macro Skill:** Broad manipulation primitive (e.g., "open", "insert").
 - **Micro Skill:** Macro skill specialized for an object category with a distinct motion profile (e.g., "open oven door sideways").
 - **Task:** Micro skill executed on a specific object instance (e.g., "unzip the round pink handbag").
- **Evaluation:** Conducted on both "seen tasks" (training objects) and "unseen tasks" (novel object instances within known categories) to assess generalization.

**Experimental Design:**
1. **Controlled Experiments (3,450 real-world rollouts):**
 - **Scaling Demonstrations per Task:** Evaluated methods by varying demonstrations from 1 to 50 per task, across 4 diverse micro skills (12 seen, 8 unseen tasks).
 - **Scaling Tasks (Fixed Total Demonstrations):** Held total demonstrations at 150, distributing them across 10 (15 demos/task), 30 (5 demos/task), and 50 tasks (3 demos/task) across 10 micro skills.
2. **Large-Scale Evaluation (2,200 additional real-world rollouts):** Taught a robot 1,000 distinct everyday tasks (31 macro skills, 534 micro skills, 402 different objects) using a *single demonstration each*. This included 100 unseen tasks. Experiments were conducted under challenging real-world conditions (distractors, varied lighting, randomized object placement).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness of trajectory decomposition and retrieval-based generalization across various data regimes and task complexities.

**Controlled Experiments (Analyzing Data Efficiency and Generalization):**
- **Performance Hierarchy:** Multi-Task Trajectory Transfer (MT3), the fully retrieval-based decomposition method (Ret-Ret), consistently demonstrated superior performance across all data regimes tested. Remarkably, MT3 with just three demonstrations per task outperformed all other methods, even when those methods were provided with 50 demonstrations per task, on both seen and unseen tasks. This highlights MT3's exceptional data efficiency and generalization to novel object instances.
- **Benefits of Decomposition:** Decomposition-based methods (Ret-BC, BC-Ret, BC-BC) generally outperformed the monolithic BC baseline (MT-ACT+). This advantage was particularly pronounced in the low-data regime (<10 demonstrations per task), where decomposition-based approaches achieved an *order of magnitude improvement* in data efficiency.
- **Retrieval vs. Behavioral Cloning within Decomposition:** Within the decomposition framework, methods utilizing retrieval for either alignment or interaction consistently achieved higher average success rates than those using BC for the respective phases.
- **Scaling Dataset Size (Demonstrations per Task):** All methods showed improved performance with more demonstrations. Decomposition-based methods exhibited rapid performance gains with 1-10 demonstrations but tended to plateau around 50. In contrast, the monolithic BC baseline (MT-ACT+) accelerated its improvement in the 10-50 demonstration range, narrowing the performance gap. This suggests that while decomposition excels with limited data due to its explicit structure, monolithic BC benefits more from abundant data to learn task structure from scratch.
- **Scaling Dataset Diversity (Number of Tasks/Object Instances):**
 - **Retrieval-based methods:** Performance on *unseen tasks* improved with increased diversity, as more object instances provided better matches for retrieval. However, performance on *seen tasks* paradoxically degraded due to an inherent trade-off in geometry-based retrieval, which had to balance object pose similarity (for trajectory transfer) and object geometry similarity (for trajectory suitability).
 - **Monolithic BC (MT-ACT+):** Benefitted significantly from increased task diversity for both seen and unseen tasks. It learned more general latent representations by identifying patterns across diverse object instances, effectively trading diversity for data efficiency (e.g., 50 tasks with 150 total demonstrations achieved performance comparable to 12 tasks with 600 total demonstrations).
 - **Decomposition with BC (BC-BC):** Did not exhibit the same diversity benefits as monolithic BC, despite using identical BC implementations. This was hypothesized to occur because decomposition disrupts the synergistic learning of monolithic BC by separating training on distinct data distributions for alignment (synthetic linear trajectories) and interaction (sparse but diverse real demonstrations).

**Large-Scale Evaluation (MT3 across 1,000 Tasks):**
- **Overall Performance:** MT3 demonstrated its ability to scale to a large number of diverse real-world tasks. It achieved an average success rate of 78.25% on seen tasks and 68% on unseen tasks across 1,000 distinct manipulation tasks, each learned from a single demonstration collected in under 24 hours.
- **Spatial Generalization:** MT3 excelled at tasks allowing small deviations (e.g., wiping, stirring, placing, grasping), achieving success rates exceeding 80%. However, it struggled with high-precision tasks or those where small geometric features (e.g., a kettle spout for grasping) were critical but often misregistered by the global-geometry-focused pose estimator, leading to orientation errors and task failure.
- **Category-Level Generalization:** Success was observed when interaction trajectories remained consistent across object instances within a category (e.g., grasping different mugs). Failures occurred when geometry changes demanded significant alterations in the required interaction trajectory, as retrieval cannot interpolate between behaviors. Tasks involving deformable objects (e.g., inserting a book into a backpack) were particularly challenging due to unobservable dynamic properties affecting manipulation success.
- **Limitations of Open-Loop Interaction:** The large-scale evaluation exposed fundamental limitations of open-loop trajectory replay. It lacks mechanisms for online error detection or adaptation during execution, making it unsuitable for tasks requiring reactive control or continuous adjustments (e.g., folding fabric, reorienting objects through contact).
- **Systematic Failure Analysis:** An expert evaluation of failures on seen tasks revealed common culprits:
 - **Retrieval (22.3%):** Failures often due to partially occluded objects or when relevant geometric variations involved small parts not reliably identified by global matching.
 - **Pose Estimation (23.9%):** Challenges with dramatic pose changes or object asymmetries creating substantially different partial point clouds.
 - **Segmentation (19.5%):** Issues with transparent objects or cluttered scenes.
 - **Grasped Objects (20.2%):** Inconsistent grasp poses between demonstration and deployment led to error propagation throughout open-loop execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of data efficiency for large-scale robot manipulation. The authors make three key contributions:
1. **Systematic Evaluation in Low-Data Regimes:** They provide the first comprehensive, real-world evaluation of multi-task imitation learning in the few-demonstration-per-task regime, filling a crucial gap in the literature dominated by data-hungry methods.
2. **Introduction of Multi-Task Trajectory Transfer (MT3):** They develop and rigorously test MT3, a novel retrieval-based decomposition method that demonstrates superior data efficiency compared to monolithic Behavioral Cloning, especially when demonstration data is limited.
3. **Large-Scale Validation:** The unprecedented feat of teaching a robot 1,000 distinct everyday tasks from just a single demonstration each in under 24 hours is a powerful demonstration. This directly challenges the prevailing assumption that complex, end-to-end neural policies are strictly necessary for large-scale robot learning.

The potential impact of this work is substantial:
- **Democratization of Robot Skills:** By drastically reducing the data and time required to teach new tasks, MT3 could democratize the deployment of robots in diverse settings, from manufacturing to homes, by making skill acquisition far more accessible to non-experts.
- **Increased Adaptability and Generality:** Robots can more quickly adapt to new objects and environments, allowing for wider task generalization and robust performance with novel instances.
- **Practical Advantages over Behavioral Cloning:** MT3 offers inherent interpretability (visualizing pose estimation, tracing retrieved trajectories) and streamlined task acquisition (simply append new demonstrations without retraining or fine-tuning complex models). This provides a practical and transparent alternative to opaque neural network policies.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The systematic analysis of strengths and failure modes provides valuable insights for future work, directing efforts towards improving pose estimation for fine features, handling deformable objects (e.g., with tactile feedback), and integrating more sophisticated perception and control mechanisms with the efficient framework of decomposition and retrieval. The exploration of Large Language Models (LLMs) for hierarchical retrieval and task chaining (as discussed in Appendix F) also points to a promising direction for enhancing natural language interaction and long-horizon planning.

While MT3 excels in data-scarce environments, the research also clarifies the trade-offs: monolithic BC, though initially inefficient, can surpass decomposition-based methods with abundant and diverse data due to its unconstrained learning capacity. Therefore, the choice of approach depends on application constraints and available data resources. Overall, "Learning a Thousand Tasks in a Day" marks a significant step towards truly adaptable and efficient robotic systems, shifting the paradigm from data-intensive training to intelligent leveraging of limited demonstrations.
