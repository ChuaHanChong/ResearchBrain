# Time-to-Collision Based Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance Using Pretrained Vision Models for Robots in Unstructured Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A data-efficient and interpretable method for dynamic obstacle avoidance in robots integrates pretrained monocular depth estimation and feature tracking models with geometric time-to-collision (TTC) calculations. The system achieved 0.49 precision and 0.38 recall in detecting imminent collisions on real-world datasets, generating appropriate evasive actions for 84% of true positive detections.

## Problem

- Autonomous mobile robots lack robust, data-efficient, and interpretable dynamic obstacle avoidance capabilities in complex, unstructured outdoor environments.
- Current learning-based methods demand extensive robot-specific training data or suffer from the "sim-to-real" gap, hindering scalability and real-world deployment.
- Geometric Time-to-Collision (TTC) estimation methods are interpretable but often sensitive to noise and camera motion, while learning-based TTC lacks transparent decision-making.

## Method

- A modular, vision-based pipeline integrates pretrained vision foundation models for monocular depth estimation (==UniDepth==) and robust feature detection/tracking (==SuperPoint==, ==SuperGlue==).
- The pipeline reconstructs 3D keypoint trajectories and refines them using ==Scaled Bundle Adjustment (SBA)== with the XM solver over a sliding window.
- **Time-to-Collision (TTC)** is computed for optimized 3D keypoints, and a 2D motion primitive is generated to steer the robot away from the minimum-TTC obstacle.

## Results

- The pipeline achieved a precision of **0.49** and a recall of **0.38** for detecting frames with Time-to-Collision (TTC) less than 1 second on the M3ED test sequences.
- It successfully identified **20 out of 22** distinct physical obstacles, generating appropriate evasive motion primitives for **84%** of true positive collision detections.
- The method demonstrated high data efficiency, requiring no model training and only **74 seconds** of data for hyperparameter tuning, contrasting with data-intensive learning approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Composing large, pretrained vision foundation models enables robust dynamic obstacle avoidance without requiring extensive robot-specific training data, effectively bypassing the data bottleneck.
> - Integrating learning-based perception with explicit geometric reasoning provides a balance of generalization and interpretability, crucial for safety-critical autonomous navigation.
> - Monocular vision, enhanced by foundation models, offers a dense and sufficiently ranged perception modality for dynamic obstacle detection in unstructured environments, overcoming limitations of stereo depth and LiDAR.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous mobile robots capable of operating safely and reliably in dynamic, unstructured outdoor environments remains a significant challenge within robotics. A central aspect of this challenge is dynamic obstacle avoidance, which requires robust perception and rapid, appropriate response generation. This work addresses this domain by proposing a method that leverages recent advancements in vision foundation models to bypass common limitations in existing approaches.

Traditional approaches to robotic perception and control often involve either training large, robot-specific models from scratch or developing policies in simulation. Large transformer-based models, such as Robot Transformer and Visual Navigation Transformer (ViNT), have demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, but their training requires vast amounts of robot-specific data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect. This data scarcity presents a significant bottleneck for scalability and widespread adoption.

Alternatively, reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained in simulation offer a way to generate extensive training data without real-world collection constraints. However, simulation-trained policies typically suffer from a "sim-to-real" gap. This gap arises from discrepancies between simulated and real-world visual realism, contact dynamics, and environmental diversity, leading to performance degradation upon deployment to physical robots. While techniques like domain randomization and SimOpt attempt to mitigate this, simulation-trained policies are often black-box models with limited interpretability, making it difficult to predict or guarantee their behavior in unforeseen real-world scenarios.

Geometric approaches to time-to-collision (TTC) estimation provide an interpretable, scale-invariant metric for reactive navigation. These methods compute TTC based on explicit spatial and temporal measurements, such as the ratio of an object's visual scale to its expansion rate derived from optical flow. While offering deterministic interpretability, they can be sensitive to camera motion, computationally intensive, and often require multi-sensor fusion for robustness. Learning-based TTC methods, on the other hand, train neural networks to directly predict TTC maps, implicitly capturing scene semantics. These methods, however, are often limited by the diversity of their training datasets, can exhibit brittle generalization, and lack the transparent interpretability crucial for safety-critical applications.

The presented research positions itself at an intersection of these paradigms. It seeks to combine the generalization benefits of learning-based models with the explicit interpretability of geometric frameworks, while simultaneously overcoming the data efficiency and sim-to-real transfer problems inherent in many contemporary robotic learning approaches. By leveraging large, pretrained vision foundation models, this work aims to achieve robust and interpretable dynamic obstacle avoidance without the need for extensive robot-specific data collection or reliance on simulation-based training. Furthermore, the focus on monocular vision is presented as advantageous over stereo depth or LiDAR due to its superior range, density, and ability to provide a dense scene representation for detecting smaller or distant obstacles.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-efficient and interpretable method for vision-based dynamic obstacle avoidance for autonomous mobile robots operating in unstructured outdoor environments. This objective is motivated by several critical limitations in the existing robotics research landscape:

1. **Addressing the Data Bottleneck for Robot-Specific Training:** Many advanced robotic perception and control models, particularly those leveraging transformer architectures, require prohibitively large datasets of robot-specific training data. Collecting such datasets, often involving months of continuous operation across multiple robots, creates a significant barrier to scalability and deployment. The authors aim to circumvent this requirement by utilizing models already extensively trained on diverse, large-scale real-world data, thereby eliminating the need for new robot-specific training data.

2. **Mitigating the Sim-to-Real Transfer Problem:** A common strategy to overcome data scarcity is to train reinforcement learning policies in simulation. However, the discrepancies between simulated and real environments (e.g., visual realism, physics, environmental diversity) lead to a "sim-to-real" gap, where policies trained in simulation do not transfer effectively to the real world. This work is motivated by the desire to develop a system that operates entirely on real-world data, thereby inherently avoiding the challenges associated with bridging this gap.

3. **Enhancing Interpretability and Generalizability:** Simulation-trained RL policies are often "black-box" models, making their decision-making processes opaque and difficult to understand or guarantee. This lack of interpretability is a significant concern for safety-critical applications, such as autonomous navigation. The authors are motivated to develop an approach that provides interpretable behavior by explicitly modeling 3D structure and motion. Furthermore, by leveraging models pretrained on broad real-world data distributions, the goal is to achieve strong generalization across diverse obstacle types and unstructured environments, unlike task-specific simulation policies.

4. **Leveraging Pretrained Vision Foundation Models:** Recent advancements in large-scale vision foundation models (e.g., transformer-based models for depth estimation, feature detection, and matching) have demonstrated robust performance and strong cross-domain generalization. The motivation is to integrate these powerful, off-the-shelf components—specifically UniDepth for monocular depth estimation and SuperPoint/SuperGlue for feature tracking—into a unified pipeline for dynamic obstacle avoidance. This approach aims to capitalize on their learned priors and representational capabilities without requiring further model training.

5. **Optimizing Sensor Modality Choice:** While stereo depth and 3D LiDAR are alternatives for depth sensing, they present certain limitations. Stereo depth typically becomes unreliable beyond 5-10 meters, restricting reaction time. LiDAR, though accurate for large objects, provides sparse measurements that may miss smaller or distant obstacles. The authors are motivated to focus on monocular vision, as it can provide dense scene representations and useful depth estimates up to 20-30 meters, which is beneficial for detecting a wider range of obstacles in unstructured environments.

In summary, the core motivation is to achieve robust, interpretable, and data-efficient dynamic obstacle avoidance in challenging real-world settings by judiciously combining state-of-the-art pretrained vision models with explicit geometric reasoning, thereby overcoming the significant training data and sim-to-real transfer hurdles faced by other common robotic learning paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology for dynamic obstacle avoidance is a modular, vision-based pipeline that leverages pretrained foundation models and geometric reasoning. It operates entirely on real-world RGB video data without requiring robot-specific model training.

The system architecture begins with processing RGB video frames and proceeds through several stages:

1. **Monocular Depth Estimation with UniDepth:**
 - The first step involves generating a dense metric depth map from each incoming RGB video frame. This is achieved using UniDepth, a transformer-based monocular depth estimation model.
 - UniDepth is chosen for its ability to produce geometrically consistent metric depth predictions across various camera intrinsics and environments, as it is trained on a large, diverse dataset with accurate metric supervision. This eliminates the need for stereo cameras or LiDAR at inference time.
 - The method acknowledges that while UniDepth offers strong generalization, its monocular nature can lead to limitations such as degraded performance in low-texture regions, extreme lighting, or at object boundaries, and potential residual scale bias or local depth inaccuracies that can propagate downstream.

2. **Keypoint Detection and Tracking with SuperPoint and SuperGlue:**
 - To enable sparse, rather than dense, TTC estimation, the pipeline utilizes SuperPoint for keypoint detection and description and SuperGlue for robust feature matching.
 - SuperPoint is a self-supervised convolutional neural network that generates repeatable keypoints robust to viewpoint and illumination changes, addressing limitations of handcrafted methods (e.g., ORB, SIFT) that may bias towards high-contrast regions and miss critical obstacles.
 - SuperGlue extends this by employing a transformer-based graph neural network to establish context-aware correspondences between keypoints across consecutive frames, improving matching accuracy even in ambiguous areas.
 - The pipeline extends this pair-wise matching to track keypoints across multiple consecutive frames, maintaining temporal correspondences of 2D pixel-space points over time.

3. **3D Keypoint Reconstruction:**
 - Each tracked 2D pixel coordinate is lifted into a 3D point in the camera coordinate frame. This is done by combining the predicted metric depth from UniDepth with the known camera intrinsic parameters.
 - A standard pinhole camera model is assumed, and the back-projection equations (`X = (u - cx)Z/fx`, `Y = (v - cy)Z/fy`, `Z = Z`) are used to convert 2D pixel coordinates (u, v) and depth (Z) into 3D camera-frame coordinates (X, Y, Z).

4. **3D Trajectory Optimization with XM Solver:**
 - To enhance the accuracy of the estimated 3D keypoint trajectories, Scaled Bundle Adjustment (SBA) is performed using the XM solver over a sliding window of the last five frames.
 - The XM solver is a certifiably optimal optimization engine for SBA, reformulating it as a Semidefinite Program (SDP) relaxation. It refines the 3D keypoint positions based on the 2D observations and initial depth estimates.
 - **Filtering Steps:** To maintain data reliability and mitigate input noise, several filtering criteria are applied:
 - Keypoints tracked for fewer than five frames are excluded from optimization and TTC computation.
 - Keypoints exceeding a depth of 20 meters are excluded, as they are considered too distant to pose an immediate collision risk.
 - 3D keypoints exhibiting a frame-to-frame displacement greater than 2 meters are filtered out. These large discontinuities are attributed to inaccuracies from monocular depth estimation. When such anomalies are detected, their associated 3D trajectories are reinitialized to prevent corrupted historical data from affecting future computations.

5. **Time-to-Collision (TTC) Computation:**
 - For the optimized 3D keypoints, their 3D velocity is computed via linear regression of their position sequence over the tracking window.
 - Keypoints are further filtered: those with a Closest Point of Approach (CPA) greater than 3 meters with the camera are discarded, as they are not considered a collision risk. This 3-meter threshold was determined through hyperparameter tuning.
 - For the remaining keypoints, TTC is calculated using the formula: `TTC(i) = d(i) / max(-v(i), 0)`, where `d(i)` is the predicted monocular depth and `v(i)` is the velocity of keypoint `i` along the camera's optical axis. This formulation ensures that TTC is only finite for points actively approaching the camera.

6. **Motion Primitive Generation:**
 - The keypoint with the minimum calculated TTC is identified.
 - A 2D motion primitive (a vector) is then generated in the ground plane. This primitive directs the robot in the opposite direction to the CPA of the identified minimum-TTC keypoint, aiming to move the robot away from the imminent obstacle.

**Dataset:** The methodology was developed and evaluated using the Multi-Robot, Multi-Sensor, Multi-Environment Event Dataset (M3ED). Specifically, the `spot-forest-easy-1` sequence was used for hyperparameter tuning, while the remaining four `spot-forest` sequences and all eight `spot-outdoor-day` sequences (featuring a Boston Dynamics Spot robot in forest and urban environments) were used for evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the obstacle avoidance pipeline focused on its ability to identify frames requiring evasive action (TTC < 1 second) and to generate appropriate corrective motion primitives. Ground truth labels for evaluation were derived from LiDAR data within the M3ED dataset, with a 1-second TTC threshold chosen to align with the Spot robot's typical operating speed of 1 m/s, providing a 1-meter buffer for maneuvers.

**Obstacle Avoidance Performance (TTC Detection):**
- **Precision:** The pipeline achieved a precision of 0.49 in detecting imminent collisions across all test sequences. This indicates a low false positive rate; out of 40,104 frames without a ground-truth TTC below 1 second, only 190 (0.47%) were incorrectly identified as threatening.
- **Recall:** The recall for detecting individual frames with a ground-truth TTC below 1 second was 0.38, meaning 185 out of 482 actual threatening frames were correctly identified.
- **Practical Impact of Recall:** Despite the low frame-level recall, the pipeline demonstrated a relevant practical capability: it correctly identified at least one frame with a TTC less than 1 second for 20 out of 22 distinct physical obstacles present in the test sequences. This suggests that while it may not detect every threatening frame, it successfully triggers an evasive action for most unique obstacles.

**Factors Contributing to Recall Limitations:**
The authors identified two primary reasons for the pipeline's limited frame-level recall:
1. **Absence of TTC Estimates:** Many missed detections occurred because no keypoint was tracked for the minimum required five consecutive frames, thus preventing bundle adjustment and TTC computation. This was attributed to:
 - Failures of SuperPoint and SuperGlue to maintain long-term feature correspondences.
 - Inaccurate depth estimates from UniDepth causing discontinuities in 3D keypoint motion, leading to reinitialization of trajectories and rendering points temporarily ineligible for bundle adjustment. The trade-off between a longer tracking window (5 frames) for noise reduction and a shorter one for more frequent detection was noted.
2. **Inaccurate TTC Values:** Even when keypoints were correctly tracked, imperfections in the optimized 3D keypoint trajectories sometimes resulted in inaccurate TTC values, leading to both false positives (a small number) and false negatives. These inaccuracies were attributed to systematic errors or scale inconsistencies propagating from the monocular depth maps produced by UniDepth, which the XM solver then optimally fits to these flawed constraints.

**Evasive Action Generation Performance:**
- For the 185 true positive detections (frames where TTC was correctly identified as below 1 second), the pipeline demonstrated strong performance in generating appropriate evasive maneuvers.
- 156 out of 185 (84%) of these true positive detections resulted in motion primitives that correctly directed the robot away from the closest point of approach (CPA), as determined by a positive dot product with the ground-truth motion primitive vector derived from LiDAR.
- Failure cases in action generation were attributed to errors in estimating the motion of the approaching keypoint or incorrectly identifying the minimum-TTC keypoint within the frame.

**Data Efficiency:**
- A notable finding is the method's high data efficiency. The pipeline requires no model training, relying entirely on pretrained vision models.
- Only 74 seconds of data from the `spot-forest-easy-1` sequence were used for hyperparameter tuning. This contrasts significantly with end-to-end learned methods that demand thousands of hours of robot-specific training data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous robotics, particularly concerning robust navigation in challenging, unstructured environments. The proposed methodology holds several implications and potential impacts:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robotics:** The work demonstrates a viable strategy for achieving complex robotic tasks like dynamic obstacle avoidance without requiring large-scale, robot-specific datasets for model training. By leveraging pretrained vision foundation models such as UniDepth, SuperPoint, and SuperGlue, the pipeline effectively capitalizes on knowledge learned from diverse real-world data, drastically reducing the data collection burden traditionally associated with developing robust robotic controllers. This has the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous systems, especially for niche or rapidly evolving applications where extensive custom datasets are impractical.

2. **Mitigating the Sim-to-Real Gap:** By operating exclusively on real-world data and entirely bypassing simulation-based training, the approach intrinsically avoids the "sim-to-real" transfer problem. This means that the observed performance and behaviors are directly relevant to real-world scenarios, which can enhance confidence in deployment compared to systems that require extensive domain adaptation. This is particularly impactful for operations in highly variable, unstructured outdoor environments where accurate simulation models are difficult to construct.

3. **Enhancing Interpretability and Trust:** Unlike many end-to-end learned (black-box) control policies, this method maintains interpretability. The explicit estimation of 3D structure, motion, and time-to-collision provides clear reasoning for evasive actions. This transparency is critical for safety-critical applications, enabling developers and regulators to understand why a robot acts in a certain way, diagnose failures, and provide guarantees about its behavior, thereby increasing trust in autonomous systems.

4. **Generalization to Unstructured Environments:** The reliance on foundation models pretrained on vast and diverse datasets contributes to the pipeline's ability to generalize across various obstacle types and complex unstructured outdoor environments (forests, urban scenes). This generalization is essential for robots intended for deployment in dynamic and unpredictable real-world settings, where encountering novel situations is common.

5. **Foundation for Future Robotic Perception:** The work highlights the utility of composable, off-the-shelf vision models for robotic tasks. The integration of monocular depth estimation, robust feature tracking, and geometrically optimal bundle adjustment provides a strong framework for real-time 3D perception that could be adapted for other tasks such as mapping, localization, or interaction.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** The elimination of extensive data collection and model training translates directly into lower costs and faster development cycles for autonomous robots.
- **Wider Adoption of Autonomous Robots:** By making the development of robust perception systems more accessible, the method could facilitate the broader adoption of autonomous mobile robots in industries such as logistics, agriculture, search and rescue, and defense, especially in challenging outdoor settings.
- **Improved Safety:** The combination of interpretable decision-making and the ability to detect and react to a wide range of dynamic obstacles contributes to safer robot operation.

**Future Work Implications:** The identified limitations, particularly concerning the recall rate due to long-term feature tracking and the impact of monocular depth inaccuracies, outline clear directions for future improvements. Incorporating more robust long-term trackers (e.g., DROID-SLAM or Kalman filter-augmented pipelines) or refining depth estimation strategies could further enhance the system's reliability. The ultimate impact will be fully validated through real-time deployment on a physical robot platform, such as the Boston Dynamics Spot, to assess end-to-end latency and practical feasibility within a complete autonomous navigation stack.
