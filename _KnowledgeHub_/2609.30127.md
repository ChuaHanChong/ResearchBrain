# Faster Visuomotor Policy Learning on Action Manifolds via Riemannian MeanFlow

> [!summary] Summary
> Riemannian MeanFlow Policy (RMFP) learns the conditioned flow map of a geodesic probability path on a robot's action manifold instead of its instantaneous velocity field. An algebraic semigroup consistency loss, anchored by Riemannian Conditional Flow Matching on demonstrations, trains single-stage and teacher-free with no differential terms. One network evaluation yields on-manifold action chunks, matching or beating multi-step baselines: 78.5% spherical Push-T coverage and 41.7% Franka Kitchen success at 1 NFE.

## Problem

- Diffusion and flow-matching policies generate actions by numerically integrating a learned field, costing one network evaluation per step and blocking fast control rates.
- Euclidean policies treat position, quaternion and gripper coordinates as a flat vector, placing probability mass off the manifold and needing an unmodeled projection at execution.
- Existing few-step fixes either distil a pretrained teacher, bounding the student, or live only in Euclidean action spaces; Riemannian flow-map work targets geospatial and biomolecular data.

## Method

- Chord-form ==average velocity== on the manifold, trained by a stop-gradient ==semigroup consistency loss== whose bootstrap target composes two frozen flow-map rollouts through an intermediate time.
- **Riemannian Conditional Flow Matching** anchor at the diagonal supplies the only data-observing term, preventing the data-free semigroup identity from collapsing to the zero field.
- Two network heads: endpoint prediction projects output onto the manifold then takes a ==log map==; velocity prediction projects orthogonally onto the ==tangent space==, imposing geometry at different forward-pass points.

## Results

- At **1 NFE** on spherical LASA, median DTW is **0.022** (endpoint head) and **0.033** (velocity head) versus **0.090** for flow matching, with markedly lower jerk.
- Franka Kitchen at **1 NFE** reaches **41.7%** success versus **20.6%** for the prior-matched flow-matching baseline and **23.7%** for the best diffusion variant; margins shrink to **0.6** points by **5 NFE**.
- Robomimic Tool Hang: velocity head hits **64%** at **1 NFE** and **76%** at **2 NFE** while diffusion collapses to **0%**; each RMFP arm spans at most **10.2** points across budgets versus **16.8-29.4** for flow matching.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning the finite-interval flow map, not the velocity field, makes sampling budget a property of the objective rather than the solver, flattening performance across NFE counts.
> - Flow-map consistency buys the low-budget regime, not a higher ceiling: advantages over flow matching largely vanish once five or more evaluations are affordable.
> - Endpoint versus velocity parameterization ordering tracks action dimension rather than curvature, so both heads should be reported instead of assuming one dominates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A visuomotor policy has to turn a sensory observation into a useful action sequence. Generative policies can represent several plausible action sequences for the same observation. Conventional diffusion and flow policies, however, spend repeated network evaluations integrating their way to a sample. Robot actions also have structure. An end-effector command can combine position, orientation, and gripper state, so treating every coordinate as unconstrained Euclidean data can produce invalid actions. Instead of stepping repeatedly along an instantaneous velocity field, the approach learns the finite-time transport itself, a jump over an interval, while keeping the generated action sequence on its action manifold. The authors call it Riemannian MeanFlow Policy (RMFP).

In a controlled trajectory-reproduction example, the LASA W on a sphere, the RMFP trajectory at one network query is visibly smoother than the Riemannian Flow Matching Policy (RFMP) baseline and follows the demonstrations more closely. This is a trajectory-reproduction illustration, not a physical robot manipulation result.

The LASA W projected onto a sphere, with RFMP on the left and RMFP on the right. Black marks are demonstrations, the executed path is colored by time, and a star marks the north pole. DTW is 0.023 for RMFP versus 0.079 for RFMP.

### 2. Learning a jump without a teacher

A manifold is a space of valid actions with its own geometry. A sequence of actions is treated as one point on the corresponding product manifold. A geodesic is the manifold's analogue of a straight path. A flow map is the transport from a point at one time to its position at a later time. The learned model represents the average tangent direction along the geodesic chord to that destination.

Training proceeds in five steps:

1. Take a point $a_{s}$ on the conditional geodesic between a prior sample and a demonstrated action chunk.
1. Choose an intermediate time $r$ and a later time $t$.
1. Use two model evaluations to jump from $s$ to $r$, then from $r$ to $t$.
1. Map the composed endpoint back to a tangent-space chord target.
1. Train a direct $s$-to-$t$ prediction to match that target.

The target rollout is stopped from receiving gradients. This enforces the semigroup idea: transport through an intermediate time should agree with transport across the whole interval.

Consistency alone is data-free, and even a zero field can satisfy it. A Riemannian Conditional Flow Matching anchor supplies the data-linked boundary by matching the model to the demonstrated geodesic velocity at equal start and end times. The combined training is single-stage and teacher-free. One training step uses four network function evaluations (NFEs), and only two of them build a gradient graph. RFMP pays one NFE per training step.

The horizontal axis is time from 0 to 1. A dashed conditional geodesic runs from prior sample $a_{0}$ to demonstrated chunk $a_{1}$. Grey arcs show the two no-gradient rollouts through $r$ to $\tilde{a}_{t}$. At $a_{s}$, the dashed grey chord is the stopped target, the teal arrow is the direct predicted jump, and the gold arrow is the geodesic velocity used by the anchor.

### 3. What one query buys

The closest comparison is [Riemannian Flow Matching Policy (RFMP)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2412.10855). RFMP already places conditional paths on the action manifold, but it learns the instantaneous velocity and samples by integrating it in steps. RMFP shares RFMP's manifold construction and differs in learning the flow map instead.

The decisive test is spherical Push-T. It maps the planar agent target onto a sphere by stereographic projection and reports a coverage score of the target region, where higher is better. At one NFE per action chunk, RMFP's velocity-prediction variant reaches 78.5% coverage, versus 67.9% for RFMP and 73.1% for the sample-prediction Riemannian diffusion baseline.

At larger budgets the gap narrows. On spherical Push-T and Franka Kitchen, RMFP's margin over flow matching is at most 1.0 point at five NFEs. The method buys a stronger low-budget regime, not a higher performance ceiling.

Table II: spherical Push-T coverage (%, higher is better) at 1, 2, 5, and 10 NFE. At one NFE, RMF-v scores 78.5 and RFM 67.9. RMF-v peaks at 85.7 at five NFEs, while RMF-$x_{1}$ reaches 85.0 at ten.

The efficiency claim is limited to NFE counts. The tables report NFE per action chunk, not elapsed latency, and the paper does not report a measured control rate or a matched wall-clock inference-time comparison.

### 4. The useful shift

RMFP makes the learned model represent finite-time transport directly. One query can then produce a whole manifold-valued jump, instead of relying on a solver to approximate that jump through repeated local steps. The Push-T comparison shows the clearest gain: stronger performance at the smallest tested budget. It does not show a higher ceiling or a measured wall-clock speedup.
