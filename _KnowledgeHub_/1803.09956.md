# Learning Synergies between Pushing and Grasping with Self-supervised Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A self-supervised deep reinforcement learning framework enables a robot to learn synergistic pushing and grasping policies directly from visual input. This approach significantly boosts grasping success and efficiency in cluttered environments by autonomously discovering complex manipulation strategies through joint action-value functions.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation research has traditionally treated prehensile (grasping) and non-prehensile (pushing) actions as distinct, limiting synergistic behaviors.
- Combining pushing and grasping typically relies on hand-crafted heuristics or explicit object models, which struggle with real-world complexities like severe clutter.
- Grasp-only policies often fail in highly cluttered or adversarial environments due to tightly packed object arrangements.

## Method

- A model-free ==Q-learning== framework is developed, jointly training two ==fully convolutional networks (FCNs)== to predict ==pixel-wise Q-values== for pushing and grasping actions.
- Actions are parameterized as ==motion primitives== executed at 3D locations derived from ==RGB-D heightmaps==, with 16 orientations for both pushes and grasps processed by FCNs.
- ==Self-supervised trial-and-error== is used, where pushing is implicitly valued by its utility in enabling future successful grasps, alongside a direct reward for successful grasps.

## Results

- In simulation, the method achieved **100%** completion and **67.7%** grasp success on random arrangements, outperforming grasping-only (**90.9%** completion, **55.8%** grasp success).
- On challenging adversarial clutter, the system yielded **82.7%** completion and **77.2%** grasp success, significantly surpassing grasping-only (**40.6%** completion, **51.7%** grasp success).
- Real-world experiments demonstrated effective policies trained in under **5.5 hours**, improving completion rates to **71.4%** (vs. **42.9%** for grasping-only) and showing generalization capabilities to novel objects.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex synergies between pushing and grasping can be learned end-to-end from scratch, directly from visual observations, without explicit object models or hand-engineered features.
> - Pushing actions are effectively learned when their reward is primarily derived from their contribution to enabling future successful grasps, demonstrating a discoverable long-term utility.
> - Fully convolutional networks are highly efficient for this task, allowing a single forward pass to compute Q-values for a large action space, making real-world training tractable within hours.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation research has traditionally focused on two distinct categories of actions: prehensile (grasping) and non-prehensile (pushing). While both areas have seen extensive development, with established model-based and more recent data-driven approaches, the combined study of these actions for sequential manipulation remains relatively underexplored.

Classical approaches to non-prehensile manipulation, such as pushing, often rely on explicit models of object dynamics and frictional forces. However, these models frequently struggle with real-world complexities like non-uniform friction or severe clutter. Similarly, traditional grasping methods depend on known 3D object models, poses, and contact points, which are often unavailable in unstructured environments.

The emergence of data-driven methods, particularly those leveraging deep learning, has enabled model-agnostic policies for grasping that learn from experience. Recent works have explored training deep grasping policies using self-supervision or by pre-training on auxiliary tasks like poking. Fully convolutional networks (FCNs) have been demonstrated to efficiently model these policies by mapping visual observations to affordance metrics.

The challenge of combining pushing and grasping has typically been addressed through hand-crafted heuristics or by training pushing policies to move objects to positions favorable for pre-designed grasping algorithms. These approaches often segment perception and control policies, rely on model-based simulations for predicting push outcomes, or are limited by the specificity of their handcrafted rules.

This work positions itself by addressing the gap in learning synergistic policies for pushing and grasping from scratch, in a model-free, end-to-end manner, directly from visual observations. It aims to overcome the limitations of prior methods by allowing the system to discover complex interaction behaviors without explicit domain knowledge or hand-tuned features, thereby moving towards more generalized and adaptive manipulation capabilities in cluttered environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that skilled robotic manipulation often benefits from intricate synergies between non-prehensile actions like pushing and prehensile actions like grasping. Pushing can be instrumental in rearranging cluttered objects, thereby creating space or isolating targets to facilitate subsequent grasping. Conversely, grasping can displace objects, making pushing movements more precise or collision-free.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Discovering and Learning Synergies from Scratch:** A central objective is to demonstrate that a robot can autonomously discover and learn these complex pushing-grasping synergies from experience. This is pursued through model-free deep reinforcement learning, specifically Q-learning. This contrasts with previous approaches that rely on predefined heuristics or explicit models of interaction dynamics.
- **Self-Supervised Learning of Joint Policies:** The research aims to develop a system where joint pushing and grasping policies are learned through self-supervised trial and error. A core motivation is to allow pushing actions to be implicitly valued based on their utility in enabling future successful grasps, rather than explicit, hard-coded rewards for pushing specific objects or achieving predefined spatial configurations. The system seeks to learn pushing motions that *enable future grasps*, while learning grasps that *leverage past pushes*.
- **End-to-End Training with Deep Networks:** The authors are motivated to train perception and control policies end-to-end using deep networks. This involves mapping directly from visual observations (RGB-D heightmaps) to expected return (Q-values) for potential pushing and grasping actions. This approach avoids the need for explicit object perception, segmentation, or hand-designed features, which are common in many traditional and some data-driven manipulation systems.
- **Enabling Complex Sequential Manipulation in Clutter:** A significant motivation is to enable robots to perform complex sequential manipulations involving both pushing and grasping in unstructured picking scenarios, particularly in the presence of severe clutter. Such environments are challenging for grasp-only policies, as tightly packed or adversarial object arrangements often prevent successful initial grasps.
- **Generalization to Novel Objects:** The research aims for a system capable of generalizing its learned manipulation policies to novel objects, i.e., objects not encountered during the training phase. This highlights the desire for robust and adaptable robotic skills.
- **Tractable Training on Real Robots:** Recognizing the high sample complexity typically associated with deep reinforcement learning on physical systems, the authors are motivated to design a method that simplifies the action space and leverages efficient neural network architectures (Fully Convolutional Networks) to make training feasible on a single real robot within a few hours.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between prehensile and non-prehensile manipulation by teaching a robot to strategically combine these actions through self-supervised learning, enabling more robust and efficient object handling in complex, cluttered environments without extensive human engineering.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors frame the task of pushing-for-grasping as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and employ off-policy Q-learning to train a greedy deterministic policy. The core of their methodology involves jointly training two fully convolutional networks (FCNs) that predict Q-values for pushing and grasping actions based on visual observations.

#### 3.1. State Representations

Each state ($s_t$) is represented as an RGB-D heightmap. This representation is generated by:
1. Capturing RGB-D images from a fixed-mount camera.
2. Projecting the data into a 3D point cloud.
3. Orthographically back-projecting upwards in the gravity direction to form a heightmap.
The heightmap contains both color (RGB) and height-from-bottom (D) channels. In experiments, the workspace is a 0.448 m$^2$ tabletop, and the 224x224 pixel resolution heightmap corresponds to 2x2 mm vertical columns of 3D space.

#### 3.2. Primitive Actions

Actions ($a_t$) are parameterized as motion primitives ($\psi$) executed at a 3D location ($q$) derived from a pixel ($p$) on the heightmap. The two primitive behaviors are:
- **Pushing:** $q$ denotes the starting position of a 10cm straight push. The push is executed using the tip of a closed two-finger gripper. To account for varying directions, the input heightmap is rotated into $k=16$ different orientations (multiples of 22.5$^\circ$), and only horizontal pushes (to the right) are considered in the rotated coordinate system.
- **Grasping:** $q$ denotes the middle position of a top-down parallel-jaw grasp. The gripper attempts to move 3cm below $q$ before closing. Similar to pushing, $k=16$ different orientations are handled by rotating the input heightmap.
Robot arm motion planning (Inverse Kinematics) is handled automatically to ensure stable and collision-free execution.

#### 3.3. Learning Fully Convolutional Action-Value Functions

The Q-function, $Q_\pi(s_t, a_t)$, is approximated by two FCNs: $\phi_p$ for pushing and $\phi_g$ for grasping.
- Each FCN takes the state $s_t$ (represented by the RGB-D heightmap) as input.
- Each FCN outputs a dense pixel-wise map of Q-values, where each pixel's value represents the expected future reward for executing its corresponding primitive at that 3D location.
- By processing the 16 rotated heightmaps, a total of 32 pixel-wise Q-value maps are generated (16 for pushes, 16 for grasps).
- The action with the highest Q-value across all 32 maps is chosen: $\operatorname{argmax}_{(\psi,p)} (\phi_p(s_t), \phi_g(s_t))$.

The FCN architecture for both $\phi_p$ and $\phi_g$ consists of:
- Two parallel 121-layer DenseNet architectures, pre-trained on ImageNet. One DenseNet processes the RGB channels, and the other processes the depth (height-from-bottom) channel (cloned three times).
- Followed by channel-wise concatenation.
- Two additional 1x1 convolutional layers interleaved with ReLU activations and spatial batch normalization.
- Bilinear upsampling to match the input heightmap resolution.

This FCN-based approach offers several benefits:
- **Spatial Locality:** Q-value predictions maintain an explicit spatial relationship to other actions and the input observation due to convolutional receptive fields.
- **Computational Efficiency:** A single forward pass can compute Q-values for a large number of possible actions (1,605,632 actions for 224x224x32) within approximately 2.5 seconds.
- **Parameter Sharing:** Convolutional features are shared across different locations and orientations, enhancing sample efficiency and promoting equivariance to translation and rotation.

#### 3.4. Rewards

A simple reward scheme is employed:
- **Grasp Reward:** $R_g(s_t, s_{t+1}) = 1$ for a successful grasp. Success is determined by thresholding the antipodal distances between gripper fingers after the attempt.
- **Push Reward:** $R_p(s_t, s_{t+1}) = 0.5$ for pushes that cause detectable changes in the environment. Change is detected if the sum of differences between the heightmaps before and after the push exceeds a certain threshold ($\tau$).
The synergy between pushing and grasping is primarily learned through the future expected rewards from successful grasps, rather than direct supervision for the utility of a push.

#### 3.5. Training Details

- **Loss Function:** Huber loss is used to minimize the temporal difference error, $\delta_t = |Q(s_t, a_t) - y_t|$, where $y_t = R_{a_t}(s_t, s_{t+1}) + \gamma \operatorname{max}_{a'}(Q(s_{t+1}, a'))$.
- **Gradient Flow:** Gradients are backpropagated only through the single pixel and network corresponding to the executed action.
- **Optimizer:** Stochastic gradient descent with momentum (0.9), fixed learning rate ($10^{-4}$), and weight decay ($2^{-5}$).
- **Experience Replay:** Prioritized experience replay with stochastic rank-based prioritization is used.
- **Exploration:** $\epsilon$-greedy strategy, with $\epsilon$ annealed from 0.5 to 0.1 over training.
- **Discount Factor:** $\gamma = 0.5$.
- **Self-Supervision Process:** During training, $n$ objects (10 in simulation, 30 in real-world) are randomly placed in the workspace. The robot performs trial-and-error actions until the workspace is clear, then the process repeats.

#### 3.6. Testing Details

- **Policy:** The policy is greedy and deterministic during testing.
- **Adaptation during Test:** To mitigate issues where the policy might repeatedly execute the same action without causing change, a small learning rate ($10^{-5}$) is maintained during testing. Gradients are backpropagated after each action.
- **Evaluation Reset:** Network weights are reset to their trained state before each new test run.
- **Completion Criteria:** A test run ends when all objects are successfully grasped or when 10 consecutive actions are executed without any detectable change to the environment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted in both simulation and real-world environments to evaluate the proposed Visual Pushing for Grasping (VPG) method against baseline policies.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

The simulation setup used a UR5 robot arm with an RG2 gripper in V-REP with Bullet Physics. Objects consisted of 9 different 3D toy blocks.

- **Comparisons to Baselines (Random Arrangements):**
 - In a scenario with 30 randomly placed objects, VPG demonstrated superior performance across all metrics compared to a Grasping-only policy and a Reactive Pushing and Grasping (P+G Reactive) policy.
 - VPG achieved a 100% completion rate, 67.7% grasp success rate, and 60.9% action efficiency.
 - Grasping-only achieved 90.9% completion, 55.8% grasp success, and 55.8% action efficiency.
 - P+G Reactive showed a lower completion rate (54.5%) and action efficiency (47.7%), with a 59.4% grasp success, suggesting it often pushed objects out of the workspace without effective grasping.

- **Challenging Arrangements (Adversarial Clutter):**
 - On 11 manually engineered challenging test cases (3-6 objects with adversarial clutter), VPG significantly outperformed baselines.
 - VPG achieved 82.7% completion, 77.2% grasp success, and 60.1% action efficiency.
 - Grasping-only struggled with 40.6% completion and 51.7% grasp success (with 0% completion in 5 out of 11 cases).
 - P+G Reactive improved completion to 48.2% and grasp success to 59.0%, but still had lower action efficiency (46.4%), indicating less succinct pushes.
 - These results indicated that the addition of pushing (VPG) enlarged the set of scenarios for successful grasping, with VPG's integrated planning leading to more effective and efficient behaviors.

- **Ablation Study: No Pushing Rewards (VPG-noreward):**
 - When the intrinsic reward for pushing ($R_p$) was removed, VPG-noreward was still capable of learning effective policies, achieving 70-80% grasp success rates.
 - It demonstrated learning a pushing policy that increasingly aided grasping, although the rate of improvement was not as rapid, and the final performance was slightly lower than full VPG. This indicates that synergies can be learned solely through future grasp rewards.

- **Ablation Study: No ImageNet Pre-training (VPG-nopretrain):**
 - Removing ImageNet pre-training for the FCN weights did not significantly impact the sample efficiency or final performance of VPG in simulation. This suggests that features relevant to heightmap images might differ from those learned on ImageNet.

- **Ablation Study: No Height-from-bottom Information (VPG-nopretrain-nodepth):**
 - Training without depth channels resulted in a similar sample complexity but a 15% lower average final grasping performance. This emphasizes the importance of geometric cues provided by the depth information for effective manipulation.

- **Ablation Study: Shortsighted Policies (VPG-myopic, $\gamma=0.2$):**
 - A shortsighted version of VPG (lower discount factor) learned faster initially but achieved lower overall average performance (79.1% completion, 74.3% grasp success, 53.7% action efficiency) on challenging test cases compared to full VPG. This supports the benefit of long-term planning for sequential manipulation.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

Real-world experiments utilized a UR5 robot arm with an RG2 gripper and an Intel RealSense SR300 camera.

- **Random Arrangements (30+ Objects):**
 - VPG exhibited a similar initial performance improvement to the grasping-only policy, which was unexpected as VPG has a larger action space. This was attributed to VPG quickly learning pushes that simplify grasping.
 - In later training steps, VPG surpassed grasping-only, demonstrating better and more stable grasping performance, avoiding long sequences of failed grasps common in highly cluttered configurations.
 - VPG achieved effective policies in less than 2000 transitions, equating to approximately 5.5 hours of wall-clock training time, indicating high sample efficiency for real-world robotic learning.

- **Challenging Arrangements (Adversarial Clutter):**
 - On 7 challenging real-world test cases, VPG showed substantial improvements over grasping-only.
 - VPG achieved 71.4% completion, 83.3% grasp success, and 69.0% action efficiency.
 - Grasping-only achieved 42.9% completion, 43.5% grasp success, and 43.5% action efficiency.
 - These results suggest that VPG's ability to plan synergistic pushes significantly enhances its capability to complete picking tasks efficiently in cluttered real-world scenarios.

- **Novel Objects:**
 - VPG models (trained on toy blocks) demonstrated generalization capabilities to novel objects (e.g., fruit, bottles, office supplies) with similar shape distributions. The system was able to plan complex pushing motions to de-clutter scenes with these unseen objects.
 - However, generalization struggled with entirely new shapes or highly reflective objects that lacked reliable depth data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by demonstrating a novel framework for learning the synergies between non-prehensile (pushing) and prehensile (grasping) actions.

**Key Significance:**

- **First Model-Free, End-to-End Learning of Synergies:** The paper introduces the first model-free system that learns complementary pushing and grasping policies simultaneously from scratch using deep reinforcement learning. This is achieved end-to-end, directly mapping visual observations to actions without relying on explicit object models, hand-engineered features, or predefined push objectives.
- **Enhanced Manipulation in Clutter:** The findings consistently show that integrating strategic pushing significantly improves grasping success rates and overall picking efficiency in cluttered and challenging environments, both in simulation and on a physical robot. This enlarges the set of scenarios where autonomous robots can perform effective manipulation.
- **Self-Supervised Discovery of Complex Behaviors:** The methodology allows the system to discover complex, non-trivial sequences of pushing and grasping, where pushing actions are inherently valued based on their contribution to enabling future grasps. This demonstrates the power of self-supervision in learning intricate robotic skills.
- **Tractable Real-World Training:** The use of fully convolutional networks and a pixel-wise action space parameterization enables the training of effective policies on a real robot within a few hours (approximately 5.5 hours), addressing a common challenge of high sample complexity in deep reinforcement learning for robotics.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** The system exhibits a degree of generalization to novel object arrangements and, to some extent, to novel object shapes, indicating its potential robustness beyond the training dataset.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Advancement in Autonomous Robotics:** This research directly contributes to advancing the capabilities of autonomous robots in unstructured environments. By enabling robots to handle clutter more effectively, it can broaden the applicability of robotic manipulators in various domains.
- **Industrial and Logistics Applications:** Industries such as logistics, warehousing, and manufacturing, which frequently deal with bins of irregularly arranged or tightly packed objects, could benefit from robots capable of intelligently de-cluttering and grasping. The improved robustness and efficiency demonstrated by this system have direct relevance to these applications.
- **Service Robotics:** In service robotics, where robots interact with diverse household or public environments, the ability to manipulate objects in varied and often cluttered contexts is crucial. This work moves towards more versatile and capable service robots.
- **Foundation for Future Manipulation Research:** The framework provides a foundation for exploring synergies between other types of manipulation primitives (e.g., rolling, toppling, squeezing, stacking). It suggests that deep reinforcement learning can be a viable path for learning complex multi-step manipulation strategies for a wider array of interactions.
- **Reduced Engineering Effort:** By minimizing the reliance on human-engineered models and heuristics, this approach could reduce the development time and expertise required to deploy robotic manipulation systems in new environments or with new objects.

In conclusion, this work represents a significant step towards more intelligent and adaptable robotic manipulation, particularly in challenging cluttered scenes, by leveraging the power of self-supervised deep reinforcement learning to uncover and exploit the inherent synergies between fundamental robotic actions.
