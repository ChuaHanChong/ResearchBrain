# Asymmetric physics enables efficient learning in quadrupedal robot swarms

> [!summary] Summary
> Asymmetric physics trains vision-based decentralized swarm control for quadrupeds: high-fidelity non-differentiable Isaac Gym rollouts generate contact-rich motion, while differentiable point-mass and rigid-body surrogates supply first-order gradients for hierarchical navigation and locomotion policies. Up to 512 quadrupeds learn together in shared environments; the policy reaches PPO's reward with 2% of samples and zero-shot transfers to six Unitree Go2 robots using only depth cameras.

## Problem

- Classical swarm models and early robot collectives assume global state, planar kinematics, precise positioning, or reliable communication, assumptions that break for contact-rich legged robots in cluttered scenes.
- Sampling-based multi-agent RL scales poorly: joint state-action space grows with robot count, and per-agent depth images push optimization cost beyond practical budgets for legged swarms.
- Differentiable physics yields low-variance gradients, but discontinuous contact makes full quadruped simulators unstable to differentiate, leaving physics-gradient methods unexplored for large contact-rich robot swarms.

## Method

- **Asymmetric physics**: high-fidelity non-differentiable ==Isaac Gym== rollouts produce realistic contact and collision behavior, while simple ==differentiable surrogates== supply backward-pass gradients, decoupling behavior generation from learning signal.
- Two surrogate models: a ==point-mass model== approximating base translation for the navigation policy, and a ==rigid-body ground-reaction-force model== for locomotion, both differentiated via ==backpropagation through time==.
- ==Hierarchical policy stack==: a depth-plus-goal navigation policy emits velocity commands, a locomotion policy converts them to joint targets, each updated by ==AdamW== at its own gradient-accumulation interval.

## Results

- Differentiable physics learning matched PPO's final reward with **2%** of the samples, converging to reward **-1.89** in **1.5 h** versus PPO's **-3.02** in **2.9 h**.
- One policy controlled **512** quadrupeds in intersecting-path mazes with under **3%** failures, and **96** robots cleared a fenced arena in under twice the **48**-robot time.
- The policy traversed gaps down to **0.5 m** where PPO already failed below **0.8 m**, and zero-shot transferred to **six** Unitree Go2 robots across **five** real scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Forward fidelity and gradient smoothness can be decoupled: differentiate a crude surrogate while rolling out real contact physics, keeping realism and first-order efficiency together.
> - Single-agent visual policies do not become swarm policies by replication; yielding, pausing, and queuing emerge only from shared multi-robot interaction during training.
> - First-order gradients route competing objectives separately into each action, explaining better speed-command tracking and tight-gap control than a summed scalar reward allows.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robot swarms capable of adapting to complex, cluttered, and dynamic environments, akin to animal collectives, has been a long-standing objective in robotics. Traditional swarm robotics often relies on simplified sensing, planar motion, global state information, or explicit communication, which restricts their applicability in real-world, contact-rich settings. Legged robots, particularly quadrupeds, present a significant challenge due to their high-dimensional whole-body dynamics, frequent ground contact, and the tight coupling required between perception, navigation, and locomotion.

While robot learning, specifically reinforcement learning (RL), has advanced single-robot control, its application to large, embodied robot swarms remains limited. The primary obstacle is training inefficiency; the joint state-action space scales rapidly with the number of agents, and high-dimensional visual inputs exacerbate this problem. For contact-rich platforms, discontinuous dynamics and low-level control further complicate learning. Existing multi-agent RL methods often struggle to scale to real-world robot swarms, especially those requiring dense interaction for emergent coordination behaviors like yielding or congestion resolution.

This paper addresses the tension between the need for high-fidelity simulation for realistic swarm training and the requirement for smooth optimization signals for efficient learning. It posits that large-scale interaction should provide first-order optimization signals, rather than being treated solely as stochastic samples for trial-and-error. Previous work in differentiable physics (DP) has shown promise in embedding dynamics into the learning loop for faster convergence but faces challenges with contact-rich systems due to gradient discontinuities and instability. This work builds upon and extends these concepts to address the unique complexities of vision-based, contact-rich quadrupedal robot swarms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable efficient end-to-end learning of vision-based, decentralized control policies for large swarms of quadrupedal robots. The authors aim to achieve this by introducing a novel training framework based on "asymmetric physics," which separates the physics used for realistic behavior generation from the physics used for efficient gradient-based policy optimization.

The motivation stems from several limitations in current swarm robotics and robot learning:
- **Scalability of Decentralized Control:** Animal collectives achieve complex coordination through local perception and interaction without centralized control or explicit communication. Reproducing this in robots, especially contact-rich ones like quadrupeds, is challenging due to the computational demands of learning such behaviors.
- **Training Efficiency for Embodied Swarms:** Standard sampling-based RL methods become prohibitively inefficient when visual perception, dense robot-robot interaction, and complex contact-rich locomotion must be learned concurrently in multi-robot settings. The joint state-action space explosion and high-dimensional visual inputs pose significant computational burdens.
- **Realism vs. Learnability:** Realistic simulation of contact dynamics is crucial for policies to transfer to the physical world. However, directly differentiating through these complex, often discontinuous, dynamics for gradient computation is difficult and can lead to unstable optimization. This creates a dilemma: high-fidelity simulation is needed for realism, but simplified, smooth models are better for efficient learning.
- **Emergent Swarm Behaviors:** Policies trained for single robots do not inherently learn multi-robot coordination behaviors such as yielding, pausing, or congestion avoidance, which are critical for effective swarm operation in shared environments. These behaviors require dense, repeated interactions during training.

The authors motivate their "asymmetric physics" approach by observing that biological systems often leverage physical reasoning to reduce exploration. They hypothesize that by using the simplest differentiable physics that captures dominant interaction and locomotion structure for gradient calculation, while still using a high-fidelity simulator for forward rollouts, they can overcome the training efficiency bottleneck and enable the emergence of complex, decentralized coordination in quadrupedal robot swarms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces an end-to-end training framework for vision-based swarm control in quadrupedal robots, centered on the principle of "asymmetric physics." This principle involves separating the physics model used for simulating realistic robot behavior during forward passes from the physics model used for generating gradients during backpropagation.

**Hierarchical Policy Stack:**
The framework trains a hierarchical policy stack:
- **High-level Navigation Policy ($\pi_{nav}$):** This policy takes depth observations from a forward-facing camera and goal information as input. It outputs velocity commands (linear and angular) for the robot.
- **Low-level Locomotion Policy ($\pi_{loc}$):** This policy receives the velocity commands from the navigation policy and translates them into desired joint positions for the quadruped's legs.

This decoupled design allows the learning of visual swarm navigation and legged locomotion within a single framework. Crucially, it facilitates training hundreds of quadrupeds simultaneously in shared environments, promoting the emergence of swarm behaviors through dense interaction rather than isolated single-robot experience. Each robot acts independently, using only local visual input and its own goal, without explicit communication, centralized planning, or global maps.

**Asymmetric Physics for Policy Optimization:**
The core of the methodology lies in the "asymmetric physics" approach:
1. **High-Fidelity Forward Simulation:** During forward rollouts, a high-fidelity, non-differentiable simulator (Isaac Gym) models realistic quadruped motion, contacts, collisions, and robot-environment interactions. This ensures that the generated behaviors are physically grounded and transferable to the real world, despite the simulator's non-differentiable nature regarding complex contacts.
2. **Differentiable Surrogate Models for Backpropagation:** For policy optimization, simple differentiable surrogate models provide smooth gradients. These models are approximations designed to capture the dominant physical interactions relevant to the policy being optimized:
 - **Point-Mass Model for Navigation Policy:** A point-mass model is used as a surrogate for the navigation policy. This model simplifies the robot's translational dynamics, focusing on position and velocity changes. It allows for efficient gradient-based optimization of the navigation policy's parameters ($\theta_{nav}$), as the loss function (Equation B1) includes terms for velocity tracking, obstacle avoidance, collision, speed, acceleration, direction, target direction, and time. Gradients for the navigation policy parameters are computed by backpropagating through this simplified model, which approximates how changes in commanded velocity affect the robot's position over time.
 - **Rigid-Body Model for Locomotion Policy:** A rigid-body model approximates the interactions between the robot's body and the ground, specifically focusing on rotational and translational dynamics caused by ground reaction forces. The low-level locomotion policy's parameters ($\theta_{loc}$) are optimized using gradients derived from this model. The locomotion loss (Equation C11) includes components for velocity tracking, orientation tracking, angular velocity tracking, body height, gravity projection, action regularization, torque regularization, and swing leg trajectory following. The ground reaction forces are derived from joint torques, which are generated by a PD controller based on the locomotion policy's output (desired joint positions).

**Gradient Calculation:**
The paper details how gradients of the loss function with respect to policy parameters are derived using backpropagation through time (BPTT). The state gradient and action gradient are recursively calculated. The key assumption is that the influence of model weights on the residual components (differences between surrogate and true dynamics) is ignored, simplifying the gradient calculation while preserving the utility of the surrogate.

**Training Procedure:**
An iterative training algorithm (Algorithm 1) is employed. Data is collected from the Isaac Gym simulation. The hierarchical policies compute commands (velocity/yaw for navigation, joint angles for locomotion). Navigation and locomotion losses are accumulated over specified steps. Gradients are computed using the respective surrogate models, and policy parameters are updated via the AdamW optimizer. For real-world deployment, the learned navigation policy can be integrated with either the learned locomotion policy or a built-in locomotion controller (e.g., Unitree's), demonstrating the flexibility of the decoupled design.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both real-world and simulated experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed end-to-end training framework.

**Real-World Demonstrations:**
Six physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robots, each equipped with a forward-facing depth camera, were deployed without additional fine-tuning. They operated using only local visual input and individual goals, without explicit communication or centralized coordination. Five scenarios were tested:
- **Forest:** Four robots navigated through a dense forest, exhibiting obstacle avoidance and adaptive terrain traversal despite camera disturbances from low vegetation.
- **Cluttered Pavilion & Narrow Gap:** Four robots navigated through a pavilion with furniture-like obstacles and a single-robot-width gap. Robots displayed emergent behaviors such as pausing to allow others to pass, leading to alternating passage and reduced collisions.
- **Bridge Crossing:** Two teams of three robots approached each other on a narrow bridge. They successfully coordinated movements using visual cues to exchange positions safely.
- **Obstacle-Dense Room:** Robots adjusted paths and occasionally paused to reduce congestion in a room with randomly placed obstacles.
These demonstrations indicated that emergent coordination behaviors arose from local sensing, physical interaction, and individual goal-directed learning.

**Vision-Based Mutual Avoidance in Dense Swarms (Simulation):**
The learned policy scaled to dense multi-robot settings and diverse cluttered environments in simulation (Isaac Gym) with up to 512 robots. Scenarios included:
- **Forest Crossing:** Three groups of robots navigated intersecting paths, dynamically adjusting speeds and directions to avoid static obstacles and other robots.
- **Grove Path:** Six robots traversed a narrow path in an orderly line, demonstrating precise control to maintain continuous movement.
- **Bridge Crossing:** Eight robots from opposing directions synchronized movements to avoid collisions in a tight space.
- **Fence Escape:** 48 robots navigated through a single narrow opening from random initial positions, exhibiting efficient collective behavior and bottleneck avoidance.

**Scalable Vision-Based Control for Large Robot Swarms (Quantitative Simulation):**
Scalability was quantitatively assessed using task progress rates and outcomes (success/timeout/turnover) in two environments:
- **Circular Fence (24-96 robots):** All robots successfully exited with up to 72 robots. With 96 robots, one robot failed due to overcrowding. The passage efficiency scaled consistently, with 96 robots taking less than twice the time of 48 robots. A single-agent baseline showed lower completion rates and more turnover events.
- **Complex Maze (128-512 robots):** Most robots successfully completed the maze. Time to 75% completion increased proportionally with robot count. Minor failures (less than 3%) occurred at larger scales due to sudden retreats leading to collisions/turnovers or limbs getting caught. The single-agent baseline performed significantly worse, especially at higher swarm sizes.

**Learned Multi-Robot Navigation Behaviors:**
The robots demonstrated several emergent coordination behaviors:
- **Predictive Avoidance:** Robots adjusted motions in response to other robots and dynamic obstacles, pausing or path-adjusting.
- **Right-Side Yielding:** In head-on encounters, robots consistently yielded to the right, an emergent pattern not explicitly programmed, reducing collision risk and improving progress.
- **Pausing in Open Areas:** Robots often stopped in open regions before entering conflict passages, reducing bottlenecks.
- **Wall-Following Tendencies:** Due to limited field of view and reactive collision response, robots tended to move along walls after contact, resembling wall-following, which aided navigation through narrow openings.
These behaviors emerged from local sensing and individual goal-directed learning, rather than centralized control or pre-specified interaction rules.

**Behavior Comparisons (Baselines):**
The proposed method was compared against a standard flocking algorithm, a depth-binning potential-based controller, and a single-agent vision policy.
- The flocking controller showed less stable interactions and more frequent reductions in clearance.
- The potential-based controller induced conservative detours and less consistent passage.
- The single-agent policy transferred basic obstacle avoidance but lacked ordered passage or consistent inter-robot spacing.
In contrast, the proposed policy produced smoother trajectories and more consistent distances to both neighboring robots and obstacles, attributed to exposure to multi-robot interactions during training.

**Baseline Comparisons (DPL vs. PPO):**
The differentiable physics learning (DPL) approach was compared to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) using identical training conditions.
- **Training Efficiency:** DPL converged with significantly fewer simulation steps and shorter wall-clock time, achieving PPO's final reward with only 2% of the samples and a higher overall reward.
- **Navigation Performance:** In intersecting paths, DPL showed more continuous and orderly swarm navigation, whereas PPO exhibited abrupt retreats. DPL yielded faster task progress and higher success rates, especially as robot density increased.
- **Speed Controllability:** DPL policies effectively scaled actual swarm velocity with commanded velocity (0.75 m/s to 1.5 m/s). PPO policies showed limited adaptability, saturating at lower realized speeds, suggesting difficulty in balancing speed and collision avoidance objectives.
- **Narrow-Region Traversal:** DPL policies successfully navigated narrow passages (down to 0.5m width), while PPO struggled below 0.8m, often failing to enter or explore randomly outside the path. This was attributed to DPL's ability to leverage deterministic gradients for precise trajectory adjustments, compared to PPO's stochastic action sampling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of swarm robotics and robot learning, particularly for embodied, contact-rich platforms. Its significance lies in several key areas:

- **Enabling Scalable, Vision-Based Swarm Navigation:** The paper demonstrates, for the first time to the authors' knowledge, vision-based learned swarm navigation in quadrupedal robots under decentralized sensing, contact-rich locomotion, and dense multi-robot interaction, both in high-fidelity simulation and in the physical world. This is a crucial step towards deploying large collectives in unstructured environments.
- **Validation of Asymmetric Physics Principle:** The core contribution is the successful application and validation of the "asymmetric physics" training principle. By decoupling the high-fidelity simulator for realistic forward dynamics from simpler differentiable surrogate models for efficient gradient computation, the authors address a fundamental challenge in robot learning: achieving realism without sacrificing learnability. This principle allows efficient extraction of first-order optimization signals from large-scale physical interaction, turning a sampling bottleneck into an effective training mechanism.
- **Emergent Decentralized Coordination:** The emergence of complex coordination behaviors like predictive avoidance, right-side yielding, pausing before bottlenecks, and wall-following—without explicit programming, communication, or centralized control—highlights the power of end-to-end visual training in multi-robot environments. These behaviors are qualitatively similar to those observed in natural swarms and human crowds, suggesting a path towards truly autonomous and adaptable robot collectives.
- **Improved Training Efficiency and Robustness:** Quantitative comparisons against PPO and heuristic baselines demonstrate superior sample efficiency, faster convergence, higher final rewards, better scalability, and finer control in constrained spaces (e.g., narrow passages). This addresses a major bottleneck in deploying multi-agent reinforcement learning to real-world systems.
- **Broad Applicability:** While demonstrated with quadrupedal robots, the authors suggest that the "asymmetric physics" principle could inform training methods for other contact-rich platforms, such as tracked robots and other legged machines, thereby extending its potential impact across various domains of embodied robotics.

**Potential Impact:**
The ability to train large, decentralized swarms of complex robots efficiently could unlock new applications in:
- **Environmental Monitoring:** Large-scale exploration and data collection in hazardous or remote areas (e.g., planetary exploration, disaster sites).
- **Search and Rescue:** Coordinated search operations in complex terrains where communication may be unreliable.
- **Logistics and Construction:** Flexible and adaptive material handling or construction tasks in dynamic environments.
- **Agriculture:** Autonomous monitoring and intervention in large-scale farming operations.

**Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several challenges for future research, including:
- Integrating RGB inputs for richer semantic understanding.
- Exploring the integration of explicit communication channels for tasks where such communication would be beneficial, while retaining the system's capability to function without it.
- Investigating policy transfer to robots with different morphologies to enhance versatility and robustness.
- Improving robustness to physical contact and handling dead-end scenarios more effectively.

Overall, this research provides a robust framework for learning complex, decentralized control in challenging robot swarms, paving the way for more autonomous and capable robotic collectives.
