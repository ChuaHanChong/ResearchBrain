# Improving Few-Shot Generalization by Exploring and Exploiting Auxiliary Data

> [!summary] Summary
> A method for Few-shot Learning with Auxiliary Data (FLAD) that models dataset selection as a Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) problem enables adaptive auxiliary data utilization. This approach boosts a 3 billion parameter model's performance by 5.6-9.2% over baselines, allowing it to surpass a 175 billion parameter GPT-3 on few-shot benchmarks.

## Problem

- Few-shot learning (FSL) struggles to generalize effectively from very limited labeled examples, hindering real-world applications.
- Previous Few-shot Learning with Auxiliary Data (FLAD) methods face severe scalability issues, typically only handling 1-3 auxiliary datasets due to increasing computational complexity.
- Manually selecting useful auxiliary data or designing training curricula for a large pool of datasets is combinatorially complex and often leads to sub-optimal results.

## Method

- The authors reformulate FLAD as a ==Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) problem==, treating each auxiliary dataset as an 'arm' to be explored and exploited.
- They design efficient ==gradient-based reward functions== (Gradient Alignment, Gradient Magnitude Similarity, Aggregate Reward) computed solely on language modeling head parameters to minimize overhead.
- Two MAB algorithms, ==EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD==, are adapted to dynamically select auxiliary datasets for model updates, balancing exploration of new data with exploitation of known beneficial data.

## Results

- EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD consistently outperformed all baselines, achieving absolute performance gains of **5.6%** to **9.2%** over Target-Only fine-tuning.
- A **3 billion parameter** T0-3B model, when utilizing MAB-FLAD with the P3 dataset, demonstrated superior performance to the **175 billion parameter** GPT-3 on the evaluated few-shot benchmarks.
- MAB-based methods exhibited robust scalability, improving performance with a larger number of auxiliary datasets (from **35** to **260**) while maintaining computational efficiency with modest increases in time-to-convergence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritized, adaptive sampling of auxiliary data via MAB methods is more effective than static mixing or loss-scaling approaches, especially with numerous auxiliary datasets.
> - An intelligent balance between exploring diverse auxiliary data and exploiting beneficial subsets is critical for improved few-shot generalization.
> - Effective data interaction strategies can enable smaller models (3 billion parameters) to achieve or exceed the few-shot performance of significantly larger models (175 billion parameters) by leveraging auxiliary data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the rapidly evolving and critically important domain of Few-shot Learning (FSL) in Natural Language Processing (NLP). FSL aims to develop models that can generalize to new tasks from very limited labeled examples, which is crucial for real-world applications where data annotation is expensive, time-consuming, or privacy-sensitive.

A specific sub-area of FSL, Few-shot Learning with Auxiliary Data (FLAD), forms the core focus of this paper. FLAD assumes access to additional auxiliary datasets, beyond the few target examples, to enhance model generalization. While FLAD has shown promise, previous approaches have encountered significant challenges, primarily related to **scalability and algorithmic complexity**. Prior FLAD methods often make strong assumptions, such as the availability of only 1-3 related auxiliary datasets, and their computational complexity typically scales linearly or worse with the number of auxiliary datasets. This limitation makes them impractical for leveraging the hundreds of diverse datasets now readily available (e.g., through platforms like Hugging Face Datasets or P3). Furthermore, manually selecting useful auxiliary data or designing training curricula for a large pool of datasets is combinatorially complex and often leads to sub-optimal results.

This paper addresses these critical limitations by introducing a novel perspective: formulating FLAD as a **Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB)** problem. MABs are a classic framework in reinforcement learning for sequential decision-making under uncertainty, where an agent must balance "exploration" (trying out new options to learn their rewards) and "exploitation" (choosing the option currently believed to be the best). By framing the selection of auxiliary datasets as "arms" in a bandit problem, the authors propose an adaptive, efficient mechanism for dynamically choosing which auxiliary data to incorporate during few-shot training.

This approach contrasts with several related paradigms in the research landscape:
- **Transfer Learning and Multi-task Learning:** These fields broadly deal with leveraging knowledge from one task or multiple tasks to improve performance on another. While FLAD uses auxiliary data, it focuses on optimizing for a *single* target task in a few-shot setting, unlike general multi-task learning which optimizes for multiple tasks simultaneously.
- **Meta-learning:** Aims to "learn to learn" across tasks, often training models that can quickly adapt to new tasks with few examples. While meta-learning can involve auxiliary data, the MAB formulation offers a distinct algorithmic strategy for *selecting* that data.
- **Data Selection:** Research on data selection typically focuses on identifying valuable subsets from a *single large dataset*. In contrast, this work deals with selecting data from *multiple, diverse auxiliary datasets*.
- **In-context Learning (ICL) with Large Language Models (LLMs):** Models like GPT-3 demonstrate impressive few-shot capabilities by conditioning on examples provided directly in the prompt, without explicit weight updates. This paper, however, aims to improve few-shot performance through *fine-tuning* based methods, providing a complementary approach that can be more computationally efficient for deployment and potentially yield stronger generalization in certain scenarios, especially for smaller models.

By innovatively applying the MAB framework, this research moves beyond the scalability constraints of prior FLAD methods, offering a principled, adaptive, and computationally efficient way to utilize vast repositories of auxiliary data. This positions the work as a significant advancement in making few-shot learning more robust and accessible, particularly for smaller models that cannot rely solely on the sheer scale of parameters as GPT-3 does.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **substantially improve few-shot generalization** of models in real-world applications by effectively utilizing auxiliary data. Specifically, the authors focus on the Few-shot Learning with Auxiliary Data (FLAD) paradigm, aiming to overcome its inherent limitations and unlock its full potential.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical challenges and desiderata:

1. **Addressing the Few-Shot Learning Challenge:** Few-shot learning is highly attractive due to its promise of efficiency in terms of cost and time, and its necessity in scenarios where data is scarce (e.g., due to privacy concerns, niche domains, or rare events). However, it remains a challenging setting where models often struggle to generalize without overfitting to the very limited labeled target data. Auxiliary data offers a path to alleviate this, but its effective integration is complex.

2. **Scalability of Auxiliary Data Utilization:** Previous FLAD methods, while demonstrating benefits, face severe scalability issues. They typically assume access to only a handful (1-3) of auxiliary datasets and their computational complexity grows linearly or worse with the number of such datasets. This limitation renders them impractical in an era where hundreds or thousands of diverse public datasets are available. The authors explicitly desire an algorithm that scales well with a large number of auxiliary datasets (e.g., 100x more than prior methods).

3. **Eliminating Manual Data Selection and Curriculum Design:** Incorporating auxiliary data introduces a vast and combinatorially large space of design choices regarding *how* and *when* to train on this data. Manually designing effective training curricula or hand-picking relevant auxiliary datasets based on heuristics (e.g., domain similarity) is not only burdensome but often leads to sub-optimal results. Therefore, a key motivation is to delegate these choices to an algorithm that can adaptively discover and leverage useful auxiliary data. The algorithm should make "no assumptions on available auxiliary data a-priori (in-domain, on-task, quality, quantity, etc.)".

4. **Minimizing Computational and Memory Overhead:** Any proposed solution for integrating auxiliary data must be efficient. Adding significant memory or computational overhead would negate the practical benefits of few-shot learning. The authors explicitly state a desideratum for their algorithm to add "minimal memory and computational overhead."

To fulfill these motivations and desiderata, the authors propose to relate FLAD to the **explore-exploit dilemma** inherent in Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) settings. This formulation is motivated by the need for an adaptive strategy that can intelligently navigate a large pool of auxiliary datasets, continuously learning which ones are most beneficial to the target task while minimizing resource consumption. By doing so, they aim to derive algorithms whose computational complexity is *independent* of the number of auxiliary datasets, thus enabling unprecedented scalability in FLAD.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research's methodology lies in reformulating Few-shot Learning with Auxiliary Data (FLAD) as a Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) problem. This innovative framing allows for the principled exploration and exploitation of a vast number of auxiliary datasets.

#### 3.1. Formulating FLAD as a Multi-Armed Bandit Problem

The authors define FLAD as a Markov Decision Process involving a **learner** and an **environment**, as illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper:
- **Environment:** Comprises a set of auxiliary datasets ($D_A$), a small target dataset ($D_T$), and the model ($f_\theta$) with parameters $\theta$.
- **Learner:** Possesses a policy ($\pi$) that dictates the selection strategy over the auxiliary datasets.
- **Interaction Cycle:** Over $N$ rounds (training steps), the learner performs the following five steps:
 1. **Select Dataset:** The learner selects an auxiliary dataset ($D_a$) from $D_A$ according to its policy $\pi$.
 2. **Sample Batch:** The environment samples a batch $\{x, y\}$ from the selected $D_a$.
 3. **Calculate Gradients & Update Model:** The model $f_\theta$ calculates gradients for the sampled auxiliary batch ($\nabla_a$) and the entire target dataset ($\nabla_T$). The model parameters $\theta$ are then updated based on the sum of these gradients ($\nabla_T + \nabla_a$).
 4. **Calculate Reward:** A gradient-based reward ($R_{a,t}$) is computed using $\nabla_a$ and $\nabla_T$.
 5. **Update Policy:** The learner updates its policy $\pi$ based on the observed reward $R_{a,t}$.

#### 3.2. Designing Efficient Reward Functions

To meet the desideratum of minimal overhead, the reward functions ($R$) are designed to be intrinsic to the model and loss optimization process, avoiding external metrics or models. Three gradient-based reward functions are proposed:
- **Gradient Alignment (R_GA):** Measures the cosine similarity between the auxiliary batch gradient ($\nabla_a$) and the target dataset gradient ($\nabla_T$). A high value indicates that the auxiliary data pushes the model parameters in a similar direction as the target data.
 $R_{GA}^{a,t} = \frac{\nabla_a \cdot \nabla_T}{\|\nabla_a\|_2 \|\nabla_T\|_2}$
- **Gradient Magnitude Similarity (R_GMS):** Quantifies how similar the magnitudes of the auxiliary and target gradients are. A value of 1 implies equal magnitudes, while values closer to 0 indicate significant differences, penalizing overshooting or undershooting the target's gradient magnitude.
 $R_{GMS}^{a,t} = \frac{2\|\nabla_a\|_2 \|\nabla_T\|_2}{\|\nabla_a\|_2^2 + \|\nabla_T\|_2^2}$
- **Aggregate Reward (R_AGG):** A sum of the normalized Gradient Alignment and Gradient Magnitude Similarity. This ensures that the policy considers both the direction and the magnitude match of the gradients.
 $R_{AGG}^{a,t} = \frac{1+R_{GA}^{a,t}}{2} + R_{GMS}^{a,t}$

The gradients for these rewards are specifically computed with respect to the language modeling head parameters, which constitute only 2.3% of the full model parameters, significantly reducing memory consumption and computational cost.

#### 3.3. Adapted Multi-Armed Bandit Algorithms

The authors adapt two classic MAB algorithms for the FLAD setting:
- **EXP3-FLAD:** Based on the "Exponential-weight algorithm for Exploration and Exploitation", designed for adversarial MAB settings where rewards can be non-stationary and highly stochastic. This is suitable for the complex, non-convex loss landscape of deep neural networks. EXP3-FLAD uses a Gibbs distribution mixed with a uniform distribution for selecting arms (auxiliary datasets), allowing for exploration. It employs an importance-weighted estimated reward to compensate for actions less likely to be chosen, ensuring robustness to non-stationary rewards. An exploration rate that decays over time is used to balance early exploration with later exploitation.
- **UCB1-FLAD:** Based on the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB1) algorithm, originally designed for stationary, normally distributed rewards. UCB1-FLAD adapts to non-stationarity by using an exponential moving average for estimating mean rewards. It adheres to the principle of "optimism in the face of uncertainty," prioritizing arms with high estimated rewards or those that have not been sufficiently explored. A unique aspect is the initialization of estimated rewards using a larger quantity of samples (1,000 examples) from each auxiliary dataset prior to training, providing a better initial approximation of true dataset gradients.

Both algorithms incorporate a gradient accumulation mechanism (parameter G) where the model is updated every G turns, summing gradients from multiple auxiliary batches before applying the full update.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Models:** The experiments utilize encoder-decoder Transformer models from the T5 family, specifically T5-LM XL (T5-XL) and T0-3B. These models have 2.85 billion parameters and are initialized from Hugging Face Transformers.
- **Target Datasets:** 11 held-out tasks are used for evaluation, covering diverse NLP tasks such as sentence completion (COPA, HellaSwag, Story Cloze), natural language inference (ANLI, CB, RTE), coreference resolution (WSC, Winogrande), and word sense disambiguation (WiC). Each task uses 20-70 few-shot examples, split into training and validation sets.
- **Auxiliary Datasets:** Two sets of auxiliary data are used:
 - **T0Mix:** 35 unique datasets originally used for multitask training of the T0 model.
 - **P3:** A larger superset of T0Mix, comprising 260 datasets from the Public Pool of Prompts (P3), after filtering to prevent overlap with target datasets. Each auxiliary dataset uses at most 10,000 examples.
- **Baseline Methods:**
 - **Target-Only:** Direct fine-tuning on the target dataset (no auxiliary data).
 - **Explore-Only:** Simultaneously trains on auxiliary and target data by mixing all auxiliary datasets equally (continuous exploration).
 - **Exploit-Only:** Computes gradient alignment pre-training and mixes auxiliary datasets according to a Gibbs distribution over these alignments (only exploitation).
 - **Loss-Scaling:** Scales auxiliary batch losses by their gradient alignment (GA) or gradient magnitude similarity (GMS) instead of modifying sampling probabilities.
- **Training Details:** Adafactor optimizer, validation-based early stopping, and consistent learning rates and batch sizes across methods for fair comparison.

This comprehensive methodology allows for rigorous testing of the proposed MAB-based FLAD algorithms against a range of baselines and across diverse tasks and auxiliary data quantities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimentation conducted in this paper reveals several significant findings that underscore the effectiveness and scalability of the proposed MAB-based FLAD methods.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance Over Baselines

- **Overall Outperformance:** Both EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD consistently outperform all pre-existing FLAD baselines (Explore-Only, Exploit-Only, Loss-Scaling) and the non-FLAD Target-Only fine-tuning. This is true across both base models (T5-XL and T0-3B) and both auxiliary dataset collections (T0Mix and the larger P3).
- **Quantifiable Gains:** When using T0Mix, EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD improve performance by 5.6% and 5.7% absolute, respectively, over the T0-3B model’s direct fine-tuning. These improvements become even more pronounced when leveraging the larger P3 dataset, reaching 9.1% and 9.2% absolute. The authors emphasize that their methods perform "equal to or better than the baselines" on every single task.

#### 4.2. Importance of Prioritized Sampling (vs. Loss-Scaling)

- The MAB-based methods significantly outperform Loss-Scaling methods by an average of 6.3%. This difference highlights a key insight: simply scaling auxiliary losses (Loss-Scaling) is less effective than *prioritizing and selecting* which auxiliary data to sample (MAB-based methods).
- Loss-Scaling struggles to leverage larger auxiliary data collections, showing minimal improvement (0.2-2%) when scaling from T0Mix to P3. In contrast, EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD demonstrate notable improvements of 2.6-4% with the same increase in auxiliary data, indicating their superior ability to discern and utilize beneficial data from a large pool. Analysis shows that for Loss-Scaling, the value of gradient alignments often converges to zero for most datasets, leading to negligible updates from auxiliary data.

#### 4.3. Crucial Role of Exploration and Exploitation

- The study empirically validates the necessity of combining both exploration and exploitation strategies. Surprisingly, Exploit-Only (which uses pre-computed relations) did not statistically outperform Explore-Only (which samples uniformly) even though it attempts to utilize relational information.
- Furthermore, both Explore-Only and Exploit-Only showed limited gains (0.7-2%) when scaling from 35 (T0Mix) to 260 (P3) auxiliary datasets. This contrasts sharply with the substantial 2.6-4% improvement achieved by EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD, confirming that an adaptive balance between exploring new datasets and exploiting known beneficial ones is critical, especially when dealing with a large and diverse collection of auxiliary data.

#### 4.4. Outperforming State-of-the-Art Few-Shot Methods, Including GPT-3

- One of the most remarkable findings is that models trained with the proposed FLAD methods, specifically the 3 billion parameter T0-3B model using R_AGG on P3, outperform several state-of-the-art few-shot learning methods, including T-Few, DEFT-Few, and the 175 billion parameter GPT-3 (using in-context learning).
- This represents a significant milestone, as it is, to the best of the authors' knowledge, "the first 3 billion parameter model that improves over 175B GPT-3 using few-shot in-context learning" on the evaluated dataset mixture. This demonstrates that intelligent data mixing strategies can lead to superior generalization even with significantly fewer parameters, offering a more resource-efficient path to high performance.
- The FLAD-based methods exhibit robust performance, ranking first or second on 8 out of 11 target datasets and never performing worst, indicating consistent generalization benefits.

#### 4.5. Insights into Reward-Generating Processes and Training Dynamics

- **Reward Function Dynamics:** Case studies reveal distinct behaviors for the reward functions. R_GA tends to produce more stationary and polarized reward distributions, with many auxiliary datasets quickly yielding close-to-zero rewards while a few maintain strong positive or negative alignment. R_GMS, on the other hand, shows more non-Gaussian and flatter distributions, with slower convergence to zero for "unhelpful" datasets.
- **Algorithm Dynamics:** The case studies on specific tasks (e.g., RTE and COPA) highlight the differences between UCB1-FLAD's greedy policy and EXP3-FLAD's stochastic policy. UCB1-FLAD tends to form bimodal sampling distributions, efficiently focusing on a subset of beneficial auxiliary datasets. EXP3-FLAD, while effective, samples more uniformly, occasionally including "bad" batches due to its higher exploration rate, which might explain its slightly inferior performance on some tasks where UCB1-FLAD excels.

#### 4.6. Scalability with Number of Auxiliary Datasets

- Empirical tests confirm the scalability of the MAB-based methods. Both EXP3-FLAD and UCB1-FLAD show increased target task performance as the number of auxiliary datasets (|A|) grows from 35 to 260. A sharp increase in accuracy is observed between |A|=35 and |A|=75, with further improvements up to |A|=260.
- Regarding time-to-convergence, while all methods require more training iterations for larger |A|, MAB-based methods show a more modest increase (∼10% for a 10x increase in |A|) compared to baselines like Explore-Only (which requires over 50% more iterations for P3 than T0Mix). This demonstrates their computational efficiency gains at scale.

In summary, the main findings strongly support the MAB formulation for FLAD, demonstrating significant performance improvements, robust generalization, and impressive scalability, making it a highly effective strategy for few-shot learning with large and diverse auxiliary data collections.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in the field of few-shot learning, offering both immediate practical benefits and opening new avenues for future research. The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:

#### 5.1. Algorithmic Innovation and Efficiency

The core contribution is the novel and principled formulation of Few-shot Learning with Auxiliary Data (FLAD) as a Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) problem. This addresses a critical, long-standing bottleneck: the inability of prior FLAD methods to scale effectively with a large number of auxiliary datasets. By designing algorithms whose computational complexity is independent of the number of auxiliary datasets, the authors have created a highly efficient framework. The use of only language modeling head gradients for reward calculation further minimizes memory and computational overhead, making the approach practical for training large language models. This efficiency is crucial for democratizing access to powerful NLP models, as it allows for strong performance without requiring exorbitant computational resources.

#### 5.2. Performance Breakthrough and Resource Optimization

The most compelling result is the empirical demonstration that models trained with the proposed MAB-based FLAD methods (3 billion parameters) can outperform the 175 billion parameter GPT-3 on specific few-shot benchmarks. This is a monumental achievement, challenging the notion that performance in few-shot settings is solely dependent on model scale. It highlights that intelligent data interaction strategies can yield equivalent or superior generalization with dramatically fewer parameters, leading to significant savings in training, deployment, and inference costs. This directly contributes to sustainable AI research and application by providing a viable path towards smaller, more efficient, yet highly capable models.

#### 5.3. Robustness and Generalization

The consistent outperformance of MAB-FLAD methods over various baselines across a diverse set of 11 target tasks underscores their robustness. The findings emphasize that dynamically exploring and exploiting auxiliary data is far more effective than static mixing strategies (Explore-Only) or pre-computed alignments (Exploit-Only), especially when the utility of auxiliary data is unknown a-priori. This improves the overall generalization capabilities of models in few-shot settings, making them more reliable for novel, unseen tasks.

#### 5.4. Enabling Large-Scale Auxiliary Data Utilization

The work unlocks the potential of vast, diverse collections of auxiliary datasets, such as the Public Pool of Prompts (P3), which were previously difficult to leverage effectively. By removing the assumptions about auxiliary data quality and making the selection process adaptive, the methods empower practitioners to utilize broader data resources without extensive manual curation. This is particularly valuable in data-scarce domains or for low-resource languages, where even seemingly unrelated auxiliary data might contain beneficial signals.

#### 5.5. Guiding Future Research Directions

This research opens several promising avenues for future work:
- **Improved Reward Functions:** Exploring more sophisticated or dynamically adapting reward functions, including those potentially using validation data while carefully mitigating overfitting risks.
- **Further Space Complexity Reduction:** Investigating techniques to reduce memory footprint even further, perhaps by more aggressively compressing gradient information.
- **Multi-Target Task Learning:** Extending the MAB framework to simultaneously optimize for multiple target tasks, potentially bridging the gap between FLAD and multi-task learning paradigms.
- **Theoretical Guarantees for Practical Performance:** While the paper omits theoretical regret bounds (due to their disconnect from actual accuracy), future work could aim to develop more meaningful theoretical insights into the performance of MAB-FLAD in deep learning contexts.
- **Understanding Dynamics:** Further in-depth analysis of the interplay between MAB policies, reward signals, and the complex dynamics of large language model training could yield deeper insights into why certain auxiliary datasets become beneficial over time.

#### 5.6. Open Science Contribution

The provision of open-source code on GitHub is a commendable aspect that fosters reproducibility, transparency, and collaborative development. This allows other researchers to build upon these methods, accelerate advancements in few-shot learning, and integrate them into various applications.

In conclusion, "Improving Few-Shot Generalization by Exploring and Exploiting Auxiliary Data" represents a significant step forward in making few-shot learning more practical, efficient, and powerful. By elegantly translating the challenge of auxiliary data selection into a well-understood MAB framework, the authors provide a scalable, high-performing solution that pushes the boundaries of what smaller models can achieve, with profound implications for the future of resource-efficient and generalizable AI.
