# FAR: Failure-Aware Retry for Test-Time Recovery and Continual Policy Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> Robot policies gain the ability to learn from their own failures at test time and autonomously improve, through a framework called Failure-Aware Retry (FAR). This approach integrates failure-contrastive preference adaptation with localized action perturbations, yielding an average 17.6% improvement in task success rates over standard diffusion policies in simulation and demonstrating enhanced data efficiency for continual learning.

## Problem

- Robot policies trained on offline demonstrations often degrade in real environments due to unforeseen failures, leading to repeated mistakes during naive retries.
- Existing failure recovery methods frequently require significant human intervention or rely on non-parametric strategies that do not adapt the underlying policy at test time.
- Continual policy improvement through offline-to-online reinforcement learning is typically data- and labor-intensive, often requiring costly environment resets after each failure.

## Method

- **FAR** employs ==Failure-Contrastive Preference Adaptation (FCPA)== and lightweight ==action perturbations== to enable immediate, ==test-time adaptation== after a failure, guiding the policy away from unsuccessful behaviors.
- It uses conservative value estimates from ==IQL-style Q and V functions==, combined with a ==failure attribution== method, to identify specific failure-inducing action chunks.
- Successful recovery trajectories generated by **FAR** are integrated into a ==continual policy improvement loop==, updating critics and the actor with ==advantage-weighted objectives== for long-term policy enhancement and data efficiency.

## Results

- FAR consistently improved task success rates, achieving an average gain of **17.6%** over standard Diffusion Policies in simulation and demonstrating similar efficacy in real-world xArm manipulation tasks.
- The framework showed superior data efficiency for continual policy improvement, reducing environment reset costs and achieving higher success rates under the same environment timestep budget.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both Failure-Contrastive Preference Adaptation (FCPA), which prevents repeated failures, and action perturbations, which encourage exploration, are crucial for optimal performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Autonomous learning from online failure experiences at test time, without human intervention or environment resets, can effectively enable rapid policy adaptation for recovery.
> - A contrastive learning objective, by explicitly optimizing against failed actions while preferring slightly perturbed successful alternatives, effectively steers diffusion policies away from past mistakes.
> - Exploiting informative 'hard failure cases' and successful recovery trajectories for continual learning significantly improves data efficiency, reducing the need for costly environment resets in online policy improvement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Robot policies, particularly those trained on offline expert demonstrations, have demonstrated capabilities across various manipulation tasks. However, their performance often degrades when deployed in real environments due to inevitable failures, such as missed grasps or dropped objects. These failure states are frequently out-of-distribution with respect to the initial training data, leading to policies repeating the same mistakes during naive retries.

Current approaches to robot failure recovery often involve significant human intervention, such as in DAgger or related human-in-the-loop methods. These methods require human operators to correct robot behavior and collect additional data, which is resource-intensive. Other recovery techniques employ pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) for failure detection, use natural language for reasoning and correction, or train reinforcement learning (RL) policies to recover from unsafe states. While effective, these methods typically do not involve active learning from failures *at test time* without human supervision, or they rely on non-parametric strategies that may not generalize well to altered environments.

This paper positions its work, Failure-Aware Retry (FAR), within the context of retry-based recovery methods, test-time adaptation (TTA), and offline-to-online reinforcement learning. Existing retry-based methods, such as Bellman-Guided Retrials, aim to prevent repeated failures but are often non-parametric and may not adapt the underlying policy. FAR distinguishes itself by studying *test-time learning* from failure experience, enabling the policy to improve its subsequent retries using immediate interaction feedback without human intervention. This bridges a gap by allowing autonomous adaptation at the point of failure.

In the broader field of test-time adaptation, which aims to adapt pretrained models to unlabeled test data during inference, FAR extends this concept specifically to robotics for failure recovery. It focuses on adapting a policy based on previous failed attempts without resetting the task, thereby enabling failure-aware retry.

Furthermore, FAR integrates with offline-to-online RL, a paradigm for improving policies pretrained on offline data through additional online interaction. While offline-to-online methods are effective, they are often data- and labor-intensive, frequently resetting the environment after a failure. FAR addresses this by emphasizing learning from "hard failure cases" that reveal the policy's limitations. By enabling recovery within an episode, FAR aims to underutilize costly environment resets and improve data efficiency during continual policy improvement.

In summary, FAR contributes to making robots more autonomous and robust by developing a framework for self-improving policies that can learn from their own failures at deployment time, reducing the need for human intervention and increasing data efficiency in ongoing learning.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent fragility of robot policies when deployed in real-world environments. Despite strong performance in controlled settings or benchmarks, robot policies inevitably encounter failures (e.g., missing a target, dropping an object) that are often due to environmental variations, unforeseen disturbances, or out-of-distribution states. When such failures occur, a simple re-execution of the same policy frequently leads to the repetition of the identical mistake.

The key objectives of the Failure-Aware Retry (FAR) framework are:

1. **Enabling Autonomous Learning from Failures at Test Time:** The central goal is to empower robots to learn from their own unsuccessful attempts during task execution, rather than solely relying on human intervention or predefined recovery strategies. This learning should occur in real-time or near real-time, allowing the robot to adapt its behavior immediately after a failure.
2. **Facilitating Autonomous Task Completion:** By learning from failures, the adapted policy should be able to recover from the failed state and ultimately complete the task autonomously, even if the initial attempt was unsuccessful.
3. **Improving Retry Success Rates and Robustness:** A direct objective is to significantly increase the probability of success on subsequent attempts after an initial failure, thereby improving the overall robustness of the robot's operation in dynamic and uncertain environments.
4. **Addressing Core Challenges in Retry-Based Recovery:**
 - **Avoiding Repetition of Mistakes:** To counteract the tendency of policies to repeat the same errors, FAR aims to steer the policy away from previously unsuccessful behaviors.
 - **Enhancing Exploration in Out-of-Distribution States:** Pretrained policies, particularly those based on offline demonstrations, may have limited support or ineffective exploration strategies in the out-of-distribution states encountered during failures. FAR seeks to encourage local exploration to discover alternative, successful actions.
5. **Generating Informative Recovery Trajectories for Continual Policy Improvement:** Successful recovery trajectories, which demonstrate how to overcome difficult failure states, are considered valuable data. A key objective is to leverage these trajectories to further improve the policy over the long term, making it more resilient and capable of handling a wider range of challenging situations.
6. **Improving Data Efficiency in Continual Online Learning:** Standard online learning often involves frequent environment resets after failures, which is costly and inefficient. FAR aims to reduce the need for such resets by allowing multiple attempts within an episode, thereby generating informative data from "hard failure cases" that reveal the policy's boundaries, leading to more data-efficient policy improvement.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond reactive recovery methods to a proactive, learning-based approach where robots can autonomously diagnose, adapt, and improve from their own failures, thereby enhancing their reliability and reducing operational costs in real-world deployments.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The Failure-Aware Retry (FAR) framework is designed to enable robots to learn from past failures and adapt their behavior for successful task completion, integrating both test-time recovery and continual policy improvement. The framework starts with a robot policy pretrained on offline expert demonstrations, which is instantiated as a Diffusion Policy.

#### 3.1. Failure-Aware Retry (Test-Time Adaptation)

This component focuses on immediate adaptation after a failure to improve subsequent retries without resetting the environment. It consists of Failure-Contrastive Preference Adaptation (FCPA) and lightweight action perturbations.

1. **Conservative Value Estimation:**
 - A Q-function ($Q_\phi$) and a value function ($V_\psi$) are trained using an IQL-style objective on offline demonstrations.
 - The value network is trained with an expectile regression loss (L_V), while the Q-function is trained with a squared TD error loss (L_Q).
 - This approach is adopted to ensure conservative value estimates, as failure states are often out-of-distribution for the pretrained policy, preventing overestimation. The Q-function evaluates action chunks, while the environment executes a single control action aggregated from the chunk.

2. **Failure Attribution:**
 - When a policy fails, the collected trajectory $\tau = \{(s_t, a_t)\}_{t=0}^T$ is analyzed retrospectively.
 - Failure-inducing action chunks are identified by measuring the change in value across the chunk: $\Delta V_t = V_\psi(s_{t+H}) - V_\psi(s_t)$.
 - A significant decrease in value indicates a failure-inducing behavior. Instead of a fixed threshold, action chunks with the largest value drops (lowest $\rho$-percentile, typically 10%) are selected as negative samples ($\tau^-$).

3. **Contrastive Sample Construction:**
 - To provide informative policy updates, contrastive pairs are constructed for each negative sample $(s_t, a_t^-)$.
 - A set of candidate action chunks $C(s_t)$ is sampled from the current policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot | s_t)$.
 - These candidates are filtered based on their $L_2$ distance $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ to $a_t^-$. Only candidates within a moderate deviation range $[d_{min}, d_{max}]$ (e.g., $d_{mean}$ to 1.0) are kept in $C_{safe}(s_t)$, ensuring they are not too similar to the failed action but also not too far from the policy's original distribution.
 - The remaining candidates in $C_{safe}(s_t)$ are ranked by their Q-values ($Q_\phi(s_t, a^{(i)}_t)$), and the top-K highest-valued ones are selected as positive samples ($A_t^+$).
 - These positive samples, paired with the negative samples, form contrastive preference pairs $\{(a_t^+, a_t^-) | a_t^+ \in A_t^+\}$.

4. **Failure-Contrastive Preference Adaptation (FCPA):**
 - The policy is adapted using a preference optimization objective, inspired by DPO.
 - For diffusion policies, the negative denoising loss $\ell_{diff}$ serves as a surrogate preference score.
 - The objective $L_{pref} = - \log \sigma(\beta (\ell_{diff}(a_t^-, s_t; \theta) - \ell_{diff}(a_t^+, s_t; \theta)))$ is optimized. This encourages the policy to assign lower denoising error (i.e., higher likelihood) to preferred (positive) chunks and higher error to failed (negative) chunks.
 - Test-time adaptation requires only 5–10 gradient steps, completing within seconds.

5. **Perturbation for Retry Exploration:**
 - To encourage exploration in out-of-distribution states, lightweight perturbations are injected into the executed actions during retries.
 - In simulation, simple Gaussian perturbations are applied.
 - For real-world deployment, to reduce high-frequency jitter, perturbations are smoothed over time. With probability $\epsilon_{explore}$, a target perturbation $\delta^\star \sim N(0, \Sigma)$ is sampled and held for $h$ steps. The actual perturbation $\delta_t$ is updated via exponential smoothing: $\delta_t = \alpha \delta_{t-1} + (1-\alpha) \delta^\star$.
 - The executed action is $\tilde{a}_t = \hat{a}_t + \delta_t$, where $\hat{a}_t$ is the aggregated action from the policy's predicted chunk.

#### 3.2. Continual Policy Improvement

Successful recovery trajectories generated by FAR are valuable for long-term policy improvement. FAR integrates into a continual policy improvement loop:

1. **Replay Buffers:** Three replay buffers are maintained:
 - $D_{exp}$: Stores offline expert demonstrations.
 - $D_{succ}$: Stores successful online trajectories, including those achieved through recovery.
 - $D_{fail}$: Stores failed trajectories.
2. **Critic Learning:**
 - The critic models ($V_\psi$, $Q_\phi$) are continually updated using the aggregated dataset $D = D_{exp} \cup D_{succ} \cup D_{fail}$. This helps refine value estimates, especially for states encountered online.
3. **Advantage-weighted Policy Update:**
 - After critic training, the advantage $A(s,a) = Q_\phi(s,a) - V_\psi(s)$ is computed for each sample.
 - A weight $w(s,a) = \exp(A(s,a)/\eta)$ (where $\eta$ is a temperature parameter) is assigned to each sample.
 - The actor (diffusion policy) is updated only on $D_{exp} \cup D_{succ}$ using an advantage-weighted denoising objective: $L_\pi = E_{(s,a_0)\sim D_{exp}\cup D_{succ}, k, \epsilon} [w(s,a_0) \| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(a_k, s, k)\|^2]$.
 - This ensures that the policy learns from high-quality behaviors and expands its support by incorporating successful recovery trajectories, particularly from challenging states where the initial policy failed, thereby improving robustness and value estimation over time.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research evaluates FAR across various simulation benchmarks (ManiSkill, RoboSuite, RoboMimic, comprising nine tasks) and three real-world manipulation tasks using an xArm platform.

#### 4.1. Test-time Failure Recovery Performance

- **Improved Success Rates:** FAR consistently improved task success rates compared to baselines. In simulation, it achieved average gains of 17.6% over the standard Diffusion Policy (DP) and outperformed Naive Retry (DP-NR) and Bellman-Guided Retrials (DP-BGR). Specifically, FAR showed an average improvement of 16.4% over the base policy in ManiSkill and RoboSuite benchmarks (Table 1), and 70.9% average success rate on RoboMimic compared to 52.2% for DP (Table 2).
- **Real-World Efficacy:** Similar trends were observed in real-world tasks, with FAR achieving higher recovery success rates, particularly in challenging scenarios like "Pot" and "Tea" (Figure 2). This demonstrates the method's practical applicability beyond simulation.
- **Limitations of Baselines:** Naive Retry (DP-NR) often repeated similar mistakes. Bellman-Guided Retrials (DP-BGR), a non-parametric method, struggled when previous failures altered the environment, leading to distribution shifts that reduced the effectiveness of its feedback. FAR's local test-time updates addressed these limitations.

#### 4.2. Continual Policy Improvement and Data Efficiency

- **Steady Improvement:** The policy's performance steadily improved with additional environment interactions and newly collected experience when integrated into the continual policy improvement loop (Figure 4, Appendix Figure 6).
- **Reduced Reset Costs:** FAR demonstrated superior data efficiency by reducing the number of costly environment resets. By allowing more attempts within each episode, it collected more training data from challenging failure cases, which are highly informative. This led to more stable learning signals and robust policy improvement.
- **Improved Timestep Efficiency:** Under the same environment timestep budget, FAR achieved higher success rates compared to baselines, further indicating its data efficiency (Appendix Figure 6).
- **Sparse Reward Robustness:** Even with an extremely sparse reward function, FAR still achieved continual improvement and effectively enhanced the data efficiency of online learning (Appendix Figure 7). This highlights its ability to learn from informative failure cases.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Contributions of FAR Components:** Both Failure-Contrastive Preference Adaptation (FCPA) and action perturbation contributed to performance. FCPA helped the policy avoid repeating failures, while perturbation encouraged exploration. Combining both components yielded the best overall performance (Table 3, Figure 5a).
- **Failure Attribution Method:** Using a percentile of value differences (Value-Percentile) to identify failure-inducing action chunks for negative samples proved more effective than a fixed threshold or using the entire trajectory. This provided more stable training samples and gradients (Table 3).
- **Adaptation Objective for FCPA:** The pairwise preference objective, which utilizes both positive and negative samples, outperformed alternatives. Training without positive samples quickly distorted the policy, while training without negative samples did not effectively exploit failure feedback. Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR) also performed worse, as it primarily used positive updates from failure trajectories without explicitly avoiding failed behaviors (Table 3).
- **Positive Sample Construction:** Critic-guided selection and filtering of positive samples was more effective than random sampling, as it ensured quality and diversity of alternatives (Appendix Table 4). A moderate number of positive samples (K=8) yielded optimal results, balancing stable guidance with avoiding noisy samples (Appendix Table 4).
- **Perturbation Strategy:** A moderate fixed perturbation probability ($\epsilon_{explore} = 0.5$) achieved the best balance between exploration and exploitation. For real-world deployment, smoothed perturbations maintained performance while being safer and reducing jitter compared to pure Gaussian noise (Appendix Table 5).
- **Number of Attempts:** Success rates generally increased with more allowed attempts per episode, eventually saturating, indicating the inherent limits of the model's capabilities (Figure 5b).

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The research on Failure-Aware Retry (FAR) holds several significant implications and potential impacts for the field of robotics and artificial intelligence:

1. **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** By enabling robots to learn from their own failures at test time and adapt their behavior, FAR moves towards truly autonomous and robust robotic systems. This reduces the need for constant human supervision and intervention, which is a major bottleneck in deploying robots in complex, unstructured, and unpredictable real-world environments. Robots can become more resilient to unforeseen circumstances and environmental variations.

2. **Improved Efficiency in Real-World Deployment:** The ability of FAR to perform test-time adaptation within seconds and successfully recover from failures without costly environment resets directly translates to greater operational efficiency. Each failed attempt becomes a learning opportunity rather than a terminal state requiring a full reset and potential human re-setup, saving time, resources, and reducing wear and tear on hardware.

3. **Data-Efficient Continual Learning:** FAR significantly improves the data efficiency of online policy improvement. By specifically exploiting "hard failure cases" and the successful recovery trajectories they generate, the framework provides highly informative supervision. This targeted learning from critical experiences helps the policy expand its capabilities and robustness more efficiently than generic data collection, addressing a key challenge in real-world reinforcement learning where data acquisition is expensive.

4. **Foundation for Advanced Recovery Systems:** The proposed framework provides a foundation upon which more sophisticated recovery mechanisms can be built. The paper explicitly mentions future work integrating principled failure detection mechanisms, such as those leveraging Vision-Language Models (VLMs), and extending the method to larger, pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This could enable stage-level retry and recovery for highly complex, long-horizon tasks.

5. **Generalizability Across Robot Platforms and Tasks:** The evaluation across diverse simulated manipulation tasks and real-world scenarios on an xArm platform suggests that the core principles of FAR—combining failure-contrastive adaptation with local exploration—are broadly applicable. This indicates potential for adoption across various robotic domains.

6. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** By improving a policy's ability to adapt to unseen failure modes in the real world, FAR contributes to overcoming aspects of the sim-to-real gap, where policies trained in simulation often struggle with the nuances of physical deployment. The real-world experiments demonstrate its effectiveness in practical scenarios.

In summary, FAR represents a step towards self-improving robots that can autonomously learn from and overcome their mistakes, thereby accelerating the development and deployment of intelligent robotic systems capable of operating reliably in dynamic and uncertain environments.
