# Simple random search provides a competitive approach to reinforcement learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Augmented Random Search (ARS), a simple derivative-free optimization algorithm utilizing linear policies, achieves competitive or superior sample efficiency compared to complex state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods on standard MuJoCo locomotion benchmarks. The work also highlights significant performance variance in RL algorithms through rigorous evaluation with 100 random seeds, advocating for more robust evaluation methodologies.

## Problem

- Contemporary reinforcement learning algorithms have become overly complex, leading to implementation difficulties, poor reproducibility, and limited practical adoption.
- Standard RL evaluation practices often lack statistical rigor, using few random seeds and failing to account for the impact of extensive hyperparameter tuning on reported sample efficiency.
- The prevailing belief that action-space exploration methods are inherently more sample-efficient than parameter-space exploration methods in RL.

## Method

- Introduces **Augmented Random Search (ARS)**, a simple ==derivative-free optimization== algorithm that builds upon ==Basic Random Search==.
- Employs simple ==linear policies==, πθ(x)=Mx\pi_θ(x) = Mxπθ(x)=Mx, for continuous control tasks, directly optimizing policy parameters.
- Incorporates three key augmentations: ==reward scaling==, ==online state normalization==, and selecting only the ==top-performing directions== for policy updates, enhancing stability and efficiency.

## Results

- Augmented Random Search (ARS) achieves sample efficiency competitive with or superior to state-of-the-art policy gradient methods on **six** standard MuJoCo locomotion benchmarks, with linear policies reaching over **11,500 average reward** on Humanoid-v1.
- The algorithm demonstrates high computational efficiency, running **15x faster** than Evolution Strategies on Humanoid-v1 and achieving high rewards (e.g., **6000** on Hopper-v1) in a median of **21 minutes** using **48 CPUs**.
- Extensive evaluations using **100 random seeds** revealed significant performance variance across RL algorithms, highlighting that reported success rates depend heavily on both random seeds and hyperparameter choices.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simple linear policies are sufficient for achieving high performance on various MuJoCo locomotion tasks, challenging the necessity of complex neural network architectures.
> - Reinforcement learning algorithms, including ARS, exhibit substantial performance variance across random seeds and hyperparameter configurations, underscoring the need for more rigorous evaluation.
> - Rigorous evaluation practices, using a high number of random seeds (e.g., 100), are crucial for accurately assessing and comparing RL algorithm performance and understanding their true variability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning has experienced significant growth in complexity, with increasingly sophisticated algorithms designed to achieve better sample efficiency on continuous control tasks. However, this paper by Mania, Guy, and Recht from UC Berkeley challenges a fundamental assumption in the field: that complex policy gradient methods exploring action spaces are inherently more sample-efficient than simple parameter space exploration methods.

The research introduces Augmented Random Search (ARS), a remarkably simple derivative-free optimization algorithm that combines basic random search with linear policies. Despite its simplicity, ARS achieves competitive or superior sample efficiency compared to state-of-the-art methods on standard MuJoCo locomotion benchmarks while being computationally efficient and easy to reproduce.

### 2. Background and Motivation

The paper addresses several critical issues plaguing modern reinforcement learning:

**The Complexity Crisis**: Contemporary RL methods have become increasingly complex, incorporating sophisticated neural architectures, intricate optimization procedures, and numerous hyperparameters. This complexity often leads to implementation difficulties, poor reproducibility, and limited adoption in real-world systems beyond research demonstrations.

**Evaluation Shortcomings**: Standard evaluation practices in RL typically involve running algorithms on a small number of random seeds (3-10), which fails to capture the true performance variability. Additionally, extensive hyperparameter tuning on benchmark tasks can artificially inflate reported sample efficiency, creating a form of overfitting to specific evaluation environments.

**The Parameter vs Action Space Exploration Debate**: The prevailing belief in RL is that methods exploring action spaces (like policy gradients) are fundamentally more sample-efficient than those exploring parameter spaces (like evolutionary strategies or random search). This paper directly challenges this assumption with empirical evidence.

### 3. Methodology: Augmented Random Search

ARS builds upon Basic Random Search (BRS), a classical derivative-free optimization method, with three key augmentations that significantly improve performance:

#### 3.1. Core Algorithm Structure

The algorithm treats RL as a noisy derivative-free optimization problem, seeking to maximize expected reward:

$$E_{\xi}[r(\pi_\theta, \xi)]$$

where $\pi_\theta$ represents a deterministic linear policy $\pi_\theta(x) = Mx$, with $M$ being a $p \times n$ matrix of policy parameters.

#### 3.2. Three Key Augmentations

**1. Reward Scaling (ARS V1)**
To handle varying reward magnitudes during training, ARS scales update steps by the standard deviation of rewards collected at each iteration:

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + \frac{\alpha}{\sigma_R} \sum_{k=1}^N \delta_k [r(\pi_{\theta_t + \nu\delta_k}) - r(\pi_{\theta_t - \nu\delta_k})]$$

This adaptive scaling prevents unstable updates when reward distributions change.

**2. Online State Normalization (ARS V2)**
Linear policies applied to states with vastly different scales can lead to poor performance. ARS addresses this by normalizing states online:

$$\pi(x) = M \cdot \text{diag}(\Sigma_j)^{-1/2} (x - \mu_j)$$

where $\mu_j$ and $\Sigma_j$ are running estimates of state means and standard deviations.

**3. Top-Performing Direction Selection (ARS V2-t)**
Instead of using all sampled directions, ARS sorts directions by their maximum achieved reward and uses only the top $b$ directions for updates. This filtering reduces the impact of noisy evaluations.

#### 3.3. Computational Implementation

The authors implemented ARS using Ray for parallelization, employing a shared noise table technique to minimize communication overhead. This approach makes ARS highly scalable and computationally efficient compared to competing methods.

### 4. Experimental Design and Evaluation

The paper employs unusually rigorous evaluation methodologies that reveal important insights about RL algorithm performance:

#### 4.1. Extensive Statistical Analysis

Unlike typical RL evaluations using 3-10 random seeds, the authors conducted experiments with **100 random seeds** per configuration. This extensive sampling revealed that RL algorithms, including ARS, exhibit substantial performance variance across different random initializations.

#### 4.2. Hyperparameter Sensitivity Studies

The research systematically evaluated performance across multiple hyperparameter configurations, demonstrating that algorithm success rates vary significantly with hyperparameter choices, similar to the variance observed across random seeds.

#### 4.3. Benchmark Environments

The evaluation focused on six MuJoCo locomotion tasks: Swimmer-v1, Hopper-v1, HalfCheetah-v1, Walker2d-v1, Ant-v1, and Humanoid-v1. Additionally, the authors proposed Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) with unknown dynamics as a new benchmark due to its known optimal solutions and ability to generate multiple problem instances.

### 5. Key Findings

#### 5.1. Sample Efficiency Performance

ARS achieves competitive or superior sample efficiency compared to state-of-the-art methods:
- **Swimmer-v1**: ARS matches performance of existing methods
- **Hopper-v1**: ARS achieves similar sample efficiency to top performers
- **HalfCheetah-v1**: Competitive performance with simpler implementation
- **Walker2d-v1**: ARS V2-t outperforms most competing methods
- **Ant-v1**: Strong performance comparable to complex alternatives
- **Humanoid-v1**: ARS finds linear policies achieving over 11,500 average reward

#### 5.2. Linear Policy Sufficiency

A striking finding is that simple linear policies prove adequate for all tested MuJoCo locomotion tasks. These policies can produce diverse behaviors including various gaits, backward walking, and complex movement patterns, challenging the necessity of neural network policies for these benchmarks.

#### 5.3. Computational Efficiency

ARS demonstrates exceptional computational efficiency:
- **15x faster than Evolution Strategies** on Humanoid-v1
- Achieves 6000 reward in median 21 minutes on 48 CPUs
- Significantly less total CPU time compared to competing methods

#### 5.4. Algorithm Variance Revelation

The extensive evaluation reveals that:
- RL algorithms exhibit **high training variance** across random seeds
- Performance can vary dramatically even with identical hyperparameters
- Current evaluation practices underestimate true algorithm variability
- Success rates depend heavily on both random seeds and hyperparameter choices

#### 5.5. LQR Benchmark Results

On the proposed LQR benchmark, ARS finds near-optimal solutions but requires more samples than model-based methods or specialized approaches like LSPI, highlighting the continued importance of problem-appropriate algorithm selection.

### 6. Implications for Reinforcement Learning

#### 6.1. Challenging Complexity Assumptions

The paper demonstrates that sophisticated neural networks and complex policy gradient methods may not always be necessary for achieving strong performance on standard benchmarks. This finding suggests the field may benefit from establishing stronger simple baselines before pursuing complex solutions.

#### 6.2. Evaluation Methodology Reform

The research provides compelling evidence for reforming RL evaluation practices:
- **Increased statistical rigor**: Using many more random seeds (100+) rather than typical 3-10
- **Transparent hyperparameter protocols**: Clearly separating tuning and evaluation phases
- **New benchmark development**: Creating problems with known optimal solutions to enable better algorithm assessment

#### 6.3. Reproducibility and Practical Deployment

ARS offers significant advantages for reproducibility and real-world deployment:
- Simple implementation with minimal hyperparameters
- High computational efficiency enabling extensive evaluation
- Deterministic linear policies easier to analyze and deploy
- Reduced complexity facilitates integration into practical systems

#### 6.4. Rethinking Sample Complexity

The paper raises important questions about how sample complexity should be measured and reported in RL. If extensive hyperparameter tuning consumes samples "behind the scenes," reported sample complexity for optimized configurations may not reflect true algorithm efficiency on new problems.

### 7. Conclusion

This research makes several important contributions to reinforcement learning. First, it demonstrates that simple random search with linear policies can achieve competitive sample efficiency on standard continuous control benchmarks, challenging assumptions about the necessity of complex algorithms. Second, it reveals significant performance variance in RL algorithms through rigorous statistical evaluation, highlighting problems with current evaluation practices. Third, it proposes methodological improvements including more extensive statistical testing and better benchmark design.

The work advocates for a return to simplicity, rigor, and transparency in RL research. Rather than pursuing ever-more-complex algorithms, the field may benefit from better understanding when simple methods suffice and developing more robust evaluation methodologies. The computational efficiency of ARS enables the kind of extensive evaluation necessary to properly assess algorithm performance, setting a new standard for empirical rigor in reinforcement learning research.

The paper's influence extends beyond presenting a new algorithm—it provides a framework for rethinking how we evaluate and compare RL methods, potentially reshaping future research directions toward more reproducible and practically deployable solutions.
