# RMBench: Memory-Dependent Robotic Manipulation Benchmark with Insights into Policy Design

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from MMLab@HKU, PKU, and collaborators developed RMBench, a benchmark for memory-dependent robotic manipulation, and Mem-0, a modular memory-oriented policy. This work established a principled metric for task memory complexity and demonstrated improved success rates on non-Markovian tasks in both simulation and real-world settings.

## Problem

- Most existing robotic manipulation policies assume a Markovian decision process, relying only on recent observations and struggling with tasks requiring extensive memory.
- Many real-world manipulation tasks are inherently non-Markovian, necessitating the retention and utilization of past observations over extended temporal horizons.
- A systematic experimental platform and standardized benchmark for evaluating and analyzing memory-dependent robotic policies have been absent, limiting targeted research.

## Method

- Introduced Task Memory Complexity (TMC), a principled metric to quantify the minimum amount of past information required for optimal decision-making in manipulation tasks.
- Developed **RMBench**, a comprehensive simulation benchmark comprising nine memory-dependent dual-arm tasks categorized by TMC, providing automated data synthesis and evaluation.
- Proposed Mem-0, a modular memory-oriented robotic policy with a dual-system architecture (Planning and Execution modules) and explicit memory components like key, anchor, and sliding memories.

## Results

- Mem-0 achieved substantial performance gains on RMBench, with an average success rate of **52.8%** on M(1) tasks (**38.4%** improvement) and **28.5%** on M(n) tasks (**21.2%** improvement) compared to baselines.
- Ablation studies showed that removing anchor memory led to a substantial reduction in success rates, and replacing the learned Subtask End Classifier with ground-truth signals improved M(n) task success from **28.5%** to **45.3%**.
- In real-world experiments on three tasks, Mem-0 achieved an average success rate of **22.50%**, outperforming ACT (**0.0%**) and Pi0.5 (**5.83%**), despite observed limitations in fine-grained manipulation precision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit memory mechanisms (anchor, sliding, and key memory) are crucial for addressing non-Markovian robotic manipulation tasks, enabling effective retention and retrieval of historical context.
> - The Subtask End Classifier is critical for coordinating high-level planning and low-level execution, but its current limitations in reliable termination detection can be a bottleneck for overall policy performance.
> - Persistent anchor memory is vital for maintaining context regarding a subtask's initial state, while sliding memory captures short-term temporal cues, both playing distinct roles in policy stability and effectiveness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robotic manipulation have enabled policies to perform various tasks, often leveraging modern approaches like Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models pretrained on extensive datasets. These policies have demonstrated capabilities in flexible object manipulation and fine-grained skills. However, a significant portion of existing work has primarily focused on short-horizon tasks, implicitly assuming that the decision-making process is approximately Markovian, meaning the current observation is sufficient for determining the next action. These approaches often rely on a fixed-length window of recent observations.

The broader research landscape for robotic manipulation is increasingly recognizing the limitations of purely Markovian assumptions, especially in complex, real-world scenarios. Many practical manipulation tasks are inherently non-Markovian, requiring robots to retain, retrieve, and utilize information from past observations and actions over extended temporal horizons. Examples include remembering object locations or reasoning over sequential attempts in trial-and-error processes.

Several research efforts have begun to address this by proposing memory-aware robotic policies, such as MemoryVLA, MemER, CronusVLA, and SAM2Act, which integrate explicit memory mechanisms. Despite these developments, a systematic experimental platform for evaluating and analyzing memory-dependent robotic policies has been largely absent. Existing benchmarks, such as MemoryBench, MIKASA, LIBERO-Long, and RoboCerebra, address aspects of long-horizon or memory-related tasks, but they often present limitations. MemoryBench has reproducibility issues and lacks principled task design. MIKASA is primarily tailored to reinforcement learning, and LIBERO-Long and RoboCerebra feature long-horizon tasks where task-relevant information often remains observable, thus not explicitly demanding long-term memory.

This work directly addresses these gaps by introducing a systematic benchmark, RMBench, specifically designed to evaluate memory capabilities in robotic manipulation. It also proposes a modular policy, Mem-0, to facilitate detailed analysis of memory-related architectural choices. By establishing a principled metric for task memory complexity and providing a standardized evaluation platform, this research aims to advance the understanding and development of robotic policies capable of handling non-Markovian, memory-dependent manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while robotic manipulation policies have made progress, their ability to handle tasks requiring extensive memory capabilities remains limited. Most existing approaches assume decision processes are approximately Markovian, relying on only recent observations. This assumption renders them inadequate for numerous real-world manipulation scenarios that are inherently non-Markovian, requiring robots to process and recall historical observations over extended periods. Examples include recalling the precise location of a previously manipulated object or understanding the sequence of past attempts to achieve a goal.

This limitation presents a significant challenge for deploying robots in complex, adaptive environments. Although some memory-aware policies have been developed, the field has lacked a standardized and systematic framework for their evaluation. Consequently, there is an incomplete understanding of how different architectural design choices within these policies influence their memory performance.

To address these identified gaps, the research articulates several key objectives:

1. **To introduce a principled metric for characterizing memory requirements in robotic manipulation tasks.** This objective aims to provide a systematic way to classify tasks based on the minimal amount of past information necessary for optimal decision-making. This metric would also serve as a guideline for designing memory-dependent tasks.
2. **To develop RMBench, a comprehensive simulation benchmark for memory-dependent robotic manipulation.** This benchmark is designed to comprise a diverse set of manipulation tasks spanning multiple levels of memory complexity, enabling controlled and large-scale studies of policy memory capabilities.
3. **To propose Mem-0, a modular memory-oriented robotic policy.** Mem-0 is designed with explicit memory components that can be easily reconfigured or replaced. This modularity facilitates systematic ablation studies, allowing researchers to analyze the contribution of individual memory mechanisms.
4. **To conduct extensive evaluations to identify memory-related limitations in existing state-of-the-art policies.** By benchmarking contemporary policies on RMBench, the research aims to empirically demonstrate their deficiencies in handling non-Markovian tasks.
5. **To provide empirical insights into how architectural design choices influence memory performance.** Through systematic ablation studies of Mem-0 in both simulation and real-world experiments, the objective is to understand which design components are critical for effective memory in robotic manipulation and to offer guidance for future policy development.

Overall, the motivation is to bridge the gap between current robotic manipulation capabilities and the demands of real-world memory-dependent tasks by providing both a robust evaluation platform and a dissectible policy architecture for systematic analysis.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces RMBench, a benchmark for memory-dependent robotic manipulation, and Mem-0, a modular policy designed for memory analysis. The methodology involves defining a metric for task memory complexity, designing tasks based on this metric, developing the Mem-0 policy with distinct memory components, and then conducting extensive experiments to evaluate policies and analyze memory module contributions.

#### 3.1. Task Memory Complexity (TMC)

The core of RMBench's task design is the **Task Memory Complexity (TMC)** metric. This metric quantifies the minimum amount of past information required for optimal decision-making in a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) that models a manipulation task.
- **Definition**: TMC is defined as the smallest integer *m* ≥ 0 such that an optimal policy π\* can determine its action *a<sub>t</sub>* at time *t* solely based on a memory state *M<sup>(m)</sup><sub>t</sub>*. This memory state *M<sup>(m)</sup><sub>t</sub>* is constructed from the interaction history *h<sub>t</sub>* and encodes information from at most *m* task-relevant past observations.
- **Annotation**: Tasks are categorized as M(0) (memory-free), M(1) (requires a single task-relevant past observation), or M(*n*) (requires *n* task-relevant past observations). This categorization guides task design and allows for stratified evaluation of policy memory capabilities.

#### 3.2. RMBench System Design

RMBench is implemented within the RoboTwin 2.0 system framework, utilizing the SAPIEN simulation engine.
- **Features**: The platform supports automated data synthesis and integrated policy evaluation, promoting scalability, consistency, and reproducibility. It also provides fine-grained language annotations for action-observation pairs, offering dense supervision for training high-level reasoning or memory modules.
- **Dual-Arm Tasks**: The benchmark focuses on dual-arm manipulation tasks, reflecting complex real-world scenarios.
- **Simulation Environments**: RMBench is available on the SAPIEN platform and has also been implemented on NVIDIA Isaac Lab - Arena for broader accessibility.

#### 3.3. RMBench Benchmark Tasks

Based on the TMC metric, RMBench comprises nine memory-dependent manipulation tasks, divided into two categories:
- **M(1) Tasks (5 tasks)**: These tasks require retaining a single past observation or a fixed, limited number of historical frames. Examples include "Observe and Pick Up," "Rearrange Blocks," "Put Back Block," "Swap Blocks," and "Swap T." Success depends on dynamically attending to task-relevant information at different stages.
- **M(*n*) Tasks (4 tasks)**: These tasks demand stronger long-term memory retention and effective retrieval mechanisms. They often involve repeated active exploration, trial-and-error interactions, or sequential execution over a task-specific number of attempts, guided by external feedback. Examples include "Blocks Ranking Try," "Press Button," "Cover Blocks," and "Battery Try."

#### 3.4. Mem-0 Policy

Mem-0 is designed as a modular, memory-oriented robotic policy with a dual-system architecture (Planning Module, Execution Module) linked by a Subtask End Classifier.

- **Planning Module**:
 - **Function**: Performs subtask-level reasoning, crucial for long-horizon tasks and M(*n*)-type memory-dependent scenarios where reasoning over multiple past subtasks is necessary.
 - **Inputs**: Initial observation (*o<sub>0</sub>*), global task goal (*g*), and `key memory window` (*M<sub>t-1</sub>*).
 - **Key Memory Window**: Aggregates all previously completed subtasks (*s<sub>i</sub>*) and their corresponding termination observations (*o<sup>end</sup><sub>i</sub>*). This enables the Vision-Language Model (VLM) to explicitly reason over past subtasks and their visual outcomes.
 - **Efficiency**: Subtask inference is triggered only upon subtask termination (identified by the Subtask End Classifier), reducing the number of planning invocations compared to per-timestep planning.

- **Execution Module**:
 - **Function**: Executes the current subtask using a diffusion-based policy. It is designed to handle M(1)-type tasks without explicit subtask decomposition.
 - **Inputs**: Current RGB observation (*o<sub>t</sub>*) and the current subtask instruction (*s<sub>t</sub>*). These are encoded into image and text token embeddings using a VLM.
 - **Memory Integration**: The image latent representation attends to two memory buffers via cross-attention:
 - `Anchor Memory (A)`: Stores the initial image latent (*z<sup>img</sup><sub>0</sub>*) at the beginning of a subtask, remaining fixed throughout its execution. This maintains a persistent reference to the subtask's initial state.
 - `Sliding Memory Window (S<sub>t</sub>)`: Stores a fixed-length window of recent image latents, updated at each timestep. This captures short-term transient information and motion trends.
 - **Action Generation**: Fused memory-conditioned representations are concatenated with the text token to form a conditioning vector. A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) then predicts a denoised action sequence over a fixed horizon, with a prefix of this sequence executed.

- **Subtask End Classifier**:
 - **Function**: A lightweight Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) that detects the completion of a subtask.
 - **Mechanism**: Operates on the conditioning vector from the Execution Module, outputting a binary signal. To enhance robustness, a subtask is considered complete only if the classifier predicts termination for *L* = 8 consecutive timesteps.
 - **Closed-Loop Interaction**: Upon confirmation of subtask completion, the final observation and subtask description are passed to the Planning Module, triggering the inference of the next subtask. This establishes a closed loop between high-level planning and low-level execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate existing policies, analyze Mem-0's architectural components, and assess its real-world performance.

#### 4.1. Evaluation of Policies on RMBench

A diverse set of policies, including non-pretrained (DP, ACT), pretrained (Pi0.5, X-VLA), and the proposed Mem-0, were benchmarked on the nine RMBench tasks. All models were trained with 50 expert demonstrations and evaluated over 100 rollout episodes.
- **Baselines' Performance**: Both non-pretrained and pretrained baselines consistently exhibited low success rates across memory-dependent tasks. This underperformance is attributed to their design based on a Markovian assumption, where decisions rely primarily on the current observation. Without access to task-relevant historical information, these models struggled with the non-Markovian nature of RMBench tasks.
- **Mem-0's Performance**: Mem-0 demonstrated substantial performance gains. On M(1) tasks, it achieved an average success rate of 52.8%, representing a 38.4% improvement over the baselines. On M(n) tasks, Mem-0 reached an average of 28.5%, a 21.2% improvement. These results indicate the effectiveness of explicitly incorporating memory mechanisms for memory-dependent manipulation.
- **Mem-0's Limitations**: Despite overall improvements, Mem-0 showed limitations in specific scenarios:
 - Tasks requiring strong semantic understanding (e.g., "Observe and Pick Up") sometimes favored pretrained models due to their large-scale pretraining.
 - Tasks demanding fine-grained manipulation accuracy (e.g., "Swap T") yielded only marginal performance gains for Mem-0.
 - Tasks with subtle action cues (e.g., "Press Button") resulted in zero success, primarily because the Subtask End Classifier struggled with reliable termination detection, leading to repeated or missed actions.

#### 4.2. Analysis on Memory-Related Modules (Ablation Studies)

Ablation studies were conducted on Mem-0's components to understand their individual contributions. For M(1) tasks, only `w/o Anchor` and `w/o Sliding` were ablated as subtask decomposition is not performed.

- **w/o Anchor Memory**: Removing the anchor memory module led to a substantial reduction in success rates across most tasks. This indicates that without an explicit mechanism to retain task-critical information (e.g., the initial state of a subtask), the policy progressively loses context as relevant memories are evicted from short-term buffers, leading to erroneous behaviors.
- **w/o Sliding Memory**: Removing the sliding memory module generally degraded performance, resulting in unstable or oscillatory behaviors. For instance, in button-pressing scenarios, the policy struggled to infer if a button had been pressed from a single observation. Interestingly, "Swap T" showed improved performance without sliding memory, suggesting that for tasks with fixed motion patterns and high sensitivity to initial orientation, sliding memory might introduce interference, and relying solely on anchor information could be more beneficial.
- **w/o Key Memory**: For M(n)-type tasks, removing the key memory window in the Planning Module (subtask inference based on single-frame observations) substantially decreased success rates. This highlights the critical role of key memory in enabling the Planning Module to infer the correct next subtask in long-term, memory-dependent scenarios.
- **GT Classifier (Ground Truth Classifier)**: Replacing the learned Subtask End Classifier with ground-truth termination signals led to significant performance improvements (e.g., from 28.5% to 45.3% average on M(n) tasks). This finding suggests that while subtask decomposition is beneficial, the current classifier design's precision in detecting subtask transitions is a bottleneck. Inaccurate termination signals can negatively impact the Planning Module's ability to infer subsequent subtasks.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiment

Mem-0 was evaluated on three physical manipulation tasks ("Put Back Blocks," "Rearrange Blocks," "Cover Blocks") using a dual-arm robotic platform, comparing it against ACT and Pi0.5. Each policy was trained on 100 real-world demonstrations and evaluated over 40 trials.
- **Performance**: Mem-0 outperformed both ACT (0.0% average success) and Pi0.5 (5.83% average success) with an average success rate of 22.50% in real-world settings.
- **Failure Analysis**: Real-world failures for Mem-0 were often attributed to imprecise block manipulation rather than high-level task planning. This was hypothesized to be due to increased variability in real-world data collection and Mem-0's lack of dedicated pretraining for fine-grained motor behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by systematically addressing the challenge of memory-dependent tasks, which are prevalent in real-world applications but often overlooked by existing policies.

The introduction of **Task Memory Complexity (TMC)** provides a principled and quantitative metric for categorizing robotic manipulation tasks based on their inherent memory requirements. This metric offers a structured approach to task design and evaluation, moving beyond qualitative assessments of task difficulty. The **RMBench** benchmark, comprising nine diverse tasks across varying memory complexities, establishes a standardized platform for reproducible and scalable evaluation of memory-aware policies. This is significant because the absence of such a benchmark has limited systematic progress in understanding and developing memory capabilities in robots.

The proposed **Mem-0 policy** serves as a modular and dissectible architecture for explicit memory integration. Its dual-system design, incorporating a Planning Module with key memory, an Execution Module with anchor and sliding memories, and a Subtask End Classifier, offers a flexible framework for exploring different memory mechanisms. The extensive **ablation studies** on Mem-0 provide empirical insights into the roles of these individual components. For instance, the findings underscore the importance of persistent anchor memory for retaining task-critical information, the contribution of sliding memory for short-term temporal cues, and the necessity of key memory for long-term subtask reasoning. The analysis also highlights the critical role of the Subtask End Classifier in coordinating planning and execution, while simultaneously pointing out its current limitations as a bottleneck.

The results, demonstrating Mem-0's superior performance over state-of-the-art baselines on memory-dependent tasks, validate the effectiveness of explicitly modeling memory. This provides strong evidence that policies capable of retaining and utilizing historical observations are essential for tackling non-Markovian manipulation challenges. Furthermore, the real-world experiments, albeit with observed limitations in fine-grained precision, illustrate Mem-0's potential for generalization beyond simulation, suggesting practical applicability.

The work identifies specific areas for future research, such as improving memory representation and fusion, enhancing the robustness of subtask termination criteria, and integrating pretraining for better semantic understanding and generalization. By clearly articulating these insights and challenges, RMBench and Mem-0 are positioned to foster principled progress towards developing more scalable, robust, and truly memory-aware robotic manipulation systems, ultimately enabling robots to operate more autonomously and effectively in complex, dynamic environments.
