# Robust Optimization for Mitigating Reward Hacking with Correlated Proxies

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a robust optimization framework to mitigate reward hacking in reinforcement learning by training agents against worst-case reward functions within a defined uncertainty set. This approach, including a general Max-Min formulation and a feature-linearized variant, yields policies with superior worst-case returns and enhanced stability across varying proxy-true reward correlations compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents often optimize imperfect proxy rewards, leading to "reward hacking" where agents achieve high proxy returns through unintended, undesirable behaviors.
- Existing mitigation methods are sensitive to the specific proxy reward chosen, which can be noisy or uncertain, and lack strong theoretical guarantees against a broad range of reward variations.
- Current black-box RL policies hinder interpretability, which is crucial for human oversight, trust, and risk mitigation in safety-critical applications.

## Method

- Formulates reward hacking as a robust ==max-min== policy optimization problem, where the policy maximizes its return against the worst-case reward from an r-correlated uncertainty set.
- Derives a tractable max-min objective function by employing a change-of-measure technique and solving the inner minimization for a closed-form adversarial reward.
- Introduces a Linear Max-Min variant that assumes a linear reward structure, leveraging feature whitening to obtain an interpretable, element-wise closed-form solution for adversarial feature weights.

## Results

- Both Max-Min and Linear Max-Min policies consistently achieved superior worst-case performance across five benchmark environments (e.g., Traffic, Pandemic, Glucose) compared to the ORPO baseline and its improved variant, ORPO*.
- The Max-Min method demonstrated higher average rewards and lower performance variance across a wide range of proxy–true reward correlation levels, indicating strong resilience to reward misspecification.
- The Linear Max-Min approach provided interpretable adversarial feature weights, diagnosing that features like 'headway' in the Traffic environment or 'early-stage transitions' in Pandemic were most exploited by the adversarial reward.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing against a worst-case reward within an r-correlated uncertainty set provides stronger theoretical guarantees against reward hacking compared to optimizing a fixed proxy.
> - The derived adversarial reward in both general and linear cases reveals which reward components or features are most vulnerable to exploitation by the agent, aiding interpretability.
> - Accurate estimation of the Radon-Nikodym derivative (occupancy ratio) through improved discriminator training is crucial for the stability and effectiveness of robust policy optimization algorithms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The design of effective reward functions in reinforcement learning (RL) systems is a recognized challenge. In practical applications, agents frequently operate with proxy rewards, which are approximations of a true objective. This imperfect specification can lead to a phenomenon known as reward hacking, where agents optimize the proxy reward by engaging in unintended or exploitative behaviors, resulting in suboptimal true outcomes. This issue has been observed in various real-world scenarios, including autonomous driving, healthcare, and large language models (LLMs), with reported instances of undesirable or catastrophic consequences. The need for robust and interpretable RL policies is therefore becoming increasingly important, particularly in safety-critical domains where understanding policy behavior and potential failure modes is essential for risk mitigation and regulatory compliance.

Recent efforts have aimed to formalize reward hacking and develop mitigation strategies. Laidlaw et al. (2025) introduced the concept of r-correlated proxies, defining a proxy reward as r-correlated with the true reward if their correlation coefficient exceeds a positive threshold (r > 0) on state-action pairs observed under a reference policy. Within this framework, reward hacking occurs when a policy optimized for an r-correlated proxy achieves a lower true reward than the reference policy. As a mitigation, Occupancy-Regularized Policy Optimization (ORPO) was proposed, which adds a regularization term to the standard RL objective, penalizing deviations in occupancy measures from a reference policy.

However, existing solutions like ORPO exhibit limitations. Their effectiveness can be sensitive to the specific proxy reward chosen, which often originates from noisy or limited data, introducing uncertainty. Current methods typically treat the reward function as a black box, producing complex policies that lack interpretability and cannot easily incorporate prior knowledge about the true reward structure. These shortcomings highlight a demand for RL algorithms that offer stronger guarantees against varying proxy rewards and provide transparency into their decision-making processes. This work positions itself within this landscape by seeking to develop more robust and transparent approaches to address reward hacking under reward uncertainty.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical challenges of reward specification in real-world reinforcement learning systems. The inherent difficulty in crafting reward functions that perfectly align with human intent often leads to the use of imperfect proxy rewards. Optimizing these proxies can result in reward hacking, where an agent achieves high proxy returns through unintended, undesirable, or even catastrophic behaviors, especially in safety-critical applications. Existing methods, while providing some mitigation, are limited by their reliance on fixed proxy rewards and their lack of strong theoretical guarantees against a broad range of correlated proxies. Furthermore, the black-box nature of current RL policies hinders interpretability, a crucial requirement for human oversight, trust, and compliance in high-stakes environments.

To address these challenges, the authors outline several key objectives:
1. **Formulate Reward Hacking as a Robust Optimization Problem:** The central objective is to conceptualize reward hacking as a robust policy optimization problem. This involves defining an uncertainty set of reward functions, specifically all rewards that are r-correlated with a given proxy reward, and then training an agent to maximize its performance against the worst-case reward within this set. This approach aims to explicitly account for uncertainty in reward design and guard against exploitative behaviors.
2. **Derive a Tractable Max-Min Formulation:** The research seeks to derive a mathematically tractable max-min objective function. In this formulation, the policy maximizes its expected true return, while an adversary simultaneously minimizes this return by selecting the most unfavorable reward function from the r-correlated set. The goal is to obtain a closed-form solution for the adversarial reward, thereby making the inner minimization computationally efficient.
3. **Develop Max-Min Policy Optimization Algorithms:** Based on the tractable max-min formulation, the authors aim to develop practical algorithms for robust policy optimization.
4. **Incorporate Structural Knowledge for Improved Interpretability and Robustness (Linear Max-Min):** A further objective is to extend the framework by assuming that the true reward function is a linear combination of known features. This aims to leverage prior structural knowledge to constrain the uncertainty set, yielding not only improved robustness but also interpretable worst-case reward functions. Such interpretability can diagnose which features are most vulnerable to hacking.
5. **Empirical Validation and Performance Evaluation:** The research intends to empirically evaluate the proposed Max-Min and Linear Max-Min algorithms across a variety of benchmark environments designed to exhibit different forms of reward hacking. The evaluation focuses on worst-case returns, average performance, and stability across different levels of proxy-true reward correlation, comparing against existing baselines.
6. **Provide Theoretical Guarantees:** The work also aims to establish theoretical guarantees for the proposed algorithms, including convergence properties and sample complexity bounds for occupancy measure estimation, which is a critical component of the methodology.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the formulation of reward hacking as a robust policy optimization problem, where an agent optimizes its policy against the worst-case reward from a defined set of possibilities. This contrasts with previous methods that optimize against a fixed proxy reward.

#### 3.1. Max-Min Policy Optimization

The authors define a set of *r-correlated* reward functions, denoted R_corr, based on the framework introduced by Laidlaw et al. (2025). This set includes all reward functions R that maintain a specified correlation coefficient 'r' with a given proxy reward (R_proxy) under a *reference policy* (π_ref), while also having fixed mean (M) and standard deviation (V) under π_ref. R_proxy is normalized to have zero mean and unit variance under π_ref.

The robust objective is formulated as a max-min problem:
`max_π min_{R∈R_corr} J(π, R) = max_π min_{R∈R_corr} E_{(s,a)∼μ_π} [R(s, a)]`
To address the mismatch between the objective (dependent on μ_π) and constraints (dependent on μ_π_ref), a *change-of-measure* technique is applied using the Radon-Nikodym derivative `L(s,a) = μ_π(s,a) / μ_π_ref(s,a)`. This allows both the objective and constraints to be expressed as expectations under μ_π_ref.

The inner minimization problem (`min_{R∈R_corr} E_{μ_π_ref} [L · R]`) is then solved using a Lagrangian formulation. By taking the functional derivative with respect to R(s,a) and setting it to zero, a closed-form solution for the optimal adversarial reward `R*(s,a)` is derived (Equation 8). This `R*(s,a)` is a linear combination of `L(s,a)`, `R_proxy(s,a)`, and constants, weighted by Lagrange multipliers (λ1, λ2, λ3). The dual variables (Lagrange multipliers) are then determined by solving a system of equations derived from setting the gradients of the dual objective to zero. Crucially, it is demonstrated that a negative λ3 ensures the minimization problem is well-posed, yielding a global optimum.

Substituting the derived `R*(s,a)` and optimal Lagrange multipliers back into the objective yields the final policy optimization problem (Equation 9). This objective resembles that of ORPO but features a modified regularization strength (`sqrt(1-r^2)/r`) and an additional term in the penalty: `χ^2(μ_π || μ_π_ref) - E^2_{μ_π}[R_proxy]`. The authors demonstrate that `χ^2(μ_π || μ_π_ref) ≥ E^2_{μ_π}[R_proxy]`, ensuring the square root term is well-defined. Theorem 1 formalizes that this objective optimizes a pessimistic lower bound on the true improvement over the reference policy, thereby promoting robustness against reward hacking. The constants M and V are shown to not affect the optimal policy, only applying a linear transformation to the objective.

#### 3.2. Structured Reward Spaces via Feature Linearization (Linear Max-Min)

To address concerns about over-conservatism and interpretability, the authors introduce a variant that assumes reward functions are linear combinations of known features: `R(s,a) = θ^T ϕ(s,a)`, where `ϕ(s,a)` is a feature vector and `θ` is a vector of uncertain weights. This assumption is motivated by its prevalence in real-world tasks and its ability to constrain the reward uncertainty space, making the problem more grounded.

The uncertainty set `R_lin_corr` is redefined in terms of these feature weights `θ`. The max-min problem then becomes `max_π min_{θ∈R_lin_corr, θ≥0} E_{μ_π} [θ^T ϕ(s,a)]`. The Lagrangian formulation is again used for the inner minimization over `θ`. To simplify the problem and obtain a closed-form solution, a *whitening transformation* is applied to the feature vector `ϕ` such that the matrix `Q` (sum of `ϕ(s,a)ϕ(s,a)^T` weighted by `μ_π_ref(s,a)`) becomes an identity matrix. This transformation decouples the problem into independent one-dimensional convex quadratic programs for each feature weight `θ_i`, allowing for an element-wise `max(0...)` closed-form solution for optimal `θ*`. The outer maximization over the policy `π` is then solved using standard RL algorithms like PPO. The derived `θ*` provides interpretability by indicating which features are most exploited by the adversarial reward.

#### 3.3. Occupancy Estimation and Convergence

Both Max-Min algorithms rely on estimating the Radon-Nikodym derivative `L(s,a) = μ_π(s,a) / μ_π_ref(s,a)`. Following prior work, a discriminator network `d_φ(s,a)` is trained to minimize a specific loss function (Equation 14), with `L(s,a)` estimated as `exp(d_φ(s,a))`. The authors identify and address a limitation in prior ORPO implementations where the discriminator was under-trained, leading to inaccurate `L(s,a)` estimates. Their approach involves substantially increasing gradient updates and tuning learning rates for the discriminator, resulting in more accurate and stable estimates.

Theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) provides a sample complexity bound for the occupancy ratio estimation, `O(n^-1/4)`, where `n` is the sample size. The overall Max-Min algorithm's convergence is framed within the context of Reinforcement Learning with General Utility (RLGU), yielding a convergence bound of `O(1/T + 1/N + n^-1/4)`, where T is the number of iterations and N is the batch size for policy updates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research empirically evaluates the proposed Max-Min and Linear Max-Min algorithms across five environments: Traffic, Pandemic, Glucose Monitoring, Tomato Watering GridWorld, and RLHF. Comparisons are made against the original ORPO baseline and an ablation study variant, ORPO*, which incorporates the improved discriminator training applied in this work.

#### 4.1. Worst-Case Performance

- **Superior Worst-Case Returns:** Across all tested environments (Traffic, Pandemic, Glucose, Tomato, RLHF), both Max-Min and Linear Max-Min policies consistently demonstrated superior worst-case performance compared to ORPO and ORPO*. Max-Min generally achieved the highest expected worst-case return, while Linear Max-Min excelled in terms of expected *linear* worst-case reward. This indicates that the proposed robust optimization framework effectively guards against adversarial reward interpretations within the defined uncertainty set.
- **Impact of Discriminator Training (ORPO*):** The ORPO* variant, which benefited from more thorough discriminator training, showed improved worst-case performance and a significant reduction in the occupancy of state-action pairs not visited by the reference policy (`Occ ↓`) compared to the original ORPO. This finding validates the importance of accurate Radon-Nikodym derivative estimation for robust policy learning.
- **Robustness to Unseen Features (Linear Worst\*):** The Max-Min policy showed minimal performance degradation when evaluated against linear worst-case rewards that included features unseen during training (Linear Worst\*). This suggests its generalized robustness beyond specific feature sets. In contrast, Linear Max-Min experienced a decline in this scenario, indicating its performance is more dependent on the accuracy of its assumed feature structure.
- **Interpretability of Adversarial Weights:** For the Linear Max-Min method, the reported adversarial weight vectors (θ) provided insights into which reward features were most vulnerable to exploitation. For instance, in the Traffic environment, the headway feature consistently received the highest adversarial weight, while acceleration was downweighted. In Pandemic, early-stage transitions were volatile, and overall infection magnitude was not dominant, suggesting smoothness and early-stage control were critical for adversarial exploitation. This interpretability allows for diagnosis of proxy reward flaws.
- **RLHF Context:** In the RLHF environment, the Max-Min policy exhibited a lower expected worst-case return compared to the Ensemble baseline, indicating a higher degree of robustness to reward model misspecification.

#### 4.2. Robustness Across Correlation Levels

- **Stability and Higher Average Reward:** When evaluating policies across a wide range of proxy–true reward correlation levels (r), the Max-Min method consistently achieved higher average rewards and lower variance in performance across the Traffic and Pandemic environments. This demonstrates its strong resilience and stability under varying degrees of reward misspecification.
- **Context-Dependent Performance:** Linear Max-Min sometimes achieved the best performance at specific correlation levels, suggesting its advantage can be pronounced when the correlation aligns with its structured assumptions.
- **Discriminator Effect on Stability:** ORPO* consistently exhibited lower variance than the original ORPO, further emphasizing that accurate discriminator training contributes to both stability and robustness.
- **Effect of *r* on Robustness:** For any fixed policy, the expected worst-case return generally increased monotonically with the evaluation 'r' (higher correlation implies less adversarial conditions). Training with moderate 'r' values (e.g., 0.3-0.4) often yielded better robustness across a wide range of evaluation 'r', striking a balance between over-conservatism and optimism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces a novel and principled framework for mitigating reward hacking in reinforcement learning by formulating it as a robust optimization problem. The development of both a general Max-Min formulation and a specialized Linear Max-Min variant offers distinct contributions to the field.

The primary significance lies in providing stronger theoretical guarantees for robustness against imperfect and uncertain proxy reward signals. By optimizing against the worst-case reward within an r-correlated uncertainty set, the proposed methods aim to produce policies that are inherently more resilient to unforeseen exploitative behaviors, which can arise from the gap between proxy and true objectives. This addresses a critical safety concern in real-world RL deployments.

A key impact of the Linear Max-Min variant is its contribution to interpretability and transparency. By assuming a linear reward structure, the method yields explicit adversarial feature weights. These weights can serve as diagnostic tools, highlighting which aspects of the reward function are most vulnerable to hacking or which features are being exploited. This transparency is crucial for human stakeholders in safety-critical domains (e.g., autonomous driving, healthcare, traffic control) who need to understand policy behavior, audit decision-making criteria, and effectively mitigate risks.

The empirical results demonstrate that the proposed algorithms consistently outperform existing baselines (like ORPO) in terms of worst-case returns and exhibit improved stability across various levels of proxy-true reward correlation. This practical validation suggests that the robust formulation can lead to more dependable and trustworthy RL systems.

The methodological contributions, including the tractable max-min formulation, the whitening transformation for linear rewards, and the improved discriminator training for occupancy estimation, provide a solid foundation for future research in robust and interpretable RL. The work lays groundwork for developing safer and more reliable AI systems by equipping practitioners with tools to explicitly account for and mitigate reward misspecification and its detrimental consequences.
