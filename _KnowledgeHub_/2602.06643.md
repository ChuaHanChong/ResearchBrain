# Humanoid Manipulation Interface: Humanoid Whole-Body Manipulation from Robot-Free Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> HuMI presents a robot-free demonstration system and a hierarchical policy learning framework to enable complex whole-body manipulation for humanoid robots. The system achieved efficient data collection and allowed the Unitree G1 humanoid to perform dexterous tasks with improved robustness and generalization across diverse environments.

## Problem

- Current humanoid skill acquisition methods, like sim-to-real RL and teleoperation, are labor-intensive, require specialized expertise, and limit the diversity of teachable skills.
- The inherent "embodiment gap" between human and robot morphology, kinematics, and stability constraints makes direct transfer of complex whole-body motions difficult.
- Achieving robust, generalizable whole-body coordination for humanoids in unstructured environments remains challenging, often confining learned tasks to controlled laboratory settings.

## Method

- A portable, robot-free demonstration system captures rich whole-body human motion using HTC Vive Ultimate Trackers and provides a human-in-the-loop Inverse Kinematics (IK) preview for real-time kinematic adaptation.
- A hierarchical policy learning framework employs a high-level Diffusion Policy to generate receding-horizon task-space trajectories and a low-level Reinforcement Learning-trained whole-body controller for joint angle execution.
- The policy interface is enhanced with features like using the previous scheduled target pose as a high-level action reference and tracking relative poses for non-vision-grounded keypoints to improve system integration and robustness against tracking errors.

## Results

- HuMI successfully enabled the Unitree G1 humanoid to perform complex whole-body tasks such as kneeling (85% success rate), precise bimanual manipulation (85% success rate), and dynamic tossing (75% success rate).
- The system demonstrated generalization capabilities, achieving a 70% success rate on a squat-and-pick-up task in unseen environments and with unseen objects from diverse "in-the-wild" data.
- Data collection efficiency was significantly improved, with HuMI collecting 62 acceptable episodes in 15 minutes for one task (96.7% acceptance), compared to 28 episodes (64.3% acceptance) from a state-of-the-art teleoperation system.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effectively bridging the human-robot embodiment gap for whole-body manipulation requires both rich human motion capture and active, human-guided kinematic adaptation to robot constraints.
> - A hierarchical control architecture with adaptive end-effector tracking rewards and variable-speed augmentation in the low-level controller is essential for balancing stability and precision in complex humanoid tasks.
> - Robust integration of high-level policies with low-level controllers for dynamic tasks necessitates careful consideration of action referencing to prevent trajectory discontinuities and managing drift for non-vision-grounded body parts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is rapidly advancing, with a primary goal of enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks in diverse, unstructured environments, akin to humans. Current state-of-the-art approaches for humanoid whole-body manipulation largely fall into two categories: visual sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning from teleoperation. However, both methods face significant hurdles.

Sim-to-real RL often demands extensive engineering for creating diverse simulation assets, intricate reward function design, and robust domain randomization to bridge the reality gap. Teleoperation, while intuitive for humans, requires highly skilled operators to manage the robot's inherent balance challenges, high degrees of freedom, and controller inaccuracies in real-time. These complexities limit the breadth and diversity of autonomous skills that can be taught to humanoids, typically confining them to controlled laboratory settings and relatively simple actions (e.g., upright walking with basic object transport). The output tasks are often restricted in terms of whole-body coordination and dynamic movements.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a novel paradigm for data collection and skill transfer. It builds upon the concept of "robot-free" data collection systems, which have gained traction for fixed-base robotic arms (e.g., UMI, Universal Manipulation Interface) and more recently for mobile platforms like quadrupeds. These systems allow humans to demonstrate tasks without directly operating a physical robot, significantly increasing data collection efficiency. HuMI extends this concept to the highly complex domain of *humanoid whole-body manipulation*, which presents unique challenges due to the humanoid's inherent instability, high degrees of freedom, and the critical need for precise whole-body coordination.

HuMI distinguishes itself by systematically tackling the "embodiment gap" between human demonstrations and robot capabilities, a problem often oversimplified in previous robot-free systems that focused primarily on end-effector trajectories. It recognizes that for humanoids, whole-body posture, balance, and dynamic movements are as crucial as end-effector precision. By offering a portable, efficient, and broadly applicable framework, HuMI aims to democratize the data collection process, making it easier to generate large, diverse datasets necessary for learning robust and generalizable humanoid skills, thereby pushing the boundaries beyond current lab-bound, limited-skill demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the HuMI project is to empower humanoid robots to execute a wide variety of tasks across diverse environments, emphasizing intricate whole-body coordination that fully leverages the dexterity of humanoid platforms. To achieve this, the authors are motivated to overcome the labor-intensive and restrictive nature of existing humanoid skill acquisition methods.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

- **Enable Diverse Whole-Body Tasks:** To allow humanoids to perform complex actions requiring coordinated use of the entire body, such as squatting, kneeling, tossing, and bimanual manipulation, which are currently challenging to implement autonomously.
- **Achieve Robot-Free, Portable, and Efficient Data Collection:** To develop a system that significantly reduces the overhead and expertise required for generating high-quality demonstration data. This involves moving beyond the need for a physical robot during data collection and making the setup portable enough for "in-the-wild" use. The motivation is to achieve higher data collection throughput and acceptance rates compared to teleoperation.
- **Bridge the Embodiment Gap Systematically:** To address the inherent differences in morphology and kinematics between humans and humanoids. This involves developing methods that ensure human demonstrations are both informative (rich whole-body information) and kinematically feasible for the robot, without compromising task requirements (e.g., precise object interaction geometry).
- **Ensure Robust Task Execution and Generalization:** To develop a learning framework that translates human motions into dexterous and feasible humanoid skills, capable of stable execution despite tracking errors. A critical motivation is to enable learned policies to generalize effectively to unseen objects and environments, moving beyond fixed lab settings.
- **Improve System Integration for High-Precision Manipulation:** To design a hierarchical control framework and policy interface that robustly coordinates high-level planning with low-level execution, accounting for non-negligible tracking errors inherent in real-world humanoid control.

In essence, HuMI seeks to democratize humanoid data collection, accelerate the learning of complex, whole-body skills, and foster the development of more generalizable and autonomous humanoid behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HuMI proposes a two-component framework: a robot-free demonstration system and a hierarchical policy learning framework.

#### 3.1. Robot-Free Demonstration System

The core of data acquisition in HuMI is a portable and precise system designed to capture informative and robot-feasible human trajectories without the physical robot.

- **Portable and Precise Hardware:** Building on existing robot-free systems like UMI, HuMI augments the setup by including five HTC Vive Ultimate Trackers attached to the grippers, waist (pelvis), and feet. This captures rich whole-body motion, crucial for specifying tasks beyond end-effector trajectories. Two 3D-printed handheld grippers with wrist-mounted GoPro cameras provide visual observations. The system is designed to be base-station-free and portable, fitting into a single backpack for diverse environment data collection.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Kinematic Adaptation:** To overcome the "embodiment gap" (morphological differences between human and robot) without scaling demonstrations (which would compromise object interaction geometry), HuMI introduces an online Inverse Kinematics (IK) preview interface. Demonstrators visualize the virtual robot's motion in real-time and intuitively adjust their movements to ensure kinematic feasibility (e.g., within reach limits, avoiding self-collisions) and task compliance. This pre-filters demonstrations for robot-specific constraints.
- **Data Processing:** Collected data (synchronized image observations, gripper widths, and SE(3) trajectories for keypoints) is processed into two streams: one for training the high-level policy (visuals and keypoint trajectories) and another for training the low-level controller (keypoint trajectories paired with whole-body IK solutions).

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Policy Learning Framework

The collected data then drives a hierarchical learning pipeline comprising a high-level policy and a low-level controller.

- **High-Level Manipulation Policy:** A Diffusion Policy is employed, running at 5 Hz, to generate receding-horizon task-space trajectories (action chunks) from camera images and proprioceptive observations. The policy predicts target keypoint trajectories for the grippers, base (pelvis), and optionally feet.
- **Manipulation-Centric Whole-Body Controller:** This low-level controller, running at 50 Hz, is trained using reinforcement learning in simulation to track the target trajectories generated by the high-level policy. It outputs robot joint angles. Key innovations here include:
 - **Adaptive End-Effector Tracking Rewards:** Instead of uniform precision, the reward dynamically scales the precision tolerance for end-effectors. High accuracy is prioritized during slow interactions (e.g., grasping), while greater flexibility is allowed during rapid movements, and EE tracking is deactivated to prioritize stability during rapid base movements. A curriculum gradually ramps up the end-effector reward weight and tightens minimum tolerance during training.
 - **Variable-Speed Augmentation:** To improve precision, the reference motion execution speed is varied during training (scaled within [s_min, s_max]). This gives the policy more time to correct small errors and learn high-precision movements.
- **Policy Interface for Improved System Integration:** Two critical modifications enhance robustness:
 - **Target Pose as High-Level Action Reference:** To prevent action chunk discontinuities caused by low-level tracking errors, the high-level policy uses the *previous scheduled target pose* (rather than the actual, possibly lagging, executed robot pose) as the reference for the next action chunk. This ensures smooth, continuous trajectories crucial for dynamic tasks.
 - **Relative Pose Tracking for Non-Vision-Grounded Keypoints:** For "blind" keypoints like the pelvis and feet, which lack direct visual feedback and accumulate drift, the system tracks *relative transforms within the current action chunk* instead of absolute global transforms. This mitigates cumulative errors and maintains robust performance for whole-body coordination.

The entire system integrates these components, enabling the Unitree G1 humanoid robot to perform whole-body manipulation using onboard sensor observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

HuMI's effectiveness was rigorously evaluated across five diverse whole-body manipulation tasks on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating significant advancements in capability, generalization, and data collection efficiency.

#### 4.1. Whole-Body Manipulation Capability

HuMI successfully learned and executed complex whole-body skills in-domain:

- **Marriage Proposal (Kneeling, Grasping, Lifting):** Achieved an 85% success rate (17/20 trials). The robot performed smooth, coordinated whole-body motion from an upright stance to a single-knee kneel, precisely grasping a ring toy. Ablations showed that removing human-in-the-loop kinematic adaptation reduced success to 10%, leading to kinematically inappropriate motions, and removing whole-body specification dropped it to 0%, highlighting the necessity of both components for stable, coordinated motion.
- **Unsheathing a Sword (Precise Bimanual Manipulation):** Achieved an 85% success rate (17/20 trials) with an average end-effector tracking error of 15.7 mm. The robot performed accurate bimanual grasps and coordinated arm movements for a smooth, continuous pull. Removing variable-speed augmentation reduced success to 50% and increased tracking error to 21.2 mm, demonstrating its role in precision and coordination.
- **Tossing a Toy (Dynamic Control):** Achieved a 75% success rate (15/20 trials). The robot executed a smooth backward wind-up and a rapid forward swing, reliably landing objects in a container. Using the actual executed end-effector pose as the action reference (instead of the target pose) caused success to drop to 40%, leading to hesitant throws and missed targets due to trajectory discontinuities.
- **Walking to Clean a Table (Long-Range Loco-Manipulation):** Achieved a 75% success rate (15/20 trials). The robot navigated 1-2 meters, positioned itself for cleaning, and performed effective wiping strokes. Ablations on the keypoint interface showed that an EE-only interface reduced success to 60%, struggling with locomotion-manipulation disambiguation. Tracking non-vision-grounded keypoints with absolute poses (instead of relative) caused success to collapse to 0%, due to uncorrected pelvis-tracking drift.
- **Adaptive End-Effector Reward:** An additional experiment (in Appendix C) for "squat and pick up a bottle" task showed that removing the adaptive EE reward (i.e., enforcing naive tight tracking throughout) decreased success from 85% to 50%. Failures typically involved loss of balance during the deep squat, proving the adaptive reward's importance in balancing stability and precision.

#### 4.2. Generalization Ability

HuMI demonstrated strong generalization due to diverse data collection:

- **Squatting to Pick Up a Bottle:** With 350 demonstrations collected across 7 diverse environments and 7 different bottle instances (in-the-wild data), HuMI achieved a **70% success rate (14/20 episodes) in unseen environments and with unseen objects.** This included challenging conditions like dim stairwells and out-of-distribution objects (e.g., a vase).

#### 4.3. Data Collection Efficiency

HuMI significantly outperformed a state-of-the-art humanoid teleoperation system (TWIST2) in efficiency:

- **Throughput Comparison (Unsheathing Task):** In 15-minute collection sessions, HuMI collected 62 episodes versus TWIST2's 28, achieving a higher acceptance rate (96.7% vs. 64.3%). HuMI's robot-free workflow reduced the average time per acceptable episode to 30% of TWIST2's.
- **Whole-Body Motion Coverage (Marriage Proposal Task):** HuMI successfully collected 50 demonstrations in 15 minutes with 100% acceptance, averaging 18 seconds per episode. In contrast, TWIST2 failed to produce any usable demonstrations for this challenging deep-kneeling motion, as its teleoperated humanoid could not reliably maintain stability. This highlights HuMI's ability to capture complex, highly articulated motions beyond the control limits of teleoperation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Humanoid Manipulation Interface (HuMI) represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robotics, offering a novel and practical solution to several long-standing challenges. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Democratization of Humanoid Data Collection:** HuMI's most profound impact lies in making the process of collecting high-quality, whole-body humanoid manipulation data vastly more accessible and efficient. By eliminating the need for a physical robot during demonstrations and simplifying the hardware to a portable, backpack-friendly setup, HuMI lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners. This could lead to an explosion in the availability of diverse, "in-the-wild" datasets, much like how UMI revolutionized data collection for robotic arms.
- **Enabling Complex, Diverse Whole-Body Skills:** The framework allows humanoids to learn and execute a broader repertoire of complex, dynamic, and bimanual whole-body manipulation tasks than previously possible. By emphasizing whole-body coordination from demonstrations and through specialized control strategies, HuMI pushes humanoids closer to human-like dexterity and versatility. This paves the way for humanoids to perform more intricate tasks in real-world settings, beyond simple pick-and-place.
- **Effective Embodiment Gap Bridging:** The human-in-the-loop kinematic adaptation, combined with the detailed whole-body specification, provides a systematic way to transfer skills from humans to morphologically different robots without compromising task constraints. This approach ensures learned motions are both natural-looking and robot-feasible, directly addressing a critical challenge in imitation learning for humanoids.
- **Robustness and Generalization through Design:** The hierarchical control framework, with its adaptive rewards, variable-speed augmentation, and refined policy interface (target pose as reference, relative blind keypoint tracking), demonstrates remarkable robustness to tracking errors and system discontinuities. Furthermore, the ability to collect data in diverse, unstructured environments leads to policies that generalize well to unseen objects and scenes, a crucial step towards deploying humanoids in real-world, dynamic environments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** HuMI provides a robust foundation for future research in several areas:
 - **Large-scale Humanoid Foundation Models:** The efficient data collection capability can fuel the development of large-scale, general-purpose humanoid foundation models trained on massive, diverse datasets.
 - **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** More natural and capable humanoid manipulation will lead to more intuitive and effective human-robot collaboration.
 - **Application in Unstructured Environments:** The demonstrated generalization capabilities suggest humanoids equipped with HuMI-trained skills could be deployed in complex, dynamic environments like homes, warehouses, or disaster zones.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its significant contributions, the paper acknowledges several limitations. The system relies on visual trackers, which require sufficient environmental texture and lighting, thus limiting operation in completely featureless or dark environments. The low-level controllers, while effective, are not yet general-purpose and require task-specific training. Finally, the evaluation was limited to a single platform (Unitree G1), although the authors anticipate easy adaptation to other humanoids. Addressing these limitations in future work will further broaden HuMI's applicability and impact.

In conclusion, HuMI represents a pivotal step towards making humanoid robots more capable, versatile, and easier to program, effectively democratizing the data collection process and paving the way for a new generation of dexterous, generalizable humanoid skills.
