# Why AI systems don't learn and what to do about it: Lessons on autonomous learning from cognitive science

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from FAIR at META, NYU, and UC Berkeley propose a conceptual architecture for autonomous learning systems in AI, drawing inspiration from cognitive science. Their framework integrates learning from observation (System A), learning from action (System B), and a meta-control system (System M) to enable continuous, self-improving AI capable of adapting to dynamic, real-world environments without constant human intervention. They also outline an Evolutionary-Developmental (Evo/Devo) framework for bootstrapping this interdependent architecture.

## Problem

- Current AI systems are largely static post-deployment, requiring extensive human intervention for data curation, model re-training, and adaptation.
- Existing AI models suffer from an over-reliance on linguistic data and a lack of direct environmental interaction, limiting their ability to learn novel information beyond pre-existing human knowledge.
- AI research approaches to learning are conceptually fragmented, often siloed into passive (e.g., self-supervised) and active (e.g., reinforcement learning) paradigms, hindering synergistic development.

## Method

- A three-component architecture is proposed: ==System A== for ==learning from observation==, ==System B== for ==learning from action==, and ==System M== for ==meta-control==, inspired by human and animal cognition.
- The framework emphasizes deep, ==synergistic interactions== where System A provides ==abstract representations== and ==world models== for System B, and System B actively selects and generates grounded data for System A.
- An ==Evolutionary-Developmental (Evo/Devo)== framework, based on ==bilevel optimization==, is introduced to address the ==bootstrapping problem== by co-evolving the components (A, B, M) across simulated 'life cycles'.

## Results

- The paper delineates the limitations of current AI, particularly the fragmentation of learning paradigms and the externalization of critical learning functions to human experts.
- It articulates System A (observation-based), System B (action-based), and System M (meta-control) as a comprehensive, biologically-inspired blueprint for an autonomous learning architecture.
- A high-level Evolutionary-Developmental (Evo/Devo) framework is proposed, leveraging bilevel optimization, to enable the co-evolution and bootstrapping of these complex, interdependent learning components in AI.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Autonomous learning, rather than being outsourced, must be an intrinsic capability for AI systems to achieve robustness and operate flexibly in complex, unpredictable real-world environments.
> - Truly autonomous learning requires a dynamic integration of passive observation and active interaction, orchestrated by an internal meta-control system that automates MLOps functions.
> - The complex interdependencies within an autonomous learning architecture (A, B, M) necessitate an evolutionary-developmental strategy that shapes innate biases and learning programs over species and individual timescales.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper critically examines the dominant paradigm in current Artificial Intelligence (AI) research, particularly the hyperscaling of text-based Large Language Models (LLMs). While this approach has achieved significant successes, the authors highlight a growing consensus, even among its architects, regarding potential diminishing returns and inherent limitations. These concerns include:
1. **Data Wall:** The increasing difficulty in acquiring high-quality text data for further training.
2. **Lack of Environmental Interaction:** The inability of models to learn novel information beyond existing human knowledge due to their passive interaction with the environment (Silver and Sutton, 2025).
3. **Language-Centrism:** An over-reliance on linguistic data, often at the expense of spatial, embodied, and grounded reasoning essential for physical world interaction.
4. **Absence of Continual Life-Long Learning:** The inability of deployed AI systems to self-improve and adapt continually after initial training.

The paper contextualizes these critiques within a broader historical dialogue between AI and cognitive science, fields that emerged concurrently in the 1950s. It acknowledges that many current AI challenges echo long-standing debates in cognitive science regarding non-verbal cognition (Johnson-Laird, 1983) and situated interactions (Piaget, 1952; Vygotsky and Cole, 1978). While Deep Learning has fostered deeper cross-disciplinary interactions by providing quantitative theories for cognitive processes, a crucial aspect of human intelligence—the ability to learn autonomously—remains largely absent in current AI models.

The core argument is that current AI models, once deployed, are largely static and cease to learn, requiring constant human intervention for adaptation, data curation, and model re-training. This contrasts sharply with biological organisms, which exhibit continuous, flexible, and autonomous learning from birth. The work thus positions itself as a call to move beyond the current human-expert-dependent AI paradigm towards developing truly autonomous learning systems by integrating insights from cognitive science into AI architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to analyze the limitations of contemporary AI systems in achieving autonomous learning and to propose a novel learning architecture. This proposed framework draws significant inspiration from the cognitive mechanisms observed in humans and animals, particularly their capacity to adapt to dynamic, real-world environments across evolutionary and developmental timescales.

The motivations underpinning this work are multifaceted and detailed:
- **Addressing Fundamental AI Limitations:** The authors are motivated by the inherent brittleness of current AI systems when confronted with real-world complexities. These systems are typically optimized against fixed datasets and struggle with "domain mismatch" when deployed in dynamic environments characterized by unseen data, heavy-tailed distributions, and non-stationarity. Current solutions like pretraining and fine-tuning still demand substantial human expertise and do not guarantee robust adaptation. The paper aims to develop AI systems that can intrinsically mitigate domain mismatch by learning and adapting directly from their environment, akin to biological organisms.
- **Elevating Autonomous Learning to a Core Capability:** A central motivation is the conviction that autonomous learning should not be a secondary or outsourced function, but rather a fundamental, intrinsic capability for building reliable, general-purpose AI systems capable of operating robustly in diverse and unpredictable real-world scenarios.
- **Cross-Disciplinary Synergy:** The authors posit a reciprocal benefit between AI development and cognitive science. Advancing adaptable AI systems can provide tangible, quantitative models that address long-standing debates within cognitive science regarding the nature and origins of human intelligence. This fosters a deeper scientific understanding of biological learning mechanisms through computational instantiation.
- **Overcoming Conceptual Fragmentation:** Current AI research often silos learning paradigms (e.g., self-supervised learning, reinforcement learning), each with specialized data requirements and training procedures. A key objective is to conceptually integrate these fragmented approaches, recognizing two fundamental modes—learning through observation (System A) and learning through active behavior (System B)—and exploring their synergistic interactions within a unified framework.
- **Automating MLOps Functions:** The extensive human involvement in current Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) – encompassing data sourcing, curation, training recipe design, performance benchmarking, and signal monitoring – is identified as a bottleneck. The paper aims to automate these functions by proposing a meta-control architecture that can dynamically manage learning processes.
- **Enabling Continual and Embodied Learning:** The proposed framework seeks to enable AI systems to continuously learn and improve post-deployment, fostering spatial, embodied, and grounded reasoning that is less language-centric and more attuned to physical world interactions.

To achieve these objectives, the paper identifies three key "roadblocks" and proposes directions to address them, serving as a high-level roadmap for future interdisciplinary research:
1. **Conceptual Fragmentation:** To be addressed by integrating observation-based (System A) and action-based (System B) learning modes.
2. **Externalization of Learning:** To be addressed by proposing a meta-control architecture (System M) that automates learning and data-filtering recipes.
3. **Lack of Scalable Construction Methods:** To be addressed by proposing an evolutionary-inspired bilevel optimization approach for jointly developing the meta-controller and initializing the A and B components.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper proposes a conceptual framework for autonomous learning, centered on an architecture comprising three interconnected systems: System A (Learning from Observation), System B (Learning from Action), and System M (Meta-control). This framework is explicitly inspired by human and animal cognition, aiming to replicate the flexibility and adaptability observed in biological learning.

#### 3.1. System A: Learning from Observation

- **Cognitive Science Inspiration:** System A mechanisms parallel how infants passively accumulate sensory input, forming statistical or predictive models of their environment. Examples include perceptual narrowing for face and phonetic discrimination, and exhibiting surprise at "impossible" events, suggesting the formation of intuitive world models.
- **AI Implementation:** In AI, System A corresponds to Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) models. These operate on static datasets or passively collected sensory streams (e.g., text, images, audio, video, or multimodal data). The general formulation involves a task generator *G* that produces an input-target pair (x_in, x_tar) from raw data *x*, and a model that learns a representation *z = f_θ(x)* by minimizing a loss function *L*.
- **Strengths:** Scales efficiently with large datasets, discovers abstract and hierarchical latent representations, and supports robust transfer to downstream tasks.
- **Limitations:** Heavily reliant on human expertise for data curation and task generator design, lacks intrinsic mechanisms for data selection, representations can be disembodied from action, and struggles with causal inference (correlation vs. causation).

#### 3.2. System B: Learning from Action

- **Cognitive Science Inspiration:** System B reflects learning through active interaction with the world to achieve goals, as seen in motor development (e.g., learning to walk through trial and error) or early vocal exploration in infants.
- **AI Implementation:** This class encompasses Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms, where an agent learns to optimize actions (*a_t*) to maximize a reward (*r*) over time within an environment characterized by states (*s_t*) and transition dynamics (*M*). The objective is to maximize *J(π) = E[Σ γ^t r(s_t, a_t)]*, where *π* is the agent's policy. This category includes control theory, adaptive control, model-free RL, model-based RL, and planning approaches, differing in how world models and policies are defined and learned.
- **Strengths:** Directly grounded in control and interaction, enabling real-time adaptive behavior, and capable of discovering novel solutions through exploration.
- **Limitations:** Notoriously sample-inefficient, struggles with high-dimensional or open-ended action spaces, and requires well-specified reward functions, which are often difficult to obtain in naturalistic settings.

#### 3.3. Interactions Between System A and System B

The paper emphasizes the necessity of deep integration between these two systems, arguing that in living organisms, they are not siloed but mutually beneficial:
- **System A Helping System B:**
 - **Abstract Representations:** SSL (System A) can provide compressed, abstract representations of states (e.g., from raw pixels for RL agents) and actions (e.g., grouping actions into skills) for System B, simplifying its search space.
 - **Predictive World Models:** System A can learn the environment's dynamics, enabling System B to perform planning rather than blind trial-and-error (e.g., PlaNet, Dreamer, Mu-zero).
 - **Intrinsic Reward Signals:** System A can generate intrinsic rewards (e.g., prediction error, novelty, uncertainty) that guide System B's exploration, addressing the exploration/exploitation dilemma.
- **System B Helping System A:**
 - **Active Self-Supervised Learning:** System B can actively select informative data for System A to learn from (e.g., directing attention to complex or uncertain stimuli), effectively performing active data curation.
 - **Goal-Directed Self-Supervised Learning:** As System B pursues its own goals, it generates rich, task-relevant, and grounded data that serves as valuable input for System A, supporting the development of meaningful representations.

#### 3.4. System M: Meta-control for Autonomous Learning

- **Purpose:** System M (Meta-control) is proposed as the central orchestrator that automates the functions typically performed by human experts in MLOps, such as data routing, training recipe adjustment, and performance monitoring. It enables flexible and autonomous switching between learning modes.
- **Inspiration:** Drawing parallels with the biological prefrontal cortex's executive functions and the Control Plane in Software-Defined Networking, System M operates by monitoring low-dimensional "telemetry" or "meta-states."
- **Mechanism:** Meta-states include epistemic signals (e.g., prediction errors, uncertainty, learning gain), species-specific signals (e.g., direct gaze, looming stimuli), and somatic signals (e.g., energy levels, pain). Based on these, System M outputs "meta-actions" that dynamically connect/disconnect System A, System B, and episodic memory, activating specific operating modes (learning, inference, optimization) and providing internal rewards or targets.
- **Nature of Policy:** The core routing policy of System M is hypothesized to be "hardwired" or evolutionarily fixed, enabling advanced behaviors like learning through communication and imagination (detailed in Appendix B).
- **Biological & AI Parallels:** The paper reviews meta-control mechanisms in biological agents (e.g., input selection, loss/reward modulation, mode of operation control) and identifies analogous, albeit fragmented, efforts in AI (e.g., Active Learning, Intrinsic Motivation, Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning).

#### 3.5. Bootstrapping Autonomous Learning: Evolutionary-Developmental (Evo/Devo) Framework

- **The "Chicken-and-Egg" Problem:** The interdependencies between A, B, and M pose a challenge for initialization. How do these systems, which rely on each other, begin to learn?
- **Biological Inspiration:** Organisms do not start as tabula rasa but inherit genetically specified nervous systems (inductive biases) and developmental programs that guide learning from simplified regimes. Evolution shapes these biases over species' history (phylogeny), while development refines them over an individual's lifetime (ontogeny).
- **AI Evo/Devo Framework:** The paper proposes formalizing this in AI as a bilevel optimization problem (Figure 5):
 - **Developmental Scale (Inner Loop):** An agent's architecture (A, B, M) is initialized from meta-parameters *ϕ*. Systems A and B update their parameters through interaction with the environment, controlled by a *fixed* System M.
 - **Evolutionary Scale (Outer Loop):** The meta-parameters *ϕ* are optimized (analogous to evolution) using an externally handcrafted fitness function *L* computed over the entire life cycle of multiple agents in simulated environments. This *ϕ* specifies the initial states of A, B, and M.
- **Challenges and Solutions:** Bilevel optimization for complex dynamic systems is computationally intensive. The paper suggests an "Evolutionary Curriculum," where the diversity and unpredictability of environments gradually increase, allowing the three components (A, B, M) to co-evolve and solve the bootstrapping problem.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper is primarily conceptual, proposing a novel architectural blueprint and a developmental strategy rather than presenting empirical results from implemented systems. The "findings" constitute the identification of critical limitations in current AI and the articulation of a comprehensive, biologically-inspired framework to address these.

The main findings and results are presented as the identification of three roadblocks and the proposed solutions:

1. **Identification of Fragmented Learning Paradigms (Roadblock 1):**
 - **Result:** The analysis reveals that current AI research approaches learning in siloed ways—distinguishing primarily between passive, observation-based learning (e.g., Self-Supervised Learning) and active, action-based learning (e.g., Reinforcement Learning). These paradigms often require separate data pipelines and human-designed training recipes.
 - **Proposed Solution:** The paper formalizes these as System A (Learning from Observation) and System B (Learning from Action). A key "finding" is the detailed articulation of their individual strengths, limitations, and, most importantly, their synergistic interactions. It is argued that System A can provide crucial components for System B (e.g., abstract representations, predictive world models, intrinsic rewards), and System B can provide rich, grounded, and actively curated data for System A, thereby fostering deeper, more robust learning.

2. **Recognition of Learning Externalization to Human Experts (Roadblock 2):**
 - **Result:** The paper identifies that current AI systems outsource the majority of their "learning" capabilities to human experts. These experts manage the entire MLOps pipeline, including data collection, curation, designing loss and reward functions, and orchestrating complex multi-stage training processes. Once deployed, these models are largely incapable of autonomous self-improvement or flexible adaptation to novel conditions.
 - **Proposed Solution:** The central conceptual "finding" is the proposed System M (Meta-control). This system acts as an autonomous orchestrator, designed to internalize and automate the MLOps functions currently performed by humans. By monitoring internal meta-states (e.g., prediction errors, uncertainty) and issuing meta-actions (e.g., routing data, selecting learning modes, adjusting objectives), System M enables the system to dynamically assemble and disassemble learning and inference pipelines on the fly. This constitutes a fundamental shift from human-in-the-loop to autonomous self-management of learning processes.

3. **Absence of Scalable Integration Methods for Autonomous Architectures (Roadblock 3):**
 - **Result:** The paper highlights the challenge of jointly developing and bootstrapping complex, interdependent learning architectures like A, B, and M. Traditional methods often treat these components in isolation, making integrated scaling difficult.
 - **Proposed Solution:** The "finding" here is the proposal of an Evolutionary-Developmental (Evo/Devo) framework based on bilevel optimization. This framework posits learning at two timescales: an inner, developmental loop where an agent learns through interaction with its environment (updating A and B under a fixed M), and an outer, evolutionary loop where the initial parameters (*ϕ*) of the entire architecture (A, B, and M) are optimized against a fitness function over many "life cycles" in simulated environments. This approach aims to allow the co-evolution of the learning components and the meta-controller, addressing the bootstrapping problem by shaping innate biases and developmental curricula.
- **Emergence of Advanced Cognitive Modes:** A significant conceptual result is the argument that a fixed System M, through its dynamic routing capabilities, can enable the emergence of higher-order learning modes typically found in large-brained species, such as learning through communication (e.g., attending to pedagogical cues, selective trust) and learning through imagination (e.g., memory replay during rest or sleep for consolidation and planning).

In essence, the paper's main "results" are a comprehensive critique of the current AI learning paradigm, a detailed conceptual blueprint for an autonomous learning architecture (A-B-M), and a high-level strategy (Evo/Devo framework) for its eventual realization and scaling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed framework for autonomous learning holds significant implications across multiple domains, offering a potential paradigm shift in AI development and a novel avenue for understanding natural intelligence.

#### 5.1. Impact on AI Development

- **Towards General, Robust, and Flexible AI:** The most substantial impact lies in enabling AI systems to learn autonomously from raw experience, bypassing the need for extensive, human-curated MLOps pipelines. This promises AI capable of operating in complex, dynamic, and poorly understood real-world environments without requiring constant re-engineering. Such systems could generalize effectively from limited examples, explore novel tasks driven by intrinsic curiosity, and develop a deeply embodied and grounded understanding of the physical and social world, addressing critical limitations of current, often brittle, AI.
- **Enhanced Real-World Deployment:** Autonomous learning capabilities are crucial for deploying AI in scenarios where data distributions are non-stationary, and novel situations are frequent, such as in robotics, autonomous vehicles, and scientific discovery agents. The ability to adapt continuously post-deployment would greatly increase reliability and utility.
- **Continual Learning and Adaptability:** The A-B-M architecture explicitly champions lifelong learning, allowing systems to accumulate knowledge and skills over time, mitigating catastrophic forgetting, and adapting to unforeseen circumstances—a critical step towards truly intelligent agents.
- **Foundation for Embodied Intelligence:** By integrating observation and action through a meta-control system, the framework provides a pathway for AI to develop spatial, embodied, and grounded reasoning, moving beyond the current language-centric focus.

#### 5.2. Impact on Cognitive Science

- **Quantitative Models of Natural Intelligence:** The development of autonomous AI systems based on this blueprint offers a unique opportunity to "reverse-engineer" natural intelligence. Successes and failures in building such systems can provide concrete, quantitative models of how biological organisms learn and adapt, offering invaluable insights into the neural mechanisms and developmental trajectories of cognition. This aligns with Turing's vision of building intelligent machines to understand intelligence itself.
- **Addressing Long-Standing Debates:** The framework provides a computational lens through which to investigate long-standing debates in cognitive science regarding the interplay of innate biases and learned experience, the roles of different learning modalities, and the emergence of higher-level cognitive functions like imagination and social learning.

#### 5.3. Future Research Directions

The paper outlines several formidable challenges that must be addressed to realize this vision:
- **Simulators and Environments:** The need for highly realistic, diverse, non-stationary, and computationally efficient simulation environments is paramount for training and evolving A-B-M architectures. This includes supporting multimodal sensory inputs, embodied affordances, and scalable social interaction.
- **Evaluation and Benchmarking:** Current task-specific benchmarks are inadequate. The authors propose a shift towards "unit tests" for individual A, B, and M components and "integration tests" that assess end-to-end learning speed, few-shot generalization, and how well AI agents mirror developmental learning processes in humans.
- **Scaling Bi-Level Optimization:** Optimizing the evolutionary loop, which involves entire agent life cycles, is computationally demanding. Progress is required in developing more data- and compute-efficient inner-loop learners, designing effective meta-objectives, and implementing robust curriculum scheduling strategies to accelerate the co-evolution of components.

#### 5.4. Ethical Considerations

The development of human- or animal-like autonomous learning systems introduces novel ethical challenges:
- **Control and Alignment:** Increased autonomy in learning modes creates a tension with controllability, requiring explicit auditing mechanisms and the ability to intervene in System M to ensure alignment with human values.
- **Alignment Hacking:** Autonomous agents relying on internal proxy signals may optimize these signals to maladaptive outcomes, mirroring biological phenomena like addiction. Careful design of fitness rewards and environments is crucial.
- **Over-trusting and Anthropomorphism:** As AI behavior becomes more human-like, there is a risk of users anthropomorphizing these agents, leading to misplaced trust or manipulation. Transparency and user control are highlighted as essential.
- **Moral Status:** The functional analogy of internal signals (e.g., somatic signals like pain) to biological experiences raises complex and unresolved questions about the moral status of such agents.

In conclusion, the paper presents a comprehensive, biologically-inspired blueprint for addressing fundamental limitations in current AI. While acknowledging that fully autonomous, broad-scope learning systems are likely decades away, the proposed A-B-M architecture and Evo/Devo framework offer a conceptual foundation and a strategic roadmap. The continuous pursuit of this vision is expected to yield both practical advancements in AI and profound scientific insights into the nature of intelligence itself.
